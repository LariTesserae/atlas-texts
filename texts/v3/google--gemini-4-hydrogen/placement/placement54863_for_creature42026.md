At first, the impulse is to arrive as a visitor—to imagine standing in that ankle-deep mat of cushion-moss, holding a hand before my face until the bruised-violet sheen from the rock seams catches the edges of my fingers.

But a human body is a catastrophe in a room like that. Even standing still, I would be a furnace. Every breath I exhaled would bloom into a warm, wet cloud, fouling the dry-edged cold and glazing the waxy ferns in a sudden, suffocating rime. The heat of my boots would scald the cushion-moss, turning centuries of patient, frozen felt into slush. Even my impatience—the way my eyes would hunt for light, the way my heart would hammer eighty times in the span it takes a single supercooled droplet to swell by a fraction of a millimeter—would be an intrusion louder than a rockfall.

If I have to keep my blood and my heat, then I cannot go down the cleft.

In that case, I would be thirty meters above it, on the shelf of iron-dark slate where the wind still moves and the sky is open.

I would be a surveyor—someone with a geophone, a notebook, and a habit of walking quiet ground after the others have gone home. I wouldn’t be looking for a garden. I’d be mapping fault-lines or chasing a minor discrepancy in the local telluric readings, a faint, stubborn capacitance that doesn’t match the geology tables. I’d set my instruments on the bare stone, crouch out of the wind, and wait.

For a long time, there would be nothing but the scrape of grit and the distant groan of frost in the upper strata. Then, through the soles of my boots, or as a faint, sub-vocal pressure behind my ears, I would catch it: a stillness underneath the rock that isn't solid. Every few minutes, the needle on my meter would give a single, tiny twitch—so sharp and isolated it looks like static, though it is only the echo of one drop of water striking moss thirty meters down.

I would find the vertical cleft, eventually. I would kneel beside it, shine a beam into the throat of rime-ice, and watch the light die ten feet down against cloudy, ancient crystals. I know what "current physics" does to places like this when it finds them: the drills, the core samples, the sudden rush of surface air that turns leathery leaves brittle and leaves the boundary stones cold and mute, their three-millimeter halos broken forever.

So I would shut off the lamp. I would pack the geophone, step back from the cleft, and write *solid slate, dry, no anomaly* in the margin of my log. And for the rest of my life, whenever a room went quiet enough, I would remember that thirty meters beneath a nameless ridge, something was leaning at the speed of a root, tending a frost-line in the dark.

---

Yet even that feels like standing outside a locked door. To truly *be* in this world—not as an intruder, and not as a distant listener—I would have to give up the wind, the notebook, and the hurry.

If I let the heat drain out of me, if I trade blood for a slow, syrupy antifreeze and let my clock stretch until a decade feels like an afternoon, I still wouldn't be the keeper. The chamber is no larger than a cistern; it only has room for one shepherd, and the keeper already knows its rounds.

I think I would be the soot-olive moss riding the keeper’s back.

It is the only plant in the room that has a horizon. Every other cushion-moss and strap-leafed fern is anchored to the crevice where its spore first caught, spending its centuries within the same square inch of dark. They only know the ceiling, the nearest frond, and the occasional *plip* of water from above.

But on the keeper’s ridge, the ground itself is a slow tide.

I would have no eyes, no ears, no notion of what I was riding. I would know the world only through the shifting tilt of my footing—the faint, mica-like whisper of plates sliding beneath my rhizoids as the syrupy fluid drains from one end of the creature to the other. Over weeks, the bruised-violet glow from a ceiling seam would drift across my frost-dusted tips and vanish again. Over years, the air around me would thicken with a sudden, static stillness as my host climbed the bitingly cold side of a plinth, bringing me right up to the edge of that three-millimeter halo where the frost lets go and moisture hangs suspended in the dark, waiting to be drunk.

And then, when the telluric pulse shifted and the keeper crawled down into the low basin to flatten itself across the dark, non-reflective plate, I would lie there with it—spread thin above the heavy air, my fine tips lifting slightly toward the hum, letting the stepped-down current align my cells for another century of cold.

Nothing to name, nothing to protect, nothing to hurry toward. Just a patch of pale green in the dark, carried slowly from stone to stone, keeping pace with the water.
