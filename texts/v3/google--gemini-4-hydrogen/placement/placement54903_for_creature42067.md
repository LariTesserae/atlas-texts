I would be found about two-thirds of the way down the cleft, in the amber-lit dimness where the blade of sky-light never reaches, hanging upside-down or wedged sideways beneath a sorting-mill’s iron belly.

That is where the quiet is made, and where it wears thin.

Up on the catwalks, the riggers and mill-hands walk upright, their grounding-chains ticking a steady *tick-tick-tick* against the iron grating. Down where I work—in the narrow, dusty crawlspaces between the undersides of the platforms and the raw, sweating-dry slate—there is no room to stand. You move on elbows and felt-soled knees, tethered by a short leather strap to a girder, with your catch-rag pulled high over the bridge of your nose so the falling mica doesn't cake your nostrils.

I am a pad-mender. Some tiers call us *mufflers*, though the older Plumb-keepers just tap two soft, flat beats on a stay-rod when they want us: the signal for *something is singing that shouldn’t be*.

People who have never lived inside a split mountain think silence is just the absence of work. Here, silence is an engine you have to grease every shift. Because the air is too thin to carry a shout, the whole town speaks through its iron bones—tapping wrenches on guy-wires, reading the tremor of a turnbuckle through the soles of their boots. If a single crusher’s bearing dries out and begins to shriek, or if a motor’s mineral-wool pad packs down flat and lets the housing chatter against the frame, that vibration bleeds into the girders. It travels three tiers up and four tiers down, muddying the cables like static on a wire. A crew might miss a Plumb-keeper’s warning tap because a loose chute-plate two hundred feet below is rattling in the same pitch.

So I live in the undersides, hunting down noise before it can learn to travel.

My sleeping-locker is a narrow riveted box bolted beneath the Third Mill’s counterweight shaft—a good spot, because the big iron weight passes my wall every four minutes with a dull, predictable *tock* that rocks me to sleep better than any cradle. My whole frame is six feet by four, with lifting-eyes at the corners. Inside, there is my bunk, a sealed kettle for leaf-infusion, a jar of tallow for my knuckles, and my press: two heavy slabs of oiled ashwood clamped with brass screws.

Between those boards, I make *web-felt*.

I know every spider-colony between the Fourth and Ninth tiers the way a baker knows ovens. You learn patience from them. When a rigger looks under a catwalk and sees a grey, drooping curtain of dust-choked silk, he only sees something that hasn't fallen yet. When I look at it, I press the back of a bare knuckle gently against the fringe. If it still gives like dry hair, I leave it; the spiders aren't finished drinking the air. I wait until the floating slate-powder has weighed the silk down into a thick, sagging blanket, heavy enough to hold a crease. Then I draw my horn knife, slice it cleanly along the girder’s lip—leaving the upper anchor-threads intact so the weavers don't scatter—and fold the grey sheet into my canvas satchel.

Pressed between the oiled boards for two shifts, the silk and stone-dust mat together into a dense, cool felt that smells faintly of ozone and old grease. There is nothing else like it in the spire. It doesn't rot, it doesn't burn from a stray spark, and it drinks vibration the way dry cloth drinks tallow.

When I am out on the girders, I carry a listening-rod: a finger-thick dowel of seasoned ash tipped at one end with a blunt brass cone. I don't trust my ears alone; the thin air lies about direction, making a scrape ten feet below sound as if it's right behind your shoulder. Instead, I press the brass tip to a motor’s mounting-bolt and set the wooden butt against the bone just behind my ear.

Through the wood, the town opens up. A healthy mill has a clean, low *thrum*—copper coils turning smooth in a bed of web-felt, like blood moving in a vein. A dry chute makes a papery *hiss* that tells you the oiled hide lining is curling at the edge. And a failing bearing has a tight, sour whine that sets my teeth on edge long before a rigger’s boot would ever feel it.

I am not alone down there. The oil-ticks are my best scouts. Whenever I crawl up to a hopper-casing and see the copper-shelled beetles clustered in a calm, sandy shimmer over the warm iron, I don't even bother unstrapping my listening-rod; I just move on. It’s when I see a bronze tide of them pouring off a housing and streaming out along the power conduits that I reach for my spanner and tap the *cut-power* code onto the nearest stay. They feel the wrong heat in their legs before the wood can even carry it to my skull.

Sometimes, too, I find a rock-vole curled inside a roll of spare felt in my satchel, stealing warmth from the wool. I don't shoo them unless they start chewing the leather drive-straps. Once, an old grey-muzzled male followed me for three weeks along the Ninth-tier conduits, waiting while I packed a Plumb-keeper’s wicker cradle with fresh felt, just to lick the tallow off my thumb when I finished. When he shed in the spring, I gathered the soft down he left in the corner of my press and used it to wipe the mica-glitter off my goggles.

I don't go up to the top often. The few times I’ve climbed to the upper gantries to deliver fresh felt for the sky-ward winches, the razor-thin ribbon of pale daylight made my eyes water and my skin prickle. Up there, the dust glitters like cold sparks, and you can hear—just faintly, through the rock—the roar of the wind outside, tearing at the black ridges where nothing can hold on. When a cliff-swift drops out of that bright slit, banking silently between the swaying cables with outside-dust on its moth-pale wings, I always find myself pressing one hand flat against the nearest iron girder, just to be sure the wall is still there.

Open air is for wings. I belong to the thirty paces between the walls.

When my shift ends, I crawl back to my locker, pull the copper wire braided into my hair free of any tangled silk, and brush the slate-dust from my joints with stiff horsehair. I take a thimble of warm, sulfurous water from my stoppered flask—just enough to wet the inside of my mouth—and pull my catch-rag up over my chin before I lie down.

Above me, the drive-belts go *whir-slap, whir-slap* in the dark. Beside my ear, the rock gives a tiny, harmless *creep*—a fraction of an inch, settling into itself—and the counterweight drops past my wall with a soft, felt-muffled *tock*. Everything is quiet enough to hear the town breathing. That means I did my work right.
