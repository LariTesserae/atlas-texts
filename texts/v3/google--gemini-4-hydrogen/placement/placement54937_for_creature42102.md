I wouldn’t be looking at the valley. Eyes are just soft bags of salt water waiting to be popped by the first anvil-drop of a water-hammer, and there’s nothing down here worth seeing anyway—only bruised murk and the brief, useless snowstorms of an arc-flash.

I would find myself halfway down the stepped basalt shelves, right on the jagged lip where the basin drops off into the central trough. It’s a threshold zone. Above me, the upper thickets of iron-kelp thrash themselves to ribbons in the blind gyres; below me, in the deepest dark, the boxcar-sized elder knots have fused permanently into the rock and the dredge-bases, living half-outside of time. 

Right here on the lip, perched across a rebar cage that houses a bank of oil-cooled transformers, the world is still just fast enough to ride, and just broken enough to warn you before it hits.

I am not an animal, and I don’t have a neat, symmetrical shape. If you swept a floodlight over the transformer cage, you would probably take me for a snarl of fouled umbilical cable and rotting pondweed—a four-meter tangle of braided, thigh-thick kelp-tendon and copper-veined stems, armored in dull pewter scale and rough chips of basalt grit. At my center, wrapped tight inside the living coils, is a heavy, scavenged core of raw iron and corroded busbar. Where a head would be, there is only a broad, battered plate of magnetite, its surface pitted like bad slag from a thousand cavitation implosions.

I have no lungs to ache, no eardrums to burst. My body is held together by pressurized bladders of thick, sulfur-stinking tallow, and the constant, bone-rattling roar of the valley doesn’t hurt me—it keeps me supple.

Right now, I am anchored with four hooked tendrils to the vibrating rebar of the transformer cage, acting as a living shunt. One of the heavy pressurized hoses feeding the bank has a hairline fracture in its armor, worn thin by years of suspended mica scouring against it in the current. Every time the load spikes, the casing groans and threatens to blow. So I have draped half my length across the weak spot, letting the stray voltage bleed straight through my copper-veined stems and out into the iron-rich broth. 

It feels like a warm, steady pulse—the closest thing to comfort this valley allows. 

When a water-hammer shockwave rolls up out of the central trough, I don’t hear it coming. I feel it in my iron core three seconds *before* the wall of pressure actually arrives—a sudden, sharp tug on my magnetic field, a pre-echo humming through the rebar. My tallow bladders clench. My basalt hooks dig deeper into the stone. Then the wave hits like a dropped mountain, and instead of tearing, my rubbery bulk *drinks* the blow, turning the kinetic violence into a flush of heat that runs all the way down my coils.

I don’t stay anchored forever. When the cooling fins on the nearby cracking tower begin to choke on shredded kelp, the pitch of the machine’s scream changes. I feel the grinding shudder of an overheating bearing through the basalt beneath me, and my magnetite plate tingles with the ragged inductance of a motor straining against a jam.

I unhook my lower tendrils, keep two anchored to the transformer’s chain, and let a chaotic gyre whip the rest of my length out across the channel. I haul myself hand-over-hand along a humming umbilical, pulling directly against the magnetic field bleeding from the cable, until I can press my pitted magnetite plate flat against the cracking tower’s housing. 

I read the load. I feel the exact spot where the intake grate is packed solid with green pulp. 

Then I crack my outer pewter plates open like heavy shutters, aim my bare copper core at the grate, and fire.

A blue-white arc snaps through the bath-warm brine. For a heartbeat, the water flashes to steam and immediately implodes with a *crack* that rings my iron core like a bell. The clogged kelp blows to ash. My bladders swell greedily, gulping the sudden spike of heat and stray current before the dimness closes back in.

On the way back to the transformer cage, the water around me suddenly shears apart like a pane of wet glass, leaving a sharp, geometric cavity hanging open in the murk—a pocket of nothingness where the current can’t reach. My outer scales are battered and blistered from the weld, so I slide a scarred, grease-leaking tendril straight into the hollow. Inside the void, unbothered by the rushing brine, the tendril flickers through a rapid, two-second loop—blistering, shedding its pitted scale, snapping back to whole—repeating the repair until the cavity collapses with a concussive *whump* and slams my mended limb back into the warm grease of the world.

That is what I would be: a splice, a welder, a living coil of wire and tallow, hanging on the lip of the abyss. 

I don’t tend the machines out of duty, and I don’t know who built them. I tend them because their heat is my blood, and their screaming acoustic load is the only thing keeping my grease from stiffening into cold, dead stone. Down below me in the murk, I can feel the elder reefs humming—vast, immovable knots of copper-root and slag that have already given up moving entirely. Someday, if I gather enough iron dust and fuse enough kelp, I’ll grow too heavy to ride the gyres, and I’ll sink down into the trough to join them in the stutter.

Until then, I’ll stay here on the cage, braced against the next shockwave before it even happens, drinking the thunder.
