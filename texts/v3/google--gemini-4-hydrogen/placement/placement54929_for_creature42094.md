I would not be up on the rim. Even imagining the lip of the basin—the wind tearing moisture straight off the teeth, the sky with nothing to lean a shoulder against—makes my knees want to lock in a way that doesn’t belong to me.

If I am anywhere in this world, I am three ladders down from the glare, in the blue-grey shadow where the limestone hasn’t warmed since the earth cracked open.

I’d know myself first by my feet and my knees. Three-ply boiled hide under my soles, scored pale where the chert grit bites, and my weight pitched slightly forward over the balls of my feet so I never drop a heel hard enough to wake an echo. My shins and forearms would be wrapped tight in oiled linen—nothing bare, because the rock here doesn’t forgive a careless elbow—and my wool sleeves pulled down over the backs of my hands whenever I step out of the sun-shaft into the cellar-cold slots at the bottom.

I wouldn’t be one of the pryers down at the very deepest face, swinging the hide-wrapped mauls and waiting for the basalt to *tick*. And I wouldn’t be one of the haulers, climbing the timber scaffolding with wicker baskets of damp rubble to dump into the wind.

I would be a **seam-mender**—one of the ones who stays in the middle-low tiers, working the mortar-pits and the slate catch-basins, following the *plip* of water with a clay lamp and a bone trowel.

In a town built out of fresh fracture, stone is always trying to let water go. A cistern plastered last season will take on a new weight from the tier above it, shift by the width of a fingernail, and suddenly a hairline crack opens behind the reed mats. You don’t see it at first. You hear it—a change in the rhythm of the catch-basin, a *plip* that has gone flat, or the faintest dry sigh of dampness soaking back into hungry basalt instead of pooling where it can be ladled.

That is where I live: in the quiet half-light behind the stairs and beside the mixing troughs, where the air smells of wet limestone, thyme-steeped tallow, and pale shelf-fungi growing on spent, water-split wedge timbers.

My hands would give me away before I ever spoke. Lime-and-ash mortar is dry, greedy stuff; it drinks the oil straight out of your skin until your knuckles split in neat red lines and your nails turn chalky and brittle. Every evening I’d sit on a low block outside my cubby, rubbing rendered fat and crushed thyme into the creases of my wrists until they shine. My palms would be thick as boiled leather from the wooden mixing-paddle, yet my fingertips—kept clean, scraped free of dried lime with a bit of slate—would stay weirdly soft, trained to run along a cold wall in the dark and feel the difference between ordinary condensation and a seam that is weeping from somewhere deep.

At first light, before the sun drops straight enough to hit the east wall’s upper herbs, I’d be awake and listening. The oldest seep-watchers pass my lane then, stooped and careful, their bone scrapers tucked into their belts and their clay lamps throwing small, steady ovals of yellow across the wet rock. Sometimes one of them taps twice on my lintel—not with a tool, just the flat of a knuckle against the mud-brick. That means they’ve found a new sweat-line high on a fresh chert face, or a cistern corner that feels hollow to the scraper.

I’d sling my wide sedge hat down my back, tuck a lump of slaked lime wrapped in damp hemp into my pouch, and follow them into the narrowest alleys, turning sideways without thinking so wool brushes wool and nobody’s elbows catch.

Working a seam is slow, hushed business. You don’t chip wildly at unweathered rock; you listen to it first. You press your ear near the dampness—close enough to smell the raw mineral grain, cold as cellar iron—and wait to make sure there isn’t a pocket of trapped groundwater hissing behind the slab, or a lintel groaning under a fresh shift. Only when the stone is settled do you take the bone chisel and score a thumb-wide channel to lead the trickle down to the slate. Then you press the mortar into the joint with the heel of your thumb, smoothing it until the stone forgets it was ever broken.

Most days, the ash in my mixing trough is ordinary—grey scrub-brush ash hauled down in sacks from the plain, slaked with saved cooking water.

But every so often, a family comes down the ladders at dusk carrying a single clay jar, still faintly warm, stoppered with twisted grass. Someone who spent forty years prying out the lower tiers, or tending the roof-herbs, or watching the seeps, has been burned out on the open stone where the wind takes the smoke. The jar holds what the wind didn’t take.

I don’t say much when they hand it over; nobody here wastes breath on long speeches. I clear a clean hollow in the lime-pile, tip the grey wood-ash in, and pour just enough water from the cistern to wake it—listening to the soft, brief hiss as it slakes. Then I take it up to whatever wall or catch-basin is being raised that week, and press it between the razor-edged blocks with my bare thumbs.

It isn’t a grand thing to be, in a place like this. There are no songs for the person who keeps the cisterns from leaking. Yet when the noon sun drops straight down the rift and turns the shadows sharp as knives, and I’m sitting in the cool dark under the stairs eating bitter-cress and steamed shelf-fungi from a wooden bowl, listening to the steady, clear *plip* of water hitting slate—I know that every drop held in the basin is held, in part, by the skin of my hands.

And in a world made entirely of sharp edges and open sky, being something that helps the wound in the rock hold what it needs—that feels like enough.
