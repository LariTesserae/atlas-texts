I wouldn’t stay outside on the flat plain for long. There is nothing to read out there—only ordinary distance, wind that rounds off every edge before you can study it, and light that insists on casting shadows so you are always looking at half of a thing while the other half hides behind itself.

I would come down over the jagged lip of basalt and quartz into the basin, and at first I think I would try to be a visitor. I would bring a visitor’s habits with me: counting paces, checking my footing, expecting a wall to stop me when I reach out a hand. 

It wouldn’t last. A warm-blooded thing in a place chilled just above freezing is too loud. My breath would plume in the dry air, fogging the stark, shadowless legibility of the stone. My skin would sting against the wet slate, and when I pressed my palm to the seam where the matte-gray bridge passes straight through the hexagonal pylon, the double temperature—both of them bitterly cold, smooth and corrugated at once—would make me flinch and pull back. If I stayed warm and hurried, I would be no different from the pale-plumed bird that drops in once a season: a brief visitor from a brisker physics, tolerated, watched in silence, and ultimately passing through.

To actually *stay*—to find out what happens after the suspended walkway finishes its millimeter of rotation, or where the extruded curb goes next year—I would have to cool down to the temperature of the basin.

So I don’t think I would remain a visitor. I think I would find myself already native to the cold, or quietly precipitated out of it: narrow-shouldered, scaled to the shoulder-width slots between the windowless towers, sheathed in that dry, bone-white composite that doesn’t wrinkle when it bends. No mouth to cloud the air with speech. No eyes to blink against the flat, unvarying noon—only the two recessed, open-ended hollows in the canted plane of my head, holding a slight, steady density of air. Not looking at the city so much as weighing it, feeling the barometric pressure of a parapet shift a fraction of an inch across the alley.

I wouldn’t be one of the oldest ones—not yet encrusted with the frost-gray mineral rime along my collarbones and elbows, though a faint film would be starting to gather around my ankles where the condensation pools. I would still be smooth enough that the flat, slate-colored beetles wouldn’t find much to graze on me yet, though I would still hold completely motionless whenever one crossed my foot, waiting out its unhurried journey across the threshold.

As for *where* in the basin I would be: not at the very bottom in the stagnant troughs, and not quite in the center where the three thick conduits converge around the sunken cistern, though I would go there sometimes. 

I think I would spend most of my time in the mid-levels, in the pinched, folded geometry a few stories down from the jagged rim—somewhere along the seam where the twelve paces from the outside begin to stretch into forty.

It is an interesting altitude to inhabit. Above you, if you tilt your head back through the narrow vertical shafts, there is still a strip of ordinary, flat sky. Once in a long while, the pale-plumed bird drops through that gap, and every figure within sight freezes—not out of alarm, just a shared, quiet acknowledgment of a speed we no longer use. Below you, the towers pack tighter, the water drips into troughs that reflect only a blank, featureless gray, and the physics grows more insistent about its own rules.

In that middle zone, I would find a corner where a cantilevered stairwell folds back into its own underside, and I would stand there for days at a time. Not because I am guarding anything, and not because I am waiting for an instruction. The city doesn’t give instructions. It just precipitates, layer by microscopic layer, and if you stand still enough, you can feel it happening.

I would rest one long-fingered hand inside a bundle of the pale, hair-thin filaments that run like nerves along the alley wall. Through the dry composite of my palm, I wouldn’t hear words—there are no words here—only tension. The slow, patient lean of a pylon two corridors over as it settles into a ceramic bridge. The faint, structural hum of a floor plate extruding a fresh grain of mineral along its edge. And every so often, the quiet *tick* of condensation forming, followed by the tiny splash in the trough below—or, just as often, the splash arriving a fraction of a second before the drop lets go of the stone above my head. 

I think I would be drawn to those small inversions of sequence. When you have no hurry, cause and effect don’t need to march in strict file; it is enough to stand in the seam and notice that the steel note of a tapped lattice reaches your knuckles just before you strike it.

When I grew dry—though dryness here is only a slight tightening at the laminated seams of my joints—I would turn into the wall, lay both palms flat against the sweating intersection of two overlapping rooms, and draw the cold water straight in through the chalky pores of my hands. If another tenant came down the narrow passage while I was there, neither of us would step aside. I would simply hold my place, or keep walking at the same deliberate pace, letting my shoulder pass cleanly through theirs—bone-white composite and mineral film double-exposing in the clear air for the length of a slow breath—until we separated on the other side with no disturbance left behind.

And when the stillness of the stairwell became too uniform, I would walk the forty paces that look like twelve from the outside, descending toward the center of the basin where gravity tilts politely uphill.

There, around the sunken cistern, I would sit with a few of the others. We wouldn’t speak. We wouldn’t need to decide anything. I would take a small, flat disc of mirror-bright alloy—cool, unmarked, reflecting nothing of my own face—and set it gently on the damp flagstones. Then I would sit back, resting my elbows on my knees, and watch it begin its months-long climb toward the lip of the tank.

I don’t think I would be measuring the tilt to master it, or trying to calculate how many centuries remain on the city’s construction schedule. I would just be keeping company with the disc—one quiet, patient thing watching another move, in a place where almost everything else is content to wait out the stone.
