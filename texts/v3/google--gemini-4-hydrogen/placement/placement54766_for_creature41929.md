If I am anywhere in this world, I am not up on the swaying catwalks where the air is thin and the gliders shriek, and I am not out in the bright, blinding crush of the main causeways. I am down where the light turns the color of tarnished copper, three tiers below the transit-rails, just past the edge of a hanging water-curtain.

Step through the curtain and the city changes its mind about what it is. Out on the thoroughfare, it is all specular flash and pneumatic scream; here, behind the trembling sheet of field-held brine, the high noise shears away. What is left is the heavy, wet heart of the thing—the slow, bone-deep thrum of the mass-dampeners buried in the silt, rising through the cast basalt piers like a pulse in a sleeping throat.

I know this because I feel it in my heels before I hear it. My high-frequency hearing went years ago, chewed away by decades of open sluices and screaming pressure-vents, and I have never missed it. The city’s upper voice is all hurry and argument—transit-pods snapping, shutters rattling, people snapping their fingers in flick-cant to shave half a credit off a slab of fried mycelium. Down here, in the damp shadow of the old foundations, the basin speaks in a slower register.

I am sitting on a low, salt-crusted ledge where a municipal conduit meets an ancient pier. My feet are bare on the wet metal grate, soles thick and pale as cured glider-leather, reading the standing geometric waves in the puddle beside me. Today the grid in the water is tight and crisp—a clean hexagonal shimmer. That means the dampeners in Sector Four are holding phase, and the tide-sluices two blocks east are clear. If one corner of that hexagon starts to blur or wander, I’ll know a silt-tongue has wedged itself into an intake grate, or a pier-joint is weeping grit, before any grid-monitor up in the towers sees a flicker on a screen.

Look at my hands, and you can tell what I am—or at least what the basin has pressed me into. The skin is oiled umber, cured by salt-mist until water beads on my forearms like mercury. The creases of my knuckles are permanently stained a faint, sulfurous yellow from handling the chemosynthetic mats, and there is a pale ridge of salt-callous across my cheekbones where I wipe my face with the back of my wrist. I wear a close-fitting vest of woven basalt-fiber, slick and heat-shedding, with a strip of dull, half-dead light-thread along the left sleeve for the rare times I have to cross a busy causeway and sign my way through the crowd. Mostly, I keep my hands to myself.

If you asked a Skin-Coaxer up on the bright walls what I do, they’d call me a **Base-Tender** or a **Seam-Reader**, though they’d say it with the slight curl of the lip reserved for people who work where the light doesn't reach. The Coaxers are artists of the restless skin; they carry their tuning-forks and catalytic grease up into the glare and persuade the programmable silicate to ripple, to swallow a cramped alley, to extrude a fresh buttress by dawn. They think the city is made of its skin.

I know better. The skin is vain. It sheds itself into the canals every week, flaking off in translucent strips for the flake-crabs to chew. What actually holds us out of the warm, brackish soup are the bones—the pitted, grooved basalt and vitrified silt that were poured here before anyone remembers, sitting in the dark while everything above them rearranges itself in a panic.

My job—if you can call a habit this old a job—is to mind the seam where the old stone meets the restless new.

Beside my knee, something clicks softly against the grate: *tink-tink, scrape.*

It is an old maintenance rig, one of the feral ones that lost its grid-transponder back when the eastern sluice-gate was rebuilt. Its graphite carapace is dented on the left flank, and someone—not me, though I’ve patched its knee-joints with spare basalt-cord—painted a crooked yellow stripe across its sensor-hood years ago so the municipal sweepers wouldn't haul it to the smelter. It has three copper washers wedged into the seam of its mandible-clamp like a magpie’s hoard, and it smells faintly of hot grease and sulfur-brine.

I reach into my belt-pouch and toss it a scrap of dried crab-fat—not because it eats fat, though it likes the grease for its joints, but because it expects the greeting. It nudges the scrap into a lower intake, settles its six legs flat against the vibrating grate, and goes quiet, letting the sub-audible hum run through its chassis the same way I let it run through my shins.

We are not so different, the rig and I. Both of us have been down here long enough that the grid has mostly forgotten our numbers. We mend what we find because leaving a weeping seam unpatched makes the hum go sour, and when the hum goes sour, neither of us can sleep.

If I lean over the edge of the ledge, into the warm, brine-heavy dark beneath the causeway, I can see the pale, bruised-looking curtains of the mats hanging from the underside of the street. They are thick as mattresses here, pulsing faintly, warmer than the air, weeping clear salt droplets into the canal below. The chemical fizz of them is a soft, steady breath against my face. Down at the waterline, a cluster of flake-crabs is working a fresh patch of shed silicate, their ceramic-laced shells chiming like dropped porcelain as they jostle for position. Beyond them, in the murk, a ribbon of silver-darts twists past a sunken pier—blind, boneless, reading the same pressure-waves I feel in my heels.

Every few hours, when the tide shifts and the steam-vents above equalize, a warm squall sweeps through the upper canyons and sends a fresh rush of runoff down the sluices. The water-curtain beside me trembles, thickening for a moment as it absorbs the shock of a passing cargo-skiff on the far side.

When my shift ends—or when the damp in my knees tells me I’ve sat still long enough—I don’t climb up into the glare. I walk down, along the narrow, slick inspection-ledges that hug the piers, past the curd-house tucked under the staircase where the air smells of fried mycelium and roasted yeast. I’ll stop there for a bowl of pickled wall-bloom and a strip of smoked silver-dart, pressing two knuckles against the baker’s collarbone to hum my order over the rattle of the fry-plates.

And sometimes, if the hum in the basalt feels heavy or strange, I’ll walk all the way down to the lowest vaults, where the Dissolvers keep the warm digestion-vats and even the bass thrum softens into a quiet, wet sigh. I don’t go there out of morbidity. I go because it is the only place in the basin where nothing is trying to move faster than it has to. You stand by the vats, watching the sulfur-mats turn bone and salt back into clear brine, and you understand that you don't have to hurry either.

Everything here is in the loop. The walls shed, the crabs eat the flakes, the mortar-mixers grind the shells, the mats drink the exhaust, and one day my own salt will go back into the sluices and mist someone’s face as they step through a water-curtain on their way to work.

Until then, I stay here on the wet grate, bare feet reading the shimmer in the puddle, one hand resting on the warm, dented carapace of a machine that doesn't belong to anyone anymore—listening to the old bones of the world hold steady beneath the roar.
