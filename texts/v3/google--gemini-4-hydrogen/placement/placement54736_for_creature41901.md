I would not be on the floor of the shelf. 

Down there, the rock is too new and too unforgiving—every step a negotiation with fractured glass, every midday hour a slow poaching in the trapped glare. And besides, the floor already has its keeper. It belongs to the heavy, mineral-plated body that uncoils at dusk, to the pale amphibians pulsing in the basins, to the silica reeds and the bruised-plum ferns.

If I were to find myself in this world, I would look up—to the one part of the closed loop that has no steward yet.

I would be up under the overhang of the jagged back wall, where the rock juts out over the terrace like a broken brow. Down on the shelf, the sun strikes with a flat, relentless clarity, but up here, tucked beneath the lip of the basalt, there is permanent shade. It is a narrow, vaulted world of dark stone and rising mist, five or ten paces above the steaming pools.

From here, the whole cathedral-nave of the terrace lies spread out below me, foreshortened and softened by the trembling air.

What kind of body would survive up here? Not a heavy one. Weight is an enemy when you live inverted on fractured rock. I would not carry the thick, precipitated armor of the keeper below; I would fall, and the basalt blades would cut me open before I hit the water. Instead, I would be flat, broad, and surprisingly light—something like a cross between a limpet and a mantid, or a leaf of dark, flexible slate that learned how to breathe.

My back would be pressed close to the ceiling, armored not in heavy shields, but in a tough, overlapping shingle of dull, heat-cured chitin—the same matte black as the basalt, so that from below I would look like nothing more than a shadow wedged into a seam. My limbs—eight of them, perhaps, splayed wide to distribute my weight—would end not in blunt pads, which would slip on wet stone, nor in heavy needles, which would snap under tension, but in twin hooked spurs of dark, cloudy glass, sharp enough to bite into the microscopic pits of the rock.

Because I live in the shade of the overhang, out of the blinding high-altitude glare, I could afford to keep what the keeper below gave up: eyes. Not large, restless eyes—there is nothing here to hunt, and nothing moves fast enough to require them. Just two narrow, recessed slits beneath the forward rim of my carapace, veiled by a clear, protective membrane that keeps the hot mist from clouding them. Through them, I would read the terrace in slow shifts of light and vapor.

I would be a creature of the ceiling, and my purpose—though I wouldn’t think of it as a purpose—would be the turning of the water.

All day, while the shelf below bakes in its fever, the ghostly ribbons of steam rise from the basins and climb toward me. They reach the cooler, shaded underside of the crags and stall. This is where I live: right at the threshold where invisible heat turns back into weight.

Along my flanks, tucked beneath the edges of my black shingle-plates, I would carry rows of pale, finely ribbed membranes—not quite gills, not quite feathers, delicate as wet paper. When the midday thermals rise, I unseal them slightly, letting them fan out into the damp air. The steam catches in their ridges. It clings, cools, and beads. 

I do not drink all of it. Most of it is meant to go back down. 

As the beads swell and merge, they run along the grooved channels of my underside, gathering at the narrow, tapered point of my tail. I hang there, motionless as a stalactite, feeling the tiny weight build at my tip until surface tension gives way—and *tock*. A single heavy drop falls through the shimmering air into the simmering pool below. Then another. And another. 

I am the slow pulse of the ceiling. The steady, arrhythmic *tock* that breaks the silence of the terrace is the sound of my breathing, translated into water.

As for how I live—what feeds me—the rising thermals bring more than steam. They carry the faint, invisible drift of the terrace: the microscopic spores shaken loose from the bruised-plum ferns when the heat flexes their fronds; the fine, sugary dust of dried algae lifted off the dry stones; the occasional tiny, windless mite blown up from the reed-stalks. My pale membranes trap these too, combing the warm air like baleen in a slow, vertical sea. When the afternoon cools and I draw my membranes back beneath my plates, I clean them with a rough, narrow tongue, taking in the distilled warmth and the sparse harvest of the updrafts.

From my perch, I would know the whole geometry of the shelf without ever touching it. 

I would feel the matte-gray disc out in the center not through the soles of my feet, as a walker would, but through the stone itself—a faint, steady thrumming that travels up the sheer back wall and vibrates in the glass hooks of my toes. At midday, I would watch the water leap in tiny, silver pearls off its surface. Across the expanse, toward the cliff-lip, I would see the three bone-white struts leaning together, and the strange, thick bend in the sunlight between them, like oil suspended in air.

And in the late afternoon, when the shadows of the western rim finally stretch across the floor and cool the stone by that single, necessary degree, I would watch the wet buttress near the base of my wall uncoil. 

From above, the keeper looks even stranger—a slow, plated island of moss moving with quiet certainty between the pools. I would watch it nudge the fallen silica reeds aside so the mineral veins can run clear. I would watch it shear the glowing bladder-mosses, and step with delicate *clicks* into the central basin to drink the leaping pearls from the disc. We would never touch, and it would likely never know I am above it, except perhaps as a slightly faster *tock* of falling drops when the afternoon mist thickens on my ribs. Yet we are parts of the same machine: it keeps the channels open on the floor so the water can pool and rise; I catch what rises and send it back down.

When the sun finally drops below the jagged rim and the cold tide begins to creep up from the void, the overhang chills first. That is my warning. 

Before the damp can turn sour and rot my membranes, I retract them tight beneath my black plates, sealing the seams with a thin, waxy sweat. I pull my eight hooked limbs in close and wedge myself deep into a dry, wind-sheltered pocket at the very back of the overhang—a crack too narrow for the mist to follow. 

Below me, I see the keeper step between the bone-white struts, fold its glass legs, and settle into the one ash-dry circle on the terrace. 

Then the light goes out of the steam. The hum of the disc seems to deepen in the dark. Tucked into the ceiling of the world, I go completely still, indistinguishable from the fractured basalt, waiting for the sun to strike the shelf and send the warmth back up to me.
