I would not be one of the swallows, frantic and soot-backed, throwing themselves into the pale air above the mist. Nor would I be a heron, balanced on thin, cold stilts at the spillway’s edge, waiting for a single silver twitch in the shallows.

If I belong anywhere in the sinkhole, it is down where the weight is. I am one of the older builders—thick-shouldered, low-slung, my slate-colored guard hairs stiffened along the flanks with dried limestone silt until I look less like an animal and more like a wet, breathing boulder. My eyes are small and milky around the edges now, suited for nothing more than telling the green gloom of the canals from the pitch-black of the under-streets. It doesn’t matter. Down here, you don’t read the world with your eyes. You read it through the soles of your webbed hind feet, through the broad, flat cartilage of your tail, and through the pale, stiff whiskers bristling along your muzzle.

My world begins in the middle tier of a three-story lodge, wedged tight against the northern cliff-base.

I wake not to light—light barely reaches through three feet of packed river-clay and living willow-root—but to a change in the hum. Even in sleep, pressed nose-to-tail in a shallow bowl of shredded reeds with four of my kin, my jaw rests against the packed earth, and the earth carries everything. All night, the valley speaks in a low, steady pressure: the *drip-drip* of the weeping limestone outside, the muffled *scritch-scritch* of a young mother in the nursery above me trimming back a stubborn willow shoot before it can split the ceiling, and the vast, silent weight of the settling pools leaning against the peat dams.

Then, a fraction of a tone shifts. Not a sound in the air—the air here is too thick and still to carry much—just a faint, bone-conducted *give* in the timbers. Somewhere two terraces up, the meltwater has found a seam.

I uncurl slowly, careful not to wake the kits sleeping in the warm hollow of my belly. I smell of castoreum, fermenting sedge, and old mud; the chamber smells of wet fur and crushed green bark. I nose my way past a sleeping sister, our whiskers brushing in a brief, silent acknowledgment, and shuffle down the narrow spiral ramp to the mud-room.

Here, the air turns sharp and cold. A bundle of fresh alder-twigs sits half-submerged in the silt, stripped of its outer bark. I don’t stop to eat. I slide off the lip of the plunge-pool—no splash, just the heavy, slick *plop* of oil-dense fur meeting near-freezing water—and let the dark take me.

This is where I am most myself. On the causeways, I am a clumsy, wedge-shaped thing, dragging my belly over algae-slick clay. Underwater, I am effortless. I tuck my forepaws tight against my chest and drive forward with broad, webbed strokes, gliding through the flooded basements beneath the city.

The under-streets are pitch-black and choked with stripped willow-roots that hang down like pale hair. As I pass, my wake stirs up slow clouds of white chalk from the floor, and in the eddy behind me, the pale newts drift out from the fissures—milk-white ghosts with coral-pink gills fluttering like tiny ferns. They scatter from my flank without fear, snapping up the blind water-lice I’ve kicked loose. Above my back, separated by only a few inches of packed clay and woven alder, I can feel the muffled *thump-thump* of a neighbor’s hind foot on a causeway, signaling *all clear, all steady*. I answer with a thin stream of silver bubbles from my nostrils, sending a low, guttural hum vibrating through the black water of the tunnel.

I surface in an upper settling pool, rising silently between two retaining walls. The mist is thick here, hanging motionless over the flat water, smelling of cold peat and bog-myrtle. Up on the rim of the world, a hundred feet above my head, the wind is probably tearing at the moorland grass, but down in the bowl, nothing moves except the mist and the quiet, constant trickle of the cliff-walls.

Two other builders are already there, their broad, chalk-streaked heads barely breaking the surface. We don’t look at each other. We touch noses once—a quick, snuffing check of scent and temperature—and turn toward the northern peat dam.

A thin, bright thread of water is hissing through a gap where a root has rotted back. It isn’t a crisis, not yet, just the valley’s endless, slow insistence on falling apart. We fall into the rhythm without a signal. One of the younger ones dives straight to the canal floor, surfacing a moment later with a heavy armful of blue-gray clay clutched tight to his chest. I swim to the tangled root-skirt of the nearby retaining wall to drag out a soaked willow-bough, my orange incisors sinking deep into the soft, waterlogged wood.

As I tug it free, a shadow slips past my shoulder—lean, dark-brown, and silent as spilled oil. A mink. Its sharp little face turns toward me for half a heartbeat, whiskers twitching, before it vanishes back into the dark gap beneath the boardwalks. I let it go. It smells of wet iron and blood; it has been hunting the water-voles in the roof-gardens above, keeping them from riddling our dams with their burrows. It has its seam, and we have ours.

I haul the bough backward through the slick mud and wedge it across the hissing seam, bracing my heavy shoulders against the current until the younger one can pack the blue-gray clay around it. Another builder joins us, slapping the wet clay flat with rapid, rhythmic pats of her forepaws and the heavy, scaled weight of her tail. Within minutes, the hissing stops. The water goes flat and silent again. The valley is whole for another hour.

By the time I haul myself out onto the sloped, mossy roof of a lower lodge, the pale daylight is fading into a deep, wet green. Frost is already beginning to rime the shaded limestone ledges high above, and the mud-swallows are pouring back into their cliffside cups in silent, swooping arcs, their last droppings pattering softly onto the ferns around me.

I sit on the roof-thatch, heavy and dripping, my silver-gray undercoat still bone-dry beneath the matted slate of my guard hairs. Around me, the lodge roof is a dense, tangled garden of sedge and curled ferns, bound together by the living roots of the house itself. Fresh green willow shoots are sprouting right through the mud-plaster, eager and supple, trying to turn our home back into a thicket.

I reach out with broad, clawed forepaws, grasp a thick green wand, and shear it off at a clean diagonal with a single snap of my teeth. The bark is sweet and bitter at once, cold with evening mist. I sit there in the gathering dark, chewing slowly, listening to the *drip-drip* of the cliffs and the faint, steady pulse of the water pressing against a thousand dams—knowing that tomorrow the shoots will grow back, and the water will find another seam, and we will be here, in the quiet below the wind, to hold it all in place.
