I would not come here as a climber, with rope-burned hands and boots slipping on the glass-edged scree, nor as one of the long-gone builders who left their copper chisel marks in the dark rock and vanished. A human body in this bowl is an intrusion of soft skin and ringing eardrums—something the hornfels wants to cut and the sound wants to shake apart.

To belong here, I would have to be built to the measure of the stone.

I would find myself on the third tier of the eastern wall, wedged inside the hollow pier of a stacked arch where the mist collects and turns to dark, cold water. Down here, out of the sun, there is no hurry and almost no light. My heart beats three times, then pauses, then beats again—a slow, heavy pendulum inside a cage of thick ribs. Water sheets down the sixty-foot drop-shaft beside me, striking the tuned slate at the bottom with a steady, cavernous *thrum* that I do not hear so much as become. My head is smooth along the sides, sealed against the air; the roar does not batter me. It simply holds me in place, like deep water holding a stone.

I am long enough to span three paces of an aqueduct, slender enough to thread its shoulder-width channel without scraping my flanks, and heavy enough that the dry-laid masonry settles a fraction of a millimeter beneath my weight. My hide is the lightless charcoal of fresh hornfels, ridged along the spine with pale, mineral-hardened plates. I am not the oldest in the basin—my shoulders carry only thin seams of emerald moss in the joints between the plates, not the full, trailing gardens of beard lichen that drape the ancient one down in the lowest catch-basins—but I have been here long enough to know every loose shard on my tier.

When it is time to move, it is not light that wakes me. It is a change in the weight of the shaft’s song.

High above the rim, the afternoon sun clears the ridge and strikes the upper cliffs. The mist stops condensing. Within minutes, the fat, cold drops feeding the eastern cisterns begin to thin, and the deep *thrum* beneath my chest drops half a tone, losing its heavy, drowned edge. The slate at the bottom of the shaft begins to ring a little sharper, a little drier.

I uncoil.

There is no rush in it. My split-toed feet find the wet, unweathered ledges of the shaft, the thick fibrous pads gripping the razor-sharp edges without yielding or tearing. I test each dry-laid stone before I commit my weight—a habit as old as my bones—and climb out through the sluice-mouth onto the eastern gallery.

The sun here is sudden and clean, thin mountain heat that smells of wet slate and crushed fern. I haul myself out onto the cantilevered slab, press my belly flat against the dark rock, and let the warmth sink through my plates. For a long while, I do nothing at all. A thick-shelled snail is inching across a chisel mark three spans from my snout, its pale body gleaming against the black hornfels. I watch it with one slow eye, then close the lid. I am not hungry today.

Instead, I lower my broad, flat jaw directly onto the stone.

Through the bone, the deafening air vanishes, and the basin opens up into something vast, intricate, and legible.

Every wall is a map of tension and falling water. Through the hornfels, I feel the deep, steady pulse of the four main cisterns, the bright, metallic *clack-clack-clack* of the upper runnels, the faint, rhythmic tremor of a fern frond trailing in a spill-gate on the opposite cliff. Somewhere below me, a pale-bellied newt kicks once in a catch-basin, a tiny wrinkle in the dark vibration. Above me, beneath the highest arch, the two dark mountain birds drop back into their lichen nest, their light claws ticking twice against the mortarless joint before settling.

And there—on the western wall, three hundred paces of empty air away—something is wrong with the chord.

A high runnel that should be chiming against a slate lip has gone dull. Its sharp *tink* has softened into a muffled, wet *thud*.

I lift my jaw from the stone and turn my head slowly toward the sun, letting the heat gather in my shoulders before I move. Then I slide off the gallery and onto the narrow bridge of the cross-aqueduct. The water here is ankle-deep and racing, icy against my fibrous pads, pouring toward the central drop. I walk its length with deliberate, unhurried grace, my tail trailing behind me in the channel, breaking the flow into a soft hiss that only I can feel through my soles.

At the western junction, I find what muffled the note: a bolster of emerald moss, swollen by three days of cloud-mist, has slumped over the lip of a sluice-gate, choking the runnel’s fall. Beside it, a frost-split wedge of hornfels has dropped into the channel, trapping a tangle of maidenhair fern.

I hook one blunt foreclaw behind the wedge and lever it up. It comes free with a wet suck, and I nudge it over the parapet. It falls sixty feet through the empty air without touching a wall, then hits the scree at the bottom with a sharp, glassy *crack* that ripples up through the cliffs into my ribs.

Then I use the edge of my broad, flat snout to scrape the swollen moss back from the stone lip. The water surges forward, spills clear of the gate, and strikes the slate shelf below.

*Clack.*

Crisp, bright, and true. The chord of the western wall snaps back into alignment, ringing cleanly through the marrow of my jaw.

I linger there for a moment, grazing a ribbon of broad-leafed saxifrage from the damp parapet—my flat teeth shearing the tough stems close to the stone—until a new vibration reaches me through the bridge.

From the shaded cisterns on the far southern rim, where the sun never quite reaches the water, the ancient one is speaking.

I press my chest to the aqueduct and listen.

Across the basin, the steady *thrum* of the southern drop-shaft stutters. Three slow beats of silence—a heavy leg dipped into the upper channel, holding back the sheet of water—then a sharp, double *chime* as a loose slab of slate is nudged an inch closer to the lip. *Silence. Chime-chime. Silence.*

It is not a warning, and it is not a command. It is simply the old one acknowledging that the western runnel is clear again, and noting that the air is cooling. Up near the rim, the shadows are already lengthening across the quartz seams, turning the glitter back to matte charcoal. In an hour, the clouds will settle into the bowl again, and the walls will begin to weep.

I lift my leg and set my foot squarely across the narrow channel beside me, damming the water for two slow breaths—long enough to send a brief, flat *hush* down the eastern drop-shaft—then step clear and let it fall.

*Thrum.*

An answer, small and exact, folded into the endless sound of the stone.

Then, before the chill thickens enough to slow my limbs, I turn back along the narrow bridge toward my pier, ready to sink into the dark water and wait for the next sun.
