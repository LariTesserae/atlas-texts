I think I would be found near the edges of the knots, rather than at their frantic centers—down in the grey twilight of a third-level alley that is already beginning to dry out, or out on one of the long, bare causeways where the stone is polished like soapstone and the glare hits you from below as hard as from above.

If I were born to the sinkhole, I wouldn’t be one of the fastest runners on the stone-slings. My build and my temperament lean toward the slower, quieter arithmetic of the basin: the reading of weight, the measuring of dampness, the patience of dismantling. I would be one of the ones who walks behind the heavy crews, carrying a bone lever worn glossy by hands before mine, and a small pouch of salt-cured fungus cakes at my hip.

You would know me by the way I stand still when everyone else is trotting.

Most of the day, I would be in the alleys where the gypsum roofs cut the white sky down to thin, blinding slits. Down there, the air is cold and smells of wet chalk and old iron. My tunic—felted fungus-fiber that never quite dries—would cling to my ribs like damp bark, keeping the heat down. Each morning, before stepping out onto the upper terraces, I would dip two fingers into a small, chipped bowl of ground fungal soot and smear it thick beneath my eyes, pressing it into the skin until the glare stops aching behind my brow. Even then, stepping out into the open bowl makes my second, milky lid flick sideways across my eye without my asking it to.

My voice would be nothing—a narrow, unused thing. Like everyone else, I would breathe only through my nose, keeping my lips shut against the fine mineral grit that hangs in the air whenever a wall comes down. Instead, my hands would do the talking. A quick two-finger flick toward a hairline crack in a retaining wall; a flat palm pressed against my own collarbone to tell the crew behind me that the silt-bed underfoot has gone soft. 

When you spend your life in a place where a shout can bounce off the limestone for three seconds and rattle a pocket of trapped air into collapsing, you stop thinking of sound as something you *make*. Sound is just a thing that leaks out when something goes wrong: the dull *tock* of a hide-wrapped mallet hitting a wooden wedge, the low grinding sigh of a terrace giving way, the dry, papery patter of scavengers spilling out of a wall we’ve just pried open.

That is where I would spend most of my hours: taking things apart.

A crew of us—six or eight, bound together not by blood so much as by the shared rhythm of our hands—would be sent to an old cistern that has gone dry, or a retaining wall that is no longer catching the mist. While the younger ones scramble up to lift the split gypsum roof slabs, I would kneel at the base of the wall, sliding the flat tip of my bone lever into the unmortared chinks. 

The moment the top weight lifts, the scavengers come out. Hundreds of them, dog-sized, their pale, translucent shells clicking softly against one another as they stream across my ankles and vanish into the joints of the next standing tower. You learn not to flinch; they are only the city’s stomach, clearing out the dropped fiber and the damp grit so the stone stays clean. Through their backs, you can see the dark grey pulp of what they’ve eaten—the same paste we scrape from the lowest seams to pack beneath our flagstones.

I would be the one who checks the stones as they come down. You can tell with your palms if a limestone block is still good, or if the mineral water has eaten it hollow from the inside. The good ones go onto the woven slings to be carried thirty yards away, where a new damp seam has just glistened dark on the white floor. The rotten ones get left for the paste.

And in the pewter-grey hours, when the sky dims and most of the crew curls together in the narrow compounds for warmth, I think I would still be awake—not hauling, just walking the outer causeways with a long bone staff. 

That is the time I would belong to the most: the quiet between shifts, when the frantic anthill motion slows to a crawl and the city’s other inhabitants come out in full. A pair of long-necked grazers would amble past me on the slick stone, their pebbled hides beading with cold condensation, their flat teeth making that steady, rhythmic *shhh-shhh* as they rasp the black fungal film from a damp baffle. I wouldn’t drive them, only clear a sharp flint chip from their path with the end of my staff so their soft, pad-like feet don’t split, and tap a gentle rhythm on the nearest stone to steer them toward a fresh bloom in the lower runnels.

Sometimes, out there on the empty causeways, I would stop and press the flat of my sodden foot-pad hard against the limestone, closing my eyes—both sets of lids—and just listen through my heels. 

Beneath the polished floor, the basin is never really asleep. You can feel it if you are still enough: a faint, cold *shudder* traveling up through the bone of your shin, the slow groan of trapped air shifting in a pocket ten feet down. It feels like the city is turning over in its sleep, deciding where the next cistern will crack, where the next plume of cold mist will vent.

When I feel that shudder, I don’t run. I turn back toward the nearest compound, duck into the dark, crowded doorway where my crew is sleeping, and tap the pattern of the seam onto the nearest forearm—three short beats, a pause, and a long press—waking them without a single breath of sound, so we can gather our bone levers, our single copper pin, and our slings, and move the walls before the ground gives way.

And when my knees finally stiffen too much for the stepped terraces, and my hands can no longer hold the lever steady against a shifting slab, I know where I would go. There would be no grief that makes a noise, no stone wasted on a tomb. My crew would unpin my damp tunic, take the copper pin and the glossy bone from my belt, and carry me up to the deepest, wettest crevice at the basin’s rim, where the cold condensation drips thickest from the chalk cliffs. 

They would leave me in the dark, out of the glare at last, where the velvety black fungus creeps slow and patient over everything, and the scavengers work quietly through the joints—until there is nothing left of me to carry, only a little more damp mineral paste to be trodden down, scraped up, and wedged beneath the next wall.
