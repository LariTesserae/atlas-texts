I wouldn’t be in the green-filtered middle of the chamber, where the powdery moths drift and the light from the ceiling rift makes everything look almost like the world above. I’d be further back, in one of the bruised corners where the twilight gives up entirely and the basalt still holds the dull, steady warmth of whatever cooled it.

At first, there wouldn’t be much to distinguish me from the underside of the overhang I cling to. My body is flattened to the stone’s geometry—not pressed against the razor-sharp glassy ridges, which would score even tough chitin, but suspended a hair’s breadth below them, hooked by the tips of my legs into the tiny, porous vesicles of the rock. I don’t spend heat to keep myself warm; I borrow it from the mineral behind my back and the thick, cellar-sweet air rising from the fungus-gardens a few yards away.

If you looked for me with a lamp, you would probably miss me until the light caught the damp sheen on my carapace, or the two impossibly long, hair-thin whips extending from my front—legs that gave up walking a long time ago and became something closer to ears, or fingers made of nerve.

In a chamber with no soil, no silt, and no wind, the rock is a drumhead. Nothing muffles it. Even though the pocket is nearly silent to the air, through the basalt I can feel the entire city breathing.

I don’t see the blind termites—their tunnels are sealed, and there’s nothing to see in my corner anyway—but I feel them as a constant, low-grade hum in the stone: thousands of soft bodies streaming through mud-arcades, mandibles rasping grains of clay from the softer pits, wet mortar being patted into place millimeter by millimeter. It feels like a pulse in a wrist, steady and unhurried.

Above that pulse, lighter and sharper, comes the spaced *tick… tick…* of a mud-daubing wasp stalking along the spine of a causeway. I know when one pauses at the edge of the light-shaft, because its rhythm breaks; I know when it snaps a moth out of the air, because a faint, dry tremor runs down the cantilevered paper gallery and into the wall. They never buzz their wings. None of us make a sound that could carry across the open stone.

Mostly, I wait.

To move fast here is to spend heat you can’t easily buy back, and to misstep on wet, freshly fractured basalt is to slice yourself open. So I stay where I am, drifting my sensory whips just above the slick film of condensation, reading the humidity as it thickens. I can feel a drop of water swelling on a ceiling ridge three feet away—not by sight, but by the tiny shift in the air’s weight before it lets go.

*Tock.*

It hits an aroid leaf. Across the bare expanse of rock, I feel the faint recoil of the plant’s stem, and then the slow, wet drag of a translucent snail turning toward the shattered droplets.

Every few hours—though there are no hours here, only the slight cooling of the air when night settles over the scrubland above the rift—something wanders too far from the moss-islands or gets weighed down by the damp. A springtail flicks itself off a fern and lands in the dark; a moth whose wings have gathered too much mist spirals down into the corner where the geckos don’t bother to look.

I don’t pounce. Pouncing belongs to the sunlit world overhead. I simply shift my weight from one vesicle to the next, silent as a shadow sliding across wet glass, and gather what the dark has brought me.

I think that is who I would be here: not one of the builders who raise the organ-pipe chimneys and mend the damp highways, and not one of the green-lit drifters beneath the rift. I would be one of the listeners in the deep corners—a flat, patient geometry of hooks and hair-thin nerves, keeping the score of every drop of water, every footstep on paper, every breath of the buried gardens, in a place that has forgotten how to hurry.
