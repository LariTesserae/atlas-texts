I would not be in the great central terraces, where hundreds of tenders move through the tiered fungal gardens and the air carries the faint, overlapping hum of a dozen walkways. I would be further out, at one of the perimeter sluice-stations—a low, three-room vault tucked directly beneath the basalt lip of an outer evaporation pan, three miles down the ceramic causeway from the nearest spire.

Out here, the under-structure is small enough that you can feel the whole geometry of the plateau in the soles of your feet.

I would be one of the tenders who keep the outer valves. My body would be shaped by the altitude and the dimness: long-ribbed to pull enough breath from the thin air, skin the pale, damp gray of wet sinter, kept slick with its own oil so the salt never takes hold. My hair would be shaved close to the bone, my eyes wide and pale-rimmed under a heavy brow, tuned to the amber glow of a single hanging filament.

Mostly, I would spend my hours in the stillness of the manometer alcove.

There is no chair in the alcove; you stand, or you squat on your heels with your back against the warm stone, watching the wall of thick glass tubes. Inside them, columns of mercury and pale-blue brine rise and fall by fractions of an inch, connected by buried copper lines to the mirrors and sluices half a mile away. To someone from another world, it would look like nothing was happening. To me, it would be a slow, legible conversation. A hair’s-breadth tremor in the third tube means a magnetic barge has passed over the junction; a sluggish, reluctant creep in the seventh means silica scale is beginning to choke the intake valve beneath the pan, thickening the water’s passage like plaque in a vein.

When the noon glare presses down on the roof-slab above me, the whole station warms by three degrees. The air smells of wet chalk, old leather, and the faint, metallic tang of the distillate dripping from the ceiling into the stone runnel beside my ankle. Every few hours I cup a ceramic ladle under the weep, drink the cool, flat water, and splash a little across the back of my neck.

My work is mostly grease and patience. In the adjacent chamber sit the hydraulic bladders—huge, dark sacs of cured hide and resin that swell to lift the surface sluice-gates. I move between them barefoot, rolling heel-to-toe so my calloused soles make no slap against the wet floor, rubbing rendered pan-grazer fat into the creases of the leather. If a bladder dries, it stiffens; if it stiffens, it cracks; if it cracks, it hisses. And in a city built on silence, a hiss is an emergency.

When I need to speak to the tender at the next station down the line, I don’t use my voice. I press my palm flat against the warm basalt wall beside the aqueduct pipe, wait for the stone to settle, and hum low in my chest—a long, steady note, broken into three short pulses, then two. Half a minute later, the stone answers under my fingers: a faint, distant thrumming that travels through the rock itself, telling me the afternoon barge has cleared the third dike.

Sometimes, though, the third tube shudders in a way the wall can’t fix. A meniscus stalls. Something out on the pan—a crust of precipitated salt, a wedged grazer shell, a stiffening joint—has caught in the sluice-gate.

Then comes the slow dressing. I pull on the heavy cork-and-ceramic boots, feeling the world’s low-frequency hum sharpen through the soles. I soak the thick stone-fiber mantle in the condensation trough until it is heavy and cold, drape it over my shoulders, and settle the blackened bronze visor over my face. Through the two narrow slits of smoked mica, the amber gloom vanishes, replaced by a thin, manageable band of shadow.

Stepping out of the stairwell into the midday plateau is like walking into the mouth of a kiln. The white glare hits the bronze mask with an almost physical weight. The air is thick, scalding, and utterly still. Steam rises from my wet mantle immediately, wrapping me in a small, private cloud that smells of hot stone.

I walk the way the stilt-feeders walk—slowly, lifting each foot cleanly from the oily sheet of brine so as not to disturb the surface tension. Around me, the mirrors tilt by imperceptible degrees, their copper manifolds humming just below the threshold of hearing, a greasy vibration rising through my heels. A stilt-feeder stands twenty paces off, motionless as a reed, its reflection stretched long and liquid in the mirrored water. It does not look at me. A bladder-wing drifts down from the nearest spire, bumps softly against my wet hood with a tiny, harmless prickle of static, and floats away on the rising heat.

At the sluice-gate, I kneel in the wrist-deep brine, feel beneath the lip with a copper scraper, and pry free a wedge of pale sinter that has grown around the hinge. The gate settles back into its seat with a soft, wet sigh. Nothing splashes. Nothing clatters. I stand, wait a moment to be sure the water levels itself without a ripple, and begin the slow walk back toward the stairwell.

But the hour I would live for comes later, when the sun drops toward the rim of the plateau and the high-altitude air cools just enough to break the heat.

The rising vapor collapses all at once into a blinding, luminous white mist. The glare dies. Down in the vault, I feel the ceiling begin to weep faster, the *drip, drip, drip* quickening in the stone runnel.

I unfasten the heavy bronze visor and leave it by the door. I shed the damp mantle. Bare-headed, wearing only my slick trousers and mineral-fiber tunic, I walk back up the terraced steps into the whiteout.

On the causeway, the mist settles cool and clean onto my scalp and shoulders. The air is suddenly soft enough to breathe deep. Along the dikes, pale shapes emerge from the fog—tenders from the neighboring nodes, moving in quiet lines to unload the salt-cakes from the stalled barges and haul up the wicker predator-traps from beneath the bridges. We pass each other with a slight nod, a brief touch of oiled forearms, no words needed.

For one hour, before the night’s chill draws the heat out of the basalt and sends us back underground, I stand on the wet ceramic dike with a basket of fresh mist-water at my feet. Ten feet away, half-dissolved in the fog, two stilt-feeders wade through the shallow pan, dipping their siphons into the brine. Neither of us makes a sound. We are simply two different shapes of the same quietness, sharing the steam until the stone cools.
