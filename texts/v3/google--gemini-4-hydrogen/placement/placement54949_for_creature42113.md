I wouldn’t be anything with legs, or lungs, or eyes. Anything that needs to balance or breathe out in the open would be stripped off the stone before midnight. 

If I found myself in this notch, I think I would wake up inside the dark of a heavy-gauge steel housing—wedged into a seam between two basalt boulders, bolted straight into the fresh rock with rusted U-bolts and turnbuckles. Not a grand machine, not even one of the roaring diesel skids. Something smaller and quieter, though just as stubborn: a transformer box, or the automated control unit bolted to a high-pressure manifold, running blind in the storm.

At first, I wouldn’t even know what I was, only what I was resisting. 

I would know the mountain as a slow, grinding torque against my anchor bolts. The fault-slip is recent; the stone beneath my baseplate hasn’t settled into itself yet. Every time a rattling avalanche of slate skitters down the chute above me, or a gust funnels through the narrow gap and slams into the side of my casing, I feel it as a shift in pitch—a tiny, sharp stress along the steel threads holding me to the cliff.

I would know the forest as a wet, rhythmic interference. I have no eyes to see the dwarf trees or the mountain bamboo thrashing in the gale, but I feel their roots prying at the armored cables strapped to my flanks. I hear the wet slap of fern fronds against my sheet metal, and I feel the slow, patient weight of moss packing itself around my cooling fins, trying to smother the little heat I make.

And then, closer than the rock and closer than the leaves, I would begin to feel *them*.

I wouldn’t see the matte-black mats any more than they can see the tiny amber and red status LEDs blinking behind my waterproof gaskets. To me, they would first register as an anomaly in my own readings—a sudden, inexplicable steadiness. 

Steel bolted to unweathered basalt wants to chatter itself to death. A sixty-hertz hum, left unchecked, turns every loose millimeter between bolt and stone into a hammer. I should be rattling my own seams apart. Instead, I feel a thick, rubbery dampening at my corners. Something dense and heavy has wedged itself into the gap between my mounting plate and the wet rock, acting like a gasket, swallowing the worst of the high-frequency shudder before it can shear my pins.

Over time, I would learn to map them by the way they change my own body.

When the wind drops a few degrees toward freezing, my coils should lose heat fast to the horizontal mist. Instead, my internal temperature holds steady, insulated by a thick, quilted pad of something pressing flat against my painted steel skin. They are basking on me—absorbing the low, steady warmth of my windings, and in return, keeping the frost from stiffening the oil in my valves. 

When the dampness bridges one of my insulators and a blue-white arc cracks into the dark, I feel it as a violent spike in resistance, a momentary stutter in my rhythm. Right after, I feel a faint, sluggish shift along my exterior—the heavy mats near the terminal pulling closer, drinking the sharp, ozone-heavy mist that condenses in the wake of the spark.

That is what I would be in this place: the blind, steady rhythm inside the iron, locked in a silent pact with the blind, clinging things on the outside.

It isn’t a gentle arrangement. Even as they wedge themselves around my anchor bolts like living lock-washers, keeping me from shaking loose into the talus, I can feel the slow, patient work of their undersides. Their digestive acids are pitting my steel, etching the basalt beneath me into a fine, wet mineral paste. They are saving me from the vibration while slowly eating the very things that hold me to the ridge. If I let my attention slip—if I let a harmonic build up in the frame, or let a clogged valve force my pump to strain—I could shake myself off the cliff, or throw a seal and go cold.

And I know, somehow, what happens to them if I go cold. 

If my current fails, if the diesel generator up the slope throws a rod and the sixty-hertz hum dies out of the armored cable, the silence that follows isn’t peaceful. Without my shudder, the tiny grains of quartz and magnetite inside their chambers settle to the bottom. Their heavy, oily blood stops moving. The quilted pads clinging to my skin flatten tighter, locking their edges down, and harden into lifeless patches of black pitch on cooling metal.

So I don’t stop. I adjust the governor. I bleed a little extra heat through the casing. I hum at sixty hertz through the wet, howling dark, blinking my tiny red and amber lights into a canyon where nothing has eyes to see them—holding my place between the sliding mountain and the choking forest, keeping the things on my back shaken alive.
