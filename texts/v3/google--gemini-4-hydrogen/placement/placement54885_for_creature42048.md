If I arrived here as I am—upright, heavy-lunged, opaque—the room would reject me in minutes.

Three paces high is just enough to stand, four paces across barely enough to turn without catching a shoulder on a keen edge of silicate. My breath would rob the closed air of its thin oxygen; my skin would dry and crack in the kiln-heat while sweat pooled uselessly at my heels, salting the fresh water. Worse, I would be the only clumsy, solid thing in a space made for translucence. Even without a directional light source to cast a clean shadow, my body would block the glow of the floor beneath me, creating a small, awkward smudge of dimness where none belongs. I would be an interruption, noisy and temporary.

So I don’t stay upright. To belong here at all, I have to let go of the vertical.

I kneel first, then lower myself toward the only margin in the room that isn’t hostile: the jagged lip of the basin. Away from the air’s dry weight, closer to the water, the world immediately changes scale. The ceiling—three paces up, impossible and seamless—stops mattering. What matters is a gradient of half a degree between the stone that hisses when a drop hits it and the stone that stays wet.

Eyes are the next thing to go. In light this fierce and directionless, sight has nothing to grip. There is no horizon to measure distance against, no shadow to give an edge depth. Even with eyelids shut, the white glare presses faintly through. So I stop looking, and the room finally becomes legible.

Without sight, I find myself not in the center of the bright floor, but tucked into the lee of a small silicate overhang, half-submerged where the water meets the air.

Here, I am flat. I am small enough that a single stone lip feels like a roof, and segmented enough—seven pale, overlapping plates—that I can curve into the tightest crease of the rock without snapping. The light still passes straight through me, warming my insides to a dull ochre, but I no longer register it as brightness. To me, it is only a steady, ambient warmth, like summer held permanently inside a stone.

I read the pocket through my underside and through the short, stubby palps tapping the wet grit ahead of me. I know the geography by slickness and by temperature: the bare, glassy rock that burns too dry; the narrow, cooler band where the chalky-orange film takes hold, gritty and faintly alive under my mouthparts.

Sound is my weather. Above me, a bead of water breaks free from a high seam and strikes a hot lower shelf with a soft *tiss*. It means nothing—too high, too dry, a brief hiss of vapor that never reaches the basin. Then another drop falls straight into the water.

*Plink.*

The sound doesn’t travel to me through ears; it arrives as a clean, bright ripple across the plates of my back, followed a heartbeat later by a pulse of cooler water that stirs loose flecks of the orange crust. Without thinking, I graze faster, scraping the mineral skin with comb-like mouthparts, leaving a faint, clean trail behind me on the pale stone.

I didn’t begin in this wide, blazing room. None of us did. Before the fracture opened, my world was the hair-thin seam itself—a tight, mineral dark (or a light so compressed it had nowhere to bounce), where you moved only by squeezing flat as a leaf-vein and following the invisible, frictionless pull of moisture through the lattice. That is still what feels like home: pressure on the back, stone close enough to touch on every side. Even now, with three paces of empty air above the basin, I keep to the overhangs, hugging the jagged rim as if the ceiling might remember itself and press back down.

When the water grows too thin of air, I don’t swim out into the middle. I climb a little way up the weeping wall, into the taut, glassy sheet of moisture clinging to the vertical rock. There, half in the moving film and half in the kiln-heat, I let the feathery gill-plates beneath my segments beat in their quiet, steady rhythm, pulling breath from the boundary where water and air meet.

If another of my kind crosses my ledge, there is no startle and no contest. Our palps tap once—a brief check of slickness and warmth—and then I either glide flatly over its amber back or kick sideways off the stone, darting across the warm basin in a single, crisp motion before tucking beneath the opposite rim.

There is no urgency in this life, and no loneliness. Nothing hunts here; nothing decays fast enough to foul the water. Every so often, when my shell grows too tight, I wedge myself into a quiet crevice on the submerged shelf and slip backward out of my plates, leaving the empty husk to drift like a flake of mica until the heat softens it or another grazer nibbles it down for the lime.

And then I go back to the edge of the orange crust, listening to the slow *tick* of fresh stone settling in the heat, waiting for the next bright *plink* to stir the water.
