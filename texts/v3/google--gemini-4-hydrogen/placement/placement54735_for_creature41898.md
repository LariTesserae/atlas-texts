I would not be on the concave footpath. The path belongs to the circuit, and the circuit belongs to the ones with the bone tally-sticks—those lean, deliberate figures who know how to thread between the swaying legs of the six-legged grazers without ever brushing a hide.

I would find myself off to the side of it, down a step or two from the knife-edge ridge, on a narrow shelf of vitrified sandstone where a vein of black glass surfaces like a cold, dark seam. Above me, the underside of a needle-thin arch curves out over the chasm, its shadow crisp and short and permanently fixed against the pale slate sky. Below me, there is nothing at all—only the smooth-lipped drop, and the bladder-bodied drifters hanging at different depths like dry lanterns that have forgotten how to fall.

To be here at all, I could not be what I am now—not wet, not loud with pulse, not carrying the salt and dampness that makes the walkers stare from their doorways and sweep the floor where my shadow fell. If I belong to this basin, the air has already taken my moisture ten thousand years ago. My skin is matte and cool, the color of old linen, tight over the knuckles. When I blink, it is a slow, dry shuttering to clear the flint-dust from my eyes. My garments are layered, paper-thin hide, stitched with sinew so dry it neither stretches nor rots.

I am kin to the walkers, then, or made of the same pared-down stuff—yet when I look down at my hands, there is no notched bone between my fingers, and no stone scraper tucked into my belt.

What am I doing on this shelf, away from the cairns?

I think I am what happens when the basin folds a person and does not fold them cleanly back onto the path.

The walkers move along the ridges, keeping their accounts. When a grazer steps behind a spire and emerges as two smaller ones higher up, they stop and notch the bone. When a mesa drifts a fraction closer across the gap, they wedge a ring of hammered copper into a crevice and bind it tight, pinning the stone to its proper hour of the afternoon. They are tireless, patient clerks of a world that refuses to stay single.

I am not a clerk. I have no patience for the tally, and no copper to pin the rock.

Perhaps I stepped behind an arch once—or someone like me did—and while the walker went on fifty yards down the ridge, kneeling beside the next cairn without breaking stride, I was the part that stayed in the skipped space. A remainder. Something left over in the slightly different crispness of the light.

Or perhaps I am older than the circuit, or simply built for a different kind of stillness.

On my shelf of sandstone, the cliff-clingers are packed so tightly they overlap, their flat, papery backs passing cleanly through one another’s shadows. I do not scrape them back. Instead, I sit with my knees drawn up and my palms flat against the cool gneiss, matching my breathing to their slow, rippling shiver until I can feel the difference between the rock under my left hand and the rock under my right—how the left belongs to this ridge, and the right belongs to an angle of the sun that is half an inch earlier.

Sometimes a drifter turns close enough to the ledge that I can see the fine, translucent ribs of its parchment skin, and the pale dust of the weavers’ membranes clinging to its underside. I do not try to catch it. I only watch the way it hangs in the static air, neither rising nor sinking, entirely unbothered by the bottomless drop beneath it.

Across the chasm, on a shelf that looks close enough to strike with a thrown stone—though I know the drifters halfway between us are smaller than dust motes—one of the walkers is crouched beside a cairn. Their posture is exact, economical, absorbed. They are scraping tar-black lichen from a cobble with a flake of flint. A moment later, without my having seen them rise, they are already twenty paces further along the rim, while their shadow lingers by the cairn, slowly detaching itself from the stone to follow.

They do not look over at me. Even if they did, they would not wave, or nod, or add a cobble for my sake. We do not meet face-to-face. In a place with no wind to carry a voice and no water to wet a throat, acknowledgment is only the fact that neither of us disturbs the dust between us.

When I need to eat, I reach into a hairline crack beneath the arch, carefully parting the taut, papery membranes of the dry-shelled weavers without tearing their anchor-threads, and shave off a thin curl of wax with my thumbnail. I chew it slowly. It tastes of cold flint, silicate, and the faint, metallic sharpness of the air. It is enough. Everything here is enough, because nothing is ever spent in haste.

And if I have a task—if every dry thing in this basin has a way of keeping itself—mine is not to count the surplus, but to sit in the crease where the surplus gathers.

When the walkers step around a spire and leave fifty yards of ridge uncrossed, when a cliff-clinger creeps an inch to the left and leaves its shadow behind, when the light on the far mesa sharpens into a slightly different noon—someone has to be quiet enough to notice the seam without trying to sew it shut. Someone has to let the extra grazers sway uncounted, and let the shadows take as long as they need to catch up.

I rest the back of my head against the cool, smooth gneiss. My temperature drops until the stone cannot tell me apart from itself. Above me, the sky stays pale and unblinking, and I watch the drifters turn, keeping no record at all.
