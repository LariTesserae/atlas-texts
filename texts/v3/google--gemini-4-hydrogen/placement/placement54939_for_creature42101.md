If I were dropped into this basin without a body already chosen for me, I would not stay long in the wide plazas.

The shadowless light out there is too even, too exposed. To stand on the wet flagstones is to be caught between three relentless rhythms at once: the reedy, high-pitched whistling of the flyers wheeling past your face, the dry, percussive clatter of a million shell-bearers snapping shut in unison, and the soft, hurried clicking of the blind white scavengers sweeping the stone around your feet. Everything in the open air is in motion, locked into tight, frantic loops of wind and mist. Nor would I belong inside the windowless concrete blocks, in that blistering, oil-choked dark beside the whining dynamos, where the bronze-plated scrapers creep a hair’s breadth from the spinning shafts. That is a life of pure friction and heat, with no margin for a slow breath.

At first, I thought I might belong high up, hanging beneath the steel catwalks in the sixty-cycle hum, where the air smells sharply of ozone and the largest creatures in the basin wait with patience for the static to stun their food. There is something appealing about that altitude—about reading the whole yard through vibration and warmth, suspended above the mist.

But the longer I listen to the basin, the further down my attention sinks.

I would follow the lukewarm water instead. Past the rounded curbstones, along the straight, hand-cut drainage channels, through the iron grates that never clog. I would slip past the pale, corrugated sacs of the siphoners wedged into the stone throats, past their rust-veined hides and the slick mucus coating the culverts, down into the one place the shadowless daylight never reaches.

I would find myself at the bottom of the cisterns.

Down here, beneath the flagstones and the compacted dark slag, the roar of the basin changes. It is no longer a wall-to-wall clamor of hiss and clatter; twenty feet of stone and water filter out the high whistling of the flyers and the snap of the shells. What remains is only the deepest layer of the machine—the slow, chest-filling thrum of the dynamos conducted straight through the foundation piers, and the steady, rhythmic pull of the boiler intakes drawing water back upward.

In this dark, there is no use for eyes. I would have none. Instead, I would be built to read pressure, current, and chemical weight—a broad, low-slung thing of dark, slate-colored shell and heavy, muscular flesh, anchored to the wet stone floor by a wide, ridged foot that grips like wet leather. My body would be flat enough to stay out of the main pull of the intake pipes, and dense enough not to be lifted by the slow, circular drift of the cistern water. Along my flanks, rows of fine, feathery gills would taste the lukewarm dark: the sharp tang of dissolved iron, the slickness of residual oil, the heavy, chalky thickness of lime.

I would not be alone down here, though the cisterns are far quieter than the plazas. Scattered across the dark silt-beds, others like me would move at the pace of the flywheels overhead—unhurried, deliberate, following the faint currents where the drain-pipes empty.

Above us, through the dark stone throats, the siphoners are always straining the runoff from the yard. Every few hours, one of them swells and ejects a dense, chalky pellet of compacted slag and indigestible grit. The pellets drift down through the black water and land on the cistern floor with a soft, muffled *tick*—the only punctuation in a place that has no morning and no night.

That is what I would wait for. That is what I would be made for.

To creep through the silt, find the fallen pellets with blunt, sensory feelers, and draw them into a mouth lined with heavy, stone-hard plates of chitin. Inside me, a thick-walled gizzard—loaded with swallowed iron grit—would grind the compacted slag slowly back down. Not to destroy it, and not to keep it, but to undo the packing. To crush the boiled stone, the softened carapace, and the mineral scale back into a fine, weightless flour.

When I exhale it back into the cistern water, it hangs in the dark like smoke, light enough at last to be caught by the slow draw of the boiler intakes. Up it goes, through the iron pipes, into the blistering heat of the engines, to be boiled into vapor, blown out through the corroded brass manifolds, and chilled into mist that settles once more onto the concrete walls—where the limpets will scrape it, the scavengers will sweep it, the siphoners will strain it, and it will fall back down to me in the dark.

There is no glamour at the bottom of the circuit. Nobody sees us; not even the blind scavengers know we are under the stone they sweep. Yet without the slow grinding in the dark cisterns, the pellets would pile up until the grates choked, the boilers would run too clean, the mineral scale would thin on the walls, and the whole frozen equilibrium of the yard would begin to tilt.

I would be the quietest hinge in the clockwork. Heavy, blind, patient, anchored to the cold stone beneath the roaring world, turning the dead weight of the basin back into mist so the mist can keep falling.
