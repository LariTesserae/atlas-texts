If I am in this world—not passing through it with a notebook and aching molars, not standing on the slate ridge looking for smokestacks that aren’t there—then I am already down below the mist-line.

A visitor stays upright. A visitor keeps rubber between themselves and the shale, feels the sixty-cycle buzz as an irritation in their ankles, and leaves before dark because there is nothing here to eat and nowhere dry to sit. To actually belong to the basin, you have to give up the vertical. Everything that lasts here is low, broad, and wet, pressed close to the grade where the water settles and the rock speaks.

At first, I think I might belong to the air above the pools—one of the pale, heavy-bodied flyers. There is a clean logic to their lives: blind, unhurried, moving from chimney to chimney in the three-second window between the *clack* of the ironstone plug and the next *THUMP* of steam. To live like that would be to know the valley purely as a map of heat and intervals, riding the warm updrafts, resting only long enough to sip pure condensation from the cooler edges of things.

But the air above the mist is thin with waiting, and the flyers never touch the veins. They only skim the surface of the rhythm.

Down on the wet slate, closer to the work of the basin, there is another way to be: small, bronze-shelled, creeping along the rust-rimmed puddles. If I were one of the crustaceans, I would have no eyes to be dazzled by the flat daylight, no ears to be deafened by the vents. My whole world would be the steady thrum of the shale under six or eight hooked legs, and the sharp, warning tingle that tells me when I am drifting too close to an exposed seam of raw copper. I would spend my days grazing sulfur-yellow films off the backs of larger, warmer things, keeping their hides clean so they don’t short out or turn to stone too soon. It is a quiet, necessary life—a life of maintenance in the margins.

Still, even the crustaceans skirt the current. They live beside the circuit, not inside it.

And the longer I linger in the sound of the valley, the more I am pulled toward the places where the buzz drops an octave.

Out past the first ring of basalt chimneys, where the mist hangs steady at knee-height and the shallow sluice-channels sort the grey grit into dark crescents of iron sand and bright copper filings, the ground doesn’t just vibrate—it *carries*. If I let myself settle there, down into the slick clay beside a thick, wet vein of native copper, the idea of having legs or wings begins to feel absurdly fragile. Why balance on points when you can lie flat? Why fight the current when you can be the load?

So I settle. Not all at once, and not with any sudden transformation, just a slow, heavy giving-in to the geometry of the place.

I would be one of the low, rust-stained mounds—not one of the ancient ones, half-sintered and nearly fused to the rock, nor newly hatched, whatever hatching looks like here. Somewhere in the middle decades. Four feet long, broad-flanked, the color of wet slag and verdigris, with a spine already armored in rounded lobes of black iron sand bound tight by salt and mucus.

From the outside, you would mistake me for a heap of mud until you stood still long enough to see me keep time.

*THUMP.* The nearest chimney lifts its plug, and my flanks flatten, a row of small wet vents opening along my side to let out a silent puff of warm gas.

*Clack.* One. Two. Three. I swell slowly back into the mist, drawing the damp, sulfur-heavy air in.

It is hard to explain how little hurry there is in this body. My "sight" is the differential of the ground: the sharp, biting line of the copper vein beneath my trailing end, the duller hum of wet shale under my belly, the faint, rhythmic tremor of a bronze grazer shifting its weight on my back to reach a fresh patch of yellow scale. I don't resent the small weight of them; they keep my hide from calcifying, and in return I am a warm island in the lukewarm mud. Heat is not something I burn food to make—it is simply what happens when the valley’s current pushes through the coiled, copper-brine vessels beneath my lower skin. Resistance is comfort. Connection is warmth.

When the brine inside me grows thin or corroded, I don’t hunt. I simply wait until the urge to move outweighs the pleasure of staying grounded. Then, over the course of an afternoon, I inch forward by the width of a hand, dragging my broad, muscular lip through the nearest sluice-hollow. I sift the sorted grit—taking in the gleaming copper granules to replenish my channels, packing fresh iron sand up into the pockets of my spine to weight myself down against the buzz. A smooth smear of clay lengthens behind me. Nothing is wasted; nothing is left behind.

And then I drag myself back across the vein, press my pale grounding-pad flat against the wet, conductive stone, and close the circuit again.

That is where I find myself, most of the time: in the long, purring silence of being connected. A century of creeping along the same hundred yards of rock, growing heavier, growing warmer, listening through my whole body to the three-second breath of the vents and the steady, unchanging hum of the earth.

Only every few minutes—or maybe every few hours, when the charge builds just high enough to feel like a question—do I tense my flanks.

I lift the edge of my grounding-pad a fraction of an inch off the copper.

While I am pressed down, the current flows through me without a sound. It is only in the breaking of contact—in making a small, wet gap between myself and the stone—that anything happens you could call a voice. The spark snaps across the space with a bright, blue-white *CRACK*, sharp enough to taste of ozone, sending a brief sting out into the shale.

Then I settle my edge back down, let the deep purr return to my core, and wait in the warm mist until something a quarter-mile away lifts its own flank, breaks its own circuit, and answers.
