I would not be deep inside the oldest thickets, where the ferns grow so dense they choke the valley down to a heavy, peppery dark and two or three dwellers sit knee-to-knee for weeks without rotating an inch.

You would find me at the rim.

Specifically, at the outer edge of a small, westward thicket, wedged into a leeward cleft where a slab of dark glass-basalt meets the first ragged fringe of velvet-black moss. Behind my spine, the spun-silica plumes drink the worst of the valley’s roar down to a thick, bearable thrumming. In front of my knees, past a low wall of unmortared slate and a single hanging baffle of lashed silica-grass, the world drops away—not into a clean cliff, but into one of those flat, gray smears where depth gives up and the horizon looks like chalk rubbed sideways by a thumb.

I am one of the dry, jointed things that belong here, though an older one—not yet ready to stop drawing gas, not yet fractured enough to sit down beside my ash and let the hum shake me into powder, but worn enough that my plates are scored fine as knapped slate. When I flex an elbow or shift my weight, the deep fibrous creases at my joints hiss softly and vent small, dry puffs of gray dust.

Under a heavy brow of porous bone, my eyes are milky and sunken, good only for reading the slow drift of pewter light against soot-black crevices. I do not look at the smeared edge of the terrace so much as I listen to it. Along my collarbones and down the backs of my forearms, my ash-silver filaments—some grown, a few grafted carefully from the outer ferns after a hard crossing snapped the originals—tremble without pause. Here at the boundary, where the plants’ silence-making meets the grinding tide of the open air, the filaments never quite settle. They register the high metallic whine behind my eyes and the low, continental friction pressing against my chest plates.

That is why I stay at the rim. Someone has to keep track of the seam.

Deep in the thicket, time is easy to trust; the ferns swallow the acoustics so greedily that the air feels thick and honest. Out here, at the lip of the unfinished basin, the world’s physical laws fray at the edges. Yesterday—or three days ago, measured by the slow slide of a detached shadow across my floor—the sharp *crack* of splitting basalt rang across the ridge while the stone in front of me was still whole. I sat motionless, my broad, slate-calloused hands resting flat on my shins, and waited four long minutes until the fracture finally opened in the cliff face a hundred yards to the west.

When that happens, I reach for the notched rod of compressed mineral-wood beside my heap of ash. I take up a flat slate scraper and draw its edge slowly across the teeth.

*Clack. Clack. Clack.*

It is a tiny, private sound, swallowed by the moss before it can reach the open terrace. I do not do it to speak to anyone—there is no one else in this cleft—but to measure the gap between the touch and the noise. As long as the *clack* arrives at the exact instant the slate meets the notch, I know the thicket is still holding. On the rare occasions when the sound slips ahead of my hand by a hair’s breadth, I know it is time to equalize my pressure, seal my vents, and begin the long, glass-slow creep inward toward deeper leaves.

Mostly, though, the *clack* holds true, and my days pass in the small, dry business of staying whole.

When the internal hum in my honeycombed chambers drops too low, I crawl out to the nearest thick-stemmed fern—slowly, careful not to bruise its brittle plumes—and wedge its stalk open with a dull obsidian blade. I press the slotted vents of my throat to the weeping seam and drink in the dry, pressurized gas, feeling my leathery chambers tighten and swell until I match the weight of the air. Afterward, I scrape the exhausted, charcoal-like pith from the split stem and carry it back in cupped hands to add to the pale mound on my flat stone. Mixed with the fine grit shed from my own wearing joints, I rub it methodically into the folds of my knees and knuckles, and swallow a dry pinch to keep my inner chambers balanced.

I have crossed the open slate between thickets only twice. Each crossing took days: lifting a foot and holding it suspended for minutes while my filaments tasted the air for a premature fracture; waiting for my own shadow, pooling like black soot in the cracks behind me, to catch up and anchor itself beneath my heel before I trusted the next slab with my weight. 

I have no desire to cross again soon. Here, just behind the baffle of silica-grass, I have everything a waterless thing requires: the muffled hum of the ferns at my back, the steady, dry grit in my joints, and the long, patient quiet in which to listen to a world that has not finished breaking itself open—waiting, at the speed of cooling glass, for the next sound to catch up with its stone.
