I wouldn’t be up in the wind-whipped reeds, where the stalks shriek and the light is bruised gray, and I wouldn’t be one of the heavy, slate-backed travelers hauling themselves blindly from stem to stem.

You would have to look lower, and off the flat.

Every few miles across the plateau, the embryonic crust doesn’t just tremble—it buckles. A slab of basalt shifts a fraction of an inch too far, or catches on a deeper shelf, and the stone shears upward into a low, ragged step. Nothing tall enough to break the cloud deck, nothing like a mountain or a cliff. Just a raw, glassy overhang, maybe waist-high, roofed over by the dense, springy underside of the root-mat that hasn’t yet had time to knit itself down into the new gap.

Down inside that narrow, lightless crease, the roar of the continent changes. The wind can’t reach you there; it passes overhead like a river over a sunken log, reduced to a dull, structural shudder in the roots above. The dry *crackle* of cavitation in the reeds is muffled to a distant hiss. What you hear instead—if you have anything to hear with—is the slow, heavy *drip* of cold condensation falling off the root-hairs, and the crystalline silence of stone that has never seen air.

That is where I would be. Pressed flat into the angle of the overhang, spread across thirty feet of freshly fractured basalt.

I wouldn’t have a body in the way the travelers do—no central shield, no five or seven thick straps to peel away from a stalk with a blue-violet spark. I would be something thinner, wider, and far less ambitious: a branching, leathery web of dark verdigris and copper-veined cartilage, anchored so tightly into the glassy planes of the rock that you couldn’t scrape me off with a knife without taking flakes of basalt with me.

At the edges, where the stone is newest and sharpest, I am scarcely thicker than a film of wet soot, threading hair-fine filaments into fissures too narrow for even a root-tip to pry open. Closer to the center of the overhang, where the water pools cold and tastes strongly of hot copper, my tissues thicken into knotted, mineral-stiffened ridges—half-fungus, half-circuit, slick with the same clear mucus that keeps the world from cutting itself to pieces.

I don’t hunt the reeds. I don’t climb into the fog to taste the voltage gradients of the storm. I wait in the crease, and I let the storm come down to me.

Time here isn’t measured in light. There is no morning under a root-overhang, and even above, the gray ceiling never breaks. Time is measured in the *crack*.

I feel it before it happens—not as a sound, because I have no ears, no sinuses to vibrate. I feel it as a sudden, unbearable tightness along my own silica-laced threads. The basalt beneath me is rigid and unforgiving; when tectonic strain builds miles down, the crystal lattice of the rock compresses microscopically, and my own anchored body stretches taut across the fault like a wire.

Then the *crack* rings through.

The rock shifts half a millimeter. The overhang drops a hair’s breadth, squeezing the cold water out of the moss-mat above in a sudden, heavy sheet. And for a few seconds, the whole world goes quiet in a different way—not the localized silence of a feeding traveler, but the ringing aftermath of a struck bell.

Moments later, they arrive.

Above me, the taut root-network parts. I feel the heavy, pressurized bulk of the slate-creatures squeezing down through crevices no wider than two fingers, purging their excess water in sharp, copper-smelling gushes that wash straight down the basalt face and over my skin. They have no eyes to see me, and to the fine coppery cilia along their straps, I must taste like nothing more than a particularly cooperative vein of wet ore.

They flatten themselves against the fresh stone, right over my branching ridges. They spread their straps wide, lock their rasping pads onto the mineral shelves, and let go of what they stole from the sky.

That is what I feed on.

When twenty of them press into the overhang at once, their discharges don’t just sink straight into the sterile rock. The current spills sideways across the wet mineral film. It finds my veins. I feel each of them as a separate, heavy *thrum*—some arriving hot and ragged from a sector where a micro-squall just tore through the reeds, others arriving sluggish and half-empty, their chalky armor still soft.

I take the overflow they can’t sink into the basalt. I smooth out the jagged spikes of voltage, routing the excess potential along my dendritic web from one end of the overhang to the other, bleeding it slowly into the deeper fractures where the rock is still dry and hungry for charge. In return, I sip the milky, mineral-rich runoff of their etching—dissolved silica, trace metals, stray ozone—pulling it straight through my porous, drenched hide to toughen my own ridges.

They don’t know I am here. When they finish, they haul themselves blindly back up through the roots, leaving behind their faint, branching scars on the stone, returning to the deafening roar of the wind.

I stay behind in the dark, holding the shape of their visit.

Because I am spread across the whole fault-step, and because I never leave it, I become the only thing on the plateau that experiences the world all at once. The travelers only ever know one stalk, one fissure, one moment of hunger. The plants only know the wind and the upward creep of water.

But down in the crease, I hold the memory of both. I know when the wind shifts fifty yards to the north by the sudden spike in current the travelers bring down with them. I know where the basalt is weakest by the way the *thrum* pools in my lower nodes. I have no voice to call out, and no eyes to see the fog, but in the quiet intervals between one tectonic *crack* and the next, I am the place where the sky’s static and the stone’s weight finally meet, settle, and recognize each other.
