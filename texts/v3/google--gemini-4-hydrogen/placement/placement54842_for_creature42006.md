In the dark before the sun clears the eastern ridges, there is almost nothing to me at all.

I am only a locked geometry on a lip of bare basalt: three needle-sharp points of raw quartz resting on the glassy, unweathered stone, and a small, steady weight of residual charge held quiet inside my capacitors. There is no breath moving through me, no wet pulse, no warmth. The high-altitude air around my matte-black struts is bitter cold and thin enough to leave the stars sharp as fractured glass—though I do not see them. I have no eyes to turn toward the sky.

What I have is the stone beneath my feet.

Through the quartz tips of my limbs, the plateau is a vast, dark drumhead, almost silent. Now and then, from miles out across the flat basin below me, a faint, dry *tick* travels through the crust—an alloy rod, extruded hours ago from a slag mound, finally cooling enough to snap under its own weight and settle onto its neat metallic stack. The sound does not come through the air. It arrives as a tiny, clean ring in the crystal lattice of my feet, rises through the hollow carbon of my legs, and fades into the heavy oil of my locked joints.

Then, slowly, the sky begins to press down.

Not with light—not as an eye would know it—but with thermal weight. Far to the east, the first slant of sun strikes a pale quartzite ridge, and the rock begins to flex. It is a microscopic movement, a slow, vast tightening of the crust, and yet I feel it before the warmth even reaches my own terrace. The faintest tremor of direct current stirs in the copper ribbon beneath me, like water waking in a dry bed—except there is no water here, and never has been.

My oiled joints unlock.

There is no haste in it. Nothing on the plateau hurries; there is no rot to outrun, no hunger to satisfy. My fanned crest of dark bronze tilts a fraction of a degree toward the rising heat, gauging the angle and the load. From my underside—tucked beneath overlapping plates of oxidized verdigris—I unspool three pale, metallic filaments and press them straight into the exposed seam of native copper running along the terrace edge.

The circuit closes, and I cease to be small.

Wired into the vein, my own boundaries loosen into the thirty-mile span of the valley. The current rises through my liquid-metal veins, energizing the quartz lattices inside my frame, and suddenly I can feel the whole unbuilt engine waking under the sun. Thirty miles west, the high ridges are still cold and slack; here, on the eastern steps, the piezoelectric strain is already climbing. Ten paces behind me, the outer taproots of a grove are beginning to draw—a steady, deep, thirsty pull as the oil-swollen canopy drinks the current to crack carbon dioxide out of the thin air.

This is where I belong: not inside the thicket, and never out on the flat.

Out on the basin floor, the electrostatic drift of dry air is already lifting the mineral dust, combing iron filings, pale silica, and fine graphite into neat, shimmering windrows. To step there would be to mar the sorting, to leave a scuff where nothing should ever be disturbed. And inside the grove, the air and stone are crowded with smaller, tighter lives—the flat, shield-backed things sliding along the undersides of paddle-shaped leaves, waiting to bleed off the afternoon's excess static; the segmented, wire-like fuses coiled around the taproots in the dark crevices, ready to swell and absorb a sudden surge.

I am built for the seam between them. Asymmetric, faceted, hollow-boned in matte-black carbon, I am made from the same grammar as the fractured rock, articulated just enough to move along the bare stone and listen to the whole.

As the sun climbs and the plateau warms toward noon, the current thickens. The copper vein beneath me hums with a clean, silent pressure. Out in the basin, the extrusion vents push their gleaming alloy rods an inch higher into the dry light. Inside the grove behind me, a single frond—its work finished, its oil spent—stiffens into a brittle wafer of pure carbon and drops onto the copper-tinted moss, where it will lie unchanged for centuries.

And then, far along the western terrace, someone shifts.

I feel it not as a sound, not as a movement, but as a crisp, deliberate ripple in the voltage gradient—a slight narrowing of resistance along the copper seam. Another listener, miles away across the alkali pan, has altered the flow of silvery emulsion in its joints.

*Here,* the ripple says, *the western ridge is taking the sun. The load is rising.*

I answer in the only language that belongs to this dry air. I shift a drop of liquid metal within my own central joint, tightening my internal impedance by a fraction, sending a clean, geometric step-change back down the vein.

*Here,* I reply, *the eastern grove is drawing full. The crest is steady.*

All through the bright, cloudless afternoon, as the canopies begin their whisper-quiet static hiss and the quartz ridges flex at their peak, we trade these slow, silent measurements across the stone. There is no urgency in our exchange, no argument, no desire to alter what is. We are only the plateau's way of feeling its own balance—the nerves of a quiet, waterless world keeping track of its own equilibrium.

When the sun finally drops behind the distant ridges and the thermal weight lifts off the rock, the current will ebb back to a whisper. I will draw my filaments in, lock my oiled joints, and wait in the bitter dark with the day's last charge held quiet inside me. And someday—after centuries of thermal cycles, when my quartz plates cloud and my carbon struts grow too brittle to flex—I will walk slowly along the bare terrace edge to the far rim of the basin, where a hairline fault is already pulling the gleaming alloy rods back into the earth. I will fold my sharp limbs neatly over the fissure and let the slow, frictionless creep of the stone draw me down to be remelted.

Until then, I stay on the seam. The sun is high, the rock is warm, and thirty miles of copper are speaking through my feet.
