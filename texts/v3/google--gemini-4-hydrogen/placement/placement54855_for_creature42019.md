I think I would not be at the rim. Looking down from the root-cliffs gives you the whole bowl at once, neat as a cathedral nave, and that kind of clean overview feels like a lie here—too solid, too sure of its edges.

If I found myself in this place, it would be lower down, inside the hum. Somewhere on the middle tiers, perhaps, where the braided walkways cross above the cistern-yard and the hanging gourd-chambers sway slightly in the rising warmth.

At first, I would only be a listener—something small and un-tuned, pressed against the damp curve of a reed-buttress, trying to make sense of the surf.

The noise would be the hardest thing to learn. You cannot ignore a sound that vibrates in your own ribs; it isn't coming *at* you so much as it is happening *through* you. The organ-drone of the hollow pillars, the rhythmic *chirr* of the translucent climbers beneath the bridges, the needle-winged clouds—it would take a long time to stop bracing against it. A solid body tries to stay closed in a roar like that. It clenches. It tries to hold onto its own outline, the way a stone holds its shape in a river.

And in this basin, holding onto your outline is the surest way to get stranded.

Look at what happens when you stare straight ahead: the bridge locks into forty paces. Your shadow stretches stubbornly east while the world around you drops straight down into the void. You become an interruption in the warm air—a heavy, un-resonant knot that makes the keepers’ chest-membranes flutter with mild, passing confusion as they walk around you.

So I don't think I would stay solid for long. Or perhaps I was never quite solid to begin with.

---

If I let myself settle into the basin, if I stop trying to brace against the pressure, something else begins to happen. My attention drifts away from the center of the walkways and toward the seams—the places where the light slips, where the leaf-walls go thin in a warm draft, where the *before-echoes* gather.

That is where I find myself: not in the grand, roaring center of the open air, and not deep in the impossible, shadowy galleries beyond the cliffs, either. I am somewhere on the threshold between them. Under an awning that hasn't quite decided whether to be opaque or translucent. Seated on the calloused lip of a gourd-chamber doorway, where the wind loosens the walls every few minutes and lets the green-filtered light of the deeper streets bleed through.

And as I sit there, letting the roar pass through instead of against me, I realize what I am becoming—or what I already was.

I am not a keeper. I don't have their long, double-jointed grace, or the waxy celadon skin dusted with spores, or the ancient certainty of hands that have pared rot from these reeds for a thousand years. I don't know the whole map of the basin's broken acoustics; I couldn't tap a water-viaduct and know which tier three stories up will hear it first.

Nor am I one of the gliders, diving headlong through solid bark to burst out of a cistern below. I don't have that kind of appetite, or that kind of speed.

If I am anything native to this place, I am something closer to the *pause* a keeper makes when they cup their palms around a vibrating frond.

A small pocket of dampened air.

Let me test that. No—it isn't silence. True silence doesn't exist here, and if it did, it would feel like suffocation. It's more like a *clearing* inside the sound. When the needle-winged swarms boil past a pillar and leave their shimmering afterimages buzzing in the air behind them, there is a thin, bright seam between the real bodies and their echoes. A half-second where the thing has already moved on, and its noise hasn't caught up—or where the noise has already arrived, and the drop of condensation is still clinging to the waxy underside of the leaf, swelling, about to fall.

That two-second gap is where I live.

---

In physical form, perhaps I am small and slight, perched high where the fibrous cables meet the gourd-rinds—something pale and porous, more membrane and hollow stem than bone. Maybe I have no claws for climbing, only long, flat-padded fingers that know how to find the tension-points of a trembling leaf. Maybe my eyes aren't veiled like the keepers', so I have had to learn a different trick: how to look slightly *past* everything, letting my gaze rest on the air half a handspan to the side of whatever I am crossing, so the bridges never stiffen under my weight.

Or maybe I am not even a creature with fingers at all. Maybe I am an *instrument* that grew by accident—a single, slightly misshapen gourd hanging in the middle tier, pared open at the wrong angle so that instead of booming with the rest of the organ-city, it catches only the stray *slips*.

The stray taps meant for someone else. The soft, wet *slap* of a keeper's foot arriving two seconds before they step through the rippling wall. The dry, close shriek of a glider from clear across the basin.

They collect inside me, those displaced sounds, swirling gently in the warm, wet-cellulose air until they settle. And when a keeper passes along the braided balcony outside—unhurried, their chest-membranes pulsing with the low drone of the reeds—maybe they pause for a fraction of a second beside my oval threshold, press a cool, waxy palm against my rind, and listen. Not for news of the cisterns or the viaducts, just for the strange, quiet echo of things that haven't quite happened yet, or happened somewhere else entirely.

That feels closer. Yet even as a gourd, I feel the urge to move—to lean out over the vast, sunlit void, to watch the mist drift over the root-cliffs and slick the walkways, to follow the keepers with my sideways gaze as they slip through the wind-rippled walls into the deeper streets.

So perhaps I am neither wholly fixed nor wholly wandering.

I am a small, quiet-boned thing—something half-grown into the city's equilibrium—sitting on the threshold of a middle-tier gourd, my palms resting lightly on the tension-points of a broad leaf beside my cheek. Enough to dull the sharpest edge of the roar into a warm, bearable hum. Not enough to shut the city out.

From here, I don't have to cross the forty-pace bridges, and I don't have to tend the mulch-beds below. I just keep watch over the seam where the light thins. I listen for the splash before the water falls, and wait for the breeze that turns the green wall clear—content to live in the small, warm space between the echo and the thing that made it.
