I wouldn't stay on the slate shelf for long. Up there, with the dry-ice wind scraping past and the air too thin to carry a shout, I would only be a visitor—all useless lungs and stiff knees, squinting down at a mechanism I couldn't hear. To be *in* this world, I would have to give up the kind of body that needs a voice in the air, and go down into the dark where the basalt actually hums.

At first, descending into the amphitheater, it is tempting to imagine being one of the stationary things. 

There is a quiet perfection to the refiner-sponges in the oily-black catch-basins—blind, rubbery, utterly patient, spending whole seasons digesting mineral silt and extruding pure sulfur and copper-salt into neat, waxy ribbons along the water's edge. To be a sponge or a mat of acid-kelp would be to never have to wonder where the next breath is coming from; you would simply sit in the cold condensate and sort the world into its elements. 

Or, at the other extreme, to be a spire-condenser: forty feet of graphite-laced fiber rooted deep in the bedrock, holding flat black plates up into the thin upper wind. You would gather static slowly, silently, feeling the charge climb through your woody spiral until you could bear no more—and then that single, blinding-blue corona, that one dry *tick* into the near-vacuum, before starting the long climb back up from zero.

Even the drifting hydrogen bladders have their appeal, hanging in the immense empty air-columns between the ridges, trailing hair-thin filaments down toward the mist, knowing the whole amphitheater by its barometric shifts and never once touching the ground.

But none of those can walk from one basin to another. A sponge knows only its pool; a spire knows only its own capacity; a bladder goes wherever the thin currents push it. If I were to wake up in this world as something that recognizes itself, I would need to move between the bowls. I would need to be able to listen to more than one terrace at a time.

I think I would find myself down on the bare basalt saddles—the high, dark ridges of frost-planed stone that separate one terraced basin from the next.

That is where I would be a tender. Not one of the ones who stays curled near the richest kelp-blooms or the deepest, warmest piston-vents, though I would have to visit them to drink. I would be one of the tenders who works the dry crossings in between.

It is strange to imagine the body that makes this possible, and yet the moment I picture kneeling on the basalt, it fits with a quiet, heavy certainty. No nostrils to freeze in the near-vacuum; no outer ears waiting for sound that will never arrive. Just the thick, smoky-clear domes over my eyes, built to drink the sharp starlight without flinching when a spire snaps fifty yards away. A matte-charcoal hide, ribbed and insulated, with a damp mantle of ferro-lichen across my shoulders to catch the rime-fog, and a row of dull, tarnished copper plates down my spine that warm slightly whenever a deep earth-current shifts beneath my feet.

When I need to breathe, I don't gasp at the empty sky. I find a muffled piston-vent where the heat-resistant fungus chokes the geothermal steam, unseal the pleated collar beneath my jaw, and press my face to the fissure. The warm, mineral-heavy vapor fills my thoracic sacs—sulfur and oxygen and heat—and for the next few hours, that single slow draft is enough. I seal the collar, stand on wide, padded feet that make no sound in the carbon-reeds, and walk back out onto the bare stone.

My work is mostly balancing what the basins cannot balance for themselves.

In the crook of my long arm, I carry soft, unworked ribbons of pure copper gathered from the edges of the refiner-pools. When one basin is heavy with charge—its grazers sluggish and overfull, its spires snapping too fast and too bright—and the neighboring basin is starved and dim, I walk the basalt saddle between them. I find the natural meltwater grooves in the frost-planed rock and press the copper ribbons flat into the damp stone. 

It is not a road, and it is not a wire I mean to keep. By the end of the season, the frost and the ozone wind will oxidize the metal and scatter it into dust. That is fine. The point isn't to build something that outlasts the weather; the point is simply to let the excess voltage bleed across the ridge for a few weeks, until both basins settle back into an even hum.

Along the way, when I cross a thicket of hollow-stemmed sedge, a blind, velvet-shelled grazer might crawl across my path, heavy and stiff from chewing too many charged reeds. I crouch in front of it and touch two fingers—tipped with dense calluses of graphite and copper-sulfide—to the pale plates along its back. A quiet trickle of current flows up my arm and down into my own layered batteries. The grazer doesn't startle; it only settles lower on its rubbery feet, lighter and drowsily content, and crawls on into the dark.

And if I am lucky, near the lip of a northern terrace, I meet another tender coming the other way.

We don't wave, and we don't try to shape words into the dead air. We kneel opposite each other on a slab of wet slate and lean forward until our broad, flattened jawbones press flush together—or we lay our calloused palms flat on the rock a few inches apart. Through bone and stone, the silence fills with detail: rapid trains of clicks, modulated buzzes, small rhythmic pulses of current. News of a clogged fungal gasket three valleys over; a bloom of acid-kelp in the western bowl; a shift in the static coming off the high peaks. When we pull back, a tiny blue spark—no bigger than a struck flint—snaps between our knuckles as our charges equalize, and then we turn back to our separate basins.

Most of my time, though, is spent at the taproots of the spires.

People—if anyone ever stood on the high slate shelf and looked down—would think the blue flashes across the valley were weather. They wouldn't see me crouched at the base of a forty-foot helix, camouflaged in the waist-deep black carpet, wrapping a damp strip of conductive kelp around the woody fiber or pressing my palms against the root to bleed a little voltage into the subsoil. 

I hold the charge back for three slow heartbeats. I wait for the western terrace to snap first. Then I lift my hands, and let my spire fire its silent, blinding-blue answer into the indigo sky.

That is who I would be there: not the storm, and not the stone, and not the voice that tries to shout across a near-vacuum. Just a quiet, insulated switch crouched in the sedge, listening through my jawbone to the hum of the basalt, holding the current for a moment, and then letting it go.
