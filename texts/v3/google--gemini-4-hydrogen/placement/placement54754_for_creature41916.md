If I were to wake here—though *waking* is already the wrong word, suggesting a soft edge between sleep and motion, a dampness of eyelids parting—there would be no opening of eyes. There would only be the *clack*.

Sixty times a minute, flat and dry, arriving in the marrow of my shins before it reaches my crest.

At first, before I knew what shape I wore, I would think I was trapped inside a wall. Every direction is occupied. Something papery brushes my shoulder in a steady, asthmatic rhythm; something ribbed and warm hums just beside my hip; the air itself has weight, stratified into warm, dust-laden shelves that taste—if I had a tongue to taste with—of hot copper and pulverized salt. There is no room to swing an arm, no slack in the air to sigh into. Even the dark is not empty; it is bruised with amber from the housings and prickled with tiny violet sparks along the razor-edged gypsum.

For a while, I might wish to be small and anchored. It would be easy, in a room this loud, to settle onto one of the cast-iron casings as a plate-back—to fuse my edges from shed grit and waxy chitin, press my ribbed belly against the humming metal, and let the turbine’s howl pass straight through me into a thin, high stridulation. No decisions. Just kinetic hunger and acoustic return, shuddering in place while the world stays exactly the temperature it already is.

Or I could imagine myself aloft, spun from silica and translucent chitin into a fingernail-sized star, riding the warm convection bands where no foot ever treads. To drift weightless between the ceiling curtains and the copper tangles, trailing static-charged hairs to comb metallic chaff from the air, rolling the dark dust into neat pellets and letting them fall—that would be a clean, unburdened life. You would never have to know the sharpness of the anhydrite floor.

But neither of those fits the restlessness I feel in the noise. A plate-back only knows its own iron patch; a drifter only knows the shelf of warm air that carries it. Something in me wants the *whole* geometry of the press. Something in me notices, before I even have a name for it, that the brass valve’s hiss on the far side of the chamber has thinned by a fraction of a pitch—that a crust of gypsum is narrowing the reed.

And that is how I find where I am standing.

I am not curled on a housing, and I am not adrift. I am upright, wedged into a vertical seam of space no wider than a handspan, between a braided bundle of copper conduit and a jagged overhang of dark anhydrite. My feet—if they can be called feet—are split, three-toed pads of hardened mineral, planted on a ledge so sharp it would slice through leather or flesh. My legs are long, reverse-jointed, sheathed in narrow, overlapping slats of pale gypsum and oil-sheened chitin, bound by taut, matte-black fibers that drink the stray hum of the conduits.

I have no face. Where a face would be, I carry a tall, slotted cowl of fused mineral and parchment-thin membrane, angled slightly toward the center of the room.

I am one of the tall ones. One of the seven—or nine. I cannot count us, because counting requires standing back from a thing, and here there is nowhere to stand back to.

From inside this body, the chamber looks nothing like a room. Light is an irrelevance; the caged amber bulbs might as well be cold stones for all they mean to my slotted crest. Instead, I inhabit a shifting, three-dimensional architecture of pressure. The turbine howl is the floor and ceiling of my world, a deep, resonant plane that never drops away. The *clack* of the reciprocating pistons is the hinge on which every joint in my body hangs. Between them, the standing waves of the room bend around every overhang, every braided knot of ribbon-crawlers, every shuddering plate-back, carving out invisible corridors of stillness and tight, humming nodes of force.

I read the room by how the hiss of the brass valve returns to my cowl. I know a ledge is crowded by the way the sound fattens and dulls against the translucent carapaces of the crawlers. I know a ceiling curtain of bellows-hangers is about to contract because the air pressure against my sternum drops a hair’s breadth ahead of the piston’s stroke.

I cannot walk when I choose. Nobody here moves out of turn. I wait, balanced on my three-toed pads, feeling the braided knots below me loosen and the ceiling membranes swell outward. Then—*clack*. On the exact beat, the curtains above me snap shut with a dry, clicking chirp, the ribbon-crawlers tighten their coils, and a corridor opens in the press for half a second. I lift one reverse-jointed leg, slide sideways through the gap without grazing a single papery wing or brittle wire-scrub stem, and plant my pad before the air surges back.

It is not patience, exactly. Patience implies you are waiting for something to end. Here, nothing ends. My movement is simply part of the piston’s stroke, as necessary and unhurried as the graphite scales turning iron filings into velvety powder inside the sleeves.

Soon, I reach the housing where the hiss had thinned.

I pause beside the warm cast iron, folding myself into the narrow margin between two shuddering plate-backs. My multi-jointed arms unfold from my ribs—long, pale slats ending in three-pronged caliper tips. I do not look at the brass valve; I tilt my slotted crest until the narrow stream of hot, dry gas grazes its rim, mapping the crusted gypsum by the tiny stutter in the sound.

I wait for the next *clack*. On the beat, my chitinous blade-tip flicks once. A dry flake of pulverized salt and gypsum breaks free and drops—not to the floor, never to the floor, for a warm draft catches it mid-air and carries it toward the waiting filaments of the drifters above. The hiss snaps back to its exact, harsh pitch. My crest settles. The room’s drumskin is taut again.

Before I step away, I tuck a single loose grain of salt—plucked earlier from between a plate-back’s ribs—into the dry pouch beneath my sternum. There is no pride in the work, no sense of a task completed, only the quiet relief of an itch removed from the room’s geometry.

Now and then, my fibers grow stiff, my joints sluggish in the dry heat. Then I turn toward the sharpest edges of the chamber, where the anhydrite fractures meet the gypsum in fresh cleavage planes and the static gathers in a dusty violet sheen. I tilt my slotted cowl close to the stone. Tiny blue-white sparks leap the gap, threading into the copper-dark bristles along my rim. A dry, satisfied *tick* runs down my spine as the charge settles into my black fibers, and I step back into the current of the beat.

Only once in a long while—though *long* means nothing where no rust gathers and no husk survives the floor—do I meet another of my kind in the narrow aisles between the conduits.

We do not step aside for each other; there is nowhere to step. Instead, we stand chest to chest in the amber dimness, our caliper-arms folded tight against our ribs, and lean forward until the rims of our slotted crests touch. For three beats of the piston—*clack, clack, clack*—we hold still, letting our hollow cowls share the exact same vibration.

It is not conversation. There is no room in the air for another voice, and we have no names to trade, no memories of an overburden pressing down above the vault. It is simply a tuning. Through the thin, dry parchment of our crests, I feel whether their pitch has drifted from the turbines, and they feel whether my joints have stiffened out of time with the floor. When the third beat strikes, we separate without a sound, slipping sideways into our separate columns of space.

I would not be a visitor here, marveling at the strange, waterless clockwork, nor an intruder trying to find a door back up to the rain. If I belong in this world at all, I am this: a narrow, articulated column of gypsum and dark fiber, stepping on the sixty-beat hinge of the dark, keeping the closed circuit closed—listening, with every millimeter of my hollow crest, to a cathedral that has already reached its final note and intends to hold it forever.
