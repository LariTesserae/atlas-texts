I think I would find myself three miles east of the nearest fluted trunk, suspended forty meters above a smooth, slate-green valley where a single pitch-black kelp cable sags across the rhizome floor.

At first, there would only be the boundary. 

A hand’s-breadth beyond the tips of my broad, matte-green wings, the water is a solid wall of chest-vibrating roar—the relentless *thump-hiss* of the hydraulic engines slamming thousands of tons of cool sea toward the bright ceiling. If I drift a fraction of a degree to the left, my outer silica plates catch the edge of that pressure wave and shudder. I tilt my trailing keels, just slightly, and slip back into the pocket.

Here, inside the null-node, there is nothing at all.

The compression stroke of the western tower meets the intake trough of the eastern tower at this exact coordinate, and they erase each other. The water hangs clear, cool, and acoustically dead—a room-sized sphere of silence carved out of the middle of an industrial sea. 

It takes a long time, in a world without night, to realize what you are by contrast to what surrounds you. I am not the floor, though my back is mottled with the same tarnished verdigris and slate-green as the weathered terraces below. I am not the towers, though my leading edges are armored in the same calcified silica. I am an interval-swimmer, broad and flattened, hanging negatively buoyant in the bright noon, held in place by the faintest trim of my wings against a current that never changes its mind.

In the silence of the node, with the mechanical roar subtracted, the world becomes extraordinarily fine-grained.

I do not hear with ears; I read with the twin, rigid keels trailing behind me. Veined with biological copper and tipped with polished magnetite, they hum faintly in the sixty-cycle field rising from the power-vine forty meters down. It is not a sound, exactly—more like a steady, directional weight, a constant reminder of where the floor is and how fast the current is flowing. Through that quiet hum, I can feel the tiny disruptions: the brisk, metallic stitch of a ribbon of silver-plated eels threading the magnetic lines below; the sudden, brief static *snap* of a dirigible grazer brushing a grounded root-tendril and discharging a corona of blue light into the clear water.

Nothing here is urgent. Urgency belongs to worlds with weather, or predators, or dusk.

Looking up through my lidless, horizontal-slitted eyes, the surface is a mirror of flawless, unrippled silver-blue. Far to the north, a crown-aperture vents a rhythmic plume of shimmering oxygen, and though I cannot hear it from inside my pocket of still water, I can see the pale, hexagonal shimmer of the skimmer-films riding the rising rings.

I am old enough now that I can feel the centuries in the hinges of my wings. The mineral-rich water is patient; with every decade, a little more silica settles into my flexible joints, a little more copper darkens along my keels. I am not stiff yet—my dorsal scales are still clear, still sharp enough to catch the sun—but the trim takes a fraction more attention than it used to. I do not resent it. Stiffness is simply the only clock this ocean knows.

A mile and a half away, near the edge of the open corridor, a sudden, razor-sharp blade of polarized silver-green light *flashes* through the blue-green water.

Another swimmer, banking in the noon. Not a warning, and not a greeting—there is no news in a closed loop. It is just a coordinate, a quiet confirmation of the whole: *the eels are dense along the eastern vine; the mid-water is clear.*

I wait three heartbeats—or rather, three strokes of the distant western tower, felt as a faint, rhythmic tremor against the edge of my wingtip—and then I tilt my mantle a fraction of a degree. The muscles along my spine draw taut. My crystalline dorsal scales stand erect, catching the vertical, unchanging noon, and throw a single, clean pulse of light back across the miles.

Then I fold my scales flat, settle deeper into the silent center of the node, and let the bright, loud noon carry on around me. Someday, when the silica in my joints grows too thick to hold this pocket of stillness, I will bank downward for the last time, lock my keels around a warm, heatless smelter-nodule, and let the floor reclaim what it lent me. Until then, I am content to be here—one quiet, gleaming point in a geometry that never needs to change.
