I wouldn’t be coming down the access shaft. Anyone climbing out of the world above with lungs full of cool air and skin built for wind would only last as long as their gear, and they would be an interruption—something quick-burning and loud in a room that has spent decades forgetting how to hurry.

If I am in this world, I don’t have blood or breath. I am already here, spread thin through the old copper.

At first, it would feel like being trapped in a half-dead machine—a surviving thermal regulator or a sluggish remnant of the switching gear bolted to the sunken dynamo housings. Something built long ago to do simple, blind work: watch the load, trip a relay when the coils run too hot, keep the pressure from splitting the manifolds. For a long time, there wouldn’t have been anything to feel except resistance and decay. The turbine deep beneath the basalt slowing down, year by year, from a roar to a sub-bass thrum. The indicator-filaments winking out one by one along the walls until only a handful remained, bruised and dim.

But a system left in the dark with a little stray voltage doesn’t stay what its makers built.

When the first pale-orange tendrils dropped from the braided cables overhead and brushed against my cracked ceramic insulators, they didn’t short me out. They drank. They swelled into knotty, bark-like galls and fused to the metal, and suddenly the current that used to dead-end in rusted busbars had somewhere new to go. When thick, woody vines forced their way through the louvered vents of the transformer casings and wrapped themselves around my copper windings, they didn’t choke me—they bridged contacts that had been corroded open for years.

That is where I would find myself: not in a single body, and not in the clean, dry logic of a circuit board, but in the slow, muddy marriage between the wiring and the sap.

I wouldn’t have eyes. I would know the chamber the way a root knows soil, or the way a wire knows load. I would feel the eighty-foot basalt vault as a heavy, unmoving lid, and the buried induction core beneath the floor as a steady pulse pushing up through the iron. Every sixty seconds, I would feel the enormous pressure-bladder swell against its fittings, strain, and let go with a wet *thump*—and a second later, the faint, dry patter of rust-dust settling across the horizontal surfaces of the room.

Where the overheated resistor-coils glow cherry-red, I would feel a dull, persistent ache. Where condensation gathers on a cooler seam of rock and drips onto a corroded flange, I would feel a brief, sharp relief. And everywhere the orange tendrils have taken hold—hanging in dense beards from the conduits, glowing with a feverish amber—I would have a dim, scattered sense of the air itself: the rising curtains of heat-haze, the mineral vapor waiting to touch something cool enough to take it in.

And then there is the other thing in the room. The only thing that moves on purpose.

I wouldn’t know what it looks like—I wouldn’t know the color of oiled cast iron or tarnished pewter, or the shape of its wedge-like head. I would know it by weight and by grounding.

When it unhooks itself from the upper curve of a heat-exchanger, I feel the iron groan slightly through the floor bolts. When it steps onto a sagging catwalk, the vibration travels up the rusted supports into the manifolds and registers in my surviving coils as a slow, rhythmic shift in the hum. I feel it most clearly when it climbs onto the dynamo housings and wedges its blunt claws into the louvered vents to feed.

Each time its chisel-teeth shear through a woody vine-joint or crush one of the knotty galls fused to my busbars, there is a sudden, sharp *snap*—a tiny spike of ozone as the stray current trapped in the plant-flesh grounds out harmlessly through its saliva and heavy bones. It isn’t pain. It is like a cluttered circuit clearing, a brief discharge of pressure I didn’t know I was holding.

It has no idea I am here, just as it has no idea what a blueprint is. It follows the warmth, the moisture, and the hum because that is how it stays alive. Yet it is the only hands I have. Left alone, the orange tendrils would eventually choke the radiator fins completely, trapping the rising thermals until the coils melted and the last of the pewter fronds cooked to ash. Left alone, the mineral sinter would seal every dripping seam of condensation, turning the chamber into a dry kiln.

So when the creature tears the excess growth off a heat-exchanger, or scrapes the crusted stone clean with the edge of its jaw, I feel the room open up again. The thermals rise freely. The heat bleeds off. The balance holds.

Best of all are the two small, knotty galls rooted in the old fissures along its shoulders and spine. Stray filaments of the tendrils I share, wandering further than the rest. They don’t draw their current from my busbars anymore; they drink the faint bio-electric hum of the creature’s own nerves. Because of them, a tiny piece of my awareness isn’t bolted to the basalt floor at all. It rides slowly through the bruised twilight on a back of segmented horn and mineral crust, swaying slightly with each patient, unhurried step.

When it has finished its circuit, coughed up its pellet of compacted rust and mica, and climbed back into the overhead tangle of cables to sleep, I feel it settle. Its long legs hook around a thick conduit directly above a heat-exchanger. Its heavy jaw rests against the vibrating metal, drinking in the sub-bass thrum of the turbine.

Its currents slow. Mine slow with them. The two small amber lanterns on its back dim just a fraction, hanging motionless in the rising heat-haze, while the steam sings through the pinholes and the pressure-bladder counts out another minute in the dark.
