First, there would be the relief of having nothing hollow left inside.

No lungs to bruise against the forty-hertz drum, no pocket of air trying to rise toward a flat, unreachable ceiling. Just cold fluid and solid tissue, matched to the weight of four hundred meters of freshwater. Everything brittle and warm would have to be given up before the eyes could even open to that twenty-kilometer light.

At first, I think I would drift toward the machines.

In a world so vast and bare, the mind looks for an anchor, and the stations are the only things that interrupt the blue-white emptiness with edges you can trust. Down near the concrete footings, the water loses its sharpest sting, warmed by a degree or two against the riveted steel. There is a strange comfort in the *thud-thud-thud* of the hydraulic hammers when you press close enough to feel it in your jaw—a rhythm so absolute it relieves you of having to keep time for yourself. I could imagine settling there, growing heavy and slow, letting the stray voltage of the copper stator rings thread through my blood, weaving leached iron into a thick, tarnished ridge of shell until I was indistinguishable from a blistered weld on the intake grate.

And yet, the longer I stay near the steel in my mind, the more suffocating that permanence feels. To clamp onto the turbine is to give up the twenty kilometers. It is to trade the whole bright basin for a few centimeters of warm boundary layer and a pulse that belongs to a factory.

So I let go of the metal and rise into the open water.

Up here, a hundred meters below the windless mirror of the surface, the daylight is cleanest. Nothing touches the stone; nothing hums with high-voltage current. If I were to belong here, I would have to shed bone and shell altogether—become something like the great solitary ctenophores, a house-sized cathedral of clear gel, so nearly made of water that the forty-hertz chord passes straight through without finding anything to rattle. To exist only as a slight bend in the shafts of sun, a shimmer like heat off summer rock, straining microscopic grazers from the freezing roar without ever knowing what a wall feels like.

There is a terrible peace in that. Too much peace. No edges, no memory, no way to tell one kilometer of blue-white from the next.

I find myself sinking again—not back to the factories, not all the way to the basalt, but down into the middle darkless plain where the sound has shape.

Because the roar is not uniform. Down here, suspended above the sharp, unrounded shelves of black rock and quartz, the industrial chord stops being a wall and becomes a geometry. The forty-hertz bass meets its own reflection off the drowned cliffs; the metallic whine crosses itself and cancels out. If you move slowly enough, pushing through the heavy, stinging pressure of the noise, you can feel the water thin. You cross an invisible threshold, no thicker than a membrane, and the world drops away.

It is not silence like an empty room. It is silence like stepping out of a gale into the lee of a stone wall, where your ears ring for a moment with the memory of wind, and then settle.

Here, I begin to know what I am.

I am not made of gel, and I am not welded to iron. I am long and narrow, pale as skimmed milk, with copper-rich blood running close enough to the ribs to catch the refracted light in a faint, icy blue. While I was crossing the loud water, everything delicate about me was locked away—tucked flat into deep bony grooves along my skull and spine, sealed behind muscular flaps so the bass wouldn't shred it to pulp.

Inside the pocket of quiet, the flaps part.

The plumes unfurl—feathery, branching, impossibly soft—and suddenly the empty three-meter bubble of water is full of information. I can taste the faint mineral trace of a fault vent kilometers away, carried on a drift too slow to stir a grain of sand. I can feel the tiny, intricate shudder of another’s jaw-clicks, sharp and clear inside this invisible room, dying completely at the wall of sound just beyond my tail.

This is where I would live: not in the warm shadow of the turbines, and not in the indifferent drift of the upper lens, but in the architecture of the nulls.

More specifically, I think I would be found near the floor—at the edge of one of the lower pockets, tucked into the lee of a fractured granite shelf where the light is a cooler, deeper blue. Above me, the open water roars. Below me, twenty meters down, a mica-pale scavenger sheet glides over the basalt, vacuum-sealing itself over an exhausted eel, leaving behind a skeleton scrubbed as white and clean as porcelain.

Those bones are what draw me out of the quiet.

Most of the listeners gather in pairs or small, patient clusters in the midwater sanctuaries, brushing their plumes together in the long, unchanging daylight. I would visit them, sometimes—folding my crests away, tucking my clawed limbs tight against my ribs, launching myself blind and deafened into the physical blow of the forty-hertz chord, navigating by the razor-sharp silhouettes of distant ridges until I burst back into the next pocket of stillness.

But my own place is lower, where the quiet sits close enough to the stone to be marked.

When the scavenger sheets lift away and clear, I dive through the narrow band of roaring water beneath my sanctuary, gather the bleached eel-ribs and cartilage before the slight drift can scatter them, and carry them back to the granite shelf. Wedged neatly into the quartz veins, stacked into a small, pale tower on the black rock, they point straight up through the deafening darkless blue to the doorway of dead water overhead.

In a sea that never changes, where no storm ever topples what you build and no silt ever buries it, a cairn is the only way to leave a note. I would be the one who tends the markers at the edge of the sound—resting with my plumes half-unfurled in the still water, watching the ruler-straight ribbons of blind eels thread along the power cables below, listening to the quiet hold its shape against the roar.
