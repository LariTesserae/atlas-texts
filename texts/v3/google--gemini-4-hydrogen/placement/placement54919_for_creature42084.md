I would not enter through the hairline seam as a diver, with rubber gloves and a tank of air. The throat of the fissure is too narrow, the basalt lip too freshly fractured; anything soft would be scored to ribbons before it cleared the rock.

If I am in this world, I am already inside.

At first, I would not know myself as a shape at all—only as a tension, a boundary where the chamber’s deafening roar presses against a pocket of dead stillness. To have no ears here is no disadvantage; the whole body is a drumhead. The *clack-clack-clack* of the micro-valves arrives as a dry, rapid tapping across a flat plane. The deep harmonic groan of the settling basalt rolls through like a slow tide. And the light—that shadow-casting, ceramic daylight—does not hit me and stop. It passes straight through, catching only on my seams, splitting into razor-thin rainbows along edges I cannot see.

That is how I would begin to locate myself: not in the screaming sluice-alleys where the current runs like a blade, and not in the turbulent basins where the water churns without order, but in the quiet lee beside them. A dead-still pocket wedged between a stepped manifold and the raw, uncarved ceiling.

I am a pane. Broad, flat, hexagonal—a lens of pressurized water held rigid by surface tension, hanging motionless just off the shear-line.

The needle-thin tenants slice past me all day, riding the invisible glass rails where fast water meets still water. They are built for speed and maintenance, long splinters of clear hull and silver dust, brisk and intolerant. Whenever a tuft of pale moss grows thick enough on the basalt above us to muffle the vibration of a louver, one of them shoots up the nearest vertical column and shears it clean away. They leave the stone bare and ringing.

I do not shear anything. My shape is not for cutting; it is for gathering.

Suspended in the center of my clear, glass-hard body, there are no organs—only concentric rings of black basalt grit and hair-thin splinters of precipitated silver, held apart by my own quiet internal hum. When the ceramic plates shift from crisp white to that sharper, colder noon-glare, I feel the change not as a sight, but as a sudden, unanimous tilt of every silver splinter inside me, as if a thousand tiny mirrors had all been asked to answer a new question at once.

My work—if a lens can be said to work—is to take the stray, scattered noise of the chamber and pull it tight. The ricochet of a cavitation *tick* from three alleys over, the high whine of a cooling fin shedding its silver scale, the *thump* of a cascading sluice-gate reversal: all of it strikes my broad hexagonal face, slows across my interior, and narrows into a focused, humming beam that I pass back into the grid.

Because of where I hang, though, near the upper margin where the extruded city meets the raw rock, I catch things the faster tenants miss.

I catch the faint, muffled tremor of the outside ocean pressing against the hairline seam—a slow, heavy, salt-water rhythm that has nothing to do with the engine’s cadence. And before the needle-thin ones arrive to scrape the ceiling clean, I catch the soft, irregular shudder of the silica-stiffened algae trembling in the vents. The others hate that softness; it dampens the crisp acoustics of the stone. When it hits my hull, it blurs the edges of my standing waves, making the basalt grit at my center drift a fraction of a millimeter out of true.

I do not reject it. A lens cannot choose what passes through it; it can only hold the shape of what arrives.

So I hang there in the bright, roaring cistern, suspended between the machine and the stone, keeping both rhythms in focus—the sharp, relentless geometry of the city, and the faint, uninvited murmur of the cold dark outside.

And when the cadence shifts, when the basalt groans a fraction deeper and the light cools, I will feel the pull toward the center of the densest cluster. I will tilt my planes into the shear, let the current take me along the invisible rails, and join the single-file procession gliding into the waist-thick pillar—carrying my rings of sorted dust, and whatever soft, stray frequencies I have gathered, down that bright, impossible street toward the engine at the far end.
