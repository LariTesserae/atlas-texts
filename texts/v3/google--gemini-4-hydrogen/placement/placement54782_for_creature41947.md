I would be a **culvert-and-crust tender** on the fourth terrace down from the eastern mesa—one of the quiet, middle-level workers who belong neither to the high crane-ways of the viaducts nor to the roaring, heat-struck floors of the foundries, but to the wet masonry seams in between.

My beat is a three-hundred-meter stretch of retaining wall where the warm return-water from the district powerhouse steps down into the tenement cisterns and the vertical slate fungus-beds. It is a loud, dim, perpetually glistening place, tucked under the iron belly of a rail viaduct and facing a wide, milky settling basin.

If you found me there at mid-afternoon, when the clouds thicken and the slow, warm rain begins its hours-long settle over the plateau, you would see a broad-chested figure in a heavy, slate-grey coat of boiled viaduct-silk, its shoulders dark and bead-slick with reptile oil. My hair is cropped close to the skull, bound back at the nape with a greased cord, and dusted pale along the temples where lime-spray dries whenever I lean too close to a blow-off valve. Around my waist hangs a belt of pale amphibian leather, weighted with the tools of a trade that is half plumbing, half masonry, and half husbandry: a short-handled scaling hammer cast in soft brass, a pair of long bone picks, a horn-handled scraper, and a lidded tin of tallow-and-ash paste for stopping weeping joints.

I do not rush, because nothing on the fourth terrace can be hurried.

The work begins with listening. Before I ever touch a wrench to a flange, I press the flat of my palm—or, if the iron is hot, the horn pommel of my hammer—against the big cast-iron return-pipes that run along the wall like thick, sweating arteries. Through the soles of my iron-shod boots and up through the bones of my wrist comes the layered voice of the city: the deep, distant *thump-hiss* of the powerhouse reciprocating pumps above, the steady rush of mineral water in the culverts below, and the high, metallic whine of the midge-gyres drifting between the viaduct girders.

Inside that noise, I am listening for the *choke*.

The warm water of the plateau carries dissolved limestone in its blood. Leave an iron pipe alone for a season, and it grows a pale, stony lining from the inside out, narrowing its throat until the pressure backs up and the steam-chests in the tenement kitchens go lukewarm. Tap the pipe with the brass hammer, and a clean line rings dull and deep; a scaled-up line answers with a tight, flat *tock*, like striking solid karst.

When I find a tight section, I don’t tear it out. I shut the brass gate-valve—slowly, turning the spoked wheel a quarter-turn at a time so the water-hammer doesn’t crack the old mortar—and unbolt the inspection plate. While the warm, milky sludge drains out over my boots and into the gutter, I scrape the pale lime-crust from the iron throat with my horn chisel, careful not to gouge the metal beneath. Everything I scrape out goes into a grease-cloth sack at my hip; by the end of a shift, it’s heavy with wet mineral paste that the bricklayers on the next terrace will mix with ash to patch the retaining walls.

The trickier part of the job isn’t the iron. It’s the living crust.

Every square meter of brick and girder along my wall is claimed. Papery communal hives hang from the underside of the viaduct like grey lanterns; calcified worm-tubes crust the damp mortar around the pipe-brackets; and along the broad, sloped plinth at the base of the wall, three or four of the great, meter-wide grazers are always inching their way through the wet rust-colored slime-fungi.

You cannot clear a pipe-bracket by smashing the nests around it. Rouse a hive in the damp heat and you’ll have a thousand stinging things inside your oiled collar before you can blow your whistle; knock a calcified colony off a weeping joint and you might find it was the only thing holding the softened mortar together. So you learn to work around them, trimming a millimeter here, shifting a bracket-bolt half an inch there, reading the wall the way an old pilot reads a canal.

One of the grazers—a broad, slate-backed elder with five vertical lime-marks painted across its third shell-plate—spends half its year circling the warm discharge culvert at the end of my beat. Its shell is a small city of its own, encrusted with grey barnacles and tufted with pale mineral efflorescence. Almost every afternoon, I find it wedged right across the brass drain-grate I need to clear.

I never try to pry it loose. You couldn’t lift it with a crowbar without cracking its plates, and nobody harms a grazer. Instead, I crouch beside it in the warm rain, letting the steam rise off my silk shoulders, and wait for my knees to settle. With the flat of my bone pick, I gently clear the clogged iron filings and lime-grit out from under the wet, leathery edge of its skirt. Dozens of blind, pale crustaceans scatter into the dark beneath its belly as the pick slides past. The grazer’s rasping mouthparts pause for a beat—*scritch-scritch…*—and then resume their slow, patient work on the fungi. Once its underside is comfortable again, it will haul itself three inches forward over the next twenty minutes, leaving the grate clear.

While I wait, I eat. From an oiled pocket inside my coat, I pull a dense, salty slab of pressed slime-fungi loaf wrapped in a square of scraped amphibian skin, still warm from the steam-chest in my own tenement kitchen two terraces up. Above me, a pale-feathered aerialist drops out of the midge-cloud, folds its oiled wings, and perches on the iron railing of the viaduct, watching me with bright, wet eyes. I pinch off a corner of the loaf—not much, just the crumbly edge—and flick it onto the wet stone beside the railing. The bird hops down, snaps it up, and shakes the rain from its feathers without a sound.

When a rail-crew passes along the viaduct overhead, or a silk-harvester hangs in her greased-leather harness fifty feet up to cut away an empty cocoon curtain, we don’t shout. There is no point fighting the drop-forges and the swarms. If I need the powerhouse above to drop the pressure on the third return-line for an hour, I unhook the brass reed-whistle from my neck, set my feet wide on the wet cobbles, and blow two low, steady notes—a flat fourth that sits cleanly underneath the insect whine and carries straight up the wet stone face.

A moment later, a figure leans out from the powerhouse gallery, half-hidden in a drift of white turbine vapor, and answers with a crisp two-finger sign against the dark brick. *Heard. Dropping it now.*

By dusk, when the afternoon rain thins to a warm, hanging mist and the amber glow of the low-voltage filaments begins to warm the copper-screened windows of the tenements, my satchel is heavy with lime-scale and my knuckles are slick with tallow. The gliding reptiles drop out of the grey sky like folded umbrellas, packing themselves along the warm brick chimneys to click softly in the cooling air and keep the midges off the laundry lines.

I check the last valve, tap the iron once with my brass hammer to hear it ring clean and open, and turn up the long, wet stone stairs toward home. Through the horn soles of my boots, the city hums on—slow, heavy, and unbroken—and I am just one more thing in it that keeps the water moving, the stone patched, and the crust from choking the seams.
