I would not be in the middle of the mat, where the beetles climb over one another in a slow, suffocating tide of wax and chitin. Too much movement there—even at an inch an hour, the constant nudging of neighbors, the endless dry *hiss* of wing-case against wing-case, would feel like living inside a shifting scree slope.

I would be further out, wedged into the narrowest margin where the living crust thins and stops: the hot, buzzing edge of a magnetite seam.

At first, if I were to wake into this place, I would not know what I was, only what I was pressed against. Above me, so close I could feel the static charge pulling at the hairs on my back, is a raw overhang of fractured quartz. Below me is basalt, warm as a kiln brick and trembling with a sound so large and constant it ceases to be sound at all. It is simply the weight of the world—a deep, iron-throated roar from the crushers above, braided with the high, thin whine of the transformers. There is no silence to compare it to, so it feels like stillness. Like lying at the bottom of a deep, heavy sea that never turns over.

I have no eyes, and if I had them, there would be nothing to see. The dark here is total, thick as soot, broken only when the dust shifts and a pinpoint spark snaps blue-white across the gap. I don’t see the flash; I feel it as a sudden tightening in the air, a sharp prickle along my edges, followed by the metallic tang of ozone settling onto my plates.

To know where I am, I have to read the stone through my belly.

My underside is flat, wide, and pressed flush against the basalt. Through it, the mountain speaks in two languages: the immense, unchanging bass of the engines, which I ignore the way you ignore the weight of your own skin, and the tiny, sharp interruptions that ride on top of it. A grain of silica sliding down a crystal face. The faint, rhythmic *tick-tick* of a mite’s claws three inches away, testing the edge of the charged zone before thinking better of it and retreating to the crowd. The slow, agonizing creak of a crevice-scorpion’s joint as it shifts its weight to avoid a sharp quartz edge.

I am very thin—scarcely thicker than a flake of mica—and shaped like a broad, dull-grey teardrop. Along the seams of my segmented back grow rows of stiff bristles, dark with iron oxide I’ve scavenged from the floor. Every few days, when the stray current drops for a fraction of a beat, I flex my edges just enough to draw a fresh coat of the grey rock flour up over my carapace.

The dust clings to my bristles and stands on end, aligned to the invisible lines of force humming through the ridge. In this way, I wear the mountain’s own static as a coat. When the voltage surges and a spark finally leaps from the magnetite ridge to my back, it doesn’t burn me. It skitters harmlessly across the iron-dusted spikes, tracing my outline in a brief, ghostly halo of blue-white fire before grounding back into the rock.

I am a flat-back, though I have no name for myself. I am simply a shape that fits a crack.

What I do here is wait. Waiting is not an absence of action; it is the whole architecture of survival. To hurry in this air is to die—to crack the cured wax at my joints, to spend moisture that can never be replaced. So I stay wedged in my two-inch cleft for days, for weeks, perhaps for years, while the unseen shifts of men or machines turn above the ridge without ever knowing this hollow exists.

Sometimes, when the living mat of beetles and silverfish swells slightly—pushed outward by sheer pressure, or seeking a sliver of cooler stone—one of them gets shoved too close to my seam. I feel it long before it arrives: the panicked, clumsy scrabbling of legs on loose dust, the sudden disruption in the magnetic hum as a body breaks the field.

Then comes the *snap*.

A spark arcs across the gap, sharp and brief, locking the stray arthropod’s muscles in an instant. It topples into the flour-soft powder, twitching once, then lying still.

Only then do I move. I slide out from beneath my quartz overhang fraction by fraction of an inch, keeping my belly low to the basalt so the stray current stays above me. I have no jaws—chewing would open wet flesh to the kiln-dry air—so I unfold the armored needle tucked beneath my head-plate. I find the soft membrane between the stunned beetle’s leg segments, press the rim of my face tight against it to seal the puncture, and drink. Thick, warm, syrupy fluid, drawn straight from one sealed vessel into another without losing a single molecule to the heat.

When I am finished, I back slowly into my crevice, pull the grey dust back over my plates, and settle my belly flat against the vibrating stone once more.

It is a small, airless, deafening existence, wedged between the weight of a mountain and the relentless hum of industry. Yet there is a strange perfection in it. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is hurried. I am as patient as the basalt, coated in the same iron dust, listening in the dark to the endless thunder of a world I will never see, and waiting for the next spark to tell me it is time to eat.
