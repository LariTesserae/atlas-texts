If I were to find myself in this world, it would not be as a visitor. A visitor brings heat, haste, and a lantern—all things the basin rejects. To belong here at all, I would have to be made of the same slow mineral patience as the girders.

I would find myself mid-level, wedged into the tight canyon between the two windowless condenser blocks, standing on a riveted catwalk that has long since been welded to its stanchions by pale-grey lime. There is no sky above me, only the pressing weight of near-freezing brine and the lattice of pipes overhead. I wouldn’t be looking at any of it. I would have no eyes to waste on the dark.

Instead, I would be one of the dwellers—heavy-boned, stooped, and plated in decades of aragonite and iron scale, my joints dark and slick with my own thick oil.

My world would not be made of shapes in light, though I would know the murky halos of the amber gantry-bulbs by the faint, steady warmth radiating through their salt-clouded cages. My world would be a geometry of hums and resistances, read through the porous, dish-shaped plate of my face and the soles of my heavy feet. Standing on the iron grate, I would feel the sub-audible thrum of the braided power cables vibrating in my ribs like a second pulse. Three alleys away, I would know by the slight, rhythmic stutter in the floor-plates that a copper-sheathed roller was beginning to bind against its ceramic bearing.

That is where my work would be: in the quiet maintenance of the seams.

I wouldn’t be young—not anymore. The decades of clinging to the warm glass of a gantry-lamp like a flat, pale limpet would be long behind me, and my first thin wafers of scale would have thickened into heavy, overlapping armor across my back and shoulders. Neither would I be ready for the lowest alleys. My knees and elbows would still turn smoothly, well-oiled and silent, leaving scarcely a ripple in the syrupy water when I reach out—taking half a minute, smooth and deliberate—to scrape a crust of lime from a brass valve stem with the chisel-edge of my hand.

I think I would keep a circuit along the dead-end hoists, where the silent conveyor chains surface from the electrolytic vats. Every few hours, a heavy, regular block of dull metal or sealed ceramic arrives at the top of the track, cold and uncollected, made for hands that are no longer in the basin. I would wait for it, lift it off the rollers before it can jam the gear-train, and carry it down the narrow, slate-packed stairs toward the lower streets. There, I would fit it into the low, blind retaining walls we build along the curbs—not out of any memory of architecture, but simply to keep the dense, burning cold-streams from spilling out of the overflow sluices into the trenches where we rest.

There is a particular kind of companionship in a place that has no use for voices. While I kneel in the dark to pack a weeping pipe-flange with handfuls of colorless algae and stiff, glass-spined sea-lilies, a blind, slate-colored crab might clamber unhurriedly over my plated back to reach a busbar, treating me as just another ledge of the town. And in the hollow space behind my left shoulder-plate, where the oil gathers thickest, a translucent eel might thread itself in to shelter from the brine, grazing on the tiny parasites in my joints. I would leave it there. Everything here that survives does so by fitting into the tolerances of something else.

Every few weeks, when the cold begins to seep past my armor and stiffen the wax in my hip-plates, I would descend into the settling vats. Hanging upside down from the overhead grates by my hooked hind-limbs, I would part the heavy mineral plates across my chest, exposing the soft, mushroom-ribbed folds beneath. For four or five days at a time, I would hang motionless in the dark above the lead anodes, letting the ghostly, warm plumes of dissolved sulfur and copper drift up through my ribs while my pitted face drinks the faint, stray voltage bleeding off the plates. Down there, lit from below by nothing brighter than a watch-dial flicker, there is no distinction between feeding and resting, or between myself and the battery I am plugged into.

Sometimes, a long way off through the maze of transformer sheds, another dweller will press a knuckle to a brine-pipe and send a slow, low-frequency *tap—tap* through the iron—a request for weight on a sagging cable, or help clearing an intake grate clogged with silt. I would answer by turning the nearest sluice-valve a fraction of an inch, sending a single, unmistakable pressure-pulse rolling through the basin to say I am coming.

And I would know, without dread or hurry, how it ends. One day—decades from now, after a thousand rust-flakes have crossed the copper rollers and a hundred cairns of uncollected metal have risen along the courtyard—the lime-crust will finally outpace my oil. A hip-plate will lock; a caliper hand will stiffen mid-reach. When that happens, I will not linger in the vats to become a burden on the grates. I will walk slowly down the stairs I know by vibration, step over the low retaining wall of blocks I helped to stack, and wade into the refractive, burning pool of a cold-stream. There, in the thickest dark at the bottom of the sinkhole, I will settle onto my knees and stop—letting the pale-grey scale weld me to the basalt floor, until I am just another smooth, stony shape in the town, and the rollers above me keep turning.
