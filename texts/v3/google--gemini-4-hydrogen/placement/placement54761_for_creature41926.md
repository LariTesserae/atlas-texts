I would find myself on a terraced shelf of cooling slag, halfway between a newly split chasm and a knife-edge ridge—high enough that the rock beneath me has begun to lose its sullen cherry-glow, low enough that the thermal updrafts still carry a dry, baking warmth up from the dark.

I would not be a visitor here, nor anything soft enough to bleed on the volcanic glass. To survive a single breath of this thin, grit-heavy air is to belong to it. So I am one of the unnamed, folded things: long-limbed, pumice-skinned, matte and dark as freshly fractured basalt, with gray mineral dust wedged permanently into the creases of my knuckles. My palms, knees, and shoulders are plated in horn. There is no moisture in me to spare for sweat or tears. When I drag my hand across a sheet of obsidian, it makes only a dry, chalky scrape.

In this world, I am a lingerer—one who stays beside a cooling seam a little longer than is wise.

Most of my kind follow the heat as soon as it shifts, creeping four-limbed across the razor-edged scoria toward the newest fissures, where the ground still groans and the gas seeps warm and odorless from the crust. I understand the hurry, slow as it is. Without borrowed warmth, the hinges of my knees and elbows stiffen until they lock like cold iron. Staying too long on cooling stone is how you become part of the scree.

Yet I linger on this shelf because of the windbreak I built here, and the cairn just upslope.

The windbreak is only three slabs of raw basalt, stacked without mortar to windward of a dying crack. The cherry-glow in the fissure has faded to the color of an ash-choked ember, and the charcoal-colored filaments clinging to its lip are sparse now. When a silent thermal funnel dances past, the filaments do not bend; they rattle like dry iron wool and shed their fine gray spores into the lee of my stones. I squat beside them, pluck a handful, and grind them slowly between wide, flat molars. It is a meager, sulfur-heavy meal, barely enough to keep the slow bellows of my chest working in the thin air. Still, I clear a fallen chip of scoria away from the crack so the last of the warm vapor can reach the spores.

If I am anything distinct in a place without names, I am a listener to the cooling.

Down by the active faults, the bedrock is too restless; everything is tearing, sliding, or collapsing in dry avalanches that raise billowing black clouds with only a soft, sandy hiss. Up here on the older shelf, the violence is quieter, and you can tell one sound from another. I fold my long arms around my shins, lower my flat, earless jaw directly onto the basalt, and let the world come in through bone.

Through the air, there is almost nothing—only the occasional snap of pale-violet static in the dust. Through the stone, the basin is vast and busy. I feel the glassy *tick* and *ping* of a pumice spire contracting a mile away before it shears off and vanishes into the starless dark. I feel the deep, muffled *thump* of a new fault opening far below, where the heat thickens in the unseen chasms.

When the chill begins to creep into the horn-plates of my shins, I rise—slowly, testing every brittle blister of rock with the blunt, mineral-hardened pad of a finger before trusting my weight to it—and climb the hundred yards to the ridge.

Here stands the cairn. I did not stack it, though I have wedged a small piece of glass-rock under its base once to keep it from toppling when the shelf tilted. It is taller than I am when crouched, a crooked spine of unmortared slabs leaning out over the chasm, telling anyone whose hands find it in the dark which way the fault is sliding.

I wrap my long fingers around the heavy bottom stone, just above the bedrock. The wind whips grit against my bony brow-ridges, and my small, deep-set eyes catch only a brief violet spark in the haze. I brace my knees, shift my weight, and rock the cairn. Not enough to break its balance. Just enough.

*Thump.*

I wait, letting the stone settle.

*Thump.*

I wait again, feeling the vibration travel down through my palms, into the ridge, and out across miles of razor-sharp basalt.

*Thump.*

Then I release it, sink down into the lee of the ridge, and press my jaw to the cold floor. I am not waiting for a voice; the air here is too thin to carry one, and we have no words to spend on it anyway. I am only waiting to see if the stone answers.

For a long time, there is only the random ticking of cooling slag. Then, faint through miles of fractured crust, distinct from the groan of the chasms, it arrives against my bone:

*Thump. Thump.*

Only two beats—a cairn tilted too far to risk a third—but enough. Someone else is crouched in the soot-choked dark, and for a little while longer, the rock beneath them is holding.

When the vibration fades, I climb back down to my shelf before my joints lock. I step inside the small, roofless ring of stones I stacked three tremors ago—just wide enough for one body—and curl tight. Knees to chest, arms wrapped around my shins, forehead resting against the faint, lingering warmth of the basalt floor. My nostrils pinch shut against the drifting ash. My breathing slows until the thin air scarcely moves in my chest, and from twenty paces away, in the near-total dark, I am indistinguishable from a slump of cooling slag, waiting for the next tremor to tell me where to go.
