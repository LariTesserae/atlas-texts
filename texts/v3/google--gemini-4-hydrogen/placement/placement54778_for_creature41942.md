I would not be down on the wet basalt flags, where the light from the caged bulbs breaks into faint, oily streaks across the drains. And I would not be up near the gray seam of the surface, where the air thins out and smells of nothing at all.

I think I would find myself somewhere in the middle third of the shaft—in the thick of the amber haze, wedged into one of the places where the geometry has been forced to compromise. A corner where a brick boiler-house crowds too close to the raw, glassy basalt of the rift wall, leaving a narrow, three-sided pocket of dead air. From there, you can lay a palm—if you have a palm—against the warm mortar on one side and the cold, sharp-edged stone on the other, and feel the whole vertical town humming through both.

At first, when I try to imagine being there, I catch myself bringing surface habits down with me: looking for a doorway, waiting for a bell to ring a shift-change, listening for footsteps on the switchback stairs. It takes a long time for that hurry to bake out. You have to let three or four hydraulic gates sigh and settle before you stop expecting anyone to come down the catwalks. You have to watch a single drop of condensation gather on the underside of a cold-return main, swell until it pulls away from the sweating iron, and vanish on a manifold below with its small, sharp *tst*, and then wait for the next one, before you realize what kind of time the shaft keeps.

In that slowness, the idea of being a human visitor falls apart. A human body is too wet, too loud, too frantic; its blood wants to boil, its lungs want to cough up the mineral dust, its eyes keep darting toward the cherry glow of the kiln-slits as if something might step out of them. To stay here—to actually *belong* to the seam—I would have to be something that doesn't fight the dry heat with sweat and panic.

Maybe I am not one of the rhizome-tenders, either. They have their work: the slow ratcheting along the trusses, the greasing of the guide-rails, the patient snapping of lime-teeth before they foul the pulleys. They are the hands of the place, knotted and practical, forever trading moisture for leverage.

If I am not the hands, then what is left to be in a shaft that only holds rock, iron, water, and salt?

I think I am something that lives in the *lagging*—or rather, in the places where the lagging fails.

Picture a seam where the tarred felt has split slightly, just enough for a hair-thin thread of steam to escape, silent and invisible in the dry air until it hits the cool face of a steel stanchion bolted through the brick. That is where I would start: not as a creature with a heart, not even a swollen rhizome, but as a crust of rust-colored moss and mineral salt that has learned to hold a shape.

No—moss is too flat, too content to stay in the mortar joints. Look closer at the things that *listen* down here.

The tenders have those brittle, hollow-stemmed reeds sprouting from their backs, trembling in the rising drafts so they can tell a clear flue from a choked louver. Down in the overflow channels, the same reeds root in the sulfur-silt, rattling softly whenever the grates breathe. What if I am something grown from that same impulse—not a mover, not a valve-turner, but a *resonator*?

Let's see what that looks like.

Wedged into that tight angle between the notched brickwork and the conchoidal scar of the basalt, I would have no reason to walk the catwalks. My body, if you could call it that, would be built the way the shaft builds everything else: by accumulation. A core of tough, fibrous reed-root anchored deep in a damp mortar joint, fed by the slow seepage of a sweating pipe overhead. Around that, years of patient mineral skin—layers of pale lime and copper-green verdigris, stiffened with fine ash from the kilns and bound together by a dry, amber resin that never quite hardens in the heat.

I wouldn't have eyes; the filament bulbs are only warmth to me, small dry suns hanging on their wires. I wouldn't have a mouth; I drink through my skin when the cold mains weep, and I let the dry air take the water back when the kilns flare.

Instead, I would be mostly hollow.

A cluster of tall, colorless stalks—dozens of them, fused at the base like organ pipes, or branching out from the salt-crust at different lengths, each one tuned by its own thickness and the amount of mineral grit packed into its joints. When the shaft is healthy, when the brass flywheels are turning at the pace of a minute hand and the brine-tanks are circulating without a hitch, the rising thermal draft moves through me and I make no sound at all—or only a dry, faint *shhh* that disappears into the visceral hum of the steam pipes.

My whole life would be the difference between one vibration and another.

I would know the shaft the way a sleeper knows the house around them: not by looking, but by the weight of the air on their ribs. I would feel the *tick* of a tender's outer crust parting three tiers below, and the soft, fibrous creak as it swells to reach a valve. I would know when a bearing on the upper catwalks is beginning to run dry, hours before the whine becomes audible to an ear, because the steel stanchion bolted through my wall would start to shiver at a slightly sharper pitch.

And when that happened—when a louver choked with lime or a rail went dry—I wouldn't climb the trusses to fix it. I wouldn't have limbs to reach with. I would simply shift the way the draft moved through my reeds. A slight change in the pitch of the rattle; a dry, hollow clicking in the sulfur-silt channels or the tight brick corners, passed along from one stalk-cluster to the next, until the vibration reached whatever bundle of tarred felt and plaster was resting nearest the trouble.

Perhaps that is all I am in this world: not a citizen of the town, and not one of its keepers, just the part of the stonework that notices. A mineral ear wedged into the gap between the brick and the raw basalt, drinking the slow drips, baking in the amber dark, and rattling softly whenever the great lung below misses a breath.
