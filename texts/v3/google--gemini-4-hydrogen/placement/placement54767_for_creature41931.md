At first, if I try to picture myself there, I make the mistake of bringing my own body with me. I imagine standing on a slumped terrace of sphagnum in a soaked coat, teeth aching from the low thrumming in the rock, shouting into the hiss of the underground conduits and watching my breath vanish into air that is already thick enough to drink.

But a body like that doesn’t belong in the valley; it only endures it. It fights the thick air, blunders into stalled curtains of rain, and trusts a horizon that is already splitting into two. If I stay long enough to actually *find* myself there—long enough for the roar to stop feeling like an intrusion and start feeling like the ground beneath my feet—that clumsy visitor dissolves.

Where I end up is not the absolute center of the basin, where the mist is heaviest and the willow-stands flicker like ghosts above themselves. That feels too much like standing in the middle of an argument between two versions of the world. Instead, I find myself in the middle terraces—the long, gentle slopes where the braided streams curve back on themselves and the sedges lean in wide, predictable whorls. It is a working edge of the marsh, close enough to the deep pools to feel the bedrock’s cyclic pulse in my ribs, far enough from the center that the world mostly agrees on where the ground is.

And what I am there is one of the quiet ones—not a hunter crouching beneath the suspended rain, though I understand that patience, and not a wanderer. If I have a shape in that wet slate light, it is long-limbed and low-shouldered, crouching half in the shallows where the silver-green moss meets the black-algae willows.

I notice my hands first. They are not built for gripping tools or striking sparks; they are long-wristed, calloused along the outer edge, pale on the underside where the water beads into trembling spheres and races off without leaving a stain. I am braiding.

That is what draws me most in this place: the idea that you do not build *against* the valley’s broken rules, you weave *along* them. When the sedges are young and pliable, I kneel in the cold silt and feel for the invisible lines of force twisting up out of the eroded lattices below. My milky-rimmed eyes aren’t good for looking across the basin, so I don’t try. I watch the refraction instead—the faint, oily thickening of the air just above the mud, the way a stem bends three inches to the left before it even meets the breeze. I coax the young reeds into those invisible currents, tucking stem under stem, letting the earth’s own warped tension pull the weave tight. In a season, it will be a springy, suspended shelf two feet above the fast-moving water-films—a place to sit without fighting the ground.

While I work, I am listening—not with ears, which would only be battered raw by the constant patter and hiss, but with the slits along my throat and the cage of my ribs. The submerged engine beneath the peat has its own grammar. Most of the time it is a slow, steady swell, a tide of pressure that rises through my heels and settles at the base of my skull. When it shifts key—a slight, tight hesitation before a pocket of air gives way—my hands stop moving. One splayed foot hovers an inch above the silt. I wait for the *crack* somewhere out in the reeds, wait for the three-yard ring of sudden, ringing silence, and wait for the marsh to rush back in.

If I have a particular habit, a thing that makes me *me* among the others, it is that I pay attention to the seams where the silence is about to happen.

Because speech here isn’t made by adding sound to a quiet room; it is made by carving a quiet room out of a roar. I spend hours learning the taut skins of the pools and the stressed pockets of mud along the terrace lips. With a flick of my wrist against a film of water that is pulling too hard against its bank, I can break the tension on purpose—snapping open a brief, hollow bell of stillness. Into that two-second void, I drop two dry clicks and a single low throat-tone, just to say *here*, or *the bank is shifting*, or simply to hear my own voice exist before the hiss of the conduits swallows it again. Then I wait, ribs humming, for the distant *crack* near the black boulders that means someone else has carved a room to answer me.

In the dimmest hours, when the bruised ceiling of cloud presses down until it nearly touches the tops of the horsetails, I leave the reed-beds and wade toward the center—slowly, so I don’t wrench a joint against the heavy air. I find one of the invisible clearings where the ancient field-structures still remember the shape of a roof. There is nothing to see, only the falling condensation curving outward around a three-yard circle of still, slightly warmer air.

I fold my long limbs onto the braided peat beside the others, shoulder to shoulder, our wet-peat backs turned to the rain-haze. Outside the circle, water climbs the lip of a bank and drops through thick air; a wading bird pauses with one foot raised in the shallows. Inside, we do not speak. There is no need to snap the air when we are already close enough to feel each other’s throat-membranes pulsing in time with the rock below. I simply sit in the quiet that isn’t quiet at all—just the steady, shared breathing of a world that has forgotten how to hold a straight line, and doesn't mind.
