If I were to find myself here, I don’t think I would stay standing on the root-mat for long. 

The visitor in boots is an interruption—a vertical, heavy thing that breaks the surface tension of the liverwort and sends a dull, ugly thud down into the warm dark. Standing upright among those crowded, cartilaginous stems, you are always in the way of the rhythm. You block the greenish-gold haze; you muffle the drone of the cane-stalks; you force the great, blind dweller beneath your feet to hold its breath and wait for you to leave.

I would not want to be the thing the room waits out. If I am in this world, I want to be part of the forty-second clock.

So first, the boots would have to go, and the spine that insists on holding a head six feet above the water. Eyes would be the next thing to give up—they are only useful at the very top of the canopy where the normal daylight hits the waxy leaves, and down here in the mist they just ache for sharp edges that don’t exist. What is left, once you let go of the upright frame, is mostly an ear, a skin, and a way of holding on.

At first I might drift toward the sump, drawn by the warmth around the submerged copper pipes, but the sump is already spoken for. The dweller owns the dark water and the concrete plinth; it has already carved its slick tunnels through the petrified peat and claimed the monumental piston rod as its salt-lick. And up on the brass junction boxes, the pale, blind slugs have already perfected the art of small, solitary indifference.

Where, then, is there room for one more quiet thing?

I find myself moving—not swimming, not walking, just *settling*—toward the middle height of the hollow, where the greenish-gold haze is thickest and the perpetual drizzle hangs almost suspended in the warm air. Specifically, toward the three leaning copper pressure vessels, held upright in their cradle of strangler-figs.

There is a seam there, right where the curved copper belly of the middle vessel presses against a thick, woody loop of fig-root. The metal is never quite hot and never quite cold; it holds a steady, feverish warmth from the pressurized water inside, and its surface is crusted with powdery verdigris—bitter, chalky, and green as old bronze. Every forty seconds, when the great flywheel completes its turn and the piston draws back, the copper vessel swells by a fraction of a hair’s breadth, pressing harder into the wood, and then sighs back into itself as the brass relief valve trips above.

That is where I would wedge myself. Not as a beast with legs to snag, and not as a plant rooted in the mat, though I might share something with both.

Let’s see what grows in a crease like that, if it stays there long enough.

At first it would just be a matter of fitting the shape of the gap—a soft, pale cushion of tissue, flattened like a mussel’s foot against the copper on one side and anchored into the damp, fibrous bark of the strangler-fig on the other. No bones to rattle against the sixty-cycle hum. Just a dense, gelatinous body, translucent at the edges like the bladder-pods hanging in the air-roots, stained the color of weak tea where the tannic drizzle washes over it.

If I am there long enough, the hollow begins to teach me what I need to grow.

I wouldn’t need a mouth for chewing, because nothing here needs to be chased or bitten. Instead, my upper surface—the side turned outward toward the rusted gantry and the steam pipes—would slowly gather into shallow, fluted folds, like the underside of a mushroom or a cluster of tiny, upturned cups. When the relief valve *clanks* and the condenser lets out its long, cavernous hiss, the warm, mineral-heavy vapor drifts down through the canopy and settles into those folds. I would drink it the way the dweller’s pink gill-fronds drink it, though on a much smaller, lazier scale: taking in the dissolved copper salts, the trace sulfur, the warm iron-scented mist, letting the minerals stiffen my outer skin just enough to keep my shape against the drizzle.

And on my underside, pressed tight against the copper vessel, I would want no skin at all—just a thin, sensitive membrane, slick with mucus, listening.

From there, I wouldn’t know the hollow by what it looks like. I wouldn’t know the color of the greenish-gold haze, or the cathedral height of the embankment walls. I would know the world as a nested set of pulses:

First, the sixty-cycle hum. It comes up through the fig-roots from the buried conduits, and down through the copper from the gantry’s tar-sheathed cables—a constant, fine-grained vibration, like sand pouring through a sieve so fast it feels solid. It keeps my tissues awake.

Second, the forty-second breath. The long, patient draw of the piston, during which the copper vessel beneath me tightens and groans; the shudder of the root-mat far below; the sudden, resonant chord of the hollow cane-stalks humming along the steam pipes like a pipe organ; and then the *clank*, the hiss, the warm rain of condensed steam, and the slow, heavy settling of everything back into place.

And third, the slower, irregular rhythms. The lazy *plip* of a water drop spilling over the lip of a parasol leaf ten feet above. The patient, muscular swell of the great dweller’s flank as it hauls itself up the neighboring vessel, its tea-colored hide brushing past my fluted edge—never tearing me, never even noticing me, because to its lateral seams I am just another knot of verdigris and fig-bark that has always been part of the vessel’s silhouette.

If I had a purpose here, it wouldn’t be anything grand. I wouldn’t be cleaning the piston rod or holding the pressurized flanges together by root-tension. Perhaps, at most, I would act as a tiny, living gasket of my own—cushioning the place where the swelling copper grinds against the woody strangler-fig, weeping a thin, acidic slime that keeps the verdigris from caking too hard, and humming back at the cane-stalks in a pitch too low and small for anything with ears to hear.

I think I would be a kind of *listener*—half-lichen, half-anemone, wholly sessile. A small, soft knot of attention wedged between the machine and the tree, counting out my life in forty-second turns, perfectly content to let the warm water rock me in the green dark until the flywheel finally stops.
