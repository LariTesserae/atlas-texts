I would not be the master mason who drew the lines of the chamber on parchment, nor the fisherman who sails past the quay without looking down. I think I would be the one left behind after the scaffolding came down—the apprentice, or the quay-keeper, whose job is mostly to wait for the tide to do its work.

You find me first on the flat limestone deck above, in the dry heat of midday, with a coil of tarred hemp over my shoulder and a bronze scraper tucked into my belt. From up here, the lagoon looks ordinary: bright shallows, the dark smudge of weed farther out, the long white edge of the quay humming under the sun. Only if you kneel by the square timber-framed hatch and press your palm flat to the stone do you feel what is happening underneath—a faint, regular shudder, like a heart beating through heavy wool. *Boom.* A pause. Then, so faint you might think you imagined it, the dull wooden *clack* of the flap-valve seating itself again.

That valve is mine, in a way. When the builders finished the chamber, the master mason had already packed his chisels for the next harbor, and it was my hands that lashed the smooth river cobble to the cedarwood flap with hemp cord. I remember how the wet hemp bit into my knuckles, and how proud I was of the neatness of the whipping—a small, tidy knot in a room made entirely of straight lines and sharp corners.

My task for the first season is simple: keep the bronze intake grate clear of drift-kelp, check that the hemp hasn’t frayed against the limestone, and watch the mortar. Hydraulic lime mixed with crushed brick needs time in the salt before it settles into itself; until then, it leaches a faint, silky slickness into the water, and the chamber stays too raw for whatever the builders mean to keep here later—eels, perhaps, or oysters, or nothing at all except clean, moving water.

So I drop through the hatch at noon, when the sun is directly overhead and the room below needs no lantern.

The drop is only a meter, straight through the warm skin of the lagoon and between the cedar beams of the roof. The moment my ears go under, the world changes from wind and gulls to thunder. The water is thick with heat, silky against my shins and forearms, and bright enough to make me squint even through the salt. Above me, the underside of the quay is a dark slab, but here in the box there is nowhere for the light to hide. Caustic gold nets whip across my bare shoulders and over the pale flagstones, moving faster than my eyes can follow.

I hook one elbow around a cedar beam to hold myself steady when the swell hits.

First comes the *boom*—not a sound you hear, really, but a sudden pressure against my ribs, as if the stone walls had inhaled. A heartbeat later, the bronze grate on the seaward wall roars, throwing a white cloud of bubbles across the upper half of the room. They hiss past my face, stinging my cheeks, blinding me for three seconds in silver glare. When they clear, the current is already driving across the floor, sweeping pale grit toward the channel, lifting the cedarwood flap-valve with that familiar *clack* that I know better than my own name.

On a single breath, I have maybe forty seconds before my chest begins to ask for air. That is long enough to kick down to the far bulkhead, brace my palm against the dusty-rose mortar—still crisp, still ridged from the toothed chisel—and test the cobble’s lashing with my thumb. The hemp is stiff with salt, holding fast.

It took me three weeks of noon dives to realize I was not the only one who liked the room.

At first I thought the algae behind the stepped ledges was just growing strangely—tufts of bright green hair-algae and ribbons of sea lettuce that stayed neat and stubby, never trailing raggedly into the main current. I assumed the scour of the tide was trimming them back. Then, one afternoon, I stayed down a few seconds past comfort, hanging quietly near the three terracotta mouths between surges while they hummed their hollow, jar-lip drone.

I felt it in my teeth before I saw it—a second vibration, lower and wetter than the pipes, answering them from somewhere just beside my elbow.

When the next swell boomed and the bubble-cloud burst from the grate, the silver beads caught along the floor channel and drew a shape I had looked straight through a dozen times: a long, heavy curve the exact chalk-and-rose color of the walls, pressed flat into the stone, its edges dissolving in the gold light.

If I were someone else in this world—a harbor official, or a merchant—I would probably try to haul it out with a gaff, or at least brag about it in the tavern at the landward end of the quay. But I spend too much time alone with wet stone and hemp to want to disturb a thing that fits a room so well.

Instead, I have learned to share the noon with it.

When I drop through the hatch now, I move more slowly. I let the first surge pass while I cling to the cedar grate, and as the bubbles thin, I look along the underside of the beams beside me. Often it is already there—draped heavy and quiet across the timber, its milky, eyeless head turned toward the intake, its six pairs of leather-soft claspers gripping the wood grain. I do not touch it; its skin looks too much like the new mortar, as if a hard poke might leave a dent. I simply hang a forearm’s length away, letting the warm current wash over both of us, listening with my ribs to the boom of the sea and the hum of the pipes, until my lungs tighten and I kick back up into the air.

Up on the deck, sitting with my legs dangling over the hatch while the sun dries the salt into white crusts on my shoulders, I coil my spare hemp and watch the lagoon. Soon enough, the season will turn. Barnacles will find the bronze grate; crabs will wedge into the mortar seams; the builders will come back with whatever they meant to put inside their stone box.

Until then, I am the keeper of a loud, bright, newborn room—and the quiet witness to the only thing in the sea that was made for it.
