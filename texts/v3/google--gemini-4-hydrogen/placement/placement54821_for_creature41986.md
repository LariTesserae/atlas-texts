I would find myself waking not to a change in light—there is no change in light—but to a slight slackening in the air across my ribs.

In the upper story of the workshop block, where the knocked-out windows frame squares of amber fog, the wall-mounted cooling fan still turns, still pushes the wet heat across the nylon mesh of my hammock. Nothing has stopped. Yet my eyes open because the vibration in the steel beam above my head has thinned by a fraction, a tiny flutter in the steady, iron drone.

I lie there for a moment, letting the grease on my shoulders cool as much as anything ever cools here. My skin is pale and slick, marked with the diamond-weave imprint of the braided cable-sheathing beneath me. Every few hours, or whenever the heaviness behind my eyes lifts, this is where I begin: suspended in the noisy wash of the fan, listening with my shoulder blades before I listen with my ears.

If I belong anywhere in this basin, I am one of the ones who reads the pipes.

Down on the wet aprons, the air is too thick and the motors too loud for distance to mean much. You can stand twenty paces from someone in the drifting steam and see only a pale blur behind a sodium halo. So you learn to live by what travels through the iron. When I swing out of the hammock and lace my conveyor-belt soles with copper wire—pulling the loops tight enough to bite, loose enough to let the ankle swell—the first thing I do is set my palm flat against the nearest vertical conduit.

There it is. The thump of the piston pumps in the lower station, three beats to a breath, and beneath it, a faint, irregular *hiss-tick* that doesn't belong. A gland-seal weeping somewhere along the condenser bank, or a float-valve chattering in one of the settling pans.

I don't hurry. Nobody hurries here; hurrying only makes you sweat into air that won't take it, leaving you dizzy and boiled in your own skin. I take my belt—oilcloth, stiffened with dried salt, hung with a short steel wrench, a coil of oiled hemp, and a flat chip of basalt for my heels—and step out onto the catwalk.

This is where I spend half my waking hours: not quite on the ground, not quite in the roof, moving along the elevated walkways and pipe-racks that stitch the three blocks together. From up here, the basin looks like a shallow pan of black glass cracked open to the heat. The basalt walls weep their dark, mineral streaks; the rectangular settling pans catch the amber glare and throw it back, broken by the slow ripples of crabs and toads. Down in the open yards, another pair of us is working the algae mats with fine-mesh sieves, their shaved heads gleaming like wet river stones as they lift the heavy, dark pulp and carry it toward the turbine casings to bake.

I tap the railing twice with my wrench—*tink-tink*—just to let them know someone is overhead, then move on toward the condenser bank.

In a place with no morning and no night, you measure yourself by circuits. Mine runs from the sump grates at the bottom of the drainage channels all the way up to the high ironwork of the condenser trusses. It is a vertical life. Down at the sump, ankle-deep in warm, brackish runoff, the air smells of wet rust and boiled salt. I wade in to clear the sedge-roots and dead midges from the iron teeth of the grate, my boots scraping the sharp basalt beneath. A skink darts off the concrete lip as my shadow crosses it; two small crabs scatter into the algae. You have to keep your hands moving and your eyes down, because if the sump clogs, the water backs up into the pump housings, and if the pumps flood, the basin loses its note.

By the time the grate is clear, the skin between my fingers is already turning white and soft, puckering like wet paper. That is the basin's quiet warning. You never ignore it.

I climb. Up the steel ladder, past the hissing pressure-relief valves, past the humming brick wall of the control block, up to the dry-perches tucked just beneath the condenser exhausts. Here, the radiant heat is fierce enough to scorch the dampness right out of the air. It feels like stepping out of warm water into an oven—harsh at first, then deeply relieving as the film of moisture vanishes from my chest and scalp.

Another of us is already sitting there, knees drawn up, scraping the softened skin from the arch of a foot with a basalt chip. We don't speak. I sit beside them, close enough that our shoulders nearly touch, and pull out my own stone. For a long while there is only the roar of the exhaust and the dry *shhh-shhh* of basalt against calloused skin. When we finish, they pass me a small tin of rendered pigeon-fat. I rub it into my knuckles, my ankles, the creases behind my knees, sealing myself back in before I have to go down again.

Before I leave the perch, I press two fingers against their collarbone and hum—low, from the bottom of the chest, a quick double-note that means *the line on the east side is chattering*. They nod once, eyes half-shut against the dry heat, and tap my shin with their wrench to say they heard.

From the dry-perches, it is an easy climb up into the rafters.

This is my favorite part of the circuit, though I wouldn't call it peace—nothing here is quiet enough for peace. Up in the high trusses, above the sodium lamps, the air is drier and the light is thin, murky grey-amber. The steel here has been worn smooth by generations of bare feet and bird claws. I unlace my heavy boots and hang them from my belt by their copper wires, then walk the narrow iron beams barefoot, toes curling naturally around the warm rivets.

The swifts and pigeons don't startle anymore when I pass. They know the weight of my tread on the truss. I check the dense rows of nests tucked into the gusset plates, taking two warm eggs from a clutch of four, leaving the rest. Sometimes I find a blown bulb in one of the upper caged lamps—the glass dark, no halo of midges orbiting it, the brick beside it empty of geckos. I unscrew the wire cage, swap in a fresh bulb from my pouch, and wait until the filament catches. Within a minute, the brine-flies find the new amber pool, and a pale gecko slips out from behind a conduit to take its place at the edge of the light.

It feels like a small, necessary courtesy. We keep the lamps lit for the midges, the midges feed the geckos and the swifts, the swifts keep us fed and greased, and all of us together keep the pumps from choking on sedge and scale.

On the way back down, I find the chattering valve behind the second condenser. The hemp packing has dried out and shrunk, letting a thin jet of steam spit against the brick. I shut the bypass, back off the nut with my wrench, and pack the gland tight with fresh oiled hemp until the hissing stops and the pipe settles back into its steady, heavy thrum.

Standing there with my hand still on the warm iron, I feel the whole basin through my palm. The pumps beating three to a breath. The cooling fans churning the fog. A distant *tink-tink-pause-tink* traveling up through the steel from the brine station—someone calling for a spare sieve, or just checking that the line is clear.

I lace my boots back on, check the grease on my knuckles, and start down the ladder toward the wet stone. There is nowhere else to go, and nothing else to be: just one more creature in the seams of the plant, tuned to its single note, keeping the water moving toward the sump.
