I would not be down on the scree, where the drum-bellies wedge their hooked foreclaws into the splits and shake the grit loose from the ledges. And I would not be out on the high, slick fronds where the tin-wings hang like torn curtains, waiting to hail down at the first hiss of steam.

I would be where the wood is thick enough to hold a pulse.

Four yards up the braided trunk of a black-barked tree, my back wedged into the fork where two heavy limbs twist together like muscle, one bare heel pressed flat against the damp bark. Below me, the pale, segmented things are already boiling out of the slate-cracks—I cannot see them, my pale-ringed eyes are half-shut against the drifting soot, but I can feel the dry, papery *tick-tick-tick* of their legs climbing the lower roots, testing to see if anything up here has gone still enough to eat.

Above me, three narrow hammocks of knotted fiber hang in the dark, wrapped in broad, waxy fronds to shed the slow, warm drip. My left hand rests lightly on the main cord that links them. Through that cord, and through the heel against the wood, the valley comes into me—not as a picture, never as a picture, but as weight and vibration.

If you found me here in the brief, greenish smear of a rotting stalk, you would see that I am not the one who throws the weighted lines into the screaming clouds. My left shoulder sits a little lower than my right, knotted with an old, pale ridge of scar where a slab of fresh slate caught me when I was too slow to read a drum-belly’s stutter. I can still climb, and I can still pry a lump of raw copper from a fault-seam with a bone wedge, but my throw pulls wide.

In a knot of four or five, a wide throw gets you nothing but a lost pebble and a hungry hinge-jaw turning its snout toward the splash of sound.

So I became the ear.

That is not a title anyone speaks—speaking is a foolishness here, a soft, wrong-shaped noise that catches the attention of things with heat-pits—it is just where my body settled in the dark. When the others sleep their two-hour bouts, wrapped tight in their waxy leaves, I sit upright with the tuber-oil drying slick on my ash-colored skin and listen to the *shape* of the roar.

It takes seasons to learn that the valley is not loud. *Loud* means there is quiet somewhere else to compare it to. Here, the sound is simply the water we swim in. You do not listen to the booming of the throat-sacs or the metallic hail of the tin-wings; you listen to the *holes* in them.

Right now, three ridges over, a drum-belly’s rhythm has gone flat—a half-tone lower than it was ten breaths ago. The bedrock beneath that slope is shifting, swelling with trapped steam. I file it away in my jawbone, the way I file everything that cannot be written down. *Do not cross the east scree.* Tomorrow, or the hour after, that whole face of jagged basalt will hiss down into the basin, and the pale scavengers will swarm the crushed burrows before the dust even settles.

Beside my hip hangs a notched seed-pod, dry and hollow, its inside scraped clean with an obsidian flake. I keep my thumb over its mouth so it cannot accidentally click against the bark. Further down my belt: a coil of braided plant-fiber, a bone gaff, and a single resin-daubed torch wrapped in oiled leaf so the dampness does not ruin it. I have lit a torch twice in my life. Both times, the sudden, spitting flare felt like striking myself across the face—a violent, blinding bloom that made the whole canopy shriek and scatter—and both times I dropped it into a mineral puddle before the echoes had even died, vanishing backward into the thick, merciful dark.

Light is for emergencies, and for the rare, crouching minutes beside an amber vapor-seam when someone’s shin has been opened by the basalt and needs to be oiled and bound, or when we need four fresh obsidian blanks knocked from a glassy vein.

Mostly, we live by the cold mud.

Before I climbed into this fork, I spent an hour down in the deepest hollow I know—a narrow, wet fold beneath an overhang where the geothermal vents do not reach and the water pools cold and gray. I scooped the mineral clay up in both hands and smeared it over my shoulders, my throat, the backs of my calves, working it into the creases behind my knees where the blind mites like to burrow. It smells of iron and dead stone. It makes me heavy and cold to the touch.

I am glad of it now, because something is moving through the middle tier.

I feel it in the bark before I hear it. A long, rhythmic *flex* of the trunk—not the slow, straining creak of a vine drinking sap, and not the sharp *tock* of splitting slate. Something with weight is bridging the gap from the neighboring tree. Six-foot strides. A hinge-jaw, mottled violet and soot-black, hunting the warm, dripping dark.

My fingers tighten—just a fraction, no sudden jerk—on the knotted cord of the hammocks.

Above me, the nearest frond-bundle shifts slightly. Not a gasp, not a rustle. Just a weight settling lower, pulling limbs inward. In the dark, one of my companions reaches down a bare foot until their toes brush the crown of my head, asking the question without a sound.

I reach up and press two fingers hard into the hollow of their ankle, holding them there. *Still. Wait.*

Ten feet away, the hinge-jaw pauses. I cannot see its eyeless head swinging like a pendulum, but I can hear the wet, clicking *snick* of its mandibles and feel the sudden, displaced warmth of its breath as it sweeps the air with the deep pits along its snout. To those pits, the tree is a tangle of lukewarm bark and cooling pulp. The three sleepers above me are wrapped in thick, waxy fronds that hold back their heat; and I am smeared in cold hollow-clay, sitting as still as a basalt outcrop.

A tin-wing, startled by a stray plume of vapor higher up, loses its grip and tumbles past us, snapping wildly in the dark. The hinge-jaw’s head whips toward the falling click—a blur of displaced air, a wet snap of jaws—and then it is gone, bounding away through the braided trunks toward the richer, warmer swarm.

I let out a breath, slow and silent through my nose, and tap two knuckles lightly against the sleeper’s heel. *Clear.*

They pull their foot back up into the leaves. Sleep resumes.

This is what I am here for: to be the cold stone at the center of the knot. To remember which overhang still holds a cache of raw copper and spare braided line, plugged with a wedged rock against the scavengers. To know, by the smell of sulfur and sweet rot rising through the wet heat, when a giant vine-cluster is about to hit its hour-long bloom three ridges away.

When that scent rolls in—heavy, musky, cutting right through the mineral damp—I will not stay in the fork. I will wake the others with a firm, flat palm against their ribs, and we will drop down the trunk like water finding a crack. We will move with our knees bent and our heels low, tapping our notched rattles in a dry *skritch-tick-tick* that weaves right into the scraping of a million chitinous legs.

For ten minutes, beneath the pale, foul-smelling burst of flowers that will be wet slime by tomorrow, there will be no hinge-jaws and no falling slate. There will only be the crack of mineral nuts against our shin-guards, the warm, sweet sap running down our chins, and the sudden, fierce closeness of twenty ash-skinned bodies converging out of the murk—sharing the harvest before the petals blacken and the valley swallows it all back down.

Until then, I sit with my heel on the bark, my fingers on the cord, and my eyes half-shut. Listening to the surf roar over us, and waiting for the sound to change its shape.
