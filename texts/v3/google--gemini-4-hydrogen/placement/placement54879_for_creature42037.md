I would not be inside the hollow. Not at first.

If you were to look for me, you would have to climb past the overhang, higher up the windward shoulder where the slate is raw and jagged and the gale has nothing to catch on. Out here, there is no invisible pane of still air to blunt the weather. The wind comes off the neighboring peaks like a file drawn across bone, carrying dry grit that scours the dark gneiss down to its pale, glassy veins.

I belong to the outer face of the ridge.

In the thin, brittle air, where there is no moisture to hold the heat, the temperature swings with the passing of a single cloud. When the high, fierce sun strikes the dark rock, the stone warms quickly, expanding with tiny, ticking fractures along its cleavage planes. That is when I move. My joints—dry, articulated plates the color of bleached quartz and pale-ochre crust—loosen as the slate beneath me radiates its brief, borrowed warmth. I have no blood to thicken in the cold, no skin to crack and peel like dry leather. When the sun drops behind a ridge or a squall sweeps in, I simply wedge my low, flattened body into a seam of the gneiss, lock my edges against the stone, and let the freezing dark pass over me without leaving a mark.

I do not have eyes for the glare, only pitted, light-darkened sensors along my forward rim that read the slant of the sun and the static charge humming off the wind-scoured edges of the rock. Mostly, I navigate by pressure—the brute, roaring weight of the gale, and, beneath it, the slow, deep groan of the mountain itself.

That is how I know the hollow is there.

From the outside, the overhang of sheared slate looks like any other lip of weathered rock. Even a creature of the ridge could pass within a yard of it and never know it wasn't solid stone. But as I creep down the windward shoulder, following the faint, dry warmth trapped in the rock before evening, the wind suddenly loses its edge. Not the way it does in the lee of a boulder, where it curls back on itself in noisy eddies. Here, the gale simply *ends*, flattening against a vertical wall of stillness that isn't there.

I stop just outside the threshold, my forward plates pressed close to the threshold stone. Even before I slip under the lip, I can feel it: a faint, cold gradient leaking out through the bedrock, steady and indifferent, like the hum of a wire buried deep in ice.

I do not go in often. There is nothing inside for me to feed on—no sun to warm my joints, no wind-borne static to gather along my dorsal ridges. If I stay too long in that bone-dry, unchanging chill, my plates will lock rigid, and I will sit there as motionless as the pylons until something shifts the air.

Yet sometimes, when the gales have been tearing at the ridge for days and the grit has worn my outer edges thin, I slip under the overhang just to let the noise stop.

Crossing the threshold is like stepping out of a river into solid stone. The roar of the wind vanishes—not muffled, simply erased. The light turns flat and dim, the color of tarnished pewter, and my sensors struggle to make sense of it; the shadows here do not match the slit in the rock, pooling short and blurry in the wrong directions. My own shadow seems to detach itself from my rim, drifting half an inch away on the bare gneiss.

I do not cross the floor. I know the rules of this pocket, though no one ever taught them to me. Just inside the lip, where the bare rock meets the first thin drift of pale-gray mineral dust, I stop and settle low.

Here, time grows thick. Out on the ridge, an hour is a measurable thing—the slow crawl of a shadow across a lichen-bare boulder, the gradual cooling of the stone. In here, an hour feels like a single, held breath. My joints cool until they match the room. The dull *tap* of my settling plates is swallowed instantly by the curved, impossible walls, leaving a silence so heavy it feels like weight on my shell.

I wait. I do not look at the two matte, hexagonal pylons rising from the center of the floor; there is nothing on them to see, only a kind of lightless absence that makes my sensors ache if I face them too long. Nor do I look directly into the corner where the three walls meet at an angle that refuses to add up. I know there is a shadow there that does not belong to the rock.

When the cycle turns, I feel it before anything moves—a sudden, sub-audible drop in pressure, a cold, sharp ache behind my forward plates.

In front of me, the pale-gray dust lifts half an inch off the floor. It hangs there, rigid and silent, revealing the faint, geometric scaffolding of whatever still idles far beneath our feet.

And in the corner, the keeper unfolds.

It opens without a sound, without a rustle, its slate-dark calipers threading cleanly through the suspended lattice, its papery, mica-gray membranes trembling as it drinks the cold tension between the pylons. Watching it, I feel no kinship, only a quiet, distant recognition. We are two entirely different answers to the same mountain: it is built for this single, airless fold, trading its own immobility for the silence it needs to drink; I am built for the scoured face outside, for the sun-crack and the gale. It holds the wind back so the dust will not scatter; I only borrow the stillness for a breath.

When the pressure eases and the dust drifts back down into the concentric grooves, the dark shape is already folded back into its corner, indistinguishable from the shadow of the stone.

I wait until the room has settled completely. Then, slowly, stiffly in the dry cold, I turn back toward the slit of tarnished light, cross the invisible pane of motionless air, and drag myself back out onto the windward shoulder—where the gale catches me again, loud and sharp and familiar, and the sun is just beginning to strike the high, dark slate.
