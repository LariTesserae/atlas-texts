I would not be on the open shelf when you found me. I would be inside the middle dome of a three-dome cluster, three feet of mud-stuffed slate between my ribs and the screaming sea, sitting with my back against a cold leviathan’s rib and my heels pressed flat to the stone floor.

That is where I live most of my life: in the long Exhale, when the tide is high and the blowholes are roaring, and the whole world outside is reduced to a steady, chest-deep thrum.

If you looked at me by the orange eye of the ash-pit, you would see nothing remarkable—another low-built, heavy-shouldered body wrapped in oiled Silt-Stalker hide, pale-irised eyes sunken beneath soot-smeared lids, tallow and ash-grease worked deep into the creases of my knuckles. My ear-rims are notched and thickened from an old frost-burn, and I have a habit of tilting my chin down so my jawbone catches the vibration of the floor. 

I am not the watcher with the bog-iron knife. My shoulders are too narrow for the long pole, and my temper is too slow for the split-second bark of a Silt-Stalker. Instead, I am the one who tends the *between*—the narrow, livable band of sound and touch that keeps a dome from going hollow.

***

When the Inhale comes—that brief, twenty-minute slack when the sea pulls back and the pressure in our sinuses eases—I am usually the third out through the slit, after the watcher and the stronger hauler. 

I crawl out onto the wet basalt with my skullcap laced tight, the wax plugs seated deep, the cupped felt pads strapped over my ears. To anyone else, stepping out into the pearl-gray mist might feel like stepping into a wall of static, even at low tide; the Pan-Clappers never stop their hail-on-tin rattle, and the Throat-Worms still drone in the mud. But through my thin-soled boots, the world is clear and legible. I can feel the difference between the hollow *tick* of a clapper-basin half a ridge away and the deeper, slower *lug* of the sea waiting down in the honeycombed dark.

My work in the twenty minutes is quiet work, even by our standards. I carry a bone hook and a flat flint scraper, and I go to the rims of the basins while the haulers empty the weirs. I peel back the rubbery black-brown microbial felt in long, wet strips—not too thin, or it tears and won't dry for the ash-pit; not too thick, or you strip the stone bare and starve the next tide's crust. When a weir's sinew-lashing has frayed against the slate, I am the one who kneels in the ankle-deep runnel to splice it, working by feel with fat-whitened fingers while the mist curls around my knees.

Every so often, a hauler will step close and tap a quick rhythm against my collarbone—*two short, one long*—telling me the baskets are full, or that a Stalker’s silhouette has been spotted drifting through the fog on stilt-legs. I don't look up. Looking up wastes breath and shows your face to the mist. I just tap back once on their forearm, gather my wet rolls of felt into my sling, and keep my heels planted so I will feel the pre-tremor the instant the sea turns.

***

It is inside, during the long dark of the Exhale, that I become most myself.

When the slate-weighted flap is wedged shut with the leviathan rib and we pull the tallow plugs from our ears, the dome fills with our real voices—low, humming murmurs and soft throat-clicks, pitched carefully in the quiet pocket between the bass groan of the bedrock and the wet hiss of the burning fat.

I sit nearest the elder’s bundle of sinew cords. Not because I am old—my thumbs are still supple, only just beginning to callous along the inner edge—but because I have the patience for the knots.

In a world with no wood to carve and no stillness for long songs, memory has to be tied down so the tide doesn't wash it away. Each cord in the bundle is a ledger of grease and sinew: a tight, double-looped knot for a winter storm that threw a leviathan onto the shelf; a loose, three-fingered hitch for a lean season when the blowholes ran cold and the clappers thinned; a tiny, tight-pulled bead of sinew for every child born in the dark of the cell.

While the others scrape hides with flint or crack pale shells against flat stones, I mend the cords that have grown brittle with salt, and I learn the old ones by running my thumbs over them with my eyes shut. Sometimes, when the sea outside hits a particularly violent surge and the mud-stuffed walls shudder hard enough to shake soot from the ceiling, one of the younger ones will crawl over and press their forehead against mine. We don't use words. I just hum—a steady, low note right in the middle of my chest—until their skull stops trembling with the rock and starts trembling with me instead.

***

If you asked me what I would be in this world, I wouldn't say a hero, or a hunter, or even a survivor—because *surviving* sounds like fighting the place, and nobody fights the Worn Shelf and lasts. 

I would be a **mender of the slack**: the one who splices the weirs so the baskets hold, who peels the felt so the ember stays red, who ties the knots so the names of the dead don't dissolve into the mist. Someone low to the stone, listening through the soles of my feet, waiting patiently in the warm, greasy dark for the next twenty-minute breath.
