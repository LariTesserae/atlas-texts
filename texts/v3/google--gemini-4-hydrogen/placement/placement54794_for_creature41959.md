I would not be passing through in boots, squinting into the pewter glare. If I belonged to this plateau, I would not know what pewter looks like.

I would know the world by weight, by dampness, and by the sixty-cycle itch along the grooves of my crest.

Start from the sound, because the sound is where I would wake: a low, steady pressure against the bone, rising through the soles of four wide pads pressed flat to slick basalt. Not a roar—the roar belongs to the shafts below—but the patient, subterranean *lub-dub* of the deep-rock pumps, arriving through fifty fathoms of wet stone like a heartbeat felt through someone else’s ribs. Above it, thinner and sharper, hangs the hum of the copper lines. Between those two frequencies—the deep tremor and the overhead drone—the whole plateau sits suspended.

I find myself at a threshold. Not out in the middle of the grand, doorway-less avenues where the lamps burn for nothing, and not racked thirty-deep in the rafters of a hollow foundry, dripping into the steam. I am at the base of one of the windowless substations that stand alone at the end of a half-mile march of pylons.

It is a good place for something like me: a boundary between two kinds of silence.

Behind me, sealed behind heavy brass dogs, the narrow hatch drops away into the deep-rock dark, where the air is thick enough to chew and loud enough to bruise. Down there, the young ones cling to the hot pipes, pale and soft-hided, blind to everything except the surge of current through iron. Ahead of me lies half a mile of empty, sweating flagstone, across which no other tender will walk within twenty paces of me, lest our fields blur together into static.

So I stay here, half-racked against the outer ironwork of the substation—hind limbs hooked over a waist-high rail, keeled chest resting on the cool grating, crimson gill-flanks hanging open to the warm draft leaking around the brass seal.

I am one of the tenders, though not one of the long-limbed walkers who patrol the avenues with ceramic-tipped poles, snapping salt stalactites before they arc. My work is smaller and more stationary. I am a listener at the hatch.

Because I have no eyes, the city is not empty frames and hollow masonry to me; it is a vast, slow-breathing circuit of resistance and flow. When a rooftop cistern three blocks away fills past its rim and begins to spill its lukewarm, rust-colored curtain, I do not see the shimmer—I feel the sudden shift in weight across the iron legs, the tiny drop in resonance along the masonry, the fresh pulse of lipid-slick mist thickening the air around my dorsal spiracles. When a mat of biofilm grows too ambitious in a sluice gate, choking the crawl of a beltless paddle wheel, the sixty-cycle hum in the overhead cables develops a faint, ragged flutter—a hesitation in the current that makes the pale filaments along my anvil crest shiver out of time.

That is what I am here for: to notice the flutter before the arc, the lag before the valve seizes.

Every so often, a walker comes across the wet flagstones from the direction of the foundries. I feel them long before they reach twenty paces—not by footsteps, which are soft as wet horn on stone, but by the way the ambient hum of the plateau bends around them, like water parting around a piling. They stop at the edge of my reach, plant their cleft pads, and lower their bone crests toward the glistening pavement.

We do not hurry. The air is too thin for haste, and the heat is too heavy.

Across the twenty paces of damp stone, I let my own crest dip. I modulate the small current gathering in my salt-scarred hide, sending a low, sub-audible thrum through the basalt—*tiss, tiss, tiss*—a dry little spark-language across the wet mineral film. I tell them what the deep-rock pumps are saying beneath the hatch: that the third shaft is running hot, that the young ones down on the lower pipes are crowding the intake, that the pressure in the western sluice is climbing by a fraction of a beat.

The walker answers in a slower, deeper pulse, carrying the taste of the upper rafters—news of a salt stalactite growing fast near the third pylon, or a bladder-winged glider that nested too close to a transformer housing and had to be nudged back into the thermals.

Then they uncoil, lift their ceramic pole, and move on across the empty plain, leaving the hum clean again.

When they are gone, I turn my attention back to the brass dogs of the hatch. With my two smaller, finer chest-arms—the ones with the blunt, ceramic-hard nails—I reach down to scrape a fresh ridge of sulfur-yellow crust from the seam of the door, clearing the joint so it won’t bind. The mat recoils sluggishly from my touch, rippling like a sea anemone before oozing back over the warm iron.

I settle my belly back onto the grating. Above me, a fat drop of condensed water rolls down a copper cable, strikes the hot housing of the substation, and vanishes with a sharp *tiss*.

In the dimness I cannot see, beneath a sky I have never looked at, I keep my filaments tuned to the gap between the deep pumps and the high wires—holding the balance, one slow breath at a time.
