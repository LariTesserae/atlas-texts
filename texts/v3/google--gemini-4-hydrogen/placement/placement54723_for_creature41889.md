I think I would find myself halfway between the water and the air, in the narrow, vertical dark of a mudflat shaft.

At first, there is only the listening. Down here, two hands’ breadth below the glare, the clay is cool and slick against my shell, holding the memory of last month’s water. I don’t see the plain, but I feel it. The whole basin is a single stretched skin of mud and lime, and everything that moves on it sends a message down into the dark. The frogs’ clicking arrives not as a sound but as a faint, persistent shiver in the walls of my tunnel—a dry, metallic tremor that starts somewhere to the east and washes over me in a wave. Then, deeper, slower, comes the *shhh-plop* of a grazer working the silt a quarter-mile off, and sometimes the low, heavy rumble of a bull’s bellow, which shakes a grain or two of dried salt loose from the rim above and drops it onto my carapace.

I would be one of the chimney-builders. Ash-colored, patient, built for the seam where the basin breathes in and out.

It suits me, I think, to live in a place where the mountains have all dissolved into coin-sized pebbles, and to spend my life making the only small, stubborn hills left on the map. My left claw is broad and flat as a mason’s trowel; my right is narrow, serrated, quick. All morning, while the sun turns the limestone shelves white and the air above the pans blurs into silver, I sit at the bottom of my shaft with my gills wet in the seepage, waiting out the light.

When the glare softens—you can tell by the way the heat stops pressing down the throat of the tube, and by the sudden thickening of the midges’ hum—I climb.

The rim of my chimney is a hand high above the flat, crusty mud. From up here, the world is almost offensively wide. Nothing interrupts it except the distant, bone-pale stilts of the wading birds, stepping slowly through the reflection of the sky, and the dark, crooked smudge of a lone tree on a shell-mound miles away. The wind comes straight across the water with nothing to slow it down, carrying the smell of warm clay, salt, and the faint, sweet rot of the bacterial mats.

I hook my narrow claw over the damp lip of the tube and go still.

To be this small on a plain this vast is to become mostly attention. A frog, mottled the exact gray-yellow of the lime, sits on a quartz pebble three body-lengths away, its copper throat pulsing. Out in the pan, a skimmer-bug skates across the tension, leaving tiny dimples that catch the late light. I don’t chase any of it. There is nowhere to chase anything to; the plain has no corners to back prey into. Instead, I let the steady wind bring the midges to me, or wait for a careless hopper to mistake my dark mud turret for a dry clod of sedge-root.

And when the water begins its slow, weeks-long rise—not a tide, just a quiet swelling of the warm seepage from beneath the porous stone—I don’t leave. I go down into the slick dark, scoop a pellet of clay with my broad claw, bring it up, and press it onto the rim. Another pellet. Another.

Around me, across the flat, glistening miles, thousands of others are doing the same thing, raising our tiny, hollow smokestacks an inch ahead of the creeping sheet of water. By the time the lagoons join and the whole plain turns into a single mirror, we are still there, each of us perched at the top of a dark island no wider than a thumb, watching the sky turn the color of tea, listening to the frogs take up their chorus in the reeds, holding our small, wet ground.
