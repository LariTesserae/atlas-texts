I would not arrive here, because nothing arrives here. I would simply be already in place, folded into the narrow gap between two oil-cooled housings, my knees drawn tight against my ribs and my three-jointed arms tucked flush along my flanks so that I take up no more space than a ceramic insulator.

In that posture, there is very little difference between me and the housing beside me. My metabolic draw is low enough that the mist condenses on my shoulders just as it does on the cast iron, beading along my collarbone and slipping down the pale, damp skin of my back in slow, warm tracks. I do not sleep. I simply idle, letting the sixty-cycle hum pass through the soles of my feet and out through the crown of my elongated head without catching on anything.

When I shift back into service, it is not because a morning has come. There is no morning—only the white, diffused pressure of the vapor above, holding the heat steady in the mid-thirties. Instead, it is a tiny dissonance that lifts me out of the shadow: a slight roughness in the chord vibrating through the perforated steel of the catwalk. Three bays down, a rectifier station has begun to run a fraction of a degree too warm, its hum thickening by half a beat.

I unfold myself—not quickly, because nothing here has the energy or the reason for haste—and step out onto the catwalk. 

I have no eyes to take in the ruler-straight horizon, no ears to catch the hush of the condensate falling into the pans. My world is a map of pressures, temperatures, and frequencies. The broad, recessed sensory plates along the sides of my head angle slightly toward the offending rectifier, reading the shape of its heat the way another creature might read a silhouette against the sky. Beneath my translucent skin, the pale blue lattice of my veins carries my own copper-weighted blood slowly toward my extremities, keeping me a few degrees cooler than the air. That coolness is my whole purpose. It is what makes me useful.

As I walk, the steel grid beneath my feet trembles with the steady, reassuring load of the plain. Below me, in the humid shadow of the catwalk, the papery flyers hang in thick, shivering shingles from the insulated cables. I feel their collective vibration—a faint, dry rustle in the electromagnetic field—and I know without looking that the bundle near the third support stanchion is growing too dense, trapping heat against the sheathing. Before I even reach the rectifier, a dozen of the outermost flyers unclasp their hooks and drift down in total silence, settling onto the surface of the tea-colored water two inches above the iron floor. Down there, something slow and ribbon-like will find them along the expansion joint.

I pause at the rectifier housing. A mantle-slug has grown too thick along the eastern fins—a broad, six-foot slab of wet amber felt that has forgotten its own thinness, muffling the metal’s breath. 

I step off the catwalk onto the housing’s beveled lip. My spatulate fingers find the edge of the slug and peel it back cleanly, without malice or resistance. The creature is heavy and slick with its own hydrophobic oil; it comes away from the iron with a soft, wet sigh, revealing the dark, unrusted metal beneath, still warm and clean from its rasping. I let the excess strip drop over the edge into the settling pan below. Somewhere to my left, the faint, dry *tick* of cartilage pads against submerged iron tells me a herd of walkers is already moving through the shallows, their needle proboscises dipping into the flush grates to thin the pulsing, slate-blue colonies before they can choke the flow.

With the slug’s thick edge removed, I press my palms flat against the bared radiator fins, then lean my narrow chest and belly against the housing. 

This is the part I am made for. The heat of the iron floods into my cooler skin, rising through my ribs, warming the slow blue current in my veins. Beneath my cheek, the sixty-cycle hum is thick and uneven, a knot in a line that wants to be straight. I stay there, motionless, letting my body act as a sink, drawing the excess warmth away from the oil-cooled core and shedding it slowly into the saturated air as a fine, steady sweat of condensation.

Another tender joins me after an hour, or perhaps two—time here does not divide itself cleanly. I feel its approach through the vibration of the housing before its pale, damp shoulder touches mine. It says nothing, having no mouth for speech, and I offer no greeting. It simply angles its sensory plates toward the fins, finds the remaining pocket of heat on the housing’s far side, and presses its long torso against the metal beside me.

We idle there together for a long stretch of the unchanging noon, two pale, unglazed instruments leaning against the dark iron, listening with our ribs as the pitch gradually flattens out, settles, and locks back into the endless, single chord of the plain. When the last *tink* of metal settling into equilibrium passes through my palms, I step back onto the catwalk, fold my arms flush against my ribs, and move on down the line—just another living gasket, keeping the sharp edges clean.
