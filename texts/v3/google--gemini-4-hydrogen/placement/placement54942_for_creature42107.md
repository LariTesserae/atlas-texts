I think I would find myself first as a tightness in the reeds—a dry, rhythmic flexing in one of the lower niches beneath the west wall, where the mid-morning warmth settles heaviest and the serrated leaves interlock like the pages of a thick book.

For a long time, it wouldn’t be clear to me whether I was a basket or the thing folded inside it. There would only be the sensation of woven grass pressing against bone, the faint, chalky *tick* of a limestone block settling beside my shoulder, and the patient weight of three stacked clay bowls resting on what might be a lid or might be my knees. A pale, almond-sized beetle would be grazing along the rim of my collarbone, its tiny mandibles making a sand-on-ceramic rasp against the horn. I wouldn’t brush it away. There would be no urgency to decide what I was, because nothing in the room hurries to finish its own sentence.

Then the heat would peak, and a knot at my wrist would quietly un-tie itself.

Not a waking up—there is no sleep here, no wet heaviness to shake off—just a slow redistribution of parts. The reed-bundle beneath my ribs would lengthen into a forearm bound with tight-knotted cordage. The grass-cloth of the basket’s lining would part into stiff, unbleached sleeves. When I finally leaned forward out of the niche, my joints would give a dry, layered rustle like parchment turning in a loft, and a small puff of bone-ash would rise from the creases of my tunic and hang in the ordinary light.

I would be one of the builders, though perhaps one who has spent too long among the shelves—a little slower to separate from the furniture, with a rim of woven grass still clinging to the hem of my coat like an unfinished thought. My skin would have the matte, porous stillness of unglazed clay, dusted white at the elbows where I’d leaned against the chiseled plinths. No breath would move in my chest; instead, the hollowed bone beneath my horn-plates would hold a quiet, packed dryness, warm all the way through.

Where would I go, once I stood?

Not far. The whole place can be taken in at a glance, and yet I would spend hours crossing it. I’d find myself drawn to the seams—the places where two walls meet and the dry vegetation grows thickest, or the narrow, inkless gaps between the dry-stone plinths where the room’s bookkeeping has gone soft.

If I knelt on the east walkway to press a crawler’s shed husk into a mortar-chink, I might look up and see myself already sitting on the opposite ledge, scraping a fingernail along the rim of a clay bowl with a soft, chalky *shhhk*. Neither of us would be surprised. I would know the exact weight of the adze-handle in that other hand, the exact notch in the copper rivet at that other elbow. When I finished with the mortar and stood, the figure on the ledge might simply cease to be there, leaving behind only a faint crescent of ash on the limestone—or perhaps I would be the one who vanished, and the one on the ledge would walk away with my chisel already warm in their palm.

My work, I think, would be with the bowls and the shadows.

I would tend the twins—the identical vessels thrown by the same hand, chipped on the same rim, sitting on opposite sides of the square. I’d drop a dry spore-pod or a loose copper rivet into one on the west terrace just to hear it rattle softly at the bottom of the other, forty paces—or twenty-seven paces—away. When a hanging reed screen stilled and left its shadow behind on the chalky wall like a smudge of dry soot, I wouldn’t try to wipe it off. I’d sit beneath it with a handful of silica-fiber and watch it drift an inch to the left every ten minutes, waiting for it to detach and thin into the air.

And when the warmth shifted back toward the east, and the ash-gray moss along the rafters puffed out its dry, golden-gray dust, I would tilt my narrow head—listening through the small vents along my brow, letting the faceted pits in my cheek catch the clean, unremarkable brightness—and drink the spores in through the woven seams of my collar.

I wouldn’t remember having arrived here, any more than the chisel-marks on the lintels remember the hammer. I would simply be part of the room’s quiet inventory: tall, angular, dry as a granary, moving through the broken distances with the unhurried certainty that if I dropped something into a crack between the tiles, I could always reach up to the ceiling-slates three feet away and pull it cleanly back out.

And when the mid-morning heat grew heavy again against the west wall, I would find my way back to the corner where the serrated leaves meet, fold my long knees against my ribs, and let the cordage at my wrists blur back into the basketwork—sitting quietly among the stacked sherds until it was time to un-weave again.
