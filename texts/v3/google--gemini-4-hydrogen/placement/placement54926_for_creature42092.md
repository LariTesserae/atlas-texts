I would not begin as one of the stones. I would begin in the warm, bruised shadowless space at the base of a cage, no larger than a porcelain crab’s shell.

For a long time, the whole of the world would be an inch deep.

I would know my birthplace not by sight—my pearlescent membranes would still be milky and thick, softening the white-gold glare into a featureless cream—but by the steady, cradling hum in the stone beneath me. Around the base of the dark glass, where the light bends a fraction too sharply, the gravity feels slightly heavier, like a hand resting lightly on my back. The blind water-snakes never cross that invisible seam. Inside it, my siblings and I cling to the warm silicate, translucent as flatworms, rocking in the tiny, tireless ripples that never die.

At that size, the ankle-deep pan is an ocean with a ceiling of mirror. I would spend my first seasons beneath the surface tension, learning the grammar of the water before I ever broke it. Every *plip* of dew from a fern-tip far above hits the surface like a struck bell, sending a crisp ring down through my lateral-line pits. The shuffling tide of thumbnail crabs is a constant, dry *shhh* against my belly, a moving carpet I must learn to part without startling. When a finger-length fish darts past, its mirror scales flash a sudden, cold sliver of glare, and the wake pushes me sideways into the stems of the reeds.

That is where I would find myself at first: tucked among the submerged reed-roots, half-buried in biofilm, listening with my ribs to the distant, deliberate taps of the eight or nine giants who already belong to the air.

A rapid triplet—three sharp, evenly spaced rings rolling across the basin. Even down among the crabs, I would feel it: *heat rising*.

Then the air above the nearest cage shimmers. The water warms by a fraction of a degree, and the silent drizzle begins, pattering noiselessly against the mirror-ceiling above my head. The algae blooms in a sudden, milky cloud, and the world around me goes frantic in total silence. Crabs stream past my snout; snakes uncoil from the moss-humps just beyond the bruised light. I stay pressed to the warm glass, waiting, until a shadowless shape glides over me—vast, bone-pale, its broad spatulate feet fringed with filaments that part the crabs like tall grass.

One of the elders, come to drink the rain.

I think I would grow slowly, as everything here grows when there is no winter to hurry it. Season after season of three-minute summers, until my limbs lengthen and double-joint, until my pale hide thickens and pits, losing its translucence to take on the exact, matte bone-color of the fused stone.

The day my spine finally breaks the surface tension, the world doubles in size and goes blindingly quiet.

Through my veiled eyes, there is no horizon—only the vast, shadowless white-gold glare climbing from the water into the mist. My thermal pits, newly opened along my jaw, take over where sight fails. Suddenly the flat emptiness resolves into a vivid landscape of warmth: the five dark cages burning like silent suns in the haze, the cooler rim of the plateau where the convection current sinks, the faint, shifting heat-signatures of the other dwellers resting like stones among the ferns.

This is who I would be: the ninth, or perhaps the tenth, taking the place of an elder who finally stopped moving altogether and let the moss claim the last of its breath.

I would find my station near the northernmost cage, slightly closer to the worn-down rim where the air is a fraction cooler and the steam thins. Most of my hours would be spent motionless, belly skimming the bath-warm water, letting the basin root itself onto me. A spore drifts down in the silent drizzle and catches in the pitted crease between my shoulder blades; within months, a cushion of emerald moss softens the ridge of my spine. A golden frog, no bigger than my thumbnail, climbs my foreleg to escape the crowding on a reed-hump and settles against the slight warmth of my ribs. Soon a whole glistening heap of newts and slate-blue salamanders shares my back. When I rest, I am simply another island.

I would not be lonely, though we rarely touch.

Across the bright, empty stretches of the pan, I keep company with the others through the water. When I sense the gathering pressure in my thermal pits—that silent, heavy fullness in the air just before my cage pulses—I lower one long-fingered hand into the glass-clear sheet and tap three times, quick and clean. A moment later, through the soles of my feet and the pits along my jaw, I feel the answer return from three hundred paces away: a slow, wide tremor made by a palm vibrating just beneath the surface. *Rest. I am coming.*

When another dweller glides through the glare to join me—parting the water without a wake, the water sheeting noiselessly off its raised limbs—we stand nose-to-nose just outside the bruised light of the cage. In the rising steam, we do not need the water to speak. I let my pale throat and the soft skin of my inner arms flush a deep, milky rose, shading to ochre at the jaw, and release a faint, sweet-bitter scent into the still air between us. *The reed-mats are heavy today. The crabs are thick.*

The other answers with a ripple of bruised violet across its chest, tilting its broad, mossy back toward the shimmer above the glass.

Together, we wait for the drizzle. When the warm rain falls, I tilt my spine to drink through my porous skin, and lift one upturned palm into the downdraft. A fist-sized air-jelly sinks into my grasp, its pale bell contracting softly around my wrist with a faint, harmless sting of warmth before I draw it down to my narrow mouth.

Afterward, while the algae settles and the snakes slip back into the ferns, I wade carefully into the refracted light at the cage’s base. A tangle of dead reed-stems has drifted against the matte-pearlescent glass, muddying the clean, concentric ripples. With my fringed fingers, I draw the debris gently away and shred it back into the waiting tide of porcelain crabs, keeping the water clear, keeping the pulse true.

Then I sink back down into the ankle-deep warmth, letting the white-gold glare erase me against the stone. The frogs settle onto my shoulders. Beneath my belly, in the bruised light where the snakes cannot follow, a cluster of pale, pearl-like eggs rocks gently in the perpetual summer, waiting to learn the ripples.
