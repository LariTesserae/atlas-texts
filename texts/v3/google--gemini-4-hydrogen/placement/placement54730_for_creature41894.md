I would find myself on the western rim of a narrow cleft, three terraces down from the highest towers, where the shadow of a dry-stacked wall cuts a wide stone cistern cleanly in half.

On one side of the line, the water is bright with the unblinking late-morning sky. On the other, it holds the dark, crisp reflection of cantilevered timber eaves and the hanging curtains of silver-edged vine. Between them, the water does not stir. It is a mirror that has forgotten how to ripple, except once, at long and even intervals, when a single fat drop gathers at the lip of the overhead channel and falls—*plink*—into the exact center of the shade.

That is where I am sitting, cross-legged on a pad of emerald moss that never grows taller than the breadth of a thumb.

If I look down at my hands, they are the color of slate just after the dew has settled: pale, matte grey, cool enough that the air around them makes no claim on their warmth. My fingers are long, stained faintly at the tips with the dark green of bruised moss and the dull, metallic rub of copper. They are not idle, though they are not hurrying either. Between my palms, three strands of damp plant fiber are turning slowly over one another—a braid that has no clear beginning and no intended end, moving at the pace of a fern frond uncurling from a stone joint.

I am one of the caretakers. Not a ruler of the basin, and not a visitor from the lower valleys who would shiver here and stain the stillness with the white plume of warm breath. I belong to the equilibrium. My skin is cool as the rim of the cistern; my lungs draw the thin, windless air without sound or haste. Beneath my heavy tunic—woven from the same tough, matte reeds that drape the balconies, weighted at the hem with pierced discs of slate and beads of dull bronze—there is no fever, no pulse that races to outrun a coming dusk.

There is no dusk here. The shadow of the wall across the cistern has not moved by the width of a hair.

Tucked into the braided belt at my waist are a stone hammer and a copper chisel. The copper is clean, unclouded by green patina; the wooden handle of the hammer shows the pale, fresh grain of rough-hewn timber. Beside me, stacked neatly on the raw bedrock where the terrace ends and the unworked cleft begins, sit seven slabs of cleaved slate, waiting to become the next course of a retaining wall. They have been waiting there for as long as the shadow has bisected the water. There is no dust on their faces, no lichen creeping over their sharp, bruised edges.

I do not lift the hammer. To strike stone against stone here would be like tearing a sheet of still water; the basin remembers silence too well to forgive it. Instead, I carry the tools as the others do—as a quiet acknowledgment that the city is not finished, only paused.

A few paces away, along the shallow channel chiseled straight into the bedrock, the condensation creeps in a thin, bright film. A velvet-shelled beetle has wandered across the lip and stalled in the currentless damp, blocking a bead of water that has begun to swell behind its carapace. I set the braided fiber across my knees, draw a splinter of scraped bone from the bone needle pinning my shoulder wrap, and lift the beetle gently onto the dry moss beside the channel. The water slips forward without a sound.

Behind me, across the narrow timber bridge that spans the cleft, another figure steps out from the doorway of a tall, unglazed dwelling. From this distance, I cannot tell if they are young or old—their face is smooth as the dry-stacked stone, their ash-colored hair bound tightly back with fiber so not a single strand strays. They pause at the bridge’s center, look down into the cleft, and then walk on toward the upper plazas. The weighted hem of their tunic hangs straight down, brushing the moss without a rustle.

We do not call out. When we meet on the causeways, a fraction of an inch of inclination from the head, or the brief, cool press of a palm against a shoulder, is enough. Even without meeting, we leave our small grammar behind us.

Yesterday—or in whatever stretch of the endless morning preceded this one—someone set a hollowed stone bowl of condensation water on the sunlit edge of the retaining wall, three paces to my left. Beside it lies a single fern frond, its stem pointed east. I understand it the way one understands the angle of a shadow: not as an order, and not as a question, simply as a placement that asks to be answered in its own time.

When I finish the length of braid I am holding, I will stand. My bare, broad soles will find the slick slate with the quiet certainty of something that has walked these steps forever. A pale, spotted salamander resting near my heel will not scatter; it will only watch with a pulsing throat as I lift my foot and step cleanly over it.

I will walk to the sunlit wall, take up the copper chisel from my belt—not to strike, only to set—and lay it beside the bowl, point-west, just inside the edge of the shadow. Then I will kneel by the cistern, dip my own stone cup beneath the mirror surface, and drink. When I lift the cup away, the water will settle back to the exact rim, brimming and unmoving, as if nothing had been taken at all.

And then I will return to the moss, or climb into the dim, unglazed room of the nearest tower, and sit with my hands resting palm-up on my knees, watching the sharp line of the timber beam’s shadow on the floor—patient, unblinking, and held in the same cool brightness as the uncrumbling peaks.
