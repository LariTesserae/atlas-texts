I think I would know where I was before I ever opened my eyes—by the hum in my teeth.

Even in sleep, lying on packed peat-felt inside a carved canyon wall, the plateau does not let go of you. It is not a sound you hear with your ears; it is a slow, steady tremor that comes up through the deadwood floor into your ribs, like resting your cheek against the hull of a boat that is always under way. When I wake in the sharp chill before dawn, the first thing I notice is that the tremor has thinned. The drum-mites have crawled back into the pores of the crust. The wind has dropped. For one brief hour, the continent holds its breath.

If I am here, I am not a traveler passing through from the stone-ribbed rim, nor am I one of the lean hunters who crouch beneath the bladder-oaks at dusk with leather bladders, waiting for the trees to blow. I do not have the nerve for the midday chaos at the seep-basin, where the husk-boars shoulder through the razor-sedge and the needle-jacks circle in the white glare.

I would be one of the ones who stays near the cisterns. The one who keeps the pitch.

To live here is to have a body that belongs to the wood and the wind. My skin would be the color of weathered ash, dry and fine-grained, always faintly powdered along the shins with pale cellulose dust. I would have no outer ears to catch the punishing gusts—only the small, baffled slits behind my jaw, which I can pinch shut with a deliberate twitch of muscle when the afternoon roar rises to a physical weight. Instead, I would have broad, calloused soles that never see more than a thin sandal of cured sail-ray skin, and a long hardwood staff whose butt I rest against my collarbone whenever I stop to stand still.

Every morning begins in the grey, damp quiet before the sun clears the horizon. I would step out from under the membrane awning of our canyon hut, wrapped in a rattling cloak of mottled sail-ray hide, and walk barefoot across the silver-haired turf. At this hour, the world glistens. The dew clings to the fine siliceous hairs of the plain, cold and brief. My work is small and urgent: tilting the resin-sealed catchment trays, funneling the clear drops into hollowed root-cisterns before the white sun climbs and drinks the moisture back into the thin air.

My kin—two or three others, no more, bound to this single seep-basin by the strict arithmetic of water—work beside me without hurry. We speak in the dawn language: low throat-clicks, soft whistles, and short, resonant hums that carry cleanly across the wet crust without waking the bellow-worms anchored in the canyon pipes below. There is no need for many words. You can tell from the way a companion’s footfalls sound on the turf whether they are tired, or whether the eastern cistern is running low.

By mid-morning, the heat arrives like a hand pressed flat against the back of the neck. The pores of the ground open, and the rasp-beetles and drum-mites begin their scraping. Within an hour, the plains are screaming. I pinch the slits behind my jaw shut, duck beneath the heavy peat-felt curtains of the hut, and let the world outside turn into a distant, rhythmic pulse.

In the cool, tannin-scented dark, behind thin-scraped gourd panes that turn the white glare into a steady amber, I find what I am actually for.

A world that never stops roaring does not forgive carelessness with sound. You cannot shout across a canyon; you cannot hear a pack of needle-jacks slipping through the sedge over the organ-drone of the wind. So I spend the blinding hours of noon working with reeds, bone, and sinew.

I sit on the cool deadwood floor with a lapful of dried acoustic reeds harvested from the edges of the seep-basin, and a small, hammered-copper awl—the only metal we own, its tip worn bright from three generations of hands, traded up from the unseen edges of the plateau. With a scraper of notched husk-boar tusk, I thin the walls of a reed and cut a tiny, angled notch at its lip. I lift it to my mouth, blow softly, and feel the vibration against my teeth. Too high, and it will vanish into the midday buzz of the beetles. Too low, and it will blend into the hollow drone of the canyon pipes. It has to sit right in the narrow seam between them—the exact pitch of a needle-jack’s whistling nostril, or the sharp, piercing note that carries two miles to the bleached root-rib watch-tower on the ridge.

Beside the doorway, a chime of hollow pith-runner bones hangs in the hot draft. I don't look up at it; I listen to it through the floorboards. As long as its clatter stays erratic and dry, the afternoon thermals are steady. If the rhythm suddenly flattens and turns frantic, I know without stepping outside that the wind has shifted and a column of click-locusts is rising like dark smoke from the southern canyons.

In the late afternoon, when the heat begins to break and the solitary bladder-oaks out on the flats start to groan like strained hulls, I take my staff and climb up to the rim of the canyon. Even with the slits behind my jaw pinched tight, the noise hits my chest like surf. The white-blue sky is mercilessly clear. Miles away, a sail-ray wheels lazily on a thermal, translucent as a scrap of shed skin.

I walk out onto the open, amber-colored crust, feeling the hollow architecture of the continent beneath my heels. Fifty yards down, the ancient vascular tubes are dry and resonant; closer to the seep-basin, the pitch of my footsteps dulls and thickens where the morning’s water still pools in the dark peat. I check the wax plugs on the buried root-cisterns, pressing fresh, tacky pine-gum into the cracks where the afternoon heat has tried to pry them open.

And then, as the sun drops and the air turns abruptly cold, the bladder-oaks begin to fire. One by one across the darkening horizon, their leathery sacs expel the day’s hot gas with heavy, flat reports like distant cannon shots. The wind steadies. The drum-mites fall silent.

This is the hour I climb the lashed root-tower above our canyon. I take the hollowed hardwood horn, press its wide mouth firmly against the main timber, and lift the weighted mallet.

I do not strike it hard. The continent is already full of noise; if you shout, you are lost in it. Instead, I give the timber three slow, deliberate taps, spaced evenly apart, and then rest the sole of my bare foot flat against the wood to wait.

Down through the braided roots, through miles of petrified pith and ancient, packed cellulose, the small pulse travels. Somewhere three miles across the heat-hazed flats, at another lonely seep-basin tucked under another overhang, someone else is sitting barefoot on a deadwood floor, or resting their chin on a hardwood staff. They will feel it rise through their heels: three steady beats. *The cisterns are sealed. The locusts passed to the south. We are still here.*

I stand on the tower until the chill settles into my bones, feeling the vast, wooden world hum beneath my feet, waiting for the faint, patient cadence to come back through the dark.
