I would wake—if waking is the right word for a place where the dark never truly lifts—not to a sight, but to a pitch.

A long, low B-flat in the soles of my feet, rising through the arches, humming in the marrow of my shins. That means the wind has shifted to the northwest and is leaning hard against the slack span toward the wind-wall. If the note climbs a half-step by the time my coal needs feeding, sleet is coming.

I wouldn’t be in the middle of a shelter, pressed warm between the shoulders of my kin. I’d be just outside the slate windbreak, crouched in the lee of the basalt cleft where three river-thick cables braid together and sink their pale tendrils into the stone. It is the loudest, dampest, most honest place in the world. Tannin-dark water weeps steadily from the underside of the junction, pattering onto my oiled-fiber hood with a dull, greasy tap. Above me, the sky is a bruised, indifferent indigo, and below—just beyond the curl of my toes—there is nothing at all for thousands of feet.

If I am anything here, I am one of the builders, though not the kind who brings back fat bladders of creeper-blood or nets the gliders in the storm-gaps. My hands are too stiff in the mornings for fine bone-needle work, and my knees prefer the steady crouch of the long spans to the vertical scramble up the razor-edged spires.

I think I would be a **span-walker**—or what passes for one in a world where no one owns the road: the one who carries the ember and the knot-cord between junctions when the mist sits thick for days and the tap-codes stop coming through the struts.

---

Wait. Let me feel my own hands before I decide that.

My palms are crosshatched with horn-hard calluses, pale as tallow against the wet-slate skin of my wrists. Around my neck, on a length of braided bast-cord, hangs a notched toggle of dark wood, worn smooth and slightly concave in the middle where my molars have gripped it for twenty years. And tucked inside my inner layers—nestled against my ribs where the rendered creeper-fat meets the warmth of my own skin—is a perforated cylinder of glider-bone. Inside it, a single thumb-sized coal of pressed pith-meal breathes softly, fed every hour with a pinch of dry fungus-tinder.

I smell of wood-smoke, sour fermented pith, and the rank, sweetish grease of my own cloak. When I breathe out, the mist takes it immediately.

No, I am not just a messenger. A messenger only runs when someone calls. In this place, junctions go quiet for reasons that have nothing to do with distance: a brazier drowns in a sudden shift of condensation; a gale tears a slate windbreak loose; a jaw-listener settles onto the main bridge and stays there, patient as frost, for three sleeps.

I would be the one who goes to check why the wood has gone silent.

---

To leave the junction is to trade the smell of smoke for the smell of wet bark and abyss.

I don’t stand upright. Nobody stands upright here; the air has teeth, and a sudden gust off the basalt will peel you off a cable like a loose scab. I drop my center of gravity until my knees are bent and my knuckles brush the waxy, beaded wood. My boots—double layers of cured glider-skin, supple as a wet tongue—grip the curve of the cable. Every tremor in the continent-wide web comes straight up through the leather.

Half a mile out, where the secondary struts fan out like the ribs of a drowned whale, I stop. There is a junction here—not a village, just a fork where a wrist-thick span branches off toward a distant, jagged tooth of rock.

I unhook the wooden toggle from my neck, wedge its notched end into a crevice in the bark, kneel, and bite down.

I close my eyes. Sight is a thin, useless thing out here anyway; even the gliders don't bother with it.

Through my teeth, the world opens up.

First comes the background roar: the high, thin scream of wind over the unweathered slate spires, and beneath it, the vast, slow *groan* of the living bridges adjusting to their own weight. Then, closer, the *scritch-thump, scritch-thump* of an under-creeper hanging upside down a hundred yards ahead, rasping pale fungus from a weeping joint. Beneath my ribs, the bone cylinder is a small, steady pocket of heat.

And then—nothing.

Wait. Not nothing. A *dampening*.

Halfway across the fork, the wood feels subtly dead, as if someone had laid a heavy sack of wet fiber across a vibrating string. No footsteps. No sleet-chatter. Just a long, flat stillness pressed tight against the bark.

A jaw-listener. Sleek, blind, its wide-hinged chin resting on the wood, drinking in the exact rhythm of my breathing if I dare to take a step.

I don’t move. I don’t even unclamp my teeth from the toggle. Instead, I wait for the weather.

Above and below me, the mist is thickening, rolling between the spires like a slow avalanche. The air pressure drops—I feel it in my ears first, a sudden, hollow pop—and then, hanging somewhere in the black void beneath the main cable, one of the colossal barometric bladders begins to drink the gust.

It swells. You can feel the papery walls stretching through the soles of your feet, a taut, dreadful tension building in the dark.

Then the squall hits it, and the world turns into a drum.

*BOOM.*

The sound isn’t in the air; it’s in my teeth, my sternum, the fluid behind my eyes. The entire quarter-mile of bridge shudders under the low-frequency violence. In that wash of thunder, while the wood is blind and deaf with its own shaking, I spit the toggle, surge forward four long, low, crouching paces, and freeze flat against the bark before the echo dies.

I wait. The bladder groans as it collapses, sighing out a gale of damp air. Three heartbeats later, another gust catches the next bladder down the line.

*BOOM.*

Four more paces. Flat. Still.

That is how I move through this world: not conquering it, not even fighting it, just borrowing its own noise to hide my small, warm heartbeat from the things that hunt by sound.

---

By the time I reach the far spire—six stops to feed the coal, twice forced to crawl inside a weeping pith-knot to thaw my fingers in the yeasty, fever-warm dark of the borers’ galleries—the sky is the color of old iron.

The shelter wedged into the basalt cleft ahead is dark. No thin thread of orange smoke curls from the vent-hole. No triple-rap answers when I strike the wrist-thick strut beside the windbreak with the pommel of my flint knife.

My chest tightens, not with fear, exactly—fear wastes breath—but with the quiet, heavy arithmetic of the spans. Twelve people lived here the last time the wind blew from the south.

I pull back the heavy slate slab wedged into the mouth of the cleft and crawl inside.

The brazier is cold, its hollowed basalt bowl filled with grey, wet ash. The windbreak didn't fail; the storm must have driven condensation straight down the vent, drowning the pith-cakes while they slept, or else they simply ran out of dry tinder during the last long mist.

And then I hear it: a low, dry rustle from the back of the cleft, where the matted fiber-bundles are piled against the rock.

They aren’t dead. They’re huddled together in a single, tight knot beneath three layers of greased glider-cloaks, sharing whatever heat is left between their ribs, too stiff and numb to strike a spark from damp flint.

I don’t speak—my throat is raw from the freezing air, and words are clumsy here anyway. I crawl over, pull the bone cylinder from against my chest, and unstopper it. Inside, the tiny coal glows a fierce, stubborn orange.

One of them—an old woman whose toes are spread wide as paddles from a lifetime on the slick wood—reaches out a trembling, tallow-pale hand. Not for the coal. For my forehead.

She presses her brow against mine, bone to bone, and murmurs so low it barely stirs the damp air between our mouths. I feel the words in my skull: *The lower span snapped two sleeps ago. The bladders are full of sleet. We thought the line was cut.*

*It wasn’t cut,* I murmur back into her forehead, feeding shreds of dry fungus-tinder onto the coal until a thin, oily thread of smoke rises between us. *I heard the pitch change.*

---

That is who I would be.

Not a ruler, not a hero, not even a hunter of the great gliders. Just the one who knows the pitch of the bridges, carries a borrowed coal against the ribs, and crawls through the dark between twelve people and the next twelve, making sure the thread doesn't break.

And when I am finally too old to outrun a jaw-listener between drumbeats—when my knees lock up in the freezing mist and my molars wear through the last toggle—they will strip my bone cylinder, my flint knife, and my greased cloak, tie a sharp shard of black basalt to my ankles, and let me go over the windward edge.

Perhaps I will fall clean into the black air below the cloud-floor. Or perhaps, if the stone slips, I will catch in some forgotten, lower tangle of fibrous struts a mile down, where the damp-winged insects and the pale, glowing fungi will find me, and the under-creepers will gather in the dark to graze—turning me into one more faint, cold lantern hanging over the abyss, visible from the high spans on clear nights like a star that never quite hit the ground.
