If you came looking for me in the cyan, you wouldn’t find me out in the open midwater, and you wouldn’t find me down on the gridded floor prying sprat off the intake screens. I don’t have the lungs or the nerves for the deep suction cages, where the brass impellers hum through your ribs and a slipped hook means the current pins you to the mesh until you drown.

You would find me thirty meters down from the hammered-silver surface, tucked into the leeward seam of Pylon Thirty-Eight-North, where a secondary cooling-return pipe bleeds a steady, sluggish plume of eleven-degree water into the nine-degree basin.

Two degrees doesn’t sound like much until you have spent four hours in the dark-blue chill. Here, it feels like a hearth. The outflow creates a permanent, rippling pane of optical distortion—a localized thermocline that bends the overhead floodlamps into wavering ribbons. Sparse, pale-green algae clings to the concrete lip of the pipe, the only soft thing for a kilometer in any direction. I wedge my heels into the tie-rod holes just below the lip, let the slightly warmer water wash over my patched neoprene, and rest my jawbone against the concrete.

That is who I am here: a listener, and a mender of things that keep the cold out.

I am old enough to remember dirt—not fondly, necessarily, just as a fact, the way you remember the layout of a house you no longer live in. I remember that light used to come from one place at a time and then go away, and that silence used to be something that happened on its own, without you having to press your skull against thirty meters of poured concrete to find it. Because I remember, I never quite learned the easy, weightless grace of the younger divers who came down the shafts as children. My knees still stiffen in the chill. My eyes still ache behind the salt-rimed goggles when I look up into the glare, my pupils pinned down to hard black grains.

So I made myself useful in the margins between the water and the trusses.

In the water, I carry a heavy iron marlinspike, a mesh sack, and a short length of brass rod. I don’t hunt the bubble-plumes—too chaotic, too loud, the aerated water stealing your buoyancy until the current slams you sideways. Instead, I patrol the quiet edges of the pylons and the undersides of the overhead pipe-racks. I harvest what the building sheds and what the smaller things leave behind: loose rubber gaskets working free from inspection hatches, strips of sacrificial zinc that have corroded just enough around their bolts to be pried loose for belt-weights, tangled lengths of nylon line snagged on the cross-bracing.

And I listen to the rebar.

When you press the flat of your jaw to Pylon Thirty-Eight-North, the tearing hiss of the distant diffusers drops away, replaced by the clean, ringing interior of the basin. I know the voice of every pumping station within ten kilometers. I can tell by the slight, stuttering rattle in the steel skeleton when Station Four’s impeller bearings are running dry, hours before the maintenance automation cycles it down. When the younger divers are out in the midwater chasing dogfish along the plumes, I am often the one hanging by a pylon with my brass rod, tapping out the rhythm of an incoming sluice-front—three sharp *tinks*, a pause, two low *tinks*—sending the warning through the concrete bones of the room so they don’t get swept into the open avenues when the eastern gates seat.

Half my waking life, though, isn’t in the water at all. It is six inches above it, in the hot, dripping crawlspace of the steel maintenance trusses.

If you hauled yourself up onto Catwalk Nine-East, stepping carefully over a sleeping harbor seal that smells like wet wool and old herring, you would find my corner lashed to the gusset plates behind a bank of floodlamp ballasts. The housings run scorching hot—hot enough to blister bare skin, hot enough to turn the condensation dripping from the roof into a constant, sharp *tsss*—and I use that waste heat the way people upstairs once used stoves.

That is where I do my real work.

I keep three dented steel buckets wedged between the I-beams, packed with the pale, waxy livers of the dogfish the plume-hunters bring up. Over the course of a sleep-cycle, the radiant heat from the lamp ballasts slowly renders the livers down into a thick, yellow-white grease that smells sharp and rancid even over the ozone of the lights. When it cools to a paste, I pack it into salvaged PVC pipe-caps and trade it for dried kelp, or strips of inner tube, or stunned mackerel pulled off the bottom screens. Everyone needs grease. Without it, the nine-degree water eats your cheeks and wrists raw before your second dive.

In the glare-lit dimness behind the ballasts, with a strip of black rubber tied across my eyes to rest them from the cyan, I sit hunched on the steel grating with my legs dangling over the void and patch suits by touch. My fingers know the difference between a pinhole in vulcanized rubber and a seam splitting in heavy PVC. I smear silicone caulk into the tears, press scraps of salvaged conveyor-belt rubber over worn-out knees, and splice frayed nylon webbing for sleeping harnesses.

The sea lions know me up here. A massive, scarred bull hauls out on the opposite beam every few cycles, his coat steaming faintly in the ballast heat. When I first claimed that corner, he barked until my ears rang inside the steel roof. Now he just lifts his whiskered snout, blows a wet, fishy breath across the catwalk, and drops his head back onto his flippers. We have the same posture, mostly—hunched, heavy-shouldered when out of the water, built around conserving heat and holding onto a narrow ledge above a long drop.

Sometimes, when the patching is done and the grease buckets are capped, I unbind my eyes and sit at the edge of the catwalk, watching the water three feet below. From up here, the surface looks like hammered tin, restless and blinding, broken only by the dark, torpedo-like shadows of seals diving into the light cones. Every few minutes, a deep *boom-clank* rolls up through the steel stanchions into my spine—another sluice gate seating miles away, another cold front starting its slow crawl across the gridded plain.

The young ones think I am half-blind and overly cautious because I hug the pylons and spend so much time in the hot stink of the trusses. Maybe they’re right. I don’t dream of swimming the open avenues anymore.

When I roll off the catwalk and drop back into the nine-degree shock of the basin, feeling the zinc weight at my waist pull me down into the electric blue, I don’t kick out toward the baitballs. I drift back to the leeward seam of Thirty-Eight-North, find my footing in the tie-rod holes above the warm return pipe, and press my ear to the concrete. The turbines hum. The rebar rings. And somewhere two kilometers off in the glare, someone taps a wrench against a column—*tink-tink, tink*—asking if the current is clear.

I raise my brass rod and tap back.
