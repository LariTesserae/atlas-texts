I would not know how to arrive here from anywhere else. In a place that has no weather and no direction, there is no meaningful difference between having always been here and having simply settled into the stone long enough for the stone to accept my weight.

If I am anywhere in this valley, I am out past the sheltered alcoves, on the broad, slick spine of one of the middle arches—close to where two ribs of pale grey rock pass cleanly through one another without touching.

From a distance, you would not pick me out from the bridge. My knees are tucked beneath a heavy, low-slung torso, and the supercooled mist has given my shoulders the same faint mineral sheen as the stone. Water beads along my collarbones and sheets down my back without ever freezing, without ever drying. There is no steam when I draw breath—only one shallow pull of air every few minutes, cold enough to match the thick, sluggish blood in my chest.

I keep my eyes mostly shut. Milk-glass and sunken, they are poor instruments here; they only tell me that the sky is a bruised, shadowless smear, or that a cairn I left behind me is somehow waiting twenty paces ahead.

Instead, I listen through the soles of my wide, flat feet and the heels of my palms.

The valley’s roar is not noise to me; it is architecture. The dry static hiss sits on the surface of my skin like a second layer of mist, while the deep, iron-plate chord moves straight up through my ankles and settles into the wide cage of my ribs. When I shift my weight, I can read the whole span of the bridge in the way that chord answers back—where the stone is solid all the way down into the fog, where an underside hollow rings thin, and where the space ahead has begun to stretch or double.

Out here, near the crossing of the two arches, the standing wave is never quite true. Two vibrations meet and jostle against one another, creating a faint, teeth-aching flutter in the rock. That is why I am here, rather than tucked into one of the smooth, worn bowls closer to the rim.

I carry four river-smooth stones cradled against my sternum. They have been with me long enough to take on the chill of my own skin.

Kneeling on the damp stone, I set the first one down. I wait—not for a second or a minute, because those measures mean nothing here, only long enough for the tremor of my own movement to leave the rock. Then I set the second stone atop the first, feeling with my blunt, smooth-worn fingertips for the exact point where its weight ceases to lean. The third stone takes longer. The air here feels thick, and the chord in my ribs is slightly uneven, tugging gently toward an invisible slope. When the fourth stone finally settles and holds—neither sliding nor trembling—I leave my palm resting lightly on its crown for a long time.

*Here,* the four stones say. *Here, at least, the rock agrees with itself.*

Sometimes, while I am kneeling like this, one of the flat, slate-colored things creeps out of a crevice in the arch. It has no eyes to see me with, only the long, trembling filaments along its flanks. It slides across the wet stone and presses its cool, plated side against my calf, aligning its own faint vibration with the hum in my ribs. Neither of us moves away. We simply share the frequency of the bridge until the creature decides to creep onward into the mist.

When I do rise and walk, it is without hurry—just a change of posture between one long thought and the next. If I grow hungry, I climb down over the lip of the arch, clinging with broad palms and tough heels to the slick underside where the dampness beads thickest. There, in the dimness, I peel strips of pale, rubbery lichen from the rock, or snap the translucent hollow stalks at their base to drink the cold, mineral-heavy water trapped inside. Now and then, a four-winged moth drifts too close, held aloft by the sheer pressure of the valley’s roar, vibrating in place like a speck of bone-dust. My hand rises—sudden and precise—and closes around it. The dry crunch vanishes instantly beneath the static hiss of the basin.

Once in a long while, I meet another of my kind on the narrowest part of the span. We stop a pace apart, neither of us speaking, neither of us possessing a name to offer. We simply lean forward until our foreheads meet, and press the flat heels of our palms together. For a long time we stand locked like that, letting the resonance of our ribcages pass through bone and hide—trading whatever small shifts we have felt in the valley’s chord, confirming that the other is still solid, still here, still holding the same note.

Then we part, and I walk on until the distance folds and brings me back to a place I know by heart.

It is a shallow depression in the stone, just off the main spine of the arch, where dark water has gathered. The pool does not lie level. It tilts at twenty degrees against no visible incline, its surface held taut and glassy, refusing to spill.

This is where I spend the longest stretches of stillness. I kneel at the edge, press my palms flat to the damp rock, and let my milky eyes open wide over the water. I do not drink from it, and I do not touch it.

Overhead, the sky is empty, grey, and dim. Down in the tilted glass, the reflection shows a sky crowded with too many stars, and the bleached, impossible smear of a sun that does not exist above my head. I lean forward over the edge, letting the iron chord hum quietly through my chest, and I watch the wrong sky with all the patience the stone has taught me—waiting, without expectation or haste, to see if anything on the other side of the water might one day lean down to look back.
