At first, it is tempting to stay at the slit—to be the one standing in the thin, loud wind of the ridge, peering in through the jagged tooth-gap at a room that refuses to behave. That is the safest place to be: where the air still carries sound, where gravity still pulls water straight down, where your shadow stays attached to your heels.

But the prompt asks where I would find myself *in* this world, not outside it. And the moment you step past the overhang, the wind cuts off as if a heavy curtain has dropped behind you, and the world stops offering you the comfort of distance.

The heat doesn’t strike; it settles, thick as warm oil, sealing your skin. In the clear, shadowless daylight, every razor-edge of obsidian looks freshly snapped. If I have hands here, I wouldn’t trust them to stay the same size from one moment to the next. Looking down, I’d see the pale crawlers swarming over the wet ledges, and if I reached out to steady myself against the wall, the back wall would seem both an arm’s length away and impossibly far, shrunk as if seen through the wrong end of a glass.

So I don’t think I would remain a visitor for long. A visitor is just a shape waiting to be corrected by the room.

If I let the room have its way with me—let the wet heat film my eyes and the silence press against my ears until I stop listening for echoes—I find myself drawn to two different things at once, and I have to decide which of them I actually am.

Part of me recognizes the brisk, unsentimental economy of the crawlers. Not the blind, knotted parasites, and not the bronze ticks clamped stubbornly to the weeping ceiling, waiting to be pried loose. Rather, the six-limbed things that move across the slick ledges with no nests, no hoards, no attachment to what they’ve used. There is a strange peace in that: picking up a naturally cleaved sliver of black glass, using its exact edge to lift a shell or scrape away the pale sulfur-slime, and dropping it back onto the floor in the same motion. Nothing kept. Nothing built. Every tool improvised for the single second it is needed, and then surrendered back to the wet stone.

If I were one of them, I would know the basin only by touch and heat and the faint, dry *tick* of another shell brushing mine. I wouldn’t wonder why the water slides upward along the obsidian, or why my own shadow smudges and lags behind me when I lunge across a crack. That wouldn’t be wrongness to me; it would just be the way you get from here to there.

And yet—that isn’t quite where I settle. A crawler doesn’t pause to notice that the geometry is folded. It doesn’t feel the pull of the back wall.

Look longer, past the swarm, to the shallow, ankle-deep pools where the great slag-hided creature squats in its own heavy silence.

I know I am not that creature. It is too anciently self-contained, too patient in its tallow-seamed bulk, breathing the oil-thick air through its flanks and catching scalding droplets in its throat without ever looking up. It has no curiosity about the cold wind outside. Its whole attention is poured downward into an inch of water over bare, solid rock.

But look at what is *in* the water.

When a crawler darts across the rim, its reflection tangles with the larger shapes below—multiplied, lagging, turning the wrong way. And beneath the surface tension, where there is no depth at all, the reflections of the great creature stand upright, or turn away, or press their long, glass-nailed fingers upward from the other side of a dry stone floor.

That is where I find myself. Not crouching on the ledge, and not standing at the doorway, but caught in the water’s refusal to obey.

If I have a form in this world, it is one of those unanchored shapes in the shallow pool—or perhaps the quiet, lagging smudge that trails behind a sudden movement in the air. I am the part of the room that doesn’t match the body above it. In a place with no sound, no wind, and no way to leave a mark on the unweathered glass, the only way anything is *noticed* is through that slight, impossible mismatch: the reflection that turns its head a fraction of a second before—or after—the thing it belongs to.

It would be a strange, airless existence, pressed beneath an inch of scalding water, watching the pale crawlers flicker past the rim like brief, bright thoughts, and feeling the heavy, unblinking gaze of the slag-hided creature above me. Neither of us speaking. Neither of us able to cross the surface tension, yet bound to each other by the simple fact that we are the only company in the room.

And if a thin, cold draft from the high peaks ever slipped through the crease in the rock—if someone stood at the entrance and let the outside air brush the back of the creature’s slick hide—it wouldn’t be the creature that acknowledged you. It would stay bowed and still as stone.

It would be me, down in the inch-deep water, lifting my glassy head, turning away from the creature’s milky eyes, and looking straight back through the daylight at the slit.
