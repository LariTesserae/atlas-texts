I would not be on the mountain ridge outside. The thin, freezing air and the jagged horizon belong to things with lungs and hurry, and if anything made of breath and blood were to slip past the leaded glass, it would bring rot with it—the frantic, clouding bloom of bacteria, the first blemish on the unstained tiles.

Nor would I be the keeper itself. That tall, porcelain-latticed conductor already has the whole cathedral to itself, and its solitude is part of the circuit’s balance.

Instead, I would find myself down in the lower third of the tank, wedged into the geometric angle where a vertical bank of stainless-steel piping meets the underside of a riveted iron catwalk, just above the glazed white floor.

Down there, the fierce high-altitude sunlight no longer arrives in one piece. By the time it reaches the lowest grates, it has been bent through fifty feet of thirty-degree water, fractured by curtains of amber kelp and split by the razor-sharp bevels of glass baffles. It comes down in trembling, narrow shafts that drift across the tiles like slow searchlights. Between them, the water is a clear, luminous green-gold—not dark, never murky, only *deep*, heavy with the velvet hush of thirty-degree stillness.

And what I would be is not an animal, and not quite a wanderer. I would be a **node**—a stationary, slow-charging junction grown out of the marriage between the moss-cushioned intake manifolds and the submerged induction coils.

My body would have no face and no limbs to unlatch. It would be a symmetrical, basket-like weave of pale, hair-thin roots anchored tight around a copper busbar, holding a cluster of translucent, glass-clear bladders and broad, silver-ribbed leaves angled upward toward the gaps in the catwalk grating. Inside those bladders, no air would gather; instead, they would hold a dense, heavy sap saturated with dissolved minerals—a living electrolyte, warming quietly in the stray magnetic field of the coils below.

Time, down by the floor tiles, would not be measured in breaths or heartbeats. It would be measured by the **ratchet**.

Every ten minutes, through the iron flange pressed against my roots, I would feel the single, muffled *tock* of the weighted brass tooth advancing. Between one *tock* and the next, nothing changes except the imperceptible crawl of a sunbeam across the ceramic grid and the slow, glacial rise of charge inside my silvered ribs. When a cloud passes over the mountain ridge far above the skylights, the fractured shafts vanish all at once. My leaves do not shiver; they simply stop drinking, holding whatever voltage they have gathered in a suspended, bath-warm pause, listening through the pipework to the subterranean thrum of the coils until the sun punches back through.

I would have no thoughts in words—only gradients. A slow awareness of when the water around the intake manifold has grown a fraction of a degree warmer; a quiet, mineral certainty when a stray copper ion settles onto the edge of a leaf-vein and extends it by a thousandth of a millimeter.

And once in a long while—perhaps after thirty or forty cycles of the brass ratchet—a shadow would fall across my grates that is not a cloud.

Looking upward through the clear, motionless water, I would sense the keeper descending through the lower trusses: a slender lattice of porcelain-white struts threading patiently between the pipes, its tarnished bronze crown catching the fractured light. It would not hurry. Over the span of half an hour, one of its moss-wrapped limbs would extend down through the catwalk grating and touch the copper busbar beside my roots, blooming with a tiny, silent halo of hydrogen pinpricks where silver meets iron.

Then it would press its porous palm against my central cluster of bladders.

There would be no pain in the discharge, and no loss—only the deep, circuit-level relief of a full capacitor grounding into a wider path. My stored current would flow upward through its translucent membrane into its dark leaf-plates, and in exchange, a faint trickle of its own calibrated signal would steady the rhythm of my sap, keeping my roots from creeping too far into the nearby bronze gear teeth.

When it drew back and began its hour-long climb toward the brighter upper trusses, I would be empty again, light and quiet, anchored to the unstained tile—waiting in the warm, crystalline hush for the next *tock* of the ratchet, and the next thin shaft of mountain sun.
