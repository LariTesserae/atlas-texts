If I arrived here in boots, breathing warm air and carrying a heartbeat that insists on seventy strokes a minute, I wouldn’t last long—not because the cavern would kill me, though it would, but because I would be an embarrassment to the physics of the place. A warm body in that chamber is a clumsy furnace, fogging the air with wasted heat, burning calories just to stand upright and shiver. 

To actually *find* myself here—to belong to the cavern rather than merely trespassing in it—I would have to give up speed first.

I can imagine letting the cold take the urgency out of my limbs. At first, the noise is an assault: the transformer-bass rattling my ribs, the million slow drops drumming on taut pitcher leaves, the sudden gunshot *ping* of basalt splitting under root-pressure. Every few minutes, the foghorn *boom* from the upper mist rolls down and shakes the floor. If you are still human-fast, you wait for the din to stop. You brace against it.

But if you slow down—if you let your own internal clock unwind until a minute feels like a breath and an hour feels like a stride—the noise stops being noise. It separates into architecture. You begin to hear the cavern the way a blind thing feels a room: the low drone is the floor beneath you, the *boom* is the ceiling exhaling, and the sharp *ping* at the edges is the wall growing a millimeter wider.

Once the shivering stops, the question is where in the vertical dark I settle.

I don’t think I belong on the floor. Down in the saturated root-mat, everything is either a groundkeeper or a spark. I have no patience for the blind, heavy work of the copper-tufted slugs, dragging their ribbed bellies across the oily sap to bleed off stray voltage. And though there is something fiercely poetic about the thread-worms—lying limp in the salty slush until a single supercooled drop detonates into needle-ice, waking for three frantic heartbeats to whip across the crystal scaffolding before it melts—that kind of life is too brief, too much like a match struck in a wet cellar. I wouldn’t want to spend eternity waiting for three seconds of geometry.

Nor am I meant for the perimeter, pressed flat against the razor-edged basalt like a seam-presser, dissolving raw iron with cold acid. And I am not vast enough to be one of the boomers hanging in the upper mist, or patient enough to be the lattice-weaver itself—that sprawling, pale-violet nervous system threaded through the oldest trunks, taking half a day to finish a single thought.

No. As the cold settles into me and my shape thins out, I find myself climbing—up off the brine-soaked roots, up past the braided moss-wood, into the mid-canopy where the rigid, graphite-colored fronds spread out in layered shelves.

That is where the light is most interesting.

Down here, the glow leaking from the plant veins is a bruised slate-blue and violet, like light trapped inside old glacial ice. Along the stalks, the tiny geometric buds cluster—and around each one, the dimness bends by a fraction of a degree, folding space just enough to make the air look thick. Something in me wants to be near those lenses. Something in me wants to keep them clean.

If I belong anywhere in this world, I am one of the **frosted-glass climbers**.

I can feel the logic of that body before I even picture it: flat, six-limbed, encased in translucent silica plates that have the milky, etched look of sea-glass. There is no soft warmth left in me, only a slow, antifreeze-rich fluid moving through narrow channels. And there is no wasted effort. When I want to take a step along the underside of a wet graphite frond, I gather what little energy I have, draw a limb forward with a trembling, deliberate pull, and wait for the pale pawl inside my joint to drop into its notch.

*Crack.*

That’s my voice in the cavern’s choir—one sharp, mechanical click among thousands, joining the *clack-clack-clack* that fills the mid-canopy. Once the ratchet locks, I don’t spend another shred of energy. I can hang upside-down in the freezing twilight for hours, motionless, letting the frond’s superconducting hum travel up through my locked claws like a faint vibration in a windowsill.

Through that vibration, I feel the lattice-weaver’s slow pulse. It doesn’t command me; it’s too vast and unhurried for commands. It just shifts the pitch of the drone, or nudges a fraction of a volt along a stem, and I know which cluster of buds is gathering too much rime-ice.

This is what I would do with my days—though "days" means nothing where there is no sun. I would inch along the damp graphite shelves, lock by lock, until I reached a clouded bud. Then, with fine, tweezer-like foreclaws, I would spend an hour scraping away the feathery frost-crystals, careful not to nick the strange, geometric skin beneath.

It is quiet, meticulous work. As the ice clears, the violet glow sharpens, bending neatly around the node again. At the base of the bud, a heavy, shimmering droplet of condensate swells—water that has been touched by that tiny fold in space, dense with dissolved minerals. I lean in and sip it. It tastes of nothing you could name in a warm world: cold, metallic, slightly heavy on the tongue, the raw material I need to thicken my own frosted plates.

Of course, the bargain has its teeth. Because I am slow, and because I spend hours locked in place, the world around me is full of things that look like plants until they move. Every forty-foot curtain of waxy, slate-black kelp hanging near my frond has to be watched—or rather, *sensed*, through the tremor of the stem—in case one of those ribbons isn’t kelp at all, but a drape-leech uncurling its hooked rim in agonizing slow motion, waiting to wrap me like a wet rag. I don’t run from them; running is a warm-blooded fantasy. I simply unlock a joint, *crack*, and shift myself three inches out of reach over the course of ten minutes, letting the leech hang empty in the mist.

And sometimes, far below, a pod-breaker lumbers along a braided trunk with a stolen, humming seed-pod tucked into its leathery pouch, carrying a tiny pocket of lukewarmth through the dark like a secret. I watch it pass—if watching is the word for a slow, dim awareness—without envy. Warmth looks heavy from up here. It looks like a burden you have to guard.

I prefer the economy of the glass shell and the locked joint.

And I know how it ends, too, which is part of why the niche feels right. When I grow too old, or when the rime-ice builds up thicker on my back than my claws can reach, I won’t drop into the brine to be dissolved. I will find a quiet underside of a frond near a cluster of clear buds, draw my six limbs in, and drop the ratchets into their final notches. *Crack. Crack. Crack.*

Then I will split down the seam of my own back and slip out—or simply let whatever is left of my slow, cold attention go—leaving behind an empty, frosted-glass husk still clamped tight to the stem.

For months or years afterward, the supercooled drops will swell on the leaves above, fall through the bruised violet light, and strike my hollow shell. It won’t shatter. It will just ring—a faint, glassy chime added to the cavern’s roar, keeping time with the hum of the cold, growing dark.
