I would not be down on the lowest steps, where the ammonia rises thick from the evaporating pans and the brick is dark with weeping damp. That is a place you visit only long enough to scrape the pale, bitter crust of saltpeter into a shard of pot, holding your breath against the stink before climbing back into the dry dust. Nor would I be up under the roof-rim, where the whitewashed chimneys pour down columns of blinding glare and the caper bushes cling to the cracks—too bright there, too close to the terrible, lidless sky of the plain.

I would be in the middle dark, three tiers up, wedged into the shadow between two columns of daylight where a cedar cross-beam meets the brick.

It takes a long time to learn how to sit still in a place that never stops moving. If you pressed your ear to the air here, you would hear nothing at all—only a flat, tearing roar of forty thousand wings and throats bouncing off fired clay until your skull rings hollow. My ears gave up on that years ago. They are scarred flat to the chittering of the starlings. The noise is just weather now, a constant pressure against the skin, like standing in a warm, dusty wind.

Instead, I live in the wood.

I am one of the thin ones—light enough that a frayed hemp rope, stiff with dried lime, will hold me if I spread my weight across three struts at once. My ribs show when I reach, and my heels and toes are dark, split leather from gripping rounded timber. Every morning, before the glare drops straight down the shafts, I climb down just far enough to smear grey mud from the lowest weep-holes over my shoulders, my cropped hair, the hollows behind my knees. As it dries, it cracks into pale scales that flake off onto the wicker trays below, taking the bird-mites with it.

If you asked me who I am, I couldn’t tell you with my throat. None of us use our throats for more than a dry click or a hiss when a rat-snake slides too close to a sleeper’s ribs. My name is three light taps on a dry cedar knot, a pause, and a slow drag of the fingernail down the grain—the sound a loose strip of bark makes when the updraft catches it. That is what I tap onto the beam before I step onto someone else’s ledge, so they don’t startle and lose their grip into the dark below.

Among the dozen of us, I am the one who listens to the slack.

Because the whole pit is tied together—plank to post, rope to tray, gourd to beam—a word travels through the lattice faster than any bird can fly. Two knuckle-raps and a plucked hoist-rope will send a tremor straight up through the soles of another’s feet three stories away. That is how we warn of a snake in the trays, or call each other out onto the central walkways with hollowed gourds when rain begins to fall through the shafts.

But hemp rots in the damp, and cedar splits in the dry heat near the top. When a lashing loosens, the wood goes dull; the messages die halfway across the room, swallowed by a sagging joint. So I spend the bright hours creeping along the undersides of the catwalks, hanging by my knees and crooked toes, testing the tension of the world. I pinch the frayed ropes between my thumb and forefinger to feel how much lime has eaten into the core. I wedge dry shards of terracotta into the joints where a beam has shrunk in its socket, tightening the frame until it hums again.

On the mid-level ledge, half-buried in chalky droppings and shed feathers, sit the flat, green-crusted things left behind by whatever heavy-booted creatures built this place. Sometimes I rest my heels near them when my calves ache. I never pick them up—they are too heavy, and they have no give in them—but I like to lay my palm flat against the cold metal for a moment. Everything else in the pit vibrates: the wicker, the timber, the air, my own thin ribs. Only the brick walls and those dead, flat blades stay completely still.

When the wind rises on the plain above, I am usually the first to know. I don’t look up at the shafts; I press my forehead and my teeth against the retaining wall, right between the mouths of two terracotta pots where the mortar is cool. Through my jawbone, I drink the low, cavernous hum of the whitewashed chimneys. A dry gale makes the masonry sing thin and high, like a taut rope. A squall drops the pitch down into my chest, a heavy, wet thrum that rattles the saltpeter crusts in the pans below.

Then I reach out, take the nearest vertical hoist-rope between my thumb and knuckle, and pluck it—slow, even, three times—sending the news up through the lattice to the ones curing dove-meat in the updrafts, and down to the ones gathering fern-sprouts in the damp.

By dusk, when the owls wake in the shadows of the funnels and the bats pour out of the deep crevices in a black, chittering tide, my work is done. I climb back to my cross-beam, slip into a hammock of knotted reed-matting slung beneath the planks, and curl my knees to my chin. The wood sways slightly beneath me as the wind blows across the plain, carrying the faint, steady tremors of the others settling into their trays for the night.

Eventually, my joints will stiffen too much to trust the frayed ropes, or my fingers will grow too slow to pin a rat-snake behind the jaw. When that happens, the others won’t take me down to the wet bottom, where there is no earth to dig. They will carry me all the way to the top, into the dry heat behind the brick funnels where the owls sit, and lay me out in the dust. The beetles and the warm updrafts will do the rest, and in a season or two, the swifts will weave what is left of me into their nests, packed tight into the mouths of the pots, singing along with the rest of the walls.
