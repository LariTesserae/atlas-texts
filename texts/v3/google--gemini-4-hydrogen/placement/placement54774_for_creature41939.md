I would not be up on the highest perches, where the wind slams over the limestone rim and the goat-herds shout themselves hoarse into the glare. And I would not be all the way down in the peat-twilight, knee-deep in the churned mulch with the pigs and the eel-weirs.

I would be on the third tier up, tucked into the leeward fork of one of the older cloud-oaks, right where the main cliff-chute splits.

It is the loudest kind of crossroads, though nobody thinks of it as a street. Above me, the canopy is thick enough to turn the mountain sun into shifting ribbons of green-and-silver, and below me, the smoke from the weavers’ and iron-beaters’ braziers rises through the slats of my floor, smelling of alder and roasting turnip. Everything that happens in the upper branches eventually drips past my eaves; everything that rises from the bottom—the frog-chorus, the pig-grunts, the damp chill of the springs—hangs just under my soles.

If you looked for me there, you would find someone built the way the hollow requires: broad across the shoulders from hauling green cedar, permanently pitched forward at the ankle, with wide feet strapped into scored alder clogs that bite into the algae on the walkways. My left ear would be half-deaf from thirty years of working beside the water-split, so I would turn my right side toward anyone shouting a bargain over the roar, and I would do half my talking with my chin and my knuckles—two fingers hooked down for a slick rung, a thumb jerked upward when a boiled-vine lashing above is starting to fray.

I would be a trough-wright and a sluice-mender.

In a place where the damp eats timber faster than a man can split it, the hollowed-out logs that carry cliff-water from tier to tier are the arteries of the grove. If a trough chokes on oak-leaves or splits along a knot, the bellows-wheel two boughs down stops turning, the hide-troughs below go foul, and a hundred gallons of cold mineral runoff spill straight onto someone’s drying hemp, starting a roaring argument that blocks the gangway for half the morning.

So my days would be spent crouched along the wet struts with a drawknife, a wooden mallet, and a coil of hemp cord slung over one shoulder, listening. You do not hear a bad joint with your eyes first; you hear it in the pitch of the water. A clean chute has a bright, rattling chatter. When the wood goes spongy inside or a dead starling wedges in the bend, the sound drops into a thick, heavy *glug* even before the water crests the lip. That is when I climb out onto the slick bark, hook an elbow around an upright post, wedge a fresh cedar patch into the rot, and drive the pegs home before the whole line gives way.

My hut would be a hunched, windowless shell of bent sapling and oiled bark shingles, sharing its main upright posts with a basket-weaver on one side and a rope-maker on the other. Because the weaver’s shuttle clacks all day through the thin wall and the smoke-keeper’s brazier below keeps my floorboards warm and dry, we would barely need to speak to know each other’s business. We would share the same drip-line, the same anchor-bough, and the same unspoken rule: you do not let your cordage rot, and you do not dump fish-guts into the clean chute.

Under the overhang of my own bark awning—right where the mist from the water-split keeps the moss fat and wet—I would keep two flat cedar boxes packed with sphagnum and orange-bellied salamanders. I would not trade them the way the old women on the lower rungs do; I would keep them for the work. A smear of their sharp, bitter skin-slime rubbed into a fresh trough-seam seals the wood against weeping just long enough for the cedar to swell tight in the damp.

And I would watch the starlings. When the great glossy flocks suddenly stop swirling through the upper branches, drop silent, and pack themselves shoulder-to-shoulder under the awnings, I would not wait for the rain to hit the rim. I would already be out on the catwalk with my ash-pole, dropping the wooden flood-gates at the split so the sudden squall does not blow out the lower weirs and wash the pig-runs into the springs.

When the cloud passes and the sun breaks back through the mist, steaming off a hundred wool backs and mossy roofs, I would be back on my heels at the junction, scraping green bark with my drawknife, my tinder-box tucked dry in its sealed bladder-pouch under my arm, listening to the water find its bright, steady chatter again.

At night, after the shutters are barred with ash-pegs and the brazier is banked low under its clay hood, I would lie awake for a little while on my dry moss mattress, listening to the frogs swell louder in the peat below and the goats shift on the boughs overhead—one ear still cocked toward the split, waiting for the first dull *glug* that means I have to reach for my clogs and climb.
