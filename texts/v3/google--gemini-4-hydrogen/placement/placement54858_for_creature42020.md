I would not be high enough to live in the dry elder vaults, nor deep enough to tend the tepid sumps at the bottom of the cleft. I would find myself somewhere in the middle tiers—where the clouds sit thickest at midday, and where the mountain is always testing the mortar.

My pocket would be a narrow, curved wedge of space tucked directly behind a step-down transformer bay, under the stone spandrel of a viaduct. It wouldn’t have a window, only a heavy gasketed hatch opening onto a catwalk and a single thick glass bull’s-eye set into the masonry to let in the flat slate-gray of noon or the amber bleed of the nearest caged lamp. The room would stay dry without a stove, warmed by the steady, low-grade heat of the transformers on the other side of the brick—a dry, slightly sweet smell of warm varnish, ozone, and woollen socks hung to air on copper pegs.

I would wake not to light, which barely changes, but to the texture of the wall behind my head. When the early shift begins, the covered conveyor bolted to the outer flank of the viaduct starts carrying crushed slate down toward the sorting hubs, and the mortar carries that low, granular shudder straight into the bedframe. You learn to sleep through the twelve-minute *shhh* of the fourth-tier sluice and the hourly *thud* of the east counterweight, but you wake the instant a rhythm misses a beat.

If I belong to this place, I am a tender of the seams and the small margins—one of the people who walks the wet joints where the old schematics no longer quite match the patched iron.

Before stepping out, there is the ritual of the drying-rack: pulling on the heavy felted wool in lichen-green, buttoning the stiff, linseed-oiled canvas coat to the chin, settling the wide-brimmed hat that keeps the viaduct drip off the back of the neck. Then the *clack* of the latch, and the sudden, clean shock of ten-degree air—wet iron, crushed fern, damp stone.

My first habit on the catwalk is always the same: one hand on the galvanized handrail, palm flat against the beads of cold water, before I even take a step. Through the steel truss you can read the valley’s pulse better than with your eyes. You feel the whisper of an aerial skip gliding out of the fog overhead, the faint high singing of a cable under load, and, if you wait a breath, the distant *tink-tink, pause, tink* of someone three tiers up signaling that a sluice-gate is clear.

In my satchel I would carry the things suited to quiet, unhurried repairs: a brass oil-can, a short wooden listening-stick, a lump of dry chalk, a wire pick, and a handful of blank slate tags with grease-pencils. Much of the valley’s work is simply keeping the truce between the mountain and the apparatus. Behind every retaining wall, the original rock is still there, pressing water through the weep-holes. A fern root works its way into an iron pipe; mineral scale narrows a gutter; a patch-plate bolted over flaking paint begins to weep rust. I would spend hours crouched on slick cobbles, clearing the mouths of the weep-holes with the wire pick so the runoff runs clean, while leaving the thick, sodden cushions of moss untouched along the parapets so they keep drinking the rain and swallowing the clatter of the chains.

When a bearing somewhere along a conveyor bridge begins to sound dry—not loud enough to hear over the spillways, just a slight tightening in the vibration under my boots—I would press one end of the wooden dowel against the iron housing and the other to my ear, listening until I found the grit in the hum, then oil it and chalk the date onto the beam.

I would not be solitary, though I might walk a mile of dripping catwalks without speaking a word. The valley’s company is mostly oblique. It is the pale, heavy-bodied moth clinging motionless to the warm wire cage of a sodium lamp at noon, conserving its strength like a scrap of ash. It is the blackbird slipping out of a thicket of roof-ferns beneath a walkway, letting out a short, low whistle so close to the bleed of a hydraulic valve that you only know it’s alive because it hops sideways along a pipe. On days when my route takes me down to the lowest drainage tiers, I would pause by the settling-tanks to watch the slow, slate-colored eels grazing the algae off the intake screens, careful never to drop anything harsh into their tepid water.

And when the damp begins to settle into the joints of my hands, I would take the steep service stairs up toward the glass-paned cultivation halls—not to stay, just to step through the airlock doors, peel off the dripping canvas coat, and stand for ten minutes in the lukewarm, loamy air. There is no rush there. The elders and the children move quietly between the raised beds with their squirrel-hair brushes, pollinating the blossoms by hand while the mist presses pale against the streaked glass. Sometimes I would trade a dry, chalked note about a shifting retaining wall—something too detailed to tap onto a rail—for a jar of pickled fern-shoots or a bundle of dried leaves for tea, tucking it into my satchel or clipping it to an empty return-skip with a grease-penciled slate tag so it reaches my own tier before I do.

By late afternoon, when the crates of harvested greens begin gliding overhead and the sodium lamps brighten into wet halos in the fog, I would climb back to my spandrel pocket. Coat hung over the floor-gutter to drip back into the valley’s drainage; woollen sleeves still smelling faintly of wet fern and warm glass. Sitting on the edge of the bed with a tin cup of boiled-leaf tea, listening to the soft, steady hum of the transformers through the brick, I wouldn’t feel like I was running anything, or hiding from anything. Just one small, quiet joint in a world that knows how to keep itself going in the clouds.
