I would not be down on the catwalk gratings, where the iron is worn smooth in narrow tracks by feet that stopped coming a long time ago. Nor would I be down in the damp pockets around the catch-basins, scraping at the mineral rime with a porcelain shell.

I would be high in the upper tiers, where the air is still fierce enough to punish haste, but where the great bundled return-lines run like cool, dark rivers through the glare.

To find myself there, I would first have to unlearn the habit of eyes. In this chamber, eyes are only good for flinching at the rare, cherry-red smudges behind soot-caked mica—useless little blisters of light that tell you nothing about where the scorching manifolds sit, or where a three-inch gap opens between a brick pier and a pressure-vessel. Instead, I would wake into a world read entirely through the crescent of deep, velvety pits across my face: a blindingly sharp architecture of heat. The main lines burn like white-hot pillars; the brick arches glow with a dull, grainy warmth; and overhead, the uninsulated copper return-lines hang like ribbons of quiet shadow.

I would be one of the climbers—flattened from side to side, built for the seams and clearances of a room never meant to be lived in. My weight would never touch the catwalks. Instead, I would hang suspended in the rigging, four long, backward-hinging limbs folded like calipers, my wide, leathery foot-pads wrapped around the curve of a pipe with a dry, quiet suction that leaves no mark on the velvety magnetite.

Most of my hours would be spent motionless, draped lengthwise along a sweating return-line. Pressing my pale belly against the copper, I would let the metal draw the accumulated heat out of my core, hoarding the chill the way a lung hoards air. Above my throat, a verdigris-crusted brass elbow would bead with moisture from the stagnant room. Before a drop could fall and *tink* into the ceramic gutter far below, I would lay the pleated pouch of my throat against the brass and take it in—mineral-heavy, tasting faintly of damp clay and scorched almonds.

Time here does not move in days or seasons; it moves in the sub-audible thrumming through the iron, and in the glacial stroke of the tree-trunk piston-rod at the center of the clutter. Through the pads of my feet, I would feel it rise—an inch every quarter-hour—until, high in the dark, a weighted brass lever tilts, a hidden valve sighs a brief plume of vapor, and the slow descent begins again. Between strokes, a pipe *pings* as it shifts a fraction of a millimeter in its brackets. Down near the catch-basins, a pale cricket answers with a single, dry chirp.

Every few days, or weeks—it makes no difference which—hunger would draw me down toward those muggier pockets near the floor. There is no hurrying the descent; to hurry in this air is to cook from the inside out. One limb reaches, pauses to test the thermal shadow of a flange, and settles; the body follows an inch at a time, my salt-dusted, mineral-plated back making a dry, papery rustle against the brick. Down in the dampness, I would graze quietly on the mats of black moss, nip the pale roots wedged into the mortar, and press my hard-rimmed mouth over a blind snail, crushing its porcelain shell with a single, sharp *click* that sounds, in the hush of the vault, just like the cricket’s chirp.

It is a solitary life, but not an empty one. Now and then, when the pipes are quiet between strokes, I would lift one calcified knuckle and tap it once against the copper line. The vibration runs cleanly through the packed labyrinth—past the valves, past the ceramic insulators, across fifty yards of stagnant heat—until, a few minutes later, a faint, dry *ping* answers back through the soles of my feet. Just an acknowledgment in the dark. *I am here, and the balance holds.*

And if I met another of my kind in the narrow gap between a pier and a pressure-vessel, there would be no crowding, no haste. We would pause face-to-face, our broad, blind brows hovering an inch apart while our thermal pits read the faint, careful warmth of each other’s flanks. Then I would flatten myself against the brick, letting the other thread slowly over my travertine-plated back, and we would part again into the glare.

I would live a very long time there—decades, perhaps, paced by the piston’s stroke and the slow *tink* of water. With every year of mineral-laden condensation, the pale plates along my spine would thicken a little more; the lime dust from the mortar seams would settle deeper into my creases; my caliper joints would grow stiffer, making the climb down to the catch-basins rarer.

And when the stiffness finally outweighed the hunger, I wouldn’t fall, and I wouldn’t leave a mark on the catwalks below. I would simply find a cool, damp angle behind a ceramic gutter, tuck my long limbs tight against my ribs, and stop. Over the years that followed, the slow seepage from the brick would wash over me, laying down fresh curtains of pale, waxy travertine across my back, until there was nothing left to tell where the sleeper ended and the wall began—and the vault would go on, ticking softly in the dark, needing no one to watch it.
