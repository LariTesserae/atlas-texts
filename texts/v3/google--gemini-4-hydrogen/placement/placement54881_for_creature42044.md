I would not find myself near one of the hollow plinths, or beside a cistern whose water runs uphill. Those places have already made up their minds, at least for an hour.

I would wake—or simply begin to be there—out on one of the long, bare plazas between structures, where the slate is laid so flat and tight that the seams look drawn with a stylus. Around me, the amber-grey dusk would hang low and motionless, smelling of wet lime and cooling iron. No wind, no horizon, only the tepid condensation beading along my sleeves—if I had sleeves—and pooling in the shallow, palm-sized basins pressed into the floor.

At first, I think I would be nothing more than an attention: a pair of eyes where no eyes belong, measuring the crispness of a chisel-mark, listening to the distant, muffled *clack* of a ceramic peg dropping into place miles away in the mist. Because this is a world that invites looking without offering anything to hold onto. If you reach out to steady yourself against a waist-high clay wall, the wall is damp and sharp-edged, and if you look back ten minutes later, it may have stretched itself into a causeway or slumped into a smooth grey apron of paste.

So I would have to learn what I am by noticing what the floor does beneath me.

I am not heavy enough to un-key the flagstones, and I am not made of slip. When I walk—and I find I *can* walk, though the effort of twenty paces sometimes carries me five yards and sometimes half a mile—my soles make no sound on the wet slate, or only the faintest *tact* of something dry touching something slick. I leave no scuff marks, no muddy tracks. Nothing here wears down, not even me.

Yet I am not invisible to the place. Beneath the thin film of water, the slate knows the difference between its own kiln-heat and mine.

My warmth is wrong for this continent. It does not flare up when the steam rolls in, and it does not ebb into the stone when the mist clears. It is steady, small, and stubbornly contained—the warmth of something that keeps its own time, rather than the exothermic fever of mortar trying to cure. Wherever I stand for more than a minute, the condensation on the flagstones around my feet dries back by a hair's breadth, leaving a faint, pale halo of salt-free dampness that vanishes the moment I move on.

That is what I would be here: an anomaly in the level. A small, mobile dry-spot. A visitor who refuses to take a set.

I would gravitate toward the unstable places—not out of courage, but because the rest of the plain is too patient for me. I would follow the faint, rising warmth through the soles of my feet until I found a sector of slate beginning to dome upward, its slabs neatly parting to show the pale, weeping brick beneath. I would kneel at the edge of a freshly un-keyed seam, peering down past the fitted stone and the damp plaster into that colorless void that smells of hot iron, waiting to be tiled over.

And it would be there, kneeling beside the seam, that I would finally meet what lives in the floor.

I wouldn't hear it coming over the sound of my own breathing—if I breathe here, it is only to taste the mineral air—until the steam behind me thickened and went strangely still. Then the quiet *shhh* of a broad, wet lobe sliding across the stone. The sharp, clean smell of slaking lime.

I wouldn't run. Where would you run to, on a plain where distances disagree with your legs and every plaza looks like the one you just left? Instead, I would stay very still and let it find my outline.

It has no eyes, so it would know me the way a mason's float knows a wall: by resistance, by temperature, by the crispness of an edge. When its mineral-grey flank pressed against my knee, and then against the flat of my open hand, it would be warmer than I expected—hot as a hearth-stone an hour after the ashes are swept—and slick with the fine, concentric ridges of its underside. Inside that thick rind, I would feel the sluggish shift of paste, the faint, hard *tink* of a scavenged copper pin settling against bone-cold ceramic.

It would press hard, driving the tepid water out from between us, trying to take my measure against the floor. Trying to hold me there until my strange, un-curing warmth gave up and left a clean, sharp negative of my palm in the grey slip—a thumbprint with fingers, a shape that had no business on a continent without hands.

And in that long, dim quiet, while it held my hand against the wet slate, I would understand what I am in this world.

Not a builder. The world builds itself, and tears itself down, without needing anyone to draw the plans. And not an inhabitant, either; I will never flatten into a puddle of slip when a steam-front passes, and I will never cure into a hollow plinth on a cool stretch of stone, waiting for the next heave of the plain to wake me.

I am the witness that carries the impression away.

When the creature finally softens its leading edge and slides back into the seam—leaving behind a damp, mirror-still basin shaped faintly like the heel of my hand—the basin will only last until the next arcade slumps and screeds itself flat over the spot. Nothing here keeps its shape for long. Every corner is sharp, and every corner is temporary.

Only I, walking out into the bruised, amber-grey haze where the horizon refuses to curve, would still hold the memory of the ridge-marks on my palm, the *clack* of the ceramic peg, the uphill slip of water over a cistern's lip. I would be the one thing on the plain that does not slake and does not harden—a quiet, walking record of a world that keeps forgetting what it just made.
