I would not be in that upper chamber—not for long, anyway. Nobody with lungs and teeth could live there.

I would be two levels below it, in the small, dry pocket of the station that the blueprints call *Auxiliary Switchgear & Watch*, though in practice it is mostly a desk bolted to the concrete, a kettle, and a bank of grey steel cabinets that hum at a steady, dry sixty cycles.

If the pressure-relief chamber up in the intake works is the station’s wet lung, this room down here is its warm, dusty pocket. There is no exterior louver here, no mountain mist drifting across the floor. The walls are the same young, razor-sharp concrete, still carrying the faint resinous scent of the timber formwork the crews stripped off last autumn, but down here the slab is bone-dry. The waste heat from the step-up transformers in the next bay bleeds through the partition wall, keeping the air at a flat, dusty sixty degrees. It smells of warm insulating varnish, ozone, and the wool socks I drape over the conduit runs to dry.

I am the off-season tender—the one who stayed behind after the heavy cranes were disassembled and the paving crews wound down the switchbacks before the first real snow. On the personnel roster down in the valley office, my title is something dry and hyphenated: *Hydro-Mechanical Watch / Junior Instrumentation*. What it actually means is that I live inside a mountain for two weeks at a time, keeping company with several hundred thousand tons of poured concrete and falling water.

My world is measured in rounds.

Most of the station runs itself, just as the engineers promised it would. Down in the main hall—a cavern blasted out of granite and lined with white-painted shotcrete—the turbines spin in their housings, turning the snowmelt’s long drop into torque and copper heat. You don’t hear them down here so much as you *wear* them. The vibration comes up through the rebar and the rubber soles of your boots, a deep, steady thrumming that settles behind your breastbone until you forget it’s there, the way you forget your own pulse. You only notice it when you step outside onto the cliffside catwalk for a smoke or a breath of thin, cloud-choked air, and the sudden absence of the floor’s trembling makes your knees feel light.

I keep a paper logbook with a grease pencil tied to its spine. Every four hours I walk the galleries. I check the oil sight-glasses on the governor bearings. I walk the long, weeping drainage tunnel bored into the mountain’s flank, where lime already hangs in pale, brittle threads from the ceiling joints like the beginnings of cave-work, and I read the piezometers that tell us how hard the groundwater is pushing against the concrete.

And twice a shift, I have to go upstairs.

I always prepare for it the same way, standing at the bottom of the steel cage ladder in the stairwell. Foam earplugs rolled tight between my fingers, seated deep until the world goes muffled and oceanic; then the heavy yellow ear-defenders clamped over my skull. I zip my slicker to the chin, pull on my rubberized gauntlets, and climb.

When I un-dog the heavy steel hatch at the top and heave it back against its stops, the sound still hits me, even through two layers of protection—not in my ears, which are sealed, but in my molars, my ribs, the hollows behind my eyes. The cold mountain air pours down from the high louvers and slaps my face. The jaundiced cones of the bulkhead lamps turn the drifting mist into something thick enough to slice.

I never stay more than two minutes. I time my entry by the vibration in the ladder rungs: I wait until I feel the sharp, twenty-second shudder of the bypass pulse finish, and the higher, brief tremor of the sump pump cut out. That gives me a little over three minutes of relative calm—if you can call a chest-rattling roar calm—to step across the slick, sloped slab, shine my flashlight onto the pressure gauge beside the blue cast-iron manifold, and check that the dull amber eye on the NEMA cabinet is still lit.

During my first month up here, I didn’t see it at all. The room is loud enough to flatten your attention; you look at the manifold, you look at the gauge, you look at the grating to make sure you don’t slip, and you get out.

The first time I noticed it, I thought the formwork crew had left a slump of uncured mortar high in the windward corner, and I made a mental note to bring a cold chisel up next week and knock it down before it broke off and clogged the sump.

The next week, it wasn’t in the corner. It was draped over the upper flange of the left-hand surge pipe, its heavy, wet-slate flank pressed flush against the iron.

I stood there in the mist with my flashlight beam trembling slightly in my hand, watching it do nothing. Through the heavy yellow cups over my ears, the room sounded like the inside of a waterfall. I watched a single drop of condensation gather on the overhead I-beam, fall, and strike the thing’s smooth, concave ridge without making it flinch.

I didn’t bring the chisel up. I didn’t write it in the logbook, either.

Down in the valley, the regional engineers think of a station like this as a finished object—a set of drawings made solid, bolted to the granite, sealed against the mountain. They don’t spend fourteen-day stretches alone in the dry hum of the switchgear, listening to the building settle into the rock. They don’t see how quickly the mountain starts taking back the margins: the lime weeping through the brand-new joints, the pale, thread-like moss already tracing the seam beneath the high louver, the way the air itself seems to thicken in rooms where nobody speaks.

So I let it be. We have an arrangement, though neither of us could name it.

When I un-dog the hatch now, I don’t sweep the flashlight into the corners anymore. I keep the beam low, on the wet concrete and the gauges. Usually it is already settled beside the NEMA cabinet, soaking up that faint, dry ghost of electrical warmth, or coiled high in the shadow of the unused chain hoist. Once, I misjudged the four-minute cycle—my watch was slow, or I had lingered too long checking a weeping flange on the sump pipe—and the pneumatic actuator twitched above my head.

I flattened myself back against the steel hatch just as the white, aerated fan of high-pressure water blasted sideways into the stilling-well. Cold spray billowed across the chamber, soaking my slicker in an instant. And through the driving mist, in the jaundiced light of the corner lamp, I saw it unfold. Those wide, translucent membranes—ribbed with pale mineral scale, looking like wet parchment or tattered fog—opened out from its heavy sides to drink the gale. It didn’t look frightening. It looked like a sail catching a wind that only blew in one room in the world.

When the needle-valve snapped shut and the membranes folded back into a dripping, concrete-grey mound, I let out the breath I’d been holding, wiped the spray off my flashlight lens with my thumb, and finished my check.

Before I step back through the hatch, I always shine the light once beneath the high windward louver, just to see if the pale-green moss is still holding its own against the draft. It always is. And sometimes, when the creature is resting just below it, I catch the faintest smudge of that same pale fuzz trembling along the ridge of its spine.

Then I pull the heavy steel door shut, throw the dogs until the gaskets bite, and climb back down the ladder into the warm, dry sixty-cycle hum—leaving the room to its four-minute breath, and its quiet, mineral tenant.
