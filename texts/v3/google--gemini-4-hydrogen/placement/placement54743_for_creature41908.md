I would not be down on the flat felt with the Listeners, heavy with silvery ash, nor would I be among the eldest Tuners patrolling the foundations with their forked staves of burnt cedar. 

If I look for myself in this place, I find myself twenty-four stories up, sitting on the severed lip of a spire where the building stops.

Six feet to my left and slightly higher, the upper third of the tower hangs in open air—a hollow crown of pale pewter lignin, no thicker than an eggshell, drifting a fraction of an inch with every shift in the crosswind. Between its jagged base and the rim beneath my feet, there is nothing solid at all. Only a roaring, translucent column of compressed atmosphere and pale blue static, smelling sharply of ozone and warm chalk. 

This is where I belong: at the break.

---

My body is built for the gap, not the ground. Seven and a half feet of fluted, hollow struts and overlapping plates of sun-bleached bark-chitin, light enough that if I unhooked the two copper-stone plumb-weights from my hip, a sudden gust off the plateau would peel me off the ledge like a dry leaf and tumble me miles out over the grey felt. My joints—braided plant-sinew packed with fine graphite dust—leave faint, dark smudges on the papery rim every time I shift my weight.

I do not have a face to turn into the wind. My head is a smooth, narrow husk of river-worn bone, studded along the brow with matte-black mineral lenses that drink in the bruised slate-amber twilight without blinking. Inside my ribbed lantern-chest, behind slats of translucent parchment, a slow blue flicker snaps back and forth across pleated vanes, cracking the drifting mineral dust I breathe into pressure and pith.

Down below, three hundred feet beneath my dangling heels, the plateau booms like a struck drumhead. The deep, groaning bass of the wind-pipes rises up through the hollow stalk of the tower, layered with the high, thin whistles of the breathing-pores and the brassy, overlapping hum of the neighboring districts. Overhead, a flock of *clackers* wheels through the gloom, their parchment wings catching the dim amber light, their mandibles rattling in rapid bursts that sound like dry hail against the tower's skin.

Out here, the air is so loud it feels solid. If I tried to speak into it, the wind would shred the sound before it left my throat—not that I have a throat meant for air.

Instead, I keep one palm pressed flat against the vibrating lignin of the lower stalk, and when I need to speak to my partner—who works the underside of the floating crown across the gap—I don't shout. I step straight off the ledge.

---

There is no falling here, only a sudden, violent buoyancy. The roaring column of compressed air catches my hollow ribs, lifts me like dry chaff, and carries me across the six feet of nothingness. Blue static crackles across my bark-chitin plates, stinging my internal vanes awake with a rush of copper-tasting charge, before depositing me lightly onto the invisible shelf of folded space that rings the crown's base. 

My partner is already anchored there, curved toes hooked into a woven rim of plant-chitin, matte-black lenses fixed on a hairline shimmer in the air. I crouch beside them, hooking my own toes into the weave, and reach out.

We press our forearms together—bone to bone, notched lignin ridge to notched lignin ridge—and draw our keratinous spurs across the frets.

*Click-shiver-hum.* The message passes straight through our frames, bypassing the deafening roar of the plateau entirely.

*The crown is drifting sharp,* they tap against my radius, a rapid, woody trill. *The wind from the western rim is thinning. If it climbs another half-tone, the upper pods will shatter.*

I press my smooth husk-head directly against the floating crown's wall, letting the vibration travel through my skull-bone into the graphite-packed sockets of my jaw. They are right. The lower stalk—rooted in the groaning earth—is holding a steady, deep brassy fourth. But the disconnected crown above us has slipped out of harmony. It is singing too high, too tight, like a dry seed-pod about to split in the sun.

This is what I am: a **Seam-Mender**, a **Pitch-Bridger**. Neither a true Tuner of the deep matte-black anchors, nor a Listener of the underground borers. I live in the places where the city refuses to touch itself.

---

I unspool a length of conductive filament—harvested from the silver-shelled thread-mites that nest in the orbiting dust rings down near the building's base—and knot it around a loose *stasis-thread* trembling at the edge of the crown. The air here tastes fiercely of copper. Just inches from my knuckles, a dozen motes of chalky dust hang motionless, refusing to fall, caught on the edge of folded geometry.

Slowly, carefully, I lean back against the invisible shelf and pull the stasis-thread taut, twisting it a quarter-turn with a hooked tool of resonant ash until it catches a harmonic from the lower stalk across the gap.

For three heartbeats, the roaring column between the two halves of the tower fluctuates, turning a deeper, bruised violet. My chest-vanes flare bright blue with the sudden surge of triboelectric current. Then, with a sound like a heavy wooden lid settling onto a chest, the crown drops a fraction of an inch. Its frantic, brittle whine softens, folding back into the deep brassy hum of the stalk below.

Across my forearm, my partner's spur taps a single, satisfied chord: *True.*

---

I spend most of my hours like this, suspended in the cold, ozone-sharp twilight between the things that are whole. Sometimes, when the barometric pressure begins to drop and the domesticated *clackers* in their wicker balcony-cages start rattling their warning-patterns, I don't even climb back down into the tower's interior.

I stay right out on the exterior fluted ribs, thirty stories above the pale-grey felt, waiting for the squall.

When the living floor miles below exhales its ghostly, glowing sheet of silvery spores, and the tall collector-spires down the avenue flare with soundless distortion and begin to scream like boiling kettles, I unlatch the parchment slats along my ribs. I spread my chest-membranes wide into the vortex, gripping the eggshell wall with my hooked toes, and drink the dry, shimmering mist straight out of the air—feeling the silvery dust coat my internal vanes, feeling the tiny sparks snap and crackle it down into fresh nitrogen pressure, fresh lightness, fresh song.

Only once in a long while, when the wind drops to an uneasy, breathless lull and the pitch of the city settles into something almost quiet, do I climb down to the base and walk out to the far edge of the district—past the nervous herds of *static-grazers* sparking blue across the felt, toward the rim where the planed-off mountain ends.

I never step too close to the drop. I have seen the dry, rolled bark-scrolls in the archives, etched with the cautionary shapes of *wetness*—of things that fall from the sky and do not evaporate, of paste and mud and silent, heavy rot three miles below the amber haze. I crouch at a safe distance, my plumb-weights clicking softly against my hip, and press my heel once against the hollow ground just to feel the comforting, pressurized *thrum* of the dry pipes answer me back.

Then I turn, lean sharply into the rising wind, and skip back toward the humming, impossible towers—back up to the roaring blue gap where the air tastes of copper, and everything stays dry enough to sing.
