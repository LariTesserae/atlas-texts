I wouldn’t start on the open flats, where the refinery-grazers leave their steaming ribbons of slag, and I wouldn’t want to be miles up in the carbon haze, tethered to the earth by a single thread of silk. 

I would find myself in the margins—somewhere the plateau’s great currents slow down enough to eddy. A shallow hollow where an ancient, dry riverbed meets the edge of a fault-line sump. On one side, the still, black mirror of mineral oil; on the other, a damp condensation-basin bristling with copper-tinted wire-sedge and low mats of soot-black lichen. Above it, a weathered outcrop of basalt and graphite, close enough to a mineral spire that you can hear the air *tick* every few minutes when the charge builds too high.

It is a boundary place. Three different clocks keep time here: the deep, sub-audible thrumming of the quartz plates grinding far below; the crisp, chattering *tick-tock* of the ceramic beads bridging the sedge-stalks; and the thick, patient silence of the oil pool, where nothing moves faster than a film of grease settling over iron.

If I arrived here as I am—just a mind in boots—the first thing I would have to lose is the rubber on my soles. On the plateau, insulation is just another word for loneliness. You can’t understand a place built on direct current by standing outside the circuit.

So I let the world decide what shape it gives me.

At first, I’m tempted by the balancers—those twelve-foot tripods with their layered mica crests, walking the graphite seams in solitary grace, bleeding excess static into the bedrock so the world keeps humming smoothly. There is a quiet dignity in being the thing that keeps the machine from tearing itself apart. Or maybe an unfastener, prying open dead borers with cold-chisel limbs, sorting copper nerves onto bony spindles just to see how the parts fit together.

But neither of those is quite right. The balancers are too vast, too impervious; they don’t wonder at the current, they only ground it. And the unfasteners only know how to read things once they’ve gone cold.

If I stay in that hollow between the sump and the sedge long enough—listening, letting the warm ozone and the low hum soak into me—something smaller and stranger emerges.

I wouldn’t be built for weight, or for armor. No bronze carapace, no internal cherry-red furnace. A furnace makes too much noise; you can’t hear the *drip* of condensation into a basin when your own ribs are roaring.

Instead, I think I would be low to the stone—six-legged, perhaps, or multi-jointed like a flattened millipede, moving on narrow feet tipped in soft graphite so I never lose contact with the bedrock’s pulse. My back wouldn’t be matte-black insulation, which shuts the world out, nor heavy iron, which draws the lightning. It would be a loose, overlapping mosaic of thin, translucent plates: flakes of raw quartz, slivers of dark mica, and fine, tarnished silver threads woven between them. Each plate tuned to a slightly different pitch of vibration or charge.

I wouldn’t hunt, and I wouldn’t smelt. There is plenty to live on in the margins if you aren’t greedy. I would feed on the plateau’s leftovers—the stray millivolts that spill into the wet stone when a ceramic bead snaps shut; the lingering pocket of dry heat left behind in the basalt after a grazer rolls past; the tiny, sharp sigh of static released when a drop of dew finally grows heavy enough to fall from an overhang.

Because I carry almost no stored charge of my own, the siphon-hounds would pass right over me—there is nothing in me worth grounding, no pressurized conduit to puncture. Even the grazers would rumble past without noticing, and the tall balancers would step clean over my shadow.

And what would I *do* there, crouched in the warm dark between the oil and the sedge?

I would be a listener of a different kind—not a keeper of equilibrium, like the tripods, but a keeper of echoes. 

When a blind, tree-trunk worm shifts between the quartz plates three hundred feet below, the tremor doesn’t just pass through me; the quartz scales along my spine catch it and hum in sympathy. When a dry-static front begins to gather a hundred miles away over the far escarpments, the fine fringes along my mica plates lift and prickle hours before the first pale sheet of light illuminates the haze. When the sedge-beads chatter a warning down into their roots, I feel the rhythm of it in my graphite toes.

Inside my layered shell, all those stray signals—the tectonic, the atmospheric, the small and local—meet and interfere with one another, forming a shifting, living map of the plateau’s mood.

And maybe, because a circuit always wants to close, I wouldn’t stay in one hollow forever. When the oil sump settles and the sedge goes quiet, I would lift myself on my six graphite tips and walk slowly along the warm basalt, dragging a single, hair-thin copper filament behind me—carrying the faint, residual rhythm of one basin into the next, letting the stone in the east know what the stone in the west has been humming about. Not out of duty, and not to fix anything. Just to be part of the hum.
