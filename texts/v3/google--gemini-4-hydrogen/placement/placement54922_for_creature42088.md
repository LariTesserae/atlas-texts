If I arrived in the valley from above, I think I would spend the first few days standing motionless on a catwalk, simply trying to keep my balance. Not because the iron piers are narrow, though they are, but because the air itself has mass here. Coming down from the dry, whistling scrub of the rim into a roar that presses against the breastbone feels less like entering a city and more like wading chest-deep into a warm, moving river.

At first, I would try to hold on to my ears. Anyone from the dry world would—cupping a hand behind an ear, leaning closer to read a mouth that never opens, flinching at the hours-long exhalations of the vapor stacks. It would take weeks, maybe a season, to realize that straining for airborne sound in the basin is like trying to read by striking matches in noon sunlight. The light is already everywhere; you just have to stop squinting and let the skin take it in.

So I don’t think I would stay a visitor for long, and I don’t think I would belong at the center of the great pump-houses, where the newest steel gantries hum at full pitch. If I found my place here, it would be out toward the older, stepped margins of the tableland—somewhere like the lower terraces of Sector Six, where the ancient travertine drops away toward the fissures and the acoustic footprint of two neighboring clusters overlaps into a slow, beating interference.

Down there, the pace is even more deliberate than on the main flats. The water arriving in the settling pans has already traveled half a kilometer across sun-warmed stone; it is thick with suspended lime and lukewarm as blood. The machines in my corner would be the oldest ones—cyclopean masonry buttresses bolted clumsily to early cast-iron housings, their gaskets weeping a constant, steady bead of brine that has to be wiped and re-greased before it crusts over.

I picture myself as a tender who has been there long enough for the lime to know the creases of my knuckles, even through the lanolin. My hair would be clipped close under an oiled cap, my coat stiffened at the hem with pale mineral wash. And if I had a particular bent—the small, quiet specialty that every solitary worker develops over decades—it would be the tending of the *thresholds*: the places where the city’s vibrations change medium.

Every quarter, when the cable-car groans down from the rim, it brings bundles of seasoned ash poles from the lowland forests—wood that has never felt a standing wave, dry and light and acoustically blank. Someone has to turn them down, balance them, and seat the blunt copper ferrules onto their tips so they don’t rattle loose against the stone. That would be my evening work, back behind six feet of porous travertine where the roar breaks into a muffled, plush stillness.

To fit a sounding-rod properly, you have to listen with your teeth. I would sit on a low slate bench with a brass file and a pot of tallow, shaving the ash shaft down a hair’s breadth at a time, tapping the copper tip against the stone threshold of my doorway, and resting the butt of the staff against my collarbone. An untuned rod chatters; it blurs the signal, turning a dry bearing three hundred yards off into an unreadable buzz. When it is right, the wood disappears between your palm and the girder, and the distant turbine sits right behind your molars—clean, round, and faintly salty on the back of the tongue.

During the bright, unhurried hours of daylight, I would walk the catwalks of my sector with one of those staves in hand, scraping the pale scale from the throats of the copper diffusers before it could choke their song. There is a deep, wordless satisfaction in watching a settling pan clear its throat. When lime builds up inside a perforated cone, the geometric lattice on the ankle-deep water goes ragged and confused, its crisp diamond ridges slumping into muddy ripples. You kneel on the wet apron, work the curved scraper around the copper lip, and peel away a crescent of chalky crust. Almost instantly, the standing wave snaps back into focus. The water locks into its patient, trembling grid again, and the fine metallic silt begins to drift downward like snow in a quiet room.

I think I would also keep the baffled cisterns beneath my buttress—the dark, warm pools where the blind, translucent amphibians graze on olive-black moss. In a world built of iron, limestone, and relentless pressure, there is something grounding about tending a creature that has no use for the machines at all. When I lower a fresh slab of moss-covered stone into the cistern, they drift toward my wrist like pale shadows, guided by the tiny displacement of the water. You can see their hearts beating in their bellies—slow, mineral-tinted pulses that seem to keep time with the hydraulic rams deep beneath the floor. Sometimes I wonder if they are the only things in the valley that truly understand it: deaf, unhurried, completely adapted to the warm dark.

By late afternoon, when the light turns flat and violet along the upper ridges, my work would be mostly looking. A tender learns to read the surface of the pans the way a sailor reads cloud-banks. A sudden stutter in the grid across Sector Four—a brief collapse of the water-lattice followed by its slow reformation—tells me without a word that a sluice-gate up-valley has been eased open, or that someone needs a spare gasket carried across the viaduct. I might answer by leaning my weight into the baffle-gate of my own nearest diffuser, sending a long, slow tremor rippling back through the brine, or by tapping three measured beats on the main steam-conduit with the brass heel of my boot.

At night, slung in a hemp-roped cot a foot above the slate floor, I would fall asleep to the quietest thing the valley offers: the faint, residual thrum of the aquifers, damped by felt curtains and six feet of stone. On the table beside me, the bronze bowl of brine would hold its miniature lattice, keeping watch while I dream. If the ridges in the bowl ever sharpened into tight, nervous diamonds, my body would wake before my mind did—reaching in the dark for the oiled wool coat, the brass-shod boots, and the ash staff leaning by the door.

And I know that if I ever had to ride the cable-car back up to the rim—to trade pressed silt cakes for ash poles and clear oil—I wouldn’t last an hour in the dry air. Standing up there in the thin wind, with nothing pressing against my ribs and nothing humming beneath my heels, I would feel as if the floor of the world had fallen out from under me. I would finish the tally as fast as politeness allowed, step back into the iron basket, and let the winch lower me down into the mist, waiting for that first heavy, familiar pressure against my chest—the moment the city catches you again, and holds you in its sound.
