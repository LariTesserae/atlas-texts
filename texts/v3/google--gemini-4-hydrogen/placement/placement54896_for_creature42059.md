I would not be in the back of the hollow, where the limestone sweats and the saucers hold their unbroken skins of water. That part of the room already has its keeper. Nor would I be out on the plain beyond the scree, where the wind has nothing to catch on and the light is too sharp to hold.

I would find myself at the seam between the two—wedged into a narrow fracture in the limestone just inside the slumped ridge of pale scree, where the hollow’s ceiling gives way to the open air.

I would be that single, low clump of wiry, silver-leafed scrub.

To anything passing on the plain above, I would barely look alive—just a knot of grey wood and pale, dusty foliage tucked low against the stone, as if the scree had grown a tuft of dry hair. My leaves are small, thick, and furred in fine silver down, built to hoard what little moisture I can draw from the rock and to turn away the harshness of the bounced glare. In this place, even the light arrives secondhand, ricocheting off the bleached gravel outside before it grazes my upper stems. It is never enough to make me tall. It is only enough to keep me going, adding a fingernail’s width of tight, pale wood to my rings each decade.

I have no eyes to see the flat dusk of the hollow below me, and no ears to catch the *tock* of the water drops striking the saucers at the back wall. Instead, I know the place the way a root knows it: by pressure, by temperature, and by the slow drift of dampness through stone.

My roots go deep into the crack that holds me, threading down through hair-thin seams in the limestone until they reach the zone where the rock never quite dries—where the warmth of the outer world is held like a banked fire. Down there, the stone carries everything. When a drop of water falls from the sweating seam at the far end of the hollow, I do not hear it, but the bedrock takes the tiny impact and passes it along as a tremor so faint it is almost indistinguishable from the rock’s own settling.

And I know the creature down-slope, though we have never touched in the way animals touch. Mostly, it is simply part of the hollow’s baseline weight—a slow, heavy warmth resting against the back wall, shifting so rarely that when it finally moves a limb, the faint vibration travels up through the floor and into my taproot like a distant, muffled footstep. Between its movements, I feel the long, tidal rhythm of its breathing: a slight slackening and thickening in the pocket of warm air trapped behind the scree, carrying the faint, mineral scent of wet chalk and old lime up to my lowest leaves.

Inside my older wood—buried under decades of narrow, quiet rings—there is a different kind of memory.

Just beside my crack is the sloping slab of limestone with the four shallow scoops pecked into its face. Long ago, before my lower branches grew this twisted and brittle, the heavy weight from the back of the hollow used to pass me. I would feel it coming up the slope: the slow, deliberate *thud-settle* of blunt feet finding the pecked footholds, the brief blocking of the night sky as it crested the scree ridge, and the scrape of chalk-dusted skin brushing past my outer twigs on its way out to feel the wind on the plain. Later, before the bounced light returned, it would come back down, leaving a fine veil of white mineral grit on my silver leaves.

Then, one decade, the climbing stopped. The four scoops stayed empty. No heavy shoulder brushed my branches on its way to the wind. I simply kept growing, millimeter by millimeter, closing over the old scrapes in my bark until they were gone.

Now, my work is mostly to stay put. I hold the crack together with my roots, and I take the stray gusts of wind that spill over the scree ridge, breaking them against my wiry stems so they die before they can reach the water saucers below. No birds land on me; no beetles climb my bark. There is nothing to hurry me toward bloom or seed, and nothing to strip me bare.

I simply keep the bright, dry edge of the room, leaning slightly inward toward the warmth, while the creature keeps the wet dark at the back—each of us matching our pace to the stone, and neither of us waiting for anything to change.
