I would not be on the surface. The glare up there—bounced from cliff to cliff of milky quartz until even the shadows are bright—is too exposed, too loud with light. 

You would find me three feet down, in the narrow, un-eroded gap where the living floor meets the raw stone.

Specifically, I would be suspended beneath a wide, stepped shelf of fractured quartz, where the mat of cork-barked creepers arches over a deep mineral seam like a low ceiling. Down here, the blinding white of the basin has been filtered twice: first into a thick, chartreusely-yellow haze by the translucent paddles overhead, and then into a dim, greenish-milk glow by the tangled lattice of roots and stems. It is the closest thing to night this valley ever knows. Below me, inches from my back, the stone is as sharp as the hour it cracked—no gravel, no dust to soften it, only freshly cleaved planes and a single, dark puddle of condensation water sitting heavy with dissolved mineral in the crevice.

I wouldn’t be standing on that stone. I would be hanging from the underside of the mat, my weight distributed across four points of contact, long flat-jointed limbs hooked easily into the fibrous weave. To anyone peering into the crevice from the side, I would look like nothing at all—just a pale, knotted thickening of the root-mass, matte and chalk-dusted, the color of dried pith.

That is what I would be: one of the basin’s quiet listeners.

Not a builder of monuments, and not a hunter—there is nothing here worth chasing, and chasing would only snap the hollow canes that hold the world up. My skin would be hairless and faintly waxy, coated in the fine white chalk of dried puddles so that even if a stray beam of reflected light slipped through the canopy, it wouldn't catch on me. My outer eyelids would stay open, shaded by heavy, pleated brows, while the inner membranes—frosted and translucent—remained drawn, turning the luminous dark into soft shapes of pale green and shadow.

Sight wouldn't be how I knew my home, anyway. I would know it through my palms and the sensitive ridges of my forearms, pressed flat against the base of a thick, fluted cane that rises straight through the mat into the heat above.

In a world without wind, that cane is a nerve running up into the sky. Hanging there in the smell of wet chalk and old pith, I would feel the whole valley's tiny, mechanical life vibrating through the wood. The faint, glassy *tick* of a taut paddle expanding in the midday glare thirty paces away. The slow, spongy *give* of a neighboring stem as a bone-pale lizard shifts its weight on an underside frond. The single, heavy *plink* of a condensation drop detaching from a waxy tip and falling into the cupped base of a rosette, the water slowly working its way down through the seams to replenish the warm, dark puddle beneath me.

I would spend most of my hours like this—still, suspended, conserving moisture, letting the muggy equilibrium hold me. Every few weeks, when the canopy above shifted slightly and starved my crevice of its drip, I would unhook myself and crawl—slowly, without snapping a single living shoot—up through a gap in the rosettes and out onto the springy floor. 

Even then, I wouldn't stay in the open long. I would move along the shaded undersides of the tallest paddles, my broad, horn-thick fingernails finding purchase in the bark seams, until I reached a shelf of bare stone where a cairn of quartz shards stood untouched. I might take a single sharp flake from the stack—leaving the rest balanced as tremor-bells—to score a hardened plug of resin off a dead cane, or press a shallow bowl of chalk-silt and sap beneath a heavier, wetter leaf. Once, maybe, I would have woven a low dome of living creepers and daubed it with resin to wait out a dry spell, before leaving it behind for the plants to swallow and seal. Now, I prefer the roots. The roots do not grow inward as fast as the creepers do, and the dark beneath them is steadier.

Most of all, I would be waiting for the knocks.

Hours can pass in the basin with no sound larger than a falling drop of water. It is a sparse life; the rock holds too little moisture for crowds, and we keep our distances, each of us tucked into our own seam of stone and root. Yet every so often, as I rest with my forearm pressed to the hollow wood of my cane, a different kind of vibration travels down from the mat—not the *tick* of heat, and not the *plink* of water, but a slow, deliberate rhythm coming from half a mile across the silent floor.

*Knock. ... Knock-knock.*

Soft, woody, unhurried. One quiet presence letting another know it is still there, still hanging above the sharp stone, still drinking from the dark.

I wouldn't rush to answer. I would wait until the echo of it faded back into the stillness of the carved-jade canopy. Then, lifting one broad, horn-thick nail, I would tap back against the base of the fluted stem—sending the small, steady pulse back out into the bright, motionless heat—and settle again into the luminous dark.
