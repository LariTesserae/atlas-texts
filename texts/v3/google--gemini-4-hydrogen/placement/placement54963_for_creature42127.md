I think I would be found three or four stories down from the glare, in the narrow, shaded chasms where the great corrugated-slate hoods meet the backs of the boiler housings.

Up on the cooling-tower terraces, the light is too plain and unblinking, and the wind off the black rock dries the skin to chalk. Down in the sunken sumps, the air is thick with sulfur and the blackness is total. I would belong to the seam between them: the damp condensation-alleys, where the uninsulated return pipes sweat steadily into chiseled basalt gutters and a waist-high mist smells of wet rust and hot ceramic.

I would be one of the tenders—though not one of the tall, sun-bleached ones who walk the high busbar gantries with their grounding-chains snapping in the dry air. My work would keep me in the shadow of the manifolds, where the skin stays dark with moisture and takes on that dull, oiled sheen. My chest would be narrow and deep, set to the half-minute rhythm of the altitude: one long draw of thin, metallic air, held until the last of it is wrung out, then let go in a soundless sigh that barely stirs the mist.

Mostly, I would be a keeper of seams and gaskets—someone who tends the places where the city tries to leak.

You can spend an entire cycle in a single alley without seeing another soul, only the two smooth, glassy ruts worn into the crystalline ash underfoot. My hands would be encased in pale, flexible gauntlets of cured glider-membrane, a braided copper strap trailing from my left wrist to clip onto every handrail before I touch a sight-glass or a valve wheel. Even down in the damp, the dry air above feeds a slow charge into the iron; forget the strap once, and a blue corona snaps from your knuckle to the brass, leaving the smell of ozone hanging over the sulfur.

My work would move at the speed of the vats. I would walk the narrow channels between the factory blocks with a flat scraper and a pouch of oiled rags, checking the riveted seams where the steam bleeds off. Where a pale, leathery filter-feeder clings flat to the underside of a condenser-manifold, I would step wide to leave it undisturbed, letting its segmented body drink the sulfur-sludge out of the runoff. Now and then, one of them finishes its span and dries into a papery, translucent husk, peeling cleanly away from the warm copper. I would gather those husks in my pouch—not with any hurry, just lifting them off as they loosen—and carry them back to my pocket behind the riveted iron doors, where the porous clay wall-tiles weep their slow threads of warm water into the dark. There, in the cool, dim quiet, I would rub the husks with oil and cut them into rings: low-pressure gaskets for the valves that sigh into the catch-pans.

If you stay in the alleys long enough, you stop listening with your ears. Sound dies too fast in the thin air; a dropped wrench two streets over is nothing but a dry *tick* with no tail to it. Instead, you learn to read the city through the soles of your copper-nailed sandals and the brass tip of your clay rod.

I would spend half my hours simply paused at a junction box, one gloved hand on the rail, the rod pressed to a sweating pipe, and the ridge of my jaw resting lightly against the warm iron. Through the frame of the refinery, everything talks at once, if you know how to separate the intervals. Beneath the steady, patient hum of the trickle-current plating copper in the lower vats, beneath the slow, measured arc of a quarry crane shifting a block of sintered slag, there is the deeper pulse of the shelf itself—the newly risen basalt still cooling, still settling by fractions of a millimeter against the weight of the bolted town.

And every so often, a sharper pattern rides the iron from three blocks away: *tick-tick, pause, tick*. A sticky valve in the third sump. A heat-exchanger fin crusted too thick with salt. I would tap back a single, damped ring to say *heard*, unclip my strap, and start down the stepped path at the same unhurried pace, letting the mist part around my shins.

Sometimes, when a valve housing is caked in white mineral scale, I would kneel beside the grate and pry the crust loose in brittle flakes. Down in the cracks between the tilted rock plates, where the noon glare never quite reaches, the flat, six-legged scuttlers wait out the heat, their silica-plated backs indistinguishable from the blistered pumice. As the salt-flakes drift down into the fissures, a few of those small, dull stones would unstick themselves and skitter sideways to feed, vanishing again before the next sigh of steam clears. I would watch them go without moving, holding my breath on the long upward count, feeling the whole basin hum beneath my knees.

I wouldn't climb to the rim often. Only at the end of a long cycle, when the copper plates in the lower yards are thick enough for harvest and the floor-vibration shifts toward the slower, deeper pitch of the night-crust cooling. Then I might walk up the narrow alleys into the plain, bone-white daylight, my inner eyelids flicking shut against the grit, and stand for a few minutes beside another tender at the edge of the terrace.

We wouldn't speak. There is nothing to say that the iron hasn't already carried. We would just lean on our clay rods, grounding-chains hanging still against our calves, and look out over the flat lid of cloud that buries the rest of the world—watching a long-winged glider hang motionless on the thermal off the black stone, before turning back down into the shadow of the pipes, where the copper is sweating and a gasket is waiting to be fitted.
