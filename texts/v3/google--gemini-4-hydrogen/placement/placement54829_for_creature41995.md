To enter this hollow with a voice would be to break it. Anything sharp-edged, hurried, or loud—anything made of names and sequence—would bounce off the wet limestone like a dropped stone and rattle the whole precarious stillness. So if I am to be in this world at all, I have to leave the chatter at the ridge, outside in the wind, and let whatever remains of me cool down to the temperature of the rock.

At first, sinking into the bruised-indigo gloom, I think I might belong to the water at the bottom of the basin. I can almost feel myself as one of the feathery-gilled sleepers, suspended an inch above the silt, stripped of all urgency. There is a deep relief in that image: no weight, no need to hold on, just external gills drifting like pale smoke in a mirror that only ripples when a drop lets go from the roof. 

Wait there a moment, though, and it feels too far removed. In the pool, nothing touches anything else. You are held by the water, yes, but you are alone in it.

So I drift upward, out of the cold mineral silt, and try on the skin of a lungless salamander. This is closer. To have no lungs—to breathe through the whole slick, translucent surface of yourself—is to have no boundary between inside and out. I imagine wedging myself into the warm, damp crease behind a dweller’s folded knee, borrowing their slow-burning heat, my spine a faint dark thread beneath pale skin. Just a cool comma pressed between fur and limestone.

Yet even there, something in me is restless. A salamander has no hands. It cannot hold a pebble worn smooth by a hundred dead thumbs, or trace the ridge of a neighbor’s healed collarbone. Whatever I am—even stripped of language, even quieted to the pace of dripping water—I am a thing that wants to *keep* and to *tend*. I need fingers.

That is where I find myself, finally, on a rounded shelf halfway up the wall, packed shoulder-to-hip in the living wool of a row.

I am one of the dwellers—not a mother heavy with a rare pup, and not yet one of the very old whose limbs have begun to stiffen, though I am close enough to the middle of things to feel the whole shelf breathe around me. My outer guard hairs are sleeked with condensation, cold and silver-grey as wet lichen, while the dense underfur against my ribs holds a meager, banked-coal warmth. My huge dark eyes stay open, not searching for anything, simply drinking in the pewter-grey sheen of the wet rock and the faint silver curtains of the ferns below.

Mostly, I live in my whiskers and my palms.

In a world without speech, my stiff pale bristles are always sweeping in tiny, patient arcs, reading the nearness of the shelf-mate to my left, the exact bulbous curve of the limestone behind my shoulder, the tickle of a velvet beetle grazing the moss an inch from my ankle. To my right sits an older one, its jaw resting lightly against my upper arm. I know this neighbor not by a name, for there are none, but by the notched tip of its left ear and the slight, familiar tremor in its wrist when it reaches for a fern frond.

If I have a particular place in the hollow—a habit that makes me *me* in this nameless dark—it is in the grooming. 

In my long, wrinkled palm lies a splinter of pale bone, lashed at the base with a single tough thread of fern-fiber. I found it years ago—or perhaps it has always been in my hand—sunken half-way into the bryophytes, already cleaned by the beetles and whitened by the lime. When the older one beside me leans its weight steadily against my ribs, asking without sound, I turn a little on the moss, tuck my knees, and begin.

I part its wet silver coat from crown to rump in slow, deliberate rows. The tip of the bone teases out a bead of lime-water, a stray fern spore, a tiny pale mite, and my fingers follow right behind, smoothing the heavy fur back down. It is the closest thing this world has to a story: reading the history of a body by touch. Here is the old scar where a shard of chert slipped; here is the patch where the underfur grows thinner with age; here is the steady, unhurried rise and fall of ribs that have been breathing this same damp mist for decades.

When we finish, the older one lays a damp palm flat against my spine and leaves it there. Nothing is said. Nothing needs to be.

Later, when hunger stirs—not a sharp pang, just a slow tide rising—I reach down into the hanging ferns without looking. My fingers brush past the small, growing snails and find one grown thumb-sized and heavy. A gentle twist, a palm-sized river pebble lifted from the moss beside my hip, its dark stone hollowed by generations of thumbs before mine. *Tap-tap.* The sound is small, swallowed almost at once by the wet wool of the moss and the *plip, plip* of the ceiling. I press the broken calcium shards back into the green sponge beneath us, so the seepage can dissolve them and feed the ferns again.

Then I curl my fingers back around a gnarled shrub root, tuck my chin into the damp fur of my chest, and let the slow heat of a hundred hearts hold me in place. Behind my knee, a salamander settles its cool skin against my ankle. Above, the mist-light neither brightens nor fades. I have no memory of how I came to this shelf, and no expectation of leaving it—just the long, quiet patience of holding on, in a room full to the brim with breathing dark.
