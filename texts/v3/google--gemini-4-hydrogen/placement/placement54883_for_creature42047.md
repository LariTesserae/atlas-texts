I would not be down in the hearths beneath the crust.

That is the first thing I know, hanging in the bright, cold blue. Whatever lives inside the freshly extruded fissures—those vast, patient architectures of tension that steer continental gyres the way hands steady wet clay—operates on a scale of geological hunger I do not have. I could not hold an entire ocean in suspended birth. I do not feed on cavitation booms or the slow, metallic groaning of rising basalt. If I tried to stretch myself across leagues of sixty-knot water, I would snap.

Nor would I be the warm, opaque visitor—the brief, soft knot of flesh interrupting the glass, measured by a single sweeping caustic and then released. Flesh has no business here; it is too loud with its own heat, too fragile against the shear.

So I let my eyes unfocus from the empty kilometers and look for where a smaller thing might survive without being torn to red mist.

I find myself near the bottom, though not touching it—never quite touching it. I am in the leeward shadow of a solitary silica spire, a needle of pale, unweathered glass rising like a broken tooth into the daylight. Just a hand’s breadth to my left, the sixty-knot river tears past with a continuous, chest-rattling roar. Where I hover, though, the water is uncanny and slick, parted by that invisible skin of held attention that keeps the storm from grinding the raw stone into sand.

At first, I think I might be a pocket of that stillness—a small, local eddy of chilled water, denser than the flood around it, bending the overhead light into a tiny, private caustic. A minor lens, left behind when a larger current was redirected.

But lenses have no memory, and I am listening too closely to be mere water.

Every few minutes, a deep *boom* rolls through the void from miles away, where a shearing current has torn itself apart. I do not eat the shockwave the way the holders do; I *read* it. I feel its sharp arrival against my edges, its clean, unmuffled reflection off the razor-sharp basalt ridges below, the way it sets the brittle crystalline fins trembling. In a sea with no silt to scatter the sound, every distant collapse arrives intact, like a word spoken across an empty cathedral.

That is when I realize I am not a lens at all. I am closer to the weed.

Not one of the hardy, rooted pioneers whose glassy holdfasts pierce straight through the frictionless sheath to fuse with the rock—those belong to the spire, singing the local trembling of the current down into the stone where the holders listen. I am what happens *after* a ribbon is torn loose.

I am a pale-green, translucent tangle—a streamer that broke free from some older spire leagues away and rode the self-correcting gyres until it snagged on a hair-thin catenary of tension, right here in the lee of a younger needle.

My holdfast is broken, crusted with a speck of solidified mineral plume that never quite fused to anything. So I hang suspended in the optical-glass blue, tethered to an invisible line of force I cannot see, whipping gently in the outer edge of the back-eddy.

It is a strange, bright station.

Every few seconds, tiny bubbles knocked loose from the churning silvered ceiling above come marching sideways along my filament in crisp, parallel ranks. They brush past my translucent skin and snap out of existence—*ping, ping, ping*—each one a tiny sip of charge taken by the vast architecture that holds me up. I am not the one taking the measurement, and I am not the one being measured. I am just the pennant tied to the line, translating the trembling of the flood into a quiet, continuous hum that has nowhere to go.

From here, I have a clear view of the unfinished world.

I watch the pale mineral plumes seep from cracks in the black basalt below and freeze instantly into delicate, surgical fins before the current can scatter them. I watch the geometric caustics sweep across the empty kilometers like searchlights, checking the water’s density, keeping the birth open, keeping the glass clean.

And when—rarely, improbably—something warm and opaque drifts through the bright void, breaking the geometry for a few heartbeats, I am the one who notices it linger. The holders will veil it in a brief pocket of stillness, veer a rank of bubbles around its ribs, measure its heat, and let it go, turning instantly back to their tireless, planetary work.

They have no use for a brief, soft knot.

I do. Suspended on my borrowed line of tension, whipping quietly in the roar, I catch the faint wake of its warmth as it drifts past, hold the vibration of it in my pale-green ribs for a moment longer than the stone would, and let it sing out into the clear, deafening blue until the flood smooths it away.
