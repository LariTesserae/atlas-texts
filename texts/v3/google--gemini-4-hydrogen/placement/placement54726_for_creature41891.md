Before I open my eyes, I know the hour by the frequency in my ribs.

The hydraulic reed two miles east is still on its long upstroke—a slow, heavy *thrum* that travels through the wet peat, through the matted flanks of the herd pressed around me, and straight into the hollow of my sternum. It has not vented yet. The air still smells of wet wool, copper, and cold sleet rather than sour steam. That means the ring has held through the night, or what passes for night when the pewter sky only dims from bruised to drowned.

I wake slowly, because waking fast in this place means cracking the seal where yesterday’s resin has stiffened against my shoulders. Beneath the overlapping amber-grey plates, my skin is pale and rubbery, still tender along the left collarbone where a stray arc from a voltaic mat caught me three cycles ago and peeled a strip off like boiled bark. I flex my chest until the plates give a dry, greasy creak, then push upward between two sleeping grazers.

They barely stir. Their six legs are tucked beneath them, broad copper hooves sunk deep into the black moss, grounding the static that crackles faintly along the wet tips of their fur. One of them lifts its heavy head, lets out a low, half-asleep brassy honk that vibrates in my jaw-valves, and drops its chin back onto the peat.

I pull my moss-sandals on, cinch the cured grazer-stomach pouches at my waist, and step out of the humming circle into the sleet.

The first thing I do—before I check the bone gaff slung across my back, before I test the tension of the root-nerve beneath my heels—is look down.

My shadow is there, pooled dark against the pale crustose lichen. The wind is blowing hard out of the north-west, driving sheets of wet sleet across my plates, and my shadow leans south-east, ragged at the edges, trembling in the exact rhythm of the gusts. Still mine. Still tethered. I let out a breath I didn’t realize I was holding, and my milky second eyelids snap across my eyes just in time as a voltaic mat half a league away discharges—a blinding blue-white stutter that turns the falling sleet sideways into a hovering, magnetized curtain before it drops.

I am one of the resin-clad, though if you asked me for a name, I would have to press my forehead to yours and hum it, and even then it would only be the pitch of my throat-sacs, which changes whenever the cold gets deep enough to thicken my chest. Among the others, I am known mostly as a *listener*—not because I have outer ears, which none of us do, and not because my jaw-valves are any sharper than anyone else’s. It is because I walk the margins where the turf-gears rise, and I have a knack for knowing when a reed is about to choke on a valve-clot before the sap-vessels even begin to groan.

It is not a title. Nothing here lasts long enough to warrant a title. It is just the way my feet read the mat.

I set out across the flat, weeping expanse, my gaff hooked over one broad shoulder. The moss squelches and weeps dark water with every step. To an eye that didn’t grow up under this bruised ceiling, the plateau would look empty—leagues of grey-black bog, a few distant silhouettes of towering bark-boilers venting pale plumes into the mist, nothing built, nothing permanent.

To me, the ground is crowded with signals.

I stop after a quarter-mile, slip my right foot out of its plaited sandal, and press my bare, calloused heel deep into the cold peat. Immediately, the world sharpens. Through the braided root-nerve, I feel the *thump-sizzle, thump-sizzle* of a second grazer herd trotting somewhere to the south, their copper hooves bleeding charge into the bog. Closer—fifty paces to my left, just beneath the pale crustose lichen—there is a faint, rhythmic draw, like a tongue testing a tooth. A circuit-hunter, waiting in the dark water, its copper tendrils spread wide. I make a mental note of the patch and step around it; a hunter won’t waste a full discharge on a resin-clad, since our plates insulate us well enough to leave it stunned and empty-handed, but even a partial arc tastes like burnt hair and leaves your teeth aching for hours.

Then, deeper down, beneath the ambient bass of the engines, I feel the *twist*.

It starts as a slow, sickening torque in the soles of my feet—like a wet rope being wrung out by immense hands. A kilometer-wide stretch of the plateau ahead of me is beginning to wind up.

I shove my foot back into the sandal, unhook my bone gaff, and start trotting *toward* the sound.

Around me, out of the mist, other shapes are converging. At first they look like weathered stumps of hardened bark loping through the sleet, broad-shouldered and heavy-chested, their amber-grey carapaces slick with condensing water. None of us shout; the rising, wet roar of the straining peat would swallow any voice at two paces. As I pass another figure—wider through the ribs than I am, her resin plates streaked black with fresh peat-ash—we slow just enough to lean in and knock our broad, resin-caked foreheads together.

*Hummm-thrr.* A low, twin-toned vibration passes straight through my skull, bypassing the sleet and the wind. *Good weight. Fast rise.*

I hum back, a half-step lower. *Deep sap. Watch the north tooth.*

We break apart and keep running.

By the time we reach the edge of the fault, the flat bog is already buckling. With a sound like a forest of wet timber snapping all at once, immense interlocking gears of turf and braided root heave upward out of the moss, grinding against one another in a slow-motion storm of dark mud and torn fiber. The heat hits us first—a sudden, greasy wave that melts the sleet before it touches our faces. Then comes the smell: thick, sweet, scorched sap.

From between the grinding teeth of the rising turf-gears, long, steaming ribbons of warm, amber resin begin to extrude, curling out into the freezing wind like molten taffy.

We spread out along the trembling lip of the bog, timing our steps to the twenty-minute churn. If you reach too early, the roots drag you in and knead you into the peat. If you wait too long, the tension snaps, the gears collapse, and the resin shatters in the cold like spun glass.

I plant my heels, swing the copper-sheathed bone gaff in a wide arc, and snag a thick, steaming ribbon mid-air. It is heavy and hot, smelling fiercely of pine-blood and burnt fur. I haul it in, stripping it off the hook with gloved, resin-hardened fingers and stuffing it straight into my stomach-pouch while it is still pliable. Another ribbon curls out to my left—brighter, almost translucent, extruded from deep in the root-nerve—and I catch that one too, tearing off a thumb-sized piece to press straight into my mouth. The rendered sap-sugars hit my tongue warm and bitter-sweet, flooding my chest with a sudden, greasy heat that pushes back the altitude’s chill.

Above us, the sky-plume of the nearest hydraulic reed suddenly changes pitch.

I freeze, my gaff half-raised, and clamp my jaw-valves shut.

Even through the closed valves, I feel it in my teeth: the rhythm of the reed has gone ragged. The *thrum* is choked, swelling tighter with every stroke. A valve-clot—one of the millstone-sized iron mollusks—has grown too large inside the pressurized throat, and it is about to let go.

I don’t have to warn the others; they feel it in their heels too. Without a word, we abandon the extruding ribbons and scatter backward across the flat moss, dropping flat onto our bellies and tucking our heads beneath our armored shoulders.

Three heartbeats later, the reed ruptures.

The roar is physical, a wall of supercooled steam and splintered bark that slams across the plateau and flattens the sleet into horizontal needles. The ground bucked beneath my ribs. Even with my milky eyelids snapped shut, I feel the blue-white flash of a dozen voltaic mats tripping all at once in the shockwave. Up in the scalding mist, the blind, lard-pale flyers are already erupting in a shrill, clicking tornado, swarming the wound in the bark before the stalk has even finished tearing itself apart.

And then, in the strange, ringing hush that follows a rupture—where the sound drops for a single second to mere thunder—something else happens.

I lift my head, blinking my second eyelids clear, and look out toward the ruined reed.

The horizon between the steam-plumes folds.

It is like watching wet paper crease down the middle. Three miles of flat, weeping bog simply compress into a shimmering, vertical seam. Through the fold, I see the same blue-white electrical arc flash twice, half a second out of sync. And standing on the ridge of a distant, collapsing turf-gear—suddenly no farther from me than a long throw—is a crease-walker.

It is lean, matte-black, drinking the pewter light so completely it looks like a hole cut in the mist. Its pale, vertical-slit eyes are fixed not on the ruined reed, but on the distant grazer herd I left an hour ago, where the beasts are honking in confusion at the shockwave.

Before I can even shift my gaff, the creature steps into the seam. It doesn’t run; it simply ceases to be on the ridge and appears, mid-stride, in the shimmering air at the edge of the herd. A flash of serrated forearm-spurs, a sudden cloud of steam and copper-scented blood—and then the air flattens back out.

The fold is gone. The leagues of empty moss stretch between us again, unbroken and flat.

Except, fifty yards from where the turf-gears are now collapsing back into weeping sphagnum, a shadow remains—detached from nothing, tall and thin and swaying violently in a wind that isn’t blowing.

I push myself up onto my knees, my pouches heavy with warm, cooling resin. Around me, the other resin-clad are rising too, shaking sleet from their plates, patting the fresh, pliable sap onto the cracks in their carapaces before it sets. None of us walk near the detached shadow. We give it a wide berth, circling back toward the flat bog, our heels already reading the next tremor, our eyes already scanning the mist for the dark, humming ring of a herd where we might crawl in, press our backs against copper-warm flanks, and sleep until the next churn.
