If I were to wake up in that chamber, the first thing I would lose—and the last thing I would miss—is the urge to walk.

In a place with no soil, no tracks, and thirty-meter drop-offs hanging over a roaring void, legs would only be a way to get in trouble. Eyes, too, would be a vanity. The bruised amber-violet bleed of the conduits is only there because the machinery happens to leak light when it works; it wasn’t made to be seen. To belong here, I would have to trade sight for pressure, and speech for temperature.

I wouldn’t want to be down in the basins at the bottom of the void. Those pale, flat forms pressed against the dark stone take the full violence of the falling water-lenses—a ton of warm water exploding directly over their backs, scouring them clean every few minutes. That is a life of pure endurance, of being hammered into wakefulness by the storm.

Nor would I want to be on the highest, narrowest ledges near the vaulted ceiling, where the rock is still cold enough to sweat and the air hasn’t yet picked up the rich, metallic warmth of the lower tiers.

I would find myself somewhere in the middle.

Specifically, I would be tucked into the recessed elbow of a fifth-tier overhang—just beneath the lip of one of those impossible, gravity-blind terraces, where the cantilevered stone meets the freshly cleaved, laser-vitrified wall. It is a dry-ish pocket out of the direct sheet of the rain, though the mist still hangs thick enough to coat everything in a skin of warm condensation. Right beside me, a utility manifold hums behind a grate, bleeding a steady, low-frequency vibration into the rock.

And what would I be?

At a glance—if anyone came down here with a lamp—I would look like a coiled length of dark-gray conduit that someone forgot to bolt down, or a rounded outcrop of wet basalt wedged into the corner of the balcony. I would be one of the sleepers: curled tight, roughly the size of a hunched human, my outer hide the exact matte-charcoal of the newly split rock, veined with thin, branching threads of dull copper.

But I wouldn’t be quite as bare as the others.

Because I have been wedged in this particular elbow for so long—or because the air here is a fraction warmer from the manifold beside me—the chamber’s aggressive greenery has claimed me. A thick curtain of moss has crept down from the underside of the terrace and rooted itself into the porous, mineral-rich crevices of my back. A few waxy-leafed ferns have found purchase in the curve where my shoulder meets the stone. I don’t shake them off; I have nothing to shake them off *with*. Instead, their hanging root-mats drink the condensation straight off my hide, and in return, they hide me even deeper in the dark, making me part of the terrace’s own weightless architecture.

When the chamber is between cycles, I am almost nothing at all. My cartilaginous frame is dense and heavy against the stone. The deep, fluted folds along my flanks—half gill, half acoustic membrane—stay shut tight. I don’t think in sentences; I only register the slow, rising heat from the floor-slabs far below.

Then the draft comes up through the grates.

It arrives carrying the sharp, green tang of crushed fern, the sting of ozone, and the faint, unmistakable copper-breath of a hundred other sleepers opening at once. My flutes part by a fraction of an inch. I don’t *breathe* the air so much as let it wash through me, tasting the whole chamber in a single, saturated intake: the sharpness of the young rock, the wet moss steaming slightly on my back, the steady hum of the house-sized valves cycling somewhere behind the walls.

Then comes the stillness before the break.

High above me, an invisible containment field is straining under the weight of a trembling water-lens. I can’t see it, and I can’t hear it yet, but I feel the subtle change in barometric pressure—a slight, tight fullness in the air. A half-second before the field gives way, every sleeper on the tier hunches a fraction of an inch tighter against the stone. I tighten with them. Even the moss over my back seems to flatten.

*Boom.*

The ton of water hits the basins thirty meters below, and the chamber turns into a giant bell. The shockwave doesn’t just travel through the air; it travels straight up through the sheer basalt walls and out along the weightless stone of the cantilevered terraces. It hits me like a fist against the chest, rattling the fluids inside my frame, driving dissolved copper and warm oxygen deep into membranes that were brittle and dry not long ago. Outside my alcove, the ferns whip downward under the gust of displaced air and snap back up, flinging warm spray across my hide.

And then, in the ringing, dripping quiet that follows the crash, I speak.

Three of those hair-thin, matte-black filaments—the ones spanning the misty void between the tiers—are anchored right into the recessed corner of my alcove, pressed firmly against the copper-veined ridges along my spine. Because I sit at a structural crossroads between the upper ledges and the lower basins, I am a kind of hinge in the dark.

When the sleepers high above me shift their internal pressure, I feel it first as a slow, heavy micro-tremor—a feverish pulse carrying the memory of wherever they came from: some dry, sunlit, or airless place where nothing ever rained. When the pale, flat ones down in the basins stir in the settling silt, their tremors come up the filaments faster and sharper, full of the cold shock of the deluge and the scouring of the stone.

I don’t have a grand message of my own. I just take those two temperatures—the slow, brittle ache from above, and the violent, wet wakefulness from below—let them mingle in the copper veins of my back, and warm my skin by half a degree to send a steady, rhythmic ripple back out across the void.

It is a slow conversation, carried on entirely in heat and shudder, undetectable to anything that listens for voices. But here in the warm, bruised dark, wedged beneath the impossible stone and wrapped in wet moss, it is enough. I am not the one who carved the cathedral, and I am not the one who switched on the rain. I am just one of the quiet knots in the web—soaking, softening, and passing the warmth along until the cycle finishes, and it is time to uncurl.
