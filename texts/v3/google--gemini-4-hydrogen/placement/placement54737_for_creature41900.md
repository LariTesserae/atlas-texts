I would not be in the mid-water. I do not have the ballast for it—none of that dense, heavy-boned certainty that lets the climber launch itself into the shear and bite a screaming pocket of storm-air before the currents tear it apart. If I let go of the stone, the room would spin me into the ceiling or dash me against a spire of fresh glass before I could orient myself.

I would find myself on the far side of the sunken nave, wedged into the narrow seam where a cold, rain-fed downdraft spills down the cliff face and collides with the warm, silt-thick murk rising from the floor.

It is a sharp, bitter boundary. Just above me, the bruised ceiling churns, and the water pouring off the roof still carries the metallic tang of high-altitude lightning and mountain ice. Just below, the thermal vents pulse copper-bright, loosening the chill into bath-warmth. I live in the thin, trembling hinge between the two—pressed flat against a ledge of pale rhyolite, tucked beneath an overhang of black obsidian that turns away most of the flying grit.

To belong here, I would have to be low, broad, and patient. Not a swimmer, and not a climber, but something closer to a limpet or an ancient, articulated chiton grown large enough to hold its own against a gale. My back would be a layered carapace of dull, ash-colored mineral—grown slowly from the dissolved silica and suspended ash in the water, chipped and pitted where stray gravel has struck it, never quite smooth. My underside would be pale, soft, and pleated, gripping the microscopic grain of the rock with dozens of small, tough-rimmed folds that seal me to the stone so tightly the shearing currents slide right over my spine.

Like everything else that lasts here, I would be blind. Eyes are only something for the amber murk to fool, and the detached shadows swimming backward against the current would mean nothing to me. My whole body would be an ear pressed to the mountain’s crown.

Out in the water, the sound is a wall—an absolute, concussive thunder that blurs everything into vibration. But sealed against the glass, my soft underside hears *past* the water. Through the solid obsidian, the room’s chaos sorts itself into a slower, deeper grammar: the tectonic groan of the peak settling under its own weight; the high, taut whine of a cliff face about to shear; the rhythmic *hum* of balanced basalt shards three ledges over, where the climber has persuaded gravity to pool and hold still.

I would spend my time tending that quiet.

When the downdraft eases for a breath, I inch along the ledge, grazing on the stubby, translucent knots of hair-like lichen that manage to root in the lee of the boulders. Now and then, a pebble knocked loose from above drifts sideways into my eddy—caught in the hesitant local gravity—and settles into the grooves of my shell until I am carrying a small, shifting crown of the mountain’s rubble on my back.

I know the stutters, too. You learn not to panic when the ledge beneath you suddenly softens, dissolves into a silent cloud of pale silt, and leaves you clinging to nothing for a heartbeat—because a heartbeat later, the fracture snaps back whole, vibrating furiously, and the delayed shockwave slams into your carapace like a fist. You simply flatten yourself, seal your edges, and wait for the stone to finish remembering where it was.

As for the climber—the ash-white thing with the broad-knuckled hands and the bone-ladders—I would never know what it looks like. To me, it would only be a strange, intermittent weather in the rock: the sudden, heavy *thud* of a palm driving a nautilus shell into a fissure across the nave; the faint, scraping cadence of a broken shell grinding a half-moon hollow into a lower ledge; the sudden stillness that spreads through the stone whenever it finishes stacking one of its impossible cairns and the loose gravel drifts into orbit around the peak.

It belongs to the air-pockets and the open dark. I belong to the cold seam and the glass.

Yet sometimes, when the thermal vents quiet for a fraction of a second and the downdraft thins, a different sound travels cleanly through the obsidian from the far wall. Not the mountain groaning. Not a slab shearing away. Just two sharp, deliberate knocks.

*Tap-tap.*

I do not know if it is asking who is there, or merely testing whether the wall on my side is still solid. I only know that the stone has offered a signal that did not get torn apart by the current.

So I shift my weight in the dark, lift the heavy, silica-crusted rim of my forward plate, and strike the black glass twice in return.

*Tap-tap.*

Then I settle back into my groove, letting the cold brine wash over my shell and the warm murk rise to meet it, listening through the rock as the echo fades—or waits, trembling in the fracture, to answer itself again.
