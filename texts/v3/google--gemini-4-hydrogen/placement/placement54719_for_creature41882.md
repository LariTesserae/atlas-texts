I would not be in the great turbine halls, nor down in the seven-story tenements where the boilers keep the frost off the brick. I would be out on the span—halfway between the smelter peak and the ridge where the drop-forges work—in one of the small, riveted-iron watch-boxes cantilevered off a viaduct piling.

It is the kind of place you only notice if you stare at the bridge for a long time. From a distance, it looks like a rust-stained junction box or an oversized transformer housing bolted to the stone. Up close, it is three paces wide, walled in double-plate steel packed with wool felt, and anchored straight into a sheer blade of pale quartzite that hasn’t seen a rounded edge since the mountain cracked open.

I am a Tender, though the others along the line don’t call me by any grander title than *the one at the third piling*. My job is mostly to keep the switch-points greased, watch the porcelain insulators for trouble, and listen to the rail.

***

A day here—if you can call it a day when the bruised twilight never brightens or fades—starts when the hammock stops swinging.

It is slung from the main iron tie-rod that holds my box to the cliff. When I wake, I lie still for a moment, letting the low-frequency thrumming of the power grid settle into my ribs. You don’t hear it anymore after your first winter at this altitude; it just becomes the floor beneath your thoughts. Above my head, condensation beads along the ceiling rivets, patient and cold, until a drop grows heavy enough to fall onto my oilskin blanket.

Before I swing my legs out, I reach for the brass plumb-bob hanging from a nail beside the tie-rod. I don’t drop it to see if the world is straight—nothing at the third piling is straight—only to see which way the iron is pulling this morning. Usually the braided copper cord hangs true enough. On heavy-current mornings, when the smelters across the chasm are drawing hard, the brass cone leans a fraction of an inch toward the main viaduct pier, drawn by the mass of the steel.

I dress slowly: slate-colored felt underneath, then the heavy canvas coat stiffened with tallow and coal-tar until it stands out from my shoulders like a dry hide. The lead weights sewn into the hem knock softly against my calves as I lace my boots. They are good boots, thick-soled and iron-shod, with fresh steel cleats bolted into the heels. Last week I clipped a freshly cleaved shelf of basalt on the lower ledge, and the rock took a clean curl of leather right off my toe without dulling itself a bit. My knuckles have the usual white nicks from tightening turnbuckles in the dark, sealed over with black pitch so the damp doesn’t get into the cracks.

Breakfast is a tin cup of mineral-thick ungulate cream, warmed over the resistance coil I tapped off the junction box, with a pinch of dried pale lichen crumbled into it. While it cools, I open the heavy iron shutter and look out across the mile and a half of empty air.

The fifty-foot flywheel on the turbine ridge is turning. I count the spokes in my head—one, two, three—watching it take its full half-minute to come around. Down below, in the dimness where the viaduct’s shadow pools *upward* against the belly of the steel trusses, a cloud of cliff-swallows bursts out of the girders, chattering like dry gravel in a tin pan. That means the morning ore-buckets are moving along the greased cable overhead, creeping out of the mist from the smelter chimneys.

***

Most of my work is waiting, and the rest is keeping things in order when the air forgets how.

Out on the catwalk beneath the rails, I walk with my knees slightly bent, cleats biting into the wet iron grating. The velvet moss grows six inches thick wherever the transformer housings leak a little warmth, and I scrape it back from the switch-blades with a flat iron spade, tossing the damp clumps down to the ledge where a pair of six-legged ungulates are waiting. They don’t thank you for it; they just stand there on a slant of quartzite that would send a lowland goat tumbling into the void, their split hooves locked onto the razor-clean stone, chewing with a slow, sideways grind of their jaws.

Then I check the insulators.

This is the quietest part of the work. Along the thick bundles of copper that sag beneath the trestle, the chitinous cable-climbers are out in force, their pale, multi-jointed bodies pressed flat against the warm porcelain. As long as they are clustered there, soaking up the heat and clicking softly to one another in the dimness, I know the current is true and the insulation is dry. If I ever walk out and find a porcelain bell bare—the climbers all skittered off into the shade-ferns—I’ll know the line is cracking somewhere in the mist before any gauge in the turbine hall registers a drop.

Around mid-shift—or what passes for it—the freight train comes across from the smelter peak.

This is where an outsider would lose their mind, and why the Tenders keep someone stationed right at the third piling. Across the chasm, the drop-forge on the far ridge is working. Three seconds *before* the giant hammer actually drops, the *thunk-hiss* of the strike arrives across the open air, bouncing off the quartzite spires until it hits my iron wall from three directions at once. You learn not to flinch. You just wait for the hammer to catch up with its own noise.

The train is worse, if you trust your eyes.

I stand by the switch-lever, my brass bob hanging steady from my belt, and watch the locomotive chug out of the mist onto the near span of the bridge. At the same moment, looking past the third piling, I can see the red lamp of the brake-van already sliding into the far tunnel on the opposite cliff. For ten long seconds, the train is stretched across its own sequence, arriving before it has finished leaving.

I don’t throw the switch by what I see. I kneel on the wet grating, press the handle of my heavy wrench against the cold steel rail, and put my ear to the iron.

Through the stone and the air, time tangles itself like wet thread. Through the steel rail, it runs straight. I feel the *clack-clack* of the front bogies, then the heavy, measured roll of the twelve ore-cars, each one distinct in the vibration of my teeth, and finally the lighter skip of the brake-van clearing the frog. Only when the rail goes back to its steady, low-frequency hum do I pull the lever and chalk a clean white mark on the slate beside my door.

***

I am not quite alone at the third piling.

Down below the watch-box, wedged into the angle where the massive steel pier meets the unweathered cliff, the darkness is thicker than the bruised twilight around it. The amber floodlight bolted to my eaves doesn’t push that shadow away; instead, the shadow leans *toward* the heavy iron of the piling, pooling against the rivets like spilled tar.

There is a Shadow-Sitter there. I have never seen its face—I don’t think it has one that an amber lamp could catch—only the crouched, lichen-draped curve of its back, like wet slate and old soot, rising and falling in a breath so slow it makes the fifty-foot flywheel look hurried.

It was there before the watch-box was bolted to the rock, or so the older Tenders say. When I grease the switch-plates, I always pare off a thick curl of tallow and drop it over the railing into the dark corner at the base of the pier. By the time I come back from checking the far insulators, the tallow is gone. We don’t speak. It doesn’t tap the rails. It just presses its flat, cool back against the humming iron, soaking up the weight of the viaduct and the faint warmth of the copper bundles, keeping the piling company.

***

At the end of the shift, before I crawl back into the hammock, I sit on the iron step outside my door with my wrench across my knees.

Down in the narrow space between the pier and the cliff—one of those frayed pockets where the wind never reaches—the cold condensation detaches from the rock in silver beads and drifts lazily upward, gathering in a trembling, upside-down puddle on the underside of my catwalk. A pale Ceiling-Eel uncurls slowly through the inverted water, blind and boneless, undulating like a ribbon of kelp in a quiet tide pool.

Somewhere two miles off, across the slate-colored void, someone taps a wrench against a rail.

*Tap-tap. Pause. Tap.*

The stone doesn’t catch it; the echoes don’t tangle it. The message travels cleanly through three miles of bolted steel, vibrating right up through the soles of my iron-shod boots: *Line clear. Flywheel steady. Rest.*

I lift my wrench and tap it back against the rail beside my heel. Then I check my plumb-bob one last time, step inside, and let the world go on turning at its own glacial speed.
