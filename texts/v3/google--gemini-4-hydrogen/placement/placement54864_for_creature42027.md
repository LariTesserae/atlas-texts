If I were to find myself in this world, I would not come crashing down the root-bound bank in heavy boots, sending ripples across the tea-colored water. A place this old, and this precisely balanced, has no real room for a visitor. Anything that arrives from outside is either a disruption to be waited out, or debris for the pale beetles to scrape away.

To be here at all, I would have to belong to the rhythm.

At first, I wouldn't know what I was—only that the world had no edges, only pressure. The roar of the columns is too thick for ears; it lives in the chest, in the soft tissue of the throat, in the flat pads of the limbs. *Thump-hiss. Thump-hiss.* A steady, muscular shudder rising up through whatever I am clinging to, followed by the high, thin whine of the reed-turbines somewhere below, like an insect trapped inside a hollow gourd.

I wouldn't be on the floor. The floor is too open, too exposed to the slow, wading stride of the tall one, and the water there belongs to the blind loaches threading through the peat. Nor would I want to be one of the armored beetles, locked into an endless, orderly march along the seams, scraping and scraping at the bark until the end of an afternoon that never turns to evening.

No—I would find myself higher up, where the air is warmest and the haze turns the amber-green light into something almost solid.

I would be one of the slick-skinned amphibians, pressed flat against the underside of a hollowed vine-conduit as it arches across the empty vault.

It is a small, unassuming place to occupy in a room this grand, and that is why it fits. The bark beneath my belly is polished smooth by centuries of condensation, warm and slick, trembling violently each time a piston-column below drives another surge of groundwater up toward the ceiling. My toes—wide, wet, and splayed—hook into the faint ridges of the vine, holding on without thought, the way a root holds soil.

From here, I don't have to keep the circuit running. I don't have to watch the catch-basins fill, or mend a hide of cooling wax, or take the blue-white arc of the root-sacs against my shoulder. My only work is to hold my place on the pipe and let my throat pulse in tight, silent counterpoint to the *thump-hiss* of the plant.

And from here, I can watch.

Above me, the bloated gourd-valves swell and snap shut, venting jets of sweet, sap-heavy steam that drift down across my back like a warm breath. Beyond them, the broad leaf-membranes filter the same unremarkable daylight they filtered a hundred years ago—soft, diffused, shadowless. Clouds of copper-winged midges wheel through the updrafts just a few lengths away, their wings catching the light like dust in a barn, close enough that a single flick of a tongue could take one without disturbing the air.

Below me, across the wide, echoing emptiness of the floor, the tall steward makes its rounds. From this height, it looks even more like a piece of the vault that simply forgot to stay rooted—stooped, pale-plated, glowing a dull, waxy green in the haze. I watch it crouch by a resin-spout, its flat fingers scooping the mineral paste. I watch it stand motionless against a pulsing column while the beetles swarm over its shins. And when the bio-electric sacs at the base of the central trunk flare with a sharp, blue-white crack, I feel the vibration of the arc travel all the way up the vine-conduit into my ribs, and watch the giant shudder once from crown to heel as its fresh wax glazes hard.

There is no malice in it, and no companionship either. We are just two pieces of the same quiet afternoon, sharing the heat.

Still, I know what happens if I lose my grip. The vibration of the pipes never stops, and the condensation never dries; one careless shift of weight, one beat out of time with the surge, and I would drop through the amber haze into the ankle-deep water below. Then the stillness of the steward would break for half a second, and I would become part of the counterweight in a different way.

So I stay pressed to the vine. Slick, quiet, unblinking, breathing in the smell of boiled sap and ozone, pulsing in time with the engines—a small, wet witness clinging to the ribs of a world that long ago finished becoming itself.
