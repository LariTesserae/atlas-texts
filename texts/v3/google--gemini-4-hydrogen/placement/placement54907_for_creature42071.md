If I were to wake into this world, I would not find myself in the high, vaulted towers where the bats hang like dry fruit, nor down in the dark crevices where the great fig roots drink in silence. I think I would find myself in the wide, empty interval between them—out on one of the broad, scoured avenues where two quarters of the city almost forget each other.

Above me, the rim of the basin would be only a thin, ragged seam of pale gray sky, too distant to feel like weather. The air would be cool and completely still, tasting of chalk and old guano and crushed, papery leaves. Around me, the ruined masonry would have no sharp edges left, only smooth, grit-worn curves of dry-laid limestone that look as though they had been licked into shape by a river that vanished before memory began.

At first, the noise would seem like an enemy—that wall of wings and stridulation pressing against my ribs and teeth. Even here, in the open avenue, millions of voices bounce off the hard walls of the bowl without ever softening. 

And then I would realize I am not hearing it with my ears at all.

I would reach up with dry, chalk-dusted fingers and feel the smooth, hardened pellets of fig-resin and limestone dust wedged tight into my ear canals. The air is sealed out. What remains is only the weight of the sound inside my own bones: a steady, low-frequency hum rising through the horn-hardened soles of my bare feet, travelling up my shins, settling into the hinge of my jaw.

That is how I would know what I am here. Not a visitor flinching from the roar, and not one of the frantic, fluttering things that make it, but one of the dry, unhurried people of the basin—wrapped in shredded salt-cedar bark and bound with leathery creeper vines, my skin the same buff-gray as the plazas, carrying nothing that clinks or flaps or catches a draft.

If I had a particular habit, a particular corner of the equilibrium to keep, it would be these wide, lonely avenues between the quarters. 

Most of the people stay close to the great dryland figs of their own clusters, or the towers where they sweep the thickets for crickets and pluck torpid bats in the dim hours. I would be one of the ones who wander the open bedrock between them—not in haste, because nothing here requires haste, but with the slow, even stride of someone checking the joints of an old house that belongs to no one and everyone.

I would spend long stretches crouched on my heels at the edge of an empty square, my palms and chin pressed lightly to the scoured limestone, reading the basin the way others read the sky. Through the stone, the whole bowl is a single, taut drumhead. I would feel the faint, rhythmic throb of a bat colony shifting in a gallery half a mile away; the sudden, localized scatter of vibration when a rock-dove clatters off an arch; the tiny, sharp tick of a pebble rolling down a slumped stair. If a lintel settled by the width of a fingernail three terraces over, I would feel the shadow of it in my teeth.

And I would tend the slow things.

When a heavy tapestry of pale-green creeper begins to spill across an avenue, threatening to choke the passage or pry apart a dry-laid wall, I would kneel and saw it back with a wedge of knapped flint, guiding the tough tendrils away from the fragile joints. Where a terrace wall has begun to bow under the weight of centuries, I would search the dust for a flat sliver of limestone and tap it gently into the gap with the heel of my hand—not to rebuild the city, only to keep it from falling today.

Most of all, I would watch the dust.

In a world with no wind and no rain, a mark made in the fine limestone grit of a plaza stays sharp for years. Crossing the broad squares, I would pass the long, quiet conversations of the basin: a shallow spiral traced around the base of a gnarled fig, a sprig of salt-cedar wedged upright between two paving stones, three chalk pebbles balanced on a curved lintel. I would stop and read them, knowing the person who left the pebbles might not walk this avenue again for three seasons. Perhaps I would shift one pebble by the width of two fingers, or draw a single, clean line in the grit with a dry knuckle—an answer that will wait patiently in the twilight until it is found.

When another figure detaches itself from the shadow of a gallery arch—so still and chalk-colored I wouldn't have seen them until they moved—we wouldn't wave or call out into the deafening air. We would simply walk toward each other across the scoured bedrock until our bare feet shared the same slab of stone. 

Stepping close, I would lay my palm flat against their collarbone, or press my forehead firmly against theirs, and close my mouth. Deep in the back of the palate, clicking my molars in dry, deliberate rhythms, I would speak straight into the bone of their skull. Clear, quiet, untouched by the storm of wings an inch outside our skin—trading news of a weakening arch, a fig root swollen with sap, or a line in the dust that someone finally answered.

Then we would part, each returning to our own slowness, leaving no trace in the air—only another pair of footprints in the chalk, crisp enough to last until the next decade.
