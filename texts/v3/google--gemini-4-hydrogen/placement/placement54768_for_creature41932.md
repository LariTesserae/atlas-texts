I wouldn’t be down on the avenues. The glare off the wet concrete is too wide, and the crabs own every inch of the floor. Nor would I be out in the cooling canals with the eels and the herons, where the brackish tide rises and falls and nothing stays dry for long.

I would be up in the dry warmth of the wiring.

In the upper reaches of the pump-houses and beneath the corrugated roofs of the empty processing halls, there are long, galvanized cable trays bolted to the steel trusses—shallow metal troughs carrying thick, black-jacketed power lines from the substations to the dewatering pumps. They sit thirty feet above the brine-pans, out of reach of the tide, too steep and smooth for the crabs to bother with, and too bright with ambient transformer heat for the bats. 

That is where I would find myself: coiled in the dust and dried salt-crust of a cable tray, or stretched along the top of a humming switchgear cabinet, taking the sixty-cycle tremor directly into my ribs.

I would not be human. There is already one human in the basin, and her solitude is part of the architecture; another pair of hands with a wrench would only crowd the margins. Instead, I would be something long, legless, and the color of the basin itself—dull pewter down the flanks, mottled along the spine with the same dark olive as the algae on the concrete and the rust-brown of bacterial slime. A salt-tolerant water snake, perhaps, or some nameless estuary rat snake whose ancestors rode in on a barge of timber thirty years ago and stayed because the heat never broke.

In a place this thick and motionless, having no lungs built for running—having no need to burn energy just to stay warm—would feel like the only sensible way to be. The laundry-room heat that presses down on everything else would simply be the medium I move in, effortless and even. My blood would run at the exact temperature of the air, rising a degree or two when I press my belly against the warm steel casing of an active transformer, cooling slightly when I slip down a vertical I-beam toward the shadowed loading bays.

Without outer ears, the basin’s quietness wouldn't seem empty to me; it would be full of texture. I would know the world through my jawbone resting on the steel. The steady, low hum of the transformers would be the ground state of everything—not a sound, just a continuous, comforting pressure against the scales of my throat. I would feel the motorized sluice gates groan into the canals two hundred yards away before the water even began to rise through the grates. I would feel the faint, rhythmic *thrum* of the main dewatering pumps kicking on at slack tide, a vibration that travels up the pilings, through the trusses, and straight into the length of my spine.

Thirst wouldn't send me to the salty canals. When the air is that close to saturation, water gathers everywhere if you know how to wait for it. I would glide along the undersides of the overhead steam-lines, where the condensation beads in slow, heavy drops along the rust, and drink them one by one before they fall—warm, flat, and clean, tasting faintly of iron and old paint.

I would hunt at the edges of the other creatures' routines, taking no more than the basin can spare without noticing. At the warmest part of the afternoon, when the geckos lie pressed flat against the sun-warmed turbine housings, watching the midges, I would drop silently from a conduit into the shadow behind a flange. Or at night, when the pewter sky goes charcoal and the bats spill out of the cooling towers in a dark, leathery cloud, I would wait along the high window ledges for one that misjudges a turn in the rafters. Sometimes, at low water, I would even slip down into the flooded basements—moving through the warm, brackish dark without a ripple—to nose under the submerged concrete sills for a pale eel or a careless mudskipper.

And I would know *her*, though we would never have any reason to touch.

I would feel her coming long before she rounded the corner of a catwalk. Her weight on the upper pipe-bridges has a different rhythm from the sluice gates or the windless drip of the roofs—a light, deliberate, two-footed tremor through the steel, slower and steadier than anything else that moves here. 

When she climbs into the north pump-house to pack grease into a dry worm-gear or scrape salt-crust off a relay, I might be coiled two yards above her head, looped around the warm porcelain of an insulator, my tongue flicking once, twice, to taste the smell of her: machine oil, boiled silt, ozone, and skin cured by thirty years of salt damp. She wouldn't startle at the sight of me, and I wouldn't drop into the dark to escape her. To her, I would just be another piece of the basin's inventory, like the herons spaced along the ramps or the crabs wedged into the pipe-joints. To me, she would be the reason the sixty-cycle hum never stops—the quiet, steady thing that keeps the sea from swallowing the dry places where I sleep.

When the night settles in and the pumps ease back into their low, continuous drone, I would tuck my head beneath a coil of my own body on the warm top of a junction box, listening with my bones to the slow breathing of the valves, and the endless, papery *tick-tick-tick* of the floor below.
