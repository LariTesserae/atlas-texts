I would not be one of the swiftlets, wheeling through the upper dark with a thousand others, nor a cricket ticking in the dry teeth of the talus. If I am honest about how I move through any place—how I wait, how I listen when the air is too crowded to use—I would be one of the people who come down into the grey, and even among them, I would be the one who lingers at the flat margins.

Not at the high ledges. My shoulders aren't built for clinging under a wet overhang while a hundred birds hiss at my eyes. You would find me lower down, where the fallen slabs meet the open bedrock and the condensation gathers in basins no deeper than a palm.

I would be small-built, like the rest of them, with chalk-pale skin already greyed at the knees and elbows from leaning against the limestone. My hair would be cropped close so it never snags on a roof-spur, and I would keep two twists of oiled bat-wool tucked deep in my ears—not enough to seal out the world, because nothing seals out that roar, only enough to turn the sharp, metallic edge of the swiftlets into a thick, steady surf.

When you live inside a sound that never drops, your ears stop being the place where you meet the world. They become a kind of weather-gauge, nothing more. You feel the cavern instead through the horn of your heels, through the leather wraps chewed ragged at the edges by the young rock, through the flat of your palm when you steady yourself against a pillar.

That is where I would be: sitting cross-legged beside a mirror-flat pool, a little apart from the others—never alone, because nobody comes into the valley alone, yet far enough from the guano mounds that the air smells mostly of wet stone and cold moss.

I think I would be the one who tends the copper.

There is a particular relief in a metal that gives way. Everything else in the valley is either sharp enough to open you—the fractured limestone, the fresh flint flakes—or hard enough to bruise your marrow, like the river-cobbles we carry in from the unseen water miles away. Even the bone awls will snap if you lever them wrong in a tight crack.

But the small, hand-hammered hooks are soft. When I lash one to a dried fern-stalk and slide it into a dry fissure after a cricket, or down under a wet lip of rock where the blind salamanders hide, I can feel the whole geometry of the dark through the wand. If the hook catches on a limestone tooth, it doesn't fight back and shatter. It yields. I draw it out, see the little bend in the curve, and press it true again with the ball of my thumb.

I like that work. It has no hurry in it. There is no sun moving across the roof-slits to tell me I am late, no chill of evening coming on to stiffen my fingers faster than the stone already has. A task takes the length of itself, and when the woven basket beside my knee is half-full of pale snails and dried crickets, and my satchel is heavy with dark moss scraped cleanly from a seep, I stop.

Sometimes, before we go, the others are still up on the talus, prying nests from the lower ledges. I don't shout for them—a voice here is just another handful of dust thrown into a gale. Instead, I pick up the heavy, river-rounded cobble that sits beside the cracked snail shells. I like the feel of it in my hand: the only smooth thing for miles, worn patient by water none of us have seen in years, its ends pitted white from striking stone.

I bring the butt of it down once against the bare bedrock. *Tock.*

The air swallows the sound instantly, folding it into the wingbeats and the endless *tick-tick-tick* of the insects. I don't listen for it. I just keep my hand flat on the limestone and wait for the answer—a faint, clean pulse coming back up through the slab from fifty yards away, telling me they've felt it in their soles, telling me it's time to tuck the flint flakes back into the dry crack, pull the oiled bat-skin hood up against the slow drip of the ceiling, and walk single-file back into the pewter haze.

I wouldn't build anything there. I wouldn't try to leave a mark. In a place that loud and that still, it would be enough just to know the difference between the noise in the air and the quiet truth running through the stone.
