I would find myself in a crease of the scree, halfway between the weeping black-bottomed pools and the southern ridge, where a slab of freshly cleaved basalt leans out over a mat of liverwort.

At first, there is only the weight of stillness and the cold film of water gathering along my spine. It does not soak in; it beads, runs down the matte hollows of my ribs, and drips from my elbows onto the moss below. *Drip. Drip.* The sound is small and local, barely louder than the shift of a frost-loosened pebble, and it takes a long time to separate it from the larger quiet of the basin.

Beneath that quiet, there is the other thing—the steady, sub-audible tremor rising through the granite. It enters through the flat of my forearms, through my chin where it rests on the stone, through the broad, cleft soles of my feet wedged against the talus. It is not a sound. It is a kind of posture. My ribcage, rigid and shallow, settles against it the way a loose slate settles into its bed, finding the angle where it stops rattling.

When the wind drops, the air thickens. I draw it in—not through my mouth, which stays shut against the cold, but through the narrow vertical slits tucked behind my jaw. The mist is heavy with coal smoke and a sharp, metallic edge of ozone. Inside my throat, the dark membranes catch the soot and hold it, slick and tingling, while the thin oxygen filters into the blood. It does not burn. It tastes like the valley’s weather: sharp, unrounded, newly made.

Something small and cold shifts against the hollow behind my ankle. A salamander, dark as wet iron, backing deeper into the mineral-stained shadow of my heel. I do not move my foot. Above my shoulders, the slight warmth rising off my wet skin draws a pale column of midges up out of the sedge-tufts. A moment later, the air parts with a soft *zip*—a swift, wheeling inches from my cheekbone, snapping the flies out of the fog before vanishing back into the pewter ceiling. To the bird, I am just another outcrop of basalt, slightly warmer than the rest.

I am one of the ones who belong to the edges of things here.

When the mist thins for a breath, I unbend. My joints do not swing easily; they unfold in sharp, deliberate planes, matching the cleavage of the rock around me. Stepping onto the scree requires attention—not fear of falling, only the quiet habit of placing my weight where the stone will not turn. My cleft feet grip the knife-edges of the basalt without scraping the lichen, without sending a single pebble clattering down the slope. Behind me, the brittle fringes of ice along the boggy shelf remain unbroken.

I stop at a shallow pool to drink. The water is black-bottomed and still, wearing a thin, bruised film of rainbowed oil—the gift of the wind from the lowlands. I kneel, lowering my flat jaw to the surface, and draw my leathery lower lip across the meniscus, skimming the iridescent sheen inward first, letting it coat my tongue before I take the freezing meltwater beneath. My throat works once, twice, with a soft, wet *clack*, flushing the trapped soot down with the cold.

Above the cloud deck, something heavy passes. The mist vibrates—not the deep, steady pulse of the bedrock, but a broad, muffled drone moving from east to west.

Around the basin, three or four others uncurl from the dark talus, tilting their narrow, angular heads toward the pewter sky. I do the same. I do not know what a cargo plane is, only that the air is humming in a register that fits the hollow of my chest. My rigid ribs tighten, and a low, wet note rises in my throat, matching the vibration overhead. It is not a call or a warning. It is simply the stone answering the sky, holding the frequency until the drone thins out and leaves only the *drip-drip* of the weeping cliffs.

As the flat light begins to fail—not darkening into night, only thickening into a deeper grey—I make my way south, placing myself carefully across the slick aprons of stone toward the higher ridge.

Others are already there, folded along the sharp crest of raw slate, knees drawn up to their chins, pale eyes unblinking. We do not speak. We do not touch. In the creases of our shoulders, the evaporating mist has left thin, sulfur-tinted crusts of pale salt, marking us like the seams of the rock.

Below us, where the cloud deck thins toward the horizon, a bruised, sodium-amber glow bleeds upward from the unseen valleys. The wind carries the tang of the smelters straight into our jaw-slits, warm enough to almost imagine, and beneath our heels, the bedrock thrums with the distant beat of turbines churning in the dark.

I fold my long limbs into a hollow of the ridge, press my chin to my knees, and fix my eyes on the amber haze—waiting, without impatience, in a world that is half-rock, half-smoke, and still trembling from whatever pushed it up out of the earth.
