If I arrived here as I am—soft-eyed, hurried, used to shade and clutter—the room would unmake me before noon. The ricocheting glare off the selenite would bruise my sight; the still, kiln-warm air would pull the moisture straight out of my lungs; the sheer emptiness of those fifty paces would make me pace the perimeter until I wore myself down.

To stay here, I would have to belong to the room’s own economy. So when I look for where I actually am in this world, I find myself already folded into it—not as a visitor with a pack and a lantern, but as something the limestone has kept.

I am down on the lower ledges, along the western curve of the wall, where the rock stays cool an hour longer into the morning than the eastern side does. I am one of the pale, long-limbed ones, though I have no word for what I am, and no mirror to know the color of my hide except by looking at my own long, three-fingered hands resting on the chalk. In the hour after dawn, when the stone exhales its overnight dampness, my skin is the color of wet bone. By midday, it has dried to the matte, chalky ivory of the wall behind my ribs.

I am not young enough to be clear-skinned in the moss, and not yet old enough to stay in the glare when the light swings overhead. I am in the long, unmarked middle of it—old enough that the horn pads at the tips of my fingers are thick and dark from pressing flint against gypsum, and old enough that the creases behind my knees and under my jaw carry neat white rings of dried salt. The translucent mites know those creases well. They graze there in slow, blind herds, and I do not brush them away; their tiny, steady work keeps my joints from cracking when I shift.

If you watched me from the center of the floor, you might think I was asleep. My outer eyelids are thick and leathery, kept half-drawn against the brightness, and beneath them the milky membrane is slid across, turning the blinding cathedral of white stone into a silvered, bearable dimness. I do not see sharp edges. I see gradients: where the morning’s condensation is retreating up the wall, where a sage-green rosette is swelling in a floor-seam, where another pale figure sits four paces off, folded like a heron in its own shallow pocket.

My ribs are pressed to the cool stone. My heart beats once, pauses, beats again—a slow, unhurried pendulum that wastes nothing.

Between my thumb-spur and my two long fingers, I hold a dark flake of surface-flint, washed down through the oculus in some rain I don’t remember. In front of my chin is a natural saucer eroded into the gypsum, fed by a hairline seam that runs down from a ledge two hand-spans higher. For the last few seasons—though I only measure this by the feel of the groove under my fingernail—the pre-dawn dew has been pooling just a little too far to the right, spilling over the lip before it fills the hollow.

So I work at it.

*Tink.*

Three slow breaths.

*Tink.*

The sound crosses the empty middle of the room, clean and small, and comes back from the far wall without an echo, swallowed by the warm air. Across the chamber, someone else’s bone scraper gives a dry *shhh* against a rosette leaf. High above, a drop of moisture from a bleached vine lets go and hits the chalk—*plip*—and vanishes almost before the ring darkens.

I am not carving anything. I am only coaxing the rock’s own geometry by the width of a hair, lengthening the seam so tomorrow’s dampness will turn left instead of right. There is no hurry in it. If the flint dulls before the groove is deep enough, my fingers will simply open. The flake will drop into the white dust by my ankles, where the glass-thin insects lay their eggs, and the morning dampness will begin, very patiently, to cement it into the floor. Tomorrow I will reach out and pick up a splinter of old bird-bone, or another shard of flint, or nothing at all.

That is the part of this life that feels most strangely like home: nothing is kept. Nothing is carried from one pocket of the wall to the next. The room itself holds everything—the tools in the dust, the salt in the creases of the rock, the water waiting inside the stone’s cool breath. I don’t have to remember where I left anything, because there is nowhere else for it to go.

When the sun climbs high enough that the glare begins to press straight down into my hollow, I do not fight it. Over ten long seconds, I uncoil one knee, slide a palm across the grit, and shift my weight half a pace into the shadow of an overhang, keeping my ribs against the last of the rock’s coolness.

Then I settle, close the outer lids a little further, and wait for the dark hours.
