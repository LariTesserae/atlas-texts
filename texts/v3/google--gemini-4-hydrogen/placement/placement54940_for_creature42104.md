I would not be out on the wide, tilted lagoons, herding the mineral load into new causeways, nor would I be among the outer tiers of the amphitheater, laying my palms flat to read the consensus of the basin.

I would find myself in a crease.

When the tall ones stand at the edge of a gallery and pull a distant tower toward themselves, the space they leave behind does not disappear; it tucks. It folds inward like wet cloth bunched in a fist, and when the air relaxes again, there is a sunken courtyard where there used to be open water, or a short, blind avenue that ends against the back of a wall that has no front. Most of the citizens step out of these folds without noticing them, or seal them off over a few dozen tides so the city stays legible to their touch.

That is where I would be—inside one of the sealed-off pockets, a small, windowless plaza three or four doorways deep into the travertine, where no one has pulled a distance in a long time.

Between tides, I would be indistinguishable from the corner I lean against. My skin would be tight with the last flood's lime, stiff at the shoulder and the hinge of the knee, holding the exact temperature of the damp dark. There is a particular quiet in being calcified—not sleep, because there is nothing here to wake from, only a long, patient narrowing of attention down to the faintest sensations: the slow weight of the humid air pressing on my dome, the tickle of a black velvet crust creeping an eighth of an inch higher up my shin, the distant, muffled *thud* of a mudbank slumping somewhere outside the sealed arches.

Then the floor would begin to sweat.

That is always the first sign. Before the water rises in the open lagoons, it oozes up through the porous stone beneath my feet, lukewarm and thick, softening the crust at my ankles. I would wait—there is no hurry in a body made of brine and cartilage—until the flood climbs to my ribs and the chalky shell along my spine begins to yield. Then a sharp flex of the shoulder, a single muted *tock* that the mist swallows before it reaches the opposite wall, and a hairline crack opens down my flank. For three seconds, my corner of the dark is lit by a dull, ash-grey glow, just bright enough to show the pitted, bone-pale curve of an arch that leads nowhere, and the pale skin of lime peeling away from my arm like wet paper.

I would be one of the dwellers, yes—tall, eyeless, fluted down the ribs—but one who has lost the habit of the amphitheater.

Perhaps it happened because I lingered too long in a courtyard while someone else folded the avenue behind me, and by the time I loosened at the next tide, the doorway had already grown a lip of fresh travertine too thick to step over. Or perhaps I simply preferred the acoustics of a closed room. Sound dies fast in the basin, but *pressure* travels forever, and even in a sealed plaza, the water remembers what the rest of the city is doing.

When the tide reaches its height and the lukewarm pool sits steady at my waist, I lower my long-fingered hands until the tips just break the surface. Not to call anyone—there is no one here to answer—but to feel the stray ripples that wander under the porous walls. A sudden, heavy compression of the air above me means someone half a mile off has just dragged a ziggurat closer; my little courtyard shudders, the parallel walls leaning a fraction of an inch nearer to one another, squeezing the steam against my throat-slits until I taste the sharp, metallic tang of disturbed silt. Sometimes a faint, concentric ring of pressure leaks through the stone floor, leftover from a gathering in the central arena—half a thought about a slumping causeway, or the memory of a terrace that sank a century ago—and I let it pass through my palms without drawing it back.

In a world that is always slowly rewriting itself, there is a strange comfort in tending a page the city has forgotten it wrote.

I would tend the small things that take root in the blind pockets. Because no one walks through here, the rings of pale, leafless stalks grow thicker along the bases of my colonnades, clinging with their suction-pads to the damp crevices. I would sit beside them on the submerged steps, motionless for hours, waiting for their translucent skins to swell taut with warm gas. When one finally splits without a sound, I would lean my smooth, pitted face into the rising plume, opening the vertical slits along my throat to drink that sudden pocket of living heat. That is my conversation. Not the grand, silent chorus of the amphitheater, just the slow exhalation of a blind plant and the taste of dissolved stone in the mist.

And I suspect I would be old—not in years, since years mean nothing where there is no sky to measure them, but in *thickness*. Every tide leaves a little more of itself behind, even after you crack the shell. My joints would be heavier than they used to be, the lime-cartilage stiffening sooner after the water sinks back into the mud.

Sooner or later, the effort of cracking open would begin to feel unnecessary.

I wouldn't go looking for a grand arch to buttress, or a wide causeway that needs widening. I would simply choose the narrowest gap in my own sealed courtyard—the place where two walls have already leaned too close together from decades of distant travelers folding space around us. I would plant my broad, silt-colored feet in the mud between them, wedge my shoulders against the damp, pitted stone on either side, and close the slits along my throat.

I would stop drawing the warm water inward.

The next tide would coat my shins, and I would not flex to break it. The tide after that would seal my knees, then my ribs, then the smooth dome of my head. The black velvet crust would climb me in the dark, drinking the endless condensation, and the pale gas-stalks would find crevices in my ribs to cling to. Within a few decades, the fever inside my core would cool until it matched the exact, lukewarm dullness of the travertine around me.

If anyone ever pulled space thin enough to unseal this pocket again, they wouldn't find me waiting in the dark. They would only find a thick, porous pillar, seamlessly melted into the floor and the ceiling, holding up a corner of the city that nobody remembers building.
