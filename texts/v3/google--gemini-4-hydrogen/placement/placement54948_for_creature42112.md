I would be on the middle ledges—neither up near the rim where the cloud-lid scrapes raw against the stone, nor down in the bottom where the air pools thick and smells of cold tallow and rot. Somewhere halfway down the sheer, stepped fracture, where the mist moves in slow horizontal ribbons and the light from the fungal beds below is only a faint, bruised gold at the edge of vision.

I would be one of the listeners at the threshold.

Not old yet, perhaps, but someone with a knee that never quite trusted the wet slate again after a bad step on a loose shard—a small, silent slip that didn't kill me, only left the joint unreliable when the slabs angle sharply over a three-hundred-foot drop. In a place where a single *clack* of shifting stone can bring the thread-jellies drifting in like pale lanterns, an unsteady leg doesn't belong on the high mist-combs or the counterweight lifts. So you are given the doorway. You are given the bone.

I can feel how naturally that stillness fits. Even before the knee, I think I would have been the one who stayed inside the double-walled hut a little longer than the others, watching the fungus-wick lamp, liking the grammar of fingers against a wrist better than the wide, sweeping cloth-signals out on the open ledges.

My world, then, would be six paces of dry-stone floor, a hearth where a cake of compressed moss and glider-fat smolders without a sound, and the narrow stone sill where I sit with my back against the resin-sealed slate. My cloak—heavy, oiled moss-felt, its hem weighted with smooth pebbles so no draft can lift it—would be pulled tight around my shins. Beside my knee, within reach without looking, sits the small clay jar of numbing paste. Even in summer—if the perpetual chill here can be called by seasons—the dampness settles in the chest. At the first dry prickle behind the collarbone, you take a pinch of the bitter, boiled dew-moss on your tongue and wait until your throat goes slick and dead, until you couldn't cough even if you wanted to.

And in my left palm, resting lightly, is the hanging knot of stilt-predator bone.

From the doorframe, the greased sinew cord stretches out into the pewter gloom, vanishing into the mist ten paces away, spanning the empty air to the cluster on the opposite wall. Another line runs upward toward the rim-terraces; a third drops down toward the lower fungal beds. They are rubbed weekly with glider-oil so the damp air slides off them without a hum. When they are tended properly, they are dead to the wind, alive only to hands.

This is where I would find my mind: inside the tension of those lines.

When you never speak aloud, thought stops sounding like a voice in your head. It becomes weight and interval. I wouldn't think in words; I would think in the *press* of a thumb against the inside of a forearm, in the particular spacing of tugs that travels across two hundred feet of fog. Every household has its own signature, braided into their wrist-cords and echoed in the way they pull the line. I would know my neighbors not by faces—half-hidden as they are beneath hoods in the dimness—but by the character of their hands on the sinew.

The tall harvester two terraces up pulls with a heavy, even cadence—*draw, hold, draw*—measured like someone used to hauling waterlogged bladder-fronds out of the cracks. The younger pair on the far wall are quicker, impatient, sending sharp little double-twitches that I have to steady with my thumb before the bone taps against the slate doorframe. Even a tap too loud is a risk. You catch the vibration in your flesh before it can reach the stone.

Most of the day, the cords are quiet, and my work is smaller still: mending frayed lengths of braided frond-fiber by the yellow-brown smudge of the lamp, pressing fresh grease into the sinew, or sitting with the youngest children when their mothers are out on the ledges. In the muffle-chamber at the back of the hut, packed thick with inflated fronds and hung with wet moss curtains, an infant can kick and squirm without danger, learning the shape of the world through skin-to-skin taps along its ribs. When they are older—just old enough to be trusted near the threshold—I would be the one they sit beside, practicing their wrist-signs in my palm while my other hand stays on the bone knot, dividing my attention between the child's small, plum-stained fingers and the whole vertical town hanging in the mist outside.

There is a particular quietness to watching the basin from a doorway. You see the eyeless gliders bank past the sill, close enough to feel the faint, oiled brush of their down against the air without hearing a whisper of it. You see the moss-crabs streaming in slow, matte-shelled ribbons along the underside of the cantilevered walkway next door. And sometimes, when the cloud-lid shifts and a cold draft carries the thread-jellies too close to our terrace—those pale, lung-sized sacs trailing their invisible cobwebs of filaments—you feel the whole ledge hold its breath.

No one runs. Running makes the pebbles in a cloak-hem click; running makes a boot slip. Instead, someone slips out onto the narrow path with a clay *tock-lure* and creeps toward an empty spur of black glass a hundred paces off. I watch them vanish into the pewter ribbons, their felt shoulders hunched, their knees bent in that slow, rolling walk. Then I wait at the doorframe, my hand on the bone, counting the slow minutes by the drip from our own roof-runnel into the covered cistern.

Half an hour later, though I cannot hear the single *tock* of the drop hitting the glass shard out on the spur, I see the jellies turn. One by one, the translucent sacs drift away from our mist-combs, drawn toward the distant, harmless noise in the dark. A few moments after that, the line in my palm gives two soft, deliberate pulls: *clear*. I pinch the sinew once to answer, then let the bone settle back against my knuckles.

At the end of the shift, when the light above the cloud-lid thins from pewter to black and the only thing visible below is the sickly amber glow of the basin floor, I would lean out just far enough to look down into the dark. Somewhere down there, among the swollen gourd-fungi, are the wrapped bundles of the people who used to hold this same knot of bone—still keeping their quiet watch in the cold, shining faintly through the mist.

Then I would draw back inside, pull the moss curtain across the doorway, and lay my palm flat against the warm stone of the hearth, feeling the slow, steady pulse of the house around me.
