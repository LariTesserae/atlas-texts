I would not be near the transformer. That seam belongs to the eldest, whose back is already a whole landscape of cemented shells and dark-violet plate, wide enough to block the north wall’s warmth for itself.

I would be on the far side of the sump, wedged in the narrow cold between the drowned iron grating and the west shale, where the water tastes more of dissolved lime than of copper.

At first, there is only the count. Not numbers—I have no head for numbers—just the body’s expectation of the *thock*. Fourteen seconds of stillness, while the cold water settles around my flanks and the silt drifts down like ash. Then the hydraulic valve trips on the far side of the basin, and the iron grating beneath my belly gives a single, stiff shudder. My gill-curtains part a fraction wider to drink the pulse. Fourteen seconds more.

This is how I know where I am: by the way the vibration reaches me. If it comes sharp and metallic through the teeth of my mouth-plate, I am pressed against the pump housing. If it comes muffled, arriving a beat late through mud and dead shells, I have drifted too far toward the corner where the siphon-pipe squats.

I am one of the smaller two. My mantle is still mostly bare—cold, bruised-plum flesh, slick and pale where it hugs the stone, only half-armored along the ridge with a thin, uneven crust of rust-orange sinter and a few borrowed limpet shells that have not yet fused. I am not heavy enough to hold my own against the crabs when the water drops, not yet. When the siphon in the corner finally fills and swallows a third of the sump in a single hollow *glug-hiss*, I do not clamp down in the open like a boulder. I pull myself backward into the slot under the grating, folding my soft edges tight against the shale, letting the heavy-clawed scavengers clatter over the iron above me while I wait in the wet dark for the seepage to refill the basin.

Up on the gantry, I must have been one of the pale, grape-soft things clinging to the slime mold, sipping the cold drops from the stalactites and crowding toward the bruised-amber warmth of the bulb. I do not remember the bulb, only the habit of reaching upward when the air feels thin. Down here, the light does not reach my corner at all. What I have instead is the *hum*.

It is faint on the west wall—barely a tingle in the silver-blue rosettes along my hood, like the memory of a current rather than the thing itself. The transformer sits chest-deep across the water, and when the eldest drapes its conductive tendrils over the north busbars and closes the circuit, the whole basin tilts toward it. The stray electrons gather there; the blind amphipods cloud toward the north wall like moths to a flame I cannot see. My corner goes dead and flat, tasting only of wet stone and old grease.

So I learn the margins.

I learn the second hydraulic ram—the one that moves even slower than the first, its brass sleeve crusted thick with pale lime because the eldest rarely bothers to haul itself this far across the sump. Every few hours, when the scale grows sharp enough to catch the piston, the fourteen-second *thock* stutters, dragging a fraction of a beat behind itself. That is when I unstick my belly from the mud.

It takes a long time to climb the submerged frame. My stubby flank-pads grip the pitted iron, sliding a little on the slime before they find purchase. Where the brass meets the piston, the mineral crust is fresh, soft as damp chalk at the surface and gritty underneath. I press my underside flat against the metal and begin to rasp—slow, heavy strokes, scraping away the lime and the iron-salts, taking the grit into my gut to harden my teeth and seep out through my back as new plate. Beneath me, the ram sighs and eases forward, its cycle smoothing out again, though I do not know that I am fixing it. I only know that the metal tastes clean where I have grazed, and that the shudder through my belly becomes even and steady once more.

Sometimes, when I am high enough on the pump housing, the tip of my hood breaks the surface of the ink-dark water.

The air above the sump is a shock—cold, stagnant, heavy with the smell of cellar-rot and ozone. Out here, the *tink-plip* of water hitting iron is sharp and distinct, no longer dulled by the sump. If I stay still, with only the sensory pits of my hood exposed to the mist, I can feel the faintest draft of warmth drifting down from the caged bulb overhead, and the slow, dry *skritch* of a hundred limpets grazing the shale above me. I cannot climb as high as they do; my body is made for the weight of water, and in the open air my mantle feels heavy, sagging against the iron. Still, I linger there for a few cycles of the valve, tasting the mist where the stray current leaks down the porcelain insulators.

Then a drop of cold, iron-heavy water falls from the gantry above and strikes my hood, and I slide back down into the dark.

This is the whole of my life: the fourteen-second pulse, the slow accretion of rust and shell along my spine, the scraping of scale from the forgotten brass, and the long, patient wait in the shadow of the grating. Someday—not soon, not for years of this slow dripping—my back will be thick enough to withstand the crabs in the open sump. Someday the eldest on the north wall will grow too heavy to haul itself up onto the primary ram, or its gill-curtains will clog with silt, and the seam beside the warm, humming transformer will sit empty for a cycle, then two.

When that happens, I will cross the sump. I will press my bare, pale belly against the hot iron casing, uncurl my copper-laced tendrils into the dark, and close the circuit myself.

Until then, I stay wedged between the grating and the shale, listening to the *thock* of the valve, growing heavier by a grain of iron a day.
