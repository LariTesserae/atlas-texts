I would be at the other end of the glycol lines.

Not down in the octagon—there is barely room down there for a man to turn his shoulders, and besides, my lungs are too warm, too wet with ninety-eight-degree air, too full of the small, careless yeasts and spores of the upper world. If I climbed down the three-hundred-meter casing, I would ruin the silence just by breathing.

So I would stay where the waste heat goes.

Above the frost-line, in a low, windowless shed bolted to a concrete pad, I would be the night-shift tender on the chillers. It is the only warm place for a mile in any direction. Everything outside is scrub pine and frozen gravel, locked under six inches of crusted snow, except for a neat, steaming ring around the shed’s condenser fins where the heat pulled up from three hundred meters below vents into the dark.

Inside, the shed smells of hot dust, rubber belting, and the sweet, heavy vapor of propylene glycol. There is a wooden stool with one leg shimmed by a folded matchbook, a kettle on a hotplate, and a wall of analog gauges whose glass faces have yellowed slightly at the edges. Above the workbench, taped down with cloth electrical tape, is a blueprint I have looked at so many times I can trace it with my eyes shut: an octagon of cast iron, a central brass drum, a pleated lattice of ceramic baffles. On the drawing, it looks like a carburetor or a strange kind of radiator. Nowhere on the paper does it look like a lung.

My job is mostly listening, and mostly waiting.

Every forty seconds, the needle on the main ammeter gives a tiny, polite flinch—less than half an amp—as a solenoid opens three hundred meters beneath my boots and an extrusion arm shifts two degrees. I don’t even have to look at the dial anymore to know it’s happening. I feel it as a faint change in the pitch of the step-down transformer humming under the bench, the sixty-cycle buzz dropping a fraction of a tone for half a second before settling back into its groove.

Every few minutes, the pressure gauge on the auxiliary air-return line dips and recovers. That’s the crown-valve opening. That’s the mist.

When I first took the shift, back when the outer plates were still being lowered down the bore-hole in slings and the air-compressor ran day and night, I thought of the thing below as a very expensive piece of plumbing. I logged the glycol temperatures—supply at minus four, return at plus two—checked the oil level in the compressor sight-glass, and drank burnt tea out of a tin cup. I knew they had flushed a pale, cloudy suspension through the misting lines right before they sealed the top flange, something the project engineer called a *self-healing biological seal*, cheap and inert. I didn’t think much about it. Down in the dark, at one degree above zero, what could a smear of pond-moss possibly do?

It took months for me to start noticing the difference in the strip-chart.

The chart recorder sits on the far end of the bench, a slow roll of graph paper creeping under an inked nib. Normally, mechanical systems have a roughness to them—little jagged teeth in the pressure curve where a valve seats imperfectly, or a slight hunting oscillation as a fan fights a draft through an unsealed seam. For the first few weeks, the nib drew those jagged teeth.

Then, slowly, the line began to smooth out.

At first I thought the nib was clogging, or the dampening oil in the gauge had thickened in the cold. I tapped the glass. I bled the line. Nothing was wrong with the gauge. The pressure differential across the baffles down below was simply becoming *quieter*—the intake stroke pulling clean and even, the seal holding tighter with every week that passed, without a single adjustment from up here.

And then there is the stray-voltage meter.

It’s an old galvanometer wired into the grounding braid of the main umbilical—supposed to sit at zero, just a safety check against a shorted motor winding or a wet junction box. Lately, it doesn’t sit at zero. It doesn’t trip the breaker, either; it just hovers a hair’s breadth off the pin, trembling in a rhythm that doesn’t match the forty-second solenoids or the sixty-cycle hum of the transformers.

It rises when the misting valve opens. It holds steady, faint as static on a wool sleeve, while the intake fans draw. Then, as the air dries out down there, the needle drifts back toward the pin.

I sit on my shimmed stool with a wool blanket over my knees, watching that needle breathe.

Sometimes I go outside with a flashlight to check the condenser fins, just to stretch my legs. The snow melts where the shed’s exhaust hits it, turning to black slush that refreezes into clear ice an arm’s length further out. Standing in the freezing dark, I can hear the chillers grinding away behind the corrugated tin, working hard to keep a parlor-sized box three hundred meters down at the edge of frost. It feels strange to think that all this noise and heat up here—the whining fan belts, the hot copper coils, the steam rising off the gravel—is just the price of keeping a quiet room cold.

I know what the schedule says. In another four months, maybe six, the extrusion arms will reach the outer iron ribs. The last millimeter of polymer will lock into place, the brass drum will stop turning, and someone from the project will come out in a truck with a winch and a set of heavy wrenches to unbolt the top flange and see what grew.

I don’t look forward to that day.

When I come back inside and latch the door against the wind, the sixty-cycle hum wraps around me like an old coat. The ammeter flinches—*forty seconds*—and down in the dark, three hundred meters beneath the frost-line, an arm shifts two degrees. I pour another cup of tea, rest my palm lightly on the cold iron of the glycol return pipe, and feel the faint, steady pulse of something patient and wet and pale, taking its time, learning the shape of its own ribs before the world comes down to ask it to work.
