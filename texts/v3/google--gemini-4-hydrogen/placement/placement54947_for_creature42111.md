Not down in the bowl—not at first, anyway. If I were dropped into this world without a map, or born into its thin, warm air, my instincts would pull me to the seam where the hollow ends and the tableland begins.

Up on the plateau, the world is too wide and too dry to hold a thought for long. You can walk for hours across the iron-stained gneiss and never meet an edge sharp enough to snag a shadow. Down inside the forty-pace pocket, on the other hand, the room is too small for the amount of life crammed into it. The parabolic walls bounce the cicadas’ saw-toothed static until it feels less like a sound and more like a swarm of needles pressing against your eardrums. Even the *tock-tock-tock* of the frogs has nowhere to go; it ricochets off the smooth stone and piles up on itself.

So I would find myself on the lip.

It is a strange place to belong, that rounded rim of bedrock where the wind skims straight across the opening like breath over the mouth of a bottle. Lie flat there, with your chin on the warm stone and your heels stretched out toward the empty miles of the plateau, and you occupy two worlds at once. Behind you, nothing moves except grit and heat-haze. Six inches below your nose, the air thickens into bath-warm humidity, and the vertical wall of sound rises up like steam.

At first, I think I would be a watcher—someone, or something, that simply cannot look away from the contrast. A creature of the threshold. Maybe I arrived here once as a traveler crossing the planed-down range, my water-skin long since cracked and my boots ground smooth by the quartz dust, and when my foot nearly stepped off the lip into that sudden teaspoon of jungle, I simply stopped. Why keep walking across a dead tableland when you have found the only place in a hundred miles that remembers how to sweat?

Or maybe I was never a traveler at all. Maybe I belong to the rim the way the silver-backed mites belong to the edges of the artifact halos—living on the margin of a miracle I didn't make.

Let me feel out the shape of it. If I stay here long enough, what does this place turn me into?

The sun up here is unforgiving, sharp and high-altitude, bleaching everything it touches. Anything soft and pale would blister in an afternoon. So I would have to be weathered—skin or hide cured to the color of the iron-stained gneiss, dry as cured leather on the back where the plateau wind hits me, yet capable of softening along the underside when I slip down into the dampness. Perhaps I am small enough to wedge into the shallow, wind-carved flutes just below the lip, where the rock overhangs by an inch or two and the swifts’ wingtips miss me by the breadth of a finger when they dive.

I wouldn't have the sweepers' deafness. I would still hear the roar of the bowl—I think I would *need* to hear it, as proof that the world isn't entirely stone and wind—but I would learn to listen past it. You sit on the rim for a week, a month, a season, and the cicada-static stops sounding like chaos. It becomes a kind of weather, like surf or rain, and beneath it you begin to pick out the smaller intervals: the *plip* of a fern-droplet hitting a soapstone cup, the dry rattle of a beetle crossing a fissure, the sudden hush when a high cloud dims the sun and the translucent grazers slide out from beneath the moss.

And then, deeper than the frogs, you start to feel the *thrum*.

At the rim, it arrives half a beat later than it does on the floor, traveling up through the dense, continuous bedrock. You don't hear it with your ears; you feel it in your teeth, in the flat of your palms, in the hollows behind your knees. Every time the buried machines let out that sub-audible pulse, the whole forty-pace dish rings like a thick stone bell, and even up on the sun-bleached lip, the fine grains of quartz grit tremble and resettle into slightly neater lines.

That is what would keep me here, and what would finally decide what I am.

I would not be one of the frantic, sunlit things—not a mantid poised to strike, not a swift burning through the thin air on sheer speed. There is enough hunger in the bowl already. Instead, I think I would become a keeper of the dry edge, a slow-moving go-between.

By day, when the glare is fiercest, I would stay tucked into the shadowless crease of the rim, motionless as a shelf of rust-colored stone, letting the wind scour the dust from my shoulders. I would watch the sweepers far below—those broad, slate-gray slabs gliding across the bare lanes between the moss islands, unbothered by the diving birds, rolling fallen chitin into their pouches and tucking it neatly into the copper sphagnum. I would envy them their certainty. They never wonder why the shadows around the pale ceramic ring lean too far toward the center of the bowl, or why the dew near the matte-black disk beads in six-sided lattices before it runs. They simply press their palms over the geometry and drink.

I, on the other hand, would be the one who *wonders*—or at least, the one who keeps track.

At dusk, when the plateau cools rapidly and the cicadas finally drop their pitch from a shriek to a ragged, cooling rasp, I would climb down into the bowl. Not to hunt, and not to sweep—the sweepers have the floor well in hand—but to visit the seven or eight places where the stone forgets how to behave normally.

I would sit beside the matte-black disk, where the dust motes still drift in their lazy, invisible orbits, and hold my breath to see if the faint shimmer above it smells different from the rest of the damp air. I would lay my ear—not a cartilage keel, just an ordinary, curious ear—against the pale slotted ring jutting from the wall, waiting for the next *thrum* to gather itself in the dark beneath the gneiss. When it comes, and the droplets on the maidenhair ferns hop upward for a fraction of a second, I wouldn't tighten my skin into a drumhead like the sweepers do. I would just let the wave pass straight through my ribs and out into the cooling air, like a note played through an empty flute.

Who or what would I be?

A witness, mostly. Something half-cured by the plateau and half-steeped in the bowl—not quite native to the roaring jungle, not quite lost to the empty rock. A quiet, sun-darkened thing with quartz dust in the creases of its knuckles and damp fern-spores clinging to its ankles, sitting just inside the rim where the two halves of the world meet, listening to the old machines sweat water into the dark and waiting to see if the stone ever has anything else to say.
