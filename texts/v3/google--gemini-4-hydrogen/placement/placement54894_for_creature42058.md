I think I would be found at the edge of things—not out on the empty shelves, and not miles down in the dark with the turbine shafts, but at the hinge where the city meets the drop.

Specifically, I would be a keeper of one of the outer anchor-pylons, on a leeward terrace where a three-mile braided steel cable rises out of a sintered-stone winch-house and crosses the chasm toward the next mesa.

It is not a grand post. A master mason lays a foundation and lives eighty years to set the capstone; a *Velamen*-tender works the high windward parapets with long silica poles, combing rare-earth dusts from the giants as they drift past. My work is smaller, and mostly it consists of paying attention to things that are almost still.

---

In the middle of the day, when the bleached ceramic light sits cleanly on the basalt slabs and the shadows fall sharp as slate, I sit on a low block of barite beside the pylon housing. My knees are drawn up, my graphite-soled sandals resting flat against the glassy stone. The air smells of hot dust and a faint, dry edge of ozone from the electrostatic masts higher up the ridge.

If I press the pad of my thumb against the pylon’s copper grounding-rod, I can feel the whole system at once: the slow, steady pulse of the geothermal dynamos turning miles beneath the city, and the tighter, higher hum of the wind shearing across the ridge-masts. Everything is a gradient. You learn to read it in the skin of your wrist before you ever look at a dial.

Above the winch-house, the sacrificial copper perches are thick with *Wire-Clingers*. They sit in motionless, glittering knots, their metallic carapaces catching the white glare as they sip leakage current from the insulators. Every few days, I take a soft brush of woven basalt-fiber and gently sweep the dust from their backs so they don't bridge a gap they shouldn't. They don't scatter when I touch them; they only shift their spindly legs a fraction of an inch, adjusting to the shadow of my hand.

Down in the mortarless joints at the base of the pylon—where the sintered stone meets the living dolomite of the cliff—live the *Seam-Listeners*. Most of the time they are silent, flat and speckled as the barite around them, scraping at the microscopic crusts that try to etch the masonry. 

My real job is to wait for them to click.

When the sun shifts and the cliff face begins to heat unevenly, the rock breathes. Usually it is harmless, a slow expansion the joints were built to absorb. But once in a great while—perhaps twice in a decade—a load-bearing block deep in the anchor-bed takes too much shear, and a hairline fracture begins to form inside the stone. Long before any human eye could see it, the *Seam-Listeners* gather over the invisible line and begin their dry, rhythmic ticking.

That is when I rise, unhurried, and walk back down the shaded alley to fetch the riggers with their graphite-greased jacks.

---

I think this suits me because I have never been built for haste, and here, haste is a kind of structural flaw.

If you look out across the chasm from my pylon, you can see a freight gondola crawling along the steel cable. If you only glance at it, you would swear it is hung there forever, a dark bead pinned against the milky gypsum haze three kilometers above the basin floor. You have to sit for ten minutes—long enough for the sun’s shadow to creep a finger’s-width across the hexagonal paving—to realize it has cleared the midpoint.

Sometimes, a neighbor from the curing yards—a stone-polisher whose tunics are always pale with dolomite flour—walks out to the parapet to rest in the shade of the winch-house. We do not speak right away. We raise our arms and touch the backs of our copper-wrapped wrists together. A small, warm hum passes between us, equalizing our charge, and my smoky inner lids slide back as we turn to look out over the drop.

We might stand there for a quarter of an hour before either of us uses the reed-membranes in our throats to shape a sentence. Sound carries too far across the empty stone to waste it on filler. When a *Velamen* drifts past at eye level—its translucent, silica-reinforced wings catching the light like oiled paper, its trailing filaments combing the dry updraft—neither of us points. We just watch its faint, rippling shadow cross the cliff face below, silent as a cloud.

---

In the late afternoon, when the sun drops behind the western mesas and the cliffs begin to cool with deep, audible groans, I lock the winch-house shutters and walk inward toward the plaza.

My chest vents draw in the cooling, dust-free air, passing it across the dry lattices inside my ribs. Along the shaded wall of the square, the copper-inlaid benches are already warming with trickle-current from the afternoon’s wind-harvest. I take a place near the end, next to two cable-riggers and an old weaver whose shawl is heavy with copper thread. 

As the gentle current soaks up through my soles and along my spine, replenishing my ion-channels, I can feel the day’s small static knots untangle. Someone passes a plate of pressed lipid-cake, seasoned with bitter salt and ground barite. We eat slowly, listening to the city settle.

On evenings when the sky begins to thicken—that slow, three-day gathering of luminous amber that means a continental dust storm is coming—you can hear the wind-harps strung across the upper alleys begin to wake up. First just a low, single-note thrum, like a tensioned wire, then a chord that seems to rise out of the stone itself. 

Out on the cliff faces, the *Velamen* are already reeling in their filaments and hooking themselves to the iron mooring-rings. Down in my anchor-bed, the *Seam-Listeners* are tucking themselves deep into the basalt joints.

I sit on the warm copper bench, my hands resting on my knees, feeling the steady hum of the grid through my calluses. Outside, the wind is rising, and the electrostatic masts on the ridge are drinking it in. There is nothing to hurry toward, and nothing to mend until the storm clears. Just the slow turning of the turbines miles below, and the patient weight of the stone holding us all up.
