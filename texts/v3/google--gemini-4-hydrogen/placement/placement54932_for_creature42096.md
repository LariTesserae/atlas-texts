I would not be in the shelter of the boats, nor up on the smooth, silent whale-backs of the stone domes. I would be out on the open swell, half a mile from the nearest bleached reed-mast, crouched low in the silver-green sedge with one cheek pressed flat against the moss.

To live here, you learn that the plateau has two voices, and neither of them is meant for human ears. Up in the air, there is only the wall of sound—the gale tearing across twenty miles of nothing, the dry, metallic hail of the copper-shelled locusts chewing at the grass. If you stand upright, that noise fills your skull until your own thoughts feel thin and scattered, blown sideways like mist. 

So I spend much of my life down near the skin.

I am one of the band’s listeners—not a title anyone speaks aloud, because we rarely speak aloud at all, but a habit that became a usefulness. When I was young, a rust-colored stalker’s teeth or a bad fall didn’t take my hearing; the plateau itself did, slowly, season by season, until the high whine of the wind and the locust-rattle blurred into a single, dull surf. What remained was bone-sense. When I kneel and lay my jawbone against the cushion-moss, or set the butt of my hollow reed-cane against my teeth and plant its tip in the weave, the air’s roar falls away. The world becomes clear again, translated into weight and pulse.

My body is shaped for this, the way the grazers are shaped for the vents. My shins and forearms are bound in overlapping plates of locust-chitin laced with sinew, scarred white where the silica-ribbed sedge has still found a way to slice through. My hair is braided tight to my scalp with sedge-twine and weighted at the ends with four small beads of cold-hammered copper so it cannot whip across my eyes when I turn my head into a gust. My feet are broad, splayed, and horn-soled—bare whenever the afternoon sun warms the mat—and I walk the way all of us walk, knees soft, weight forward over the balls of my toes, never letting a heel drop hard enough to wake what sleeps below.

My work is the drumhead itself.

Every band needs someone who walks the paths ahead of the pack-bearers and the reed-tappers, testing the skin. The gleaners down in the dark never stop chewing the oldest, deadest roots from the underside of the ceiling, and the wind in the voids sweeps their sawdust away. Over a season, a path that felt solid as limestone can thin out until it is no thicker than a cured hide stretched over a twenty-foot drop. 

So I walk with a weighted staff of split reed-cane, tapping a slow, steady cadence as I go—*tap, step, tap, step*—feeling the answer travel up the wood into my palm. Healthy mat over a shallow hollow gives back a tight, deadened *thump*, warm and springy. A thinned patch, chewed hollow from beneath, answers with a dry, papery *tock* that rings slightly in the bones of my wrist. 

When I hear that ring, I stop. I drop to one knee, brace my chitin-wrapped shins against the turf, and press my chin to the moss.

Down there, beneath the weave, the world is vast and organ-deep. I can feel the slow, steady *boom* of the gale funneling through the limestone chambers, and sometimes, if I hold my breath until my ribs ache, I can feel the faint, rhythmic *scritch-scritch* of gleaners working the ceiling directly beneath my face. Worse is when I feel nothing at all—only a sudden, heavy stillness where the moss sags a fraction of an inch, and the faintest brush of something whiskered testing the tension from below.

An under-pack.

When that happens, I don’t run. Running is a heavy heel-strike, an invitation. I rise slowly, keeping my weight spread wide, and raise my arms against the blinding sky to cut the sharp, flat-palmed angles of blade-talk to whoever is watching from the distant camp or the path-poles: *Thin skin. Pale pack. Turn east toward the dome.*

Then I take a small leather pouch of ground locust-ash and red iron-moss from my belt and scatter a ring around the sagging turf, marking the wound so no child’s foot or heavy hide-bundle finds it in the morning mist.

I like the quiet hours best—if you can call them quiet. Just before dawn, when the warm mist curls up over the plateau’s rim from the unseen valleys far below, dampening the locusts until they hang heavy and sluggish on the sedge-blades. That is when I walk out to the giant reeds. Thirty feet above me, the wind-weavers’ silk funnels hum like slack harp-strings in the damp air, and inside the pale, hollow trunks, I can hear the tiny, wet *tick* of reed-skinks darting up the sweating walls. I drive a slender bone spile into the base of a stalk, catch the cool, mineral-sweet condensation in a bladder-skin, and then sit with my back against the reed, letting the warmth of the decaying root-mat soak up through my spine.

In those minutes before the sun clears the rim and wakes the swarms, I feel most like what I am: not a master of this place, and not a guest, just another thread woven into the skin between the sky and the dark. 

One day, when my knees stiffen too much for the crouch and my soles grow too thick to read the *tock* of a rotted chamber, the band will carry me up onto the highest, smoothest stone dome—the one place where the drum goes silent and solid. They will leave me to the bright air and the wheeling harrier-hawks. And later, when the wind has finished its work, someone younger will come back for my skull, rub it smooth with grazer-tallow, bore a hole through the crown, and hang it from a path-pole beside a strip of hammered copper.

Even then, I won’t leave the noise. I’ll just hang there in the gale, clattering softly against the metal, still marking the safe ground for the ones who walk below.
