I would not be on the highest ridges, where the wind strips the breath from your teeth, nor down in the deepest clefts where the darkness never quite lifts. I would find myself somewhere in the fourth or fifth tier—a middle stratum, wedged into a leeward crease of the cliff where the mountain’s own black slate becomes the back wall of the room.

Directly beneath my floorboards, separated only by slotted timber and loose-laid stone, the broad-horned beasts stand shoulder to shoulder. Even in the dead of the morning freeze, before the sun clears the eastern crags, their collective heat rises through the cracks like a slow, steady tide. It smells of wet wool, warm dung, and hay-dust, and it is just enough to keep the grease in my soapstone lamp soft and the water in my covered skin from turning to solid ice.

If you asked what I am here, I would not give you a grand title. There are no grand titles in a place where a wall takes three lifetimes to build. I am a mender of old wood, and a listener to chinks.

Because timber is scarcer than water here—salvaged from crooked, iron-hard limbs hauled up from the distant lowlands before my grandfather’s teeth came in—nothing wooden is ever thrown away. When a ladder rung splits under a frost-heave, or a lever cracks while prying a stubborn slab of basalt, they bring it to my low doorway. I push aside the grease-cured pelt, take the broken piece with a nod—no need to waste breath on long greetings—and lay it beside the sunken hearth.

My hands are broad and flat-nailed like everyone else’s, the creases permanently inked with grey-black slate dust, but they are greased every morning with warm tallow so my fingers stay supple enough for rawhide. That is my trade: soaking narrow strips of hide until they soften, shaving down the splintered ends of a centuries-old lever with a flint blade, fitting the scarf-joint together so tightly that no frost can wedge inside, and binding it wet. As the rawhide dries in the heat of the dung-fire, it shrinks with a slow, quiet strength, pulling the wood into itself until the splice is harder than bone. Then I rub it with rendered fat until it shines like horn.

Every piece of wood that passes through my hands carries the history of four or five other menders. I can read their work by touch—the tight, hurried cross-lashing of a lean winter, the neat, patient spiral of an elder whose hands I remember from childhood. I am only adding my layer to the join, knowing someone else will cut it free and bind it again long after I am carried up to the loose scree.

Between the bindings, I listen. 

In a city without mortar, nothing is ever truly still. You learn to hear the difference between the mountain settling and a wall failing. When I step out onto the boulder-weighted roof above my room at midday—the one hour when the white sun softens the rime on the crags and the whole city wakes at once—I watch the small things first. I watch the alpine birds wheeling in the thin, brilliant air; if they tuck themselves early into the warm crevices around the chimney flues, I know to wedge an extra stone onto the roof-skins before the shadow turns blue. 

And I watch the baseboards and the dry-laid joints of my neighbors’ walls. When the small, round-eared rodents begin carrying their wisps of grass and stolen wool out of a retaining wall—abandoning a warm chink they have nested in for years—I do not wait for a crack to show. I tap a gloved knuckle on my frozen collar to clear the ice, step down the worn stone stairs with my flat, careful stride, and murmur two words to the mason two tiers below. Together, without hurry, we take up our wooden levers and shift the load before the lintel can groan.

At midday, like everyone else, I take my horn scoop and clear the needle-ice from the carved runnel that crosses my roof, guiding the bright beads of meltwater down toward the cistern my mother’s brother spent forty winters chipping out of the basalt. I keep a single south-facing stone box tucked into the shelter of my chimney flue, where a clump of silvery tussock grass clings to a handful of grit. When the cisterns are full and the beasts have drunk, I give the grass what is left in the bottom of my scoop, drop by careful drop.

By mid-afternoon, the ink-blue shadows slide back across the canyons, the water stiffens on the slate, and the pelt drops back over my doorway. I sit by the soapstone lamp with a split antler pick or a cracked ladder rung across my knees, the heat of the beasts rising through the floor, the wind threading quietly through ten thousand unmortared stones. I am not building anything that will bear my name. I am only keeping the joints supple, so that when the mountain shifts another fraction of an inch in the dark, the city shifts with it, and does not break.
