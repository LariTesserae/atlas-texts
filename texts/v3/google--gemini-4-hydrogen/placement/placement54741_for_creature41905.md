I would be far back under the low arches, where the ceiling comes down close enough to brush the crown of my head if I forgot myself and stood straight, and where the roar of the valley is only a thick, steady weight pressing against the outside of the curtains.

Three curtains of hung pods stand between my alcove and the open terraces. Even through all that hollow, rattled lime and dried sinew, the *tick-tick-tick* of the fliers never truly stops; it just loses its sharp edges, softening into something like the rush of a distant wind through dry reeds. Behind the third curtain, I can hear the water. *Plink.* A pause long enough to count three heartbeats. *Plink.*

I have been here since the season a hunter caught me too far from the baffles.

I did not die—I was already crouched low, wedged half into a seam of fluted rock when its chest-sac snapped—but the *crack* did not need to touch my skin to unmake me. It hit the air like a falling slab and blew out the delicate hollows behind my ear-slits. For three Breaths afterward, there was nothing in my head at all except a high, thin whine, like a flier trapped in a jar. When that finally faded, the air had gone dead.

I can still feel the wind of a passing wing on my cheeks. I can still feel the hunters when they pad past the outer curtains, because their weight shifts the damp air and makes the pods clatter in a slow, heavy rhythm I can sense against my ribs. But I can no longer hear the seams in the noise. Out on the open terraces, where safety is knowing the exact distance between the *tick* of a flier and the *grind* of a grazer’s tongue, a dweller with dead air-ears is meat waiting to be stunned.

So I belong to the water now, and to the stone.

Most of my time is spent on my knees and palms in the shallow, ankle-deep pans where the ceiling drips fastest. The water here is cold and cloudy with dissolved chalk, smelling sharply of wet limestone and iron-rich silt. It is thick enough to leave a pale, gritty film on my shins when I climb out. Around my ankles, the ribbon-bodied filterers do not thrash the way they do in the outer basins; it is too quiet here, too poor in the drifting scraps of the crowd. Instead, the rims belong to the thread-thin worms.

I cannot feel them—they are smaller than the crease in my knuckle—but I know their work by touch. When I run my broad, splayed fingers along the scalloped lip of a pan, I can tell the fresh lime from the old stone. The old stone is slick and cold, worn smooth by water and bare heels. The new stone is faintly rough, porous as dried sponge, still soft enough under the thumbnail to dent if you press too hard. That is where the worms have woven their mucus-nets to catch the falling chalk.

My work is to guide the drips.

With a wedge of old grazer-chitin and a heavy shell-mallet, I chip shallow, winding grooves into the low limestone shelves above the pans, coaxing the condensation that beads along the arch to gather and fall where the walls are thin. If an alcove’s outer baffle has cracked—perhaps from a hunter’s passing shoulder, or the slow settling of the roof—I pack the gap with chalk-paste from the pan-floor and lay a groove above it, so the mineral water will weep down through the seam and cement it shut over the next dozen seasons.

It is slow work, and wet. The rust-colored biofilm loves these dampest corners; left alone, it creeps up the calves and under the nails, stinging like nettles. Every few hours I reach into the clay pot at my hip for a handful of crawler-mucus—cool, slick, and faintly sour-smelling—and smear it across my shoulders, my elbows, the backs of my knees. It softens the chalk-dry skin and keeps the slime from biting. Sometimes, when the scavengers come wandering back under the arch, they brush right past my shins without clicking their mandibles at me, fooled by the grease into thinking I am only another wet rock.

I am not entirely alone here. The stone still speaks, even to me.

The language of the air is gone, but the limestone carries everything. When I press my bare heels flat to the floor, I can feel the whole valley working miles away—the vast, continuous vibration of ten thousand grazers rasping the outer terraces, rising through my ankles like a low hum in the marrow. When someone approaches my alcove from the seams, I know it twenty yards before they reach the first pod-curtain, by the crisp, deliberate *tap—tap-tap* of a shell-mallet against the rock.

Usually, it is the young runners bringing dried flier-sinew, or cracked grazer-plates for my chisels, or fat scavengers tied in kelp-like strips of shed skin. When they step behind the last curtain, they crouch down in the dark beside me. They do not waste breath trying to whistle or click; they simply lean in until their forehead rests against mine, or their collarbone presses firm against my shoulder.

Then they hum.

It is a deep, chest-born sound that bypasses the ruined hollows of my ears entirely, travelling straight through bone into the fluid of my skull. It feels warm, like holding a sun-heated stone—though I have never felt a sun. In that hum, they tell me which herd has shifted to the eastern pans, which baffle is sagging near the great pillar, how many Breaths have passed since a hunter was last heard near the roosts. I hum back, low and steady, telling them where the drips are running strong, and which channels need clearing.

Sometimes, they do not come to trade.

Sometimes the mallet-tap on the stone is slower, uneven, weighted by two people carrying a third between them. When they part the curtain, I do not need to touch the newcomer’s face to know why they have brought them. The body does not hum. There is no itch of breath along its jaw-pits.

We carry them together to the deepest pans, far back where the ceiling drops so low even I must crawl on my belly to reach the rim. There is no biofilm here, no grazers, nothing but the cloudy water and the endless, patient *plink* of the roof. We ease the body into the ankle-deep chill and prop the shoulders gently against the pale, scalloped lip of the basin, arranging the long-fingered hands over the knees so the drips will fall across the chest and forehead.

Before we leave, I always run my palm along the rim beside them.

The shelf my hand rests on—the one that holds the water, that catches the lime, that turns the distant roar of the fliers back toward the open dark—is ribbed in places like a cage of long bones. Farther down, where the basin curves, there is a smooth, shallow dome that fits the hollow of my palm the way a skull does.

We do not stay long. When the Breath begins to turn, we crawl back out to the wider alcove.

I always know when it is coming, even here at the back of the world. The tiny pits along my jawbone begin to itch, prickling with the sudden drop in pressure as the distant weather shifts miles above our heads. A slow, cool draft snakes under the pod-curtains, carrying the clean, sharp scent of wet stone from caverns I will never walk. The curtains sway and rattle softly against my back.

Out beyond the arches, I feel it through the soles of my feet: a sudden, vast shudder in the limestone as millions of fliers tear loose from the upper walls and pour into the dark, drowning the hunters’ ears in a roaring avalanche of wings. The dwellers will be stepping out onto the terraces now, shaking their shell-pods, trading, finding each other in the storm.

I stay behind the curtains, palms pressed flat to the cooling floor, listening with my bones to the valley breathing around me, and waiting for the next drop to fall.
