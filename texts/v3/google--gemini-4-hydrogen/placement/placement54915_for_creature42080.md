I would not last an hour here as what I am now. My eyes would blister in the bounced glare off the wet calcite; my eardrums would bruise and weep under the steady pressure of the wind; my fingers would stiffen on the slick gypsum until I slid down into the runoff and lay there, shivering, a bright, noisy wreck in a world that has no use for either.

To be in this valley at all—to *find* myself in it rather than simply die in it—I would have to be made of its grammar.

I would find myself not out in the open, razor-ribbed reaches where the wind hits the first rank of towers at full throat, nor down in the lowest, greasiest chutes where the water sheets thickest. I would be deeper in, toward the leeward shoulder of a high ridge, where the travertine blades grow slightly thicker at their roots and crowd together until the alleys narrow to a handspan. There, ten feet above the slick floor, the fierce whiteness overhead dims to a bruised, wet silver, and the brassy scream of the valley drops an octave into a heavy, steady thrum.

That is where I would wedge myself: sideways, ribs compressed, knees and broad-padded heels locked against opposite walls of weeping stone.

I would be one of the dwellers, though even among them I think I would be known—if they have a word-silence for it—as one who lingers in the seams. My skin would be the color of leached bone, cold and slick as wet tallow, with a dark, greasy marbling of mineral-eating bacteria along the hollows of my spine where the runoff slows. My eyes would be nothing more than two pale, milky membranes, sealed shut against the glare, giving me only the gentle tilt of brighter above and dimmer below. I would not miss sight. Sight is for distances, and in a packed thicket of stone, there are no distances—only the width of the gap my shoulders are currently braced against, and the long, unbroken grain of the alley ahead.

Instead, I would have my jawbone, and my collarbones, and the flat of my sternum.

To be this creature is to live inside an instrument that never stops playing. Even asleep—locked upright between two pinching fins, breathing slowed to match the steady weep of the water—I would feel the valley’s chord humming in my teeth. Every blade around me has its own note, fixed by its height and the weight of the water-film sliding down its face. Most of the time, the chord is so constant it disappears, becoming the simple texture of being alive.

What I would actually *do*—what would make me myself, even here—is listen for the small imperfections in it.

I would spend my waking hours chimneying slowly through the tighter, quieter crevices where the wind drops out, scraping the slate-gray bacterial sheen with a wide, rasping tongue, prying the blind, thick-shelled limpets from the rock with chisel-edged nails. When I needed to speak to another dweller—perhaps to mark a dense bed of arthropods, or simply to know where someone else was braced three alleys over—I would not open my mouth. I would press both palms and the flat of my chest hard against the vibrating edge of a calcite fin, taking its tremor into my own rubbery fat until the stone went dead.

A crisp, deliberate hole in the roar. Hold it for three slow heartbeats. Release. Wait for the answering *blank* in the bone behind my jaw.

That is the part of this life that feels closest to me: a language made entirely of stopping things. No shouting over the wind, no adding more noise to a world already full of it—just stepping in front of a single note so that someone else, braced in the wet silver twenty yards away, feels the absence of it like a hand laid gently on their collarbone.

And if I had a habit, a small quirk that set me apart along the grain of the valley, it would be with the bones.

When a pale glider finally clips a crest high above—when the standing wave in a fin shudders with that brief, messy flutter of feathers wedging ten feet up—I would be among the ones who converge slowly along the wet chutes. After the marrow is sucked and the tendons swallowed, while the others drift back toward the limpet beds, I think I would linger with the long, hollow wing-bones.

I would carry one back to my favorite leeward seam, where two blades pinch just tightly enough to hold my ribs when I sleep. I would wedge the dry, reed-light bone upright into a wet crack—not only to mark the place, though it would do that too, but to feel what it does to the pitch. The wind-hum passing over an open hollow shifts the note of the stone by a fraction, softening the edge of the vibration, adding a faint, reedy undertone to the steady thrum against my ribs.

I would brace my cheek against the rock, close my milky lids over nothing at all, and stay there—pale, cold, and unhurried—listening to the small, tuned silence I had made inside the wind.
