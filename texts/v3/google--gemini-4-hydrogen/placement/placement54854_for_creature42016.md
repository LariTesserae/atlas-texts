I would not be inside the newly ruptured blister. The air there is too stretched, the floor too much like a drumhead, and I do not have the dweller’s gift for stillness—that flat-palmed patience that lets them share a room with thunder. If I were dropped into that bright, booming oval, I would flinch at the first drop, and the chamber would throw my flinch back at me until the walls shook.

Instead, I would find myself further back, in the older wood—the dry, bypassed arteries of the root-system where the sap no longer runs.

In a world where roots have replaced geology, there must be miles of dead taproots: hollow, winding tunnels where the inner pith rotted away long before the tree forgot which way was down. There is no stolen noon here. There is only the dark, the cool, papery rustle of dried bast underfoot, and a smell like attic dust and old cedar. The hydraulic groan of the living xylem is distant here, muffled by layers of dense, dormant heartwood into a low, steady purr you feel in your ribs rather than your teeth.

If I belong anywhere in this world, it is in these quiet, structural hollows. I would not be pale and waxy like an inner shoot; I would be dry-skinned and dark-eyed, dusted with fine wood-powder and pale spores, my joints stiffened from squeezing through narrow, knotty clefts. I wouldn’t carry a shard of vanished stone to pry living seams open. I wouldn’t want to make a room. I would only carry a thin strip of dried, fibrous bark—something soft enough to chew for the faint, lingering sweetness of old sap, or to press into a damp crease where a living root grazes a dead one, wicking moisture silently without ever letting a drop fall.

My eyes would be wide and useless in the dark, so I would navigate the way the rest of the root-system does: by temperature, by grain, and by the faint tremors of the biological engine running somewhere behind the walls. Living wood is warm and taut; dead wood is cool and gives a little under the knuckles. When you have no sky and no stone, warmth is north, and silence is ground.

And yet, even in the dead channels, you cannot escape the blisters entirely. They are too loud, and their light leaks.

I imagine myself moving along a narrow, dry corridor of crumbling heartwood, one shoulder brushing the cool wall, when the reality of the place begins to slip its gears ahead of me. First comes the smell—the sharp, green tang of crushed stems, arriving before there is any moisture to carry it. Then a faint, impossible shadow stretches across the dry floor at my feet, leaning slightly upward, pointing back toward a wall that looks completely solid.

That is how I would find the blister: not by prying it open, but by stumbling upon a place where the wood has grown thin enough to let the stolen noon bleed through.

I would stop there, pressing my cheek—not against the booming membrane of a floor, but against a wall of translucent, ambered heartwood. Through an inch of living grain, the light would filter in, no longer crisp and shadow-sharp, but softened into the thick, dusty gold of late afternoon. I would feel the low hum of the xylem vibrating against my cheekbone, and hear the muffled *thud* of condensation striking cambium on the other side, like someone knocking softly on a heavy door.

Through the glowing grain, I might catch the blur of a shadow lifting upward—the dweller’s raised wrist as they twist their pale stone in the seam, or the tiny, dark smudge of the blind woodlouse waiting by the basin. I would never try to break through to them. A room like that is already full with two kinds of patience; a third would only crowd the air and make the floor boom.

Instead, I would sit in the dry, cool dark on my side of the wall, letting the borrowed warmth soak into my knees. I would drink the thin seep of moisture that always sweats through the edges of a young wound, and watch the golden blur shift and stutter as the wood heals backward and snaps open again.

I would stay only as long as the light lasted. Eventually, the dweller would pull their stone from the seam and unknot their three lengths of fiber. The muffled *thuds* would stop. The seam would heal shut for good, and the stolen noon would go out like a candle pinched between wet fingers, leaving the heartwood opaque and cool again.

Then I would rise, brush the dry pith from my palms, and turn back into the dark corridors—carrying nothing with me except the memory of where the warmth had been, listening with my fingertips for the next thin place where the tree was remembering the sky.
