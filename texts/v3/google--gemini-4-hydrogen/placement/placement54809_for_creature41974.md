I would not begin on the flatlands. Up there, under the thin, blinding blue, there is too much light and nothing to hold onto—only the wind trying to peel you off the bark and scatter you like dry husk.

I would find myself deeper down, where the glare thins to an ash-gray twilight and the canyon walls rise on either side like the ribs of a buried hull. Here, the continent’s deafening roar breaks apart into something you can actually live inside. Every hollow reed in the wall has its own pitch; every fissure has its own breath. If you press the flat of your palm—heavy, horn-shod, crusted white with silica—against the dry lignin, the whole plateau stops being a blank expanse and becomes a room with furniture you can touch.

At first, I wouldn’t even know what I was, only what I was listening for. 

My eyes—if you could call the narrow, sunken slits behind my milky horn-lids eyes—would be useless here, and glad of it. The real seeing happens in the chest. My ribcage is a frame of hollow bone, stretched tight with pleated, papery drum-skin that hums in sympathy with whatever the floor is doing. Three miles north, a dynamo-spire is nearing the end of its cycle; I can feel the low, rhythmic *thrum-thrum* of its fermentation chambers in my heels, slow as a sleeping heart. Closer by, somewhere up on the pale steppe, a keratin-plated grazer is boring into a vascular ridge: *tap-tap-scurry, tap-tap-pause*. 

And right here, at shoulder height in the canyon wall, something is out of tune.

That is what draws me upright, off my knuckles, balancing heavy-hipped in the cool draft. A fissure in the silicified reeds—one that ought to hold a steady, low fifth, marking this bend as a safe wintering hollow—has begun to drift. The note is going flat, frayed at the edges by a season of silica-laden gales scouring the wood smooth. If it goes unchecked, in a few months it will whistle sharp and discordant, sounding like a methane leak, and whoever comes down this canyon next will turn back onto the open steppe in the middle of a dust squall.

So I lean in. My face is nearly featureless, built to deflect glare and grit rather than to speak, but my jaw is heavy and my molars are flat and hard. I gnaw a fresh, sharp bevel into the rim of the hollow reed, spitting out the dry, tasteless splinters. Then I work a wad of resin-wax between my teeth until it softens, and press it into a hairline crack just below the notch. 

The pitch snaps back into place. Clean. Steady. A fifth that you can feel in your teeth.

This, I realize, is what I am: not a builder, not a hunter, not anything the wind would bother to remember. I am a **listener**—one of the quiet-stepping, cold-blooded people who belong to the plateau’s intervals. Nothing we do lasts. We leave no walls, no roads, no marks that the next storm won’t sand away. Our entire history is just maintenance: re-chewing the wax, re-cutting the flutes, stamping coded *thrums* with a calcified heel into a vascular ridge so someone five miles away knows the squall is clearing.

Still, there is a restlessness in it. You can spend days in the canyons tending the chords, scooping up the occasional stunned beetle or chewing the oily moisture out of a fallen droplet of resin-glass, and feel yourself slowly turning into part of the dead wood.

Especially when the sun drops.

Night on the plateau is not a gentle thing. The sky bruises into a freezing, star-pricked indigo, and the wind—though it never truly dies—slacks off just enough for the frost to crust over the vascular mats. Without the glare, without the constant thrash of the gales, my cold blood begins to thicken. My rubbery, sap-oiled hide stiffens inside its chalky crust. The vibrations in the floor grow muffled, as if someone had stuffed dry moss into my chest-drums. I can barely read the spires anymore; the thirty-mile map shrinks to the length of my own reach.

That is when I have to go up.

I climb out of the canyon’s mouth on all fours, knuckle-walking low against the grooved, woody pavement, staying out of the wind’s teeth. A mile away across the pale dark, a solitary dynamo-spire rises like a leafless mast. Its crown is spinning even now, copper-veined fronds grinding through the cold air, pouring a silent river of static down its trunk.

I don’t wait for the detonation. I’m not here for the roasted scavengers or the warm glass. I approach between cycles, when the ground beneath my palms is quiet except for the faint, deep *groan* of the bellows-slugs shifting in the dark chambers far below.

Near the base of the massive trunk, I find a damp, resinous fissure and wedge the tip of my long, trailing tail-tendon firmly inside. Grounded. Then I rise up onto my hind legs, reach out with one mineral-crusted palm, and lay it just shy of the bristling, needle-like bark.

The spire never sleeps. 

Tiny, blue-white arcs snap across the dry gap before my skin even touches the needles. The smell of sharp ozone and hot pine hits me all at once. My chalky hide crackles; the current leaps into my palm, races along the conductive sheaths of my nerves, and floods my chest. 

My pleated drums pull taut. My stiff joints twitch, then unlock, flooding with sudden, electric heat. And in that fraction of a second, the whole continent wakes up inside my skull—every hollow reed for ten miles around humming in crystal-clear, terrifying harmony, from the high whistling shriek of the canyon mouths to the deep, pressurized *thrum* building right beneath my heels.

I hold on until the groan under the floor sharpens into a metallic *ping*—the ironwood valve’s latch straining, seconds from the *crack*. 

Then I let go, drop back to my knuckles, and vanish into the ash-gray dark of the canyon, charged and wakeful, ready to keep the wood in tune for one more cycle.
