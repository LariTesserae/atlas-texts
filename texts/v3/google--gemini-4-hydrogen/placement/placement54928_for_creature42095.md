I would not be down in the central tangle, where the shelves stack six deep and the air is a solid broth of shed wings and spore-chaff. Nor would I be up against the selenite ceiling, where the raw light pours through the cracks like hot flour and the gliders slam into the upper reeds hard enough to shake the teeth in your skull.

I would be on the shoulder of the basin—the seam where the salt-crust wall meets the first great buttresses of cork-wood.

Here, the floor is still rock: raw, sulfur-stained shale pitched at a steep angle, dusted in warm talc that slides under the heel if you aren’t careful. The plants don’t grow straight up from it so much as they *brace* against it, driving thick, resin-gorged roots into the cracks like wedges and throwing their fibrous cables outward to hold the rest of the valley from tearing itself loose in the thermals. It is the one place where something still touches something that doesn't move.

If you looked for me there, you wouldn't see me at first. You’d see a low blister of woven reed-bark wedged into a shelf of rock, plastered thick with amber wax and roofed with three overlapping carapace-plates from a dead glider. Outside, a bundle of cured sinew, a gourd-sized seed-bladder cured hollow for carrying oil, and three lengths of femur ground to chisel-points, hanging from a peg of horn.

I would be one of the *anchor-minders*—though the basin has no grand names for things, only what a person’s hands are good for before the next collapse.

My skin would be the color of smoked tallow, tight over the knuckles and ribs, kept supple with daily rubbings of warm ester-oil so the updrafts don't crack me open like an overripe pod. Around my eyes and mouth, the pale wax would be thick enough to catch the dust. My hair—what little is left of it—would be shorn to stubble and stiffened with pitch, and my ear-plugs would be rolled tight from the pith of young reeds, changed every three sleeps when the salt-grit works into them and starts to chafe.

What I do is listen to the tension.

Every major bridge and suspended shelf out in the hazy yellow middle is held by primary cables—thick, braided cords of cellulose that drink oil from the deep rock and pull taut as bowstrings when the heat rises. Most people read them when they have to: a tapper hooks a flint gaff into a line to send a three-twang call, or a scavenger checks a bridge with a heel before crossing. They are always moving, always chasing the next fallen tower or fresh sap-run.

I stay by the rock where the cables end.

I sit barefoot on a flat slab of shale—the leather soles peeled back so the calloused skin takes the rock directly—and I keep one hand on the braided anchor-loop where it cinches around a fossilized resin-trunk. Through the soles of my feet comes the deep, steady bass of the basin: the blind borers grinding their obsidian teeth into the salt-plates fifty yards down, the slow shudder of a thermal building in a fissure. Through my palm comes the treble: the frantic, high-pitched *singing* of the lattice out in the glare.

When a cable is healthy, it hums like a fat wasp—a warm, even vibration that sits comfortably in the wrist. When the dry air bakes the oil out of it, or when a swarm of grazers has chewed half its strands through two towers over, the pitch climbs. It gets thin and sharp, a wire-whine that makes the fillings of your back teeth ache.

That is when I uncoil my gut-line, tie on my horn visor, and go out onto the shelves—not to harvest, not to scavenge, just to cut the bad line before it snaps and whips back through a blind, or to splice in a fresh green shoot while the wood is still pliable enough to knot.

It is solitary work, mostly. Out on the high spans, with the milky glare pressing down and the stridulators sawing away in the light-shafts until the air feels thick enough to chew, you stop thinking in words altogether. Words need quiet to stand up in. Out there, there is only weight, balance, and the small, dry arithmetic of where to put your next foot: *this strut is hollow, this one is swelling, this cable is singing too high.*

When I meet someone on a narrow bridge—a sap-tapper weighted down with reed-joints, or a pair of bone-gatherers hauling a glider-shield between them—we don't waste breath. We flash quick, sulfur-yellowed fingers through the drifting chaff: *Bridge ahead is soft. Borers at the base. Two towers east, a shelf just went down.* Sometimes, if it is someone from my own cluster of blinds, we lean in, brace our feet against the swaying wood, and press our foreheads together, humming low in the chest until the bone carries the greeting across—a dull, warm buzz behind the eyes that says *still here, still dry, still holding.*

People think a place with no memory must be empty, or tragic. They think because nothing lasts—because a path notched at dawn is swallowed by amber shoots before dark, and your own dead are woven into the rafters before their joints go cold—there is nothing to hold onto.

But that isn't how it feels from the rock.

When I crawl back into my baffled hut at the end of a shift, pull the wax from my ears, and let the roar drop to a heavy, distant surf behind the carapace-plates, I don't miss yesterday. Yesterday was a brittle shelf that already fell. What I have is the small, dim pocket of still air, the taste of toasted husk and turpentine sap at the back of my throat, and the faint, steady tremor of the shale under my knees—telling me that the basin is still loud, still hungry, and still throwing itself up toward the salt sky.

I scrape the day’s resin from my knuckles with a shard of flint, oil the hinge of my visor, and wait for the pitch of the cables to change.
