At first, there is only the wrongness of my own speed.

If I arrive here as I am—carrying the habit of quick breaths, of eyes darting from ridge to ridge to map an exit—the basin quietly refuses me. The air is too thick to hurry through; every inhale is a warm, mineral weight that insists on being taken slowly. There is no wind to cool the skin or carry a voice more than a few paces. Up on the weathered rim, looking out over the flat white ocean of clouds, I would only be a visitor waiting to leave.

So I would have to step down off the rim, past the gnarled, epiphyte-draped trees, into the broad saddle where the light breaks across wet stone.

Here, in the middle terraces, the valley’s real work is loud enough to feel in the teeth and shins, even though the air stays hushed. *Tock, tock, tock*—water dropping from the tip of a giant fern onto a waxy, copper-tinted leaf below. A soft, continuous *hiss* from a fissure cushioned in liverwort. And then, rising straight through the soles of whatever feet I have: the *thump*.

Forty seconds. Then the *thump*, and the pressurized sigh from the vents.

To belong here, I would have to shed whatever in me expects a faster clock. A human pulse—sixty, seventy, eighty beats a minute—feels frantic in this steam, like a moth battering itself against a lantern. If I stand in the shallow, braided channel long enough, listening to the forty-second piston beneath the basalt, my own tempo begins to feel like a thin, nervous static. I don’t want to be the dragonfly patrolling the updrafts, all quickness and appetite, nor the seed-pod on the bank waiting to snap its small blue spark into the air and start over.

I want to be down in the brine, where the current actually travels.

At first I think I might be one of the pale, translucent amphibians—cool-bellied, gliding between the drowned root-mats, reading the subtle bends in the water’s voltage. There is an appeal to that: to have skin so thin that the valley’s electricity is not something you *hear* about, but something you feel as clearly as a hand brushing your ribs. To shelter in the dark, mossy shadow of a dweller’s flank when the bank-side pods discharge, safe in the lee of something ancient.

Yet the amphibians are still hunters, still darting after larvae, still threaded with the small, sharp urgency of hunger. Even in this paradise of warm steam, they have to chase.

As I linger in the shallows—letting the warm, stinging water creep higher, letting the mist bead and run—something heavier settles into place. It isn’t a sudden transformation, more like noticing that I haven’t shifted my weight in twenty minutes, and that I don’t need to.

If I am to truly live in this basin, I think I am not darting *between* the boulders. I am beginning to suspect I *am* one of the smaller, younger dwellers—not yet centuries-broad, not yet so laden with copper that a forty-second surge can barely lift me, but already committed to the floor of the channel.

Let’s test the weight of it.

Four splayed, pillar-like legs, flat-palmed and clawless, gripping the slick mineral crust so the surge doesn’t slide me downstream. My back is dark and rough, already collecting its first seams of pale sulfur and dull green-brown moss in the creases between the plates. A heavy-shelled snail is working its slow, scraping path across my left shoulder; I don’t shake it off. Its grazing keeps the copper from locking my joints stiff, and in exchange I am a warm, steady island that does not sink.

This is what I didn’t understand from the rim: how good it feels to have no fingers. To have nothing to grip, nothing to build, nothing to pry open. Only broad palms to press against the stone, and a pale, thin-skinned belly to lower into the gravel when the tremor begins.

And the tremor *always* begins.

Thirty-eight, thirty-nine, forty.

The rock beneath my chest gives its muffled, subterranean *thump*. My neck-frills spread wide without my having to decide it, tasting the fresh rush of copper and zinc spilling out of the vents. The brine washes across my throat, stinging-warm, and the lattice of veins beneath my pale skin drinks the low-voltage current straight from the flow. It isn’t food, and it isn’t breath—it is closer to waking up, or being tuned. For a few seconds, the whole basin hums through me as cleanly as through the reeds, and there is no boundary between the valley’s work and my own pulse.

Then the water settles. The capacitor-shrubs along the bank resume their quiet, metallic ripening.

In the interval, I rise—slowly, feeling the mineral crust creak faintly in my joints—and take three deliberate paces up the channel. My broad tail drags through the gravel bed, turning over a cluster of fallen copper nodules and pale sulfur ingots so the stream doesn’t choke on its own harvest. A pair of translucent amphibians scatter from under my belly, not in fear, just making room for the tide of my passing.

Up ahead, where three braided streams widen into a flat, steaming pan, two other dark shapes lie half-submerged in the mist. From above, a visitor on the ridges would see three rounded basalt boulders, draped in liverwort, crowned with resting dragonflies.

I settle into the shallow water a body’s length away from them, lowering my chin to the surface, and wait for the next forty-second *thump* to pass.

When the quiet interval returns, I don’t lift my head to look at them. There is nothing to see through the steam, and no reason to trouble the heavy air with sound. Instead, I let a slow, patterned tingle ripple out from the organs along my flank—not a shock, just a warm shift in resistance, a small bend in the brine’s steady current. *Here,* it says, or maybe only: *Heavy. Warm. Still moving.*

A few seconds later, the water answers. A broader, deeper pulse comes back from the larger boulder to my left, carrying the faint, metallic taste of long decades—the signature of a hide thick with precipitated ore, of joints that take longer to rise. Then a third, lighter current joins from downstream.

We lie there in the terraced pan, an archipelago of wet stone, trading slow sentences of voltage through the electrolyte while the ferns drip *tock, tock, tock* around us. Once, the smaller one beside me shifts forward in the gravel, lifts its blunt snout just clear of the surface film, and brings it close to mine. Across the damp millimeter of air between us, a tiny, dry *snap* of pale blue static jumps—brief as a blink, gone before the mist can swallow it.

Then the stone trembles under our bellies, and we flatten ourselves against the porous basalt to drink the surge together.

I wouldn’t mind staying until the copper grows too thick to lift. Until the waxy reeds send their metallic roots over my ribs, and the last forty-second *thump* finds me already turned to stone, still carrying the current without having to move at all.
