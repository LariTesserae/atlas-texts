I would not be in the center, where the bladder-fronds crowd like pale grapes and the water-veins twist around the cairn. That middle belongs to the one with the four-fingered hands, the one who keeps the knots tight and the pebbles balanced.

I would be further out, at the seam where the chamber stops being green and becomes stone again.

There is a narrow, angled crease behind the hardened grey clay—a place where the basalt split unevenly, leaving a lip of rock no wider than a thumb, too tight for the tender’s chalky knuckles to wedge into, too sharp for the velvet bryophyte to have carpeted yet. Here, gravity has given up entirely; the crease is neither ceiling nor floor, just a hard, wet *against*. The bruised slate-blue dimness barely reaches this far back, and what water fills the gap is thick with the metallic tang of fresh cleavage—iron and sulfur weeping straight from the wound in the earth.

If I am here, I have no need for eyes, and no use for limbs that might snag in the ribbon-weed.

I would be low and flat, pressed so close to the basalt that at first you might take me for a thicker smear of the mineral crust itself. My back would be the color of wet slate—dark, matte, slightly grainy with the microscopic grit that sheds from the walls—while my underside would be pale and soft, a cold, dense foot sealing itself against the razor-edged stone. No shell to crack against the tight walls, no delicate gill-fringes to be torn by a shifting frond; only a rim of tiny, stubby feelers tucked under my mantle, tasting the rock rather than the water.

I would not move by mooring, the way the tender does, pulling itself from clay-groove to knotted loop across an afternoon. I would move at the speed of the stone dissolving.

It takes me days to cross the width of a hand. Because of this, the water around me never quite catches me in the act of leaving. Where the tender’s slow reach leaves a pale, smeared ghost of an elbow hanging in the dimness for hours, my own afterimages never pull free of my skin. They stay nested inside me, thickening my edges slightly, so that I look a little blurred in the dark—less like a creature in motion and more like a bruise on the rock that is very gradually changing its mind about where to sit.

I spend my time grazing the rawest parts of the fracture.

Where the basalt has split clean and still sheds its invisible splinters, I creep over the edge and drink the thin, sour film of bacteria and iron-weep that gathers on the wound. My passage leaves the stone slightly less sharp than it was, coated in a cold, faint trail of mucus that catches the drifting grit and holds it down—the first quiet bedding where, in a few years or a few decades, a stray filament of moss might finally take hold.

I do not hear the chamber the way the tender does, through the drift of thermal currents. I hear it through my belly, pressed flat against the ribs of the earth. I feel the *tick* of a detaching slate-flake as a tiny, dry shudder through the rock long before the grain settles into the weeds. I feel the hour-long turn of the cold water as a faint, rhythmic pressure against my back, like a hand resting lightly and then lifting away.

And sometimes, once every long while, the dimness at the mouth of my crease thickens.

A pale, smeared shadow arrives—first the echo of a wrist, then the wrist itself, long and thin, its four fingers tipped with dark-creased pads. The tender is bringing a loose shard of slate it found near the cairn, tucking it safely into the tight gap where it won’t sever the ribbon-weed.

Its fingertip brushes my mantle—cold, calloused, dusted with the same grey grit I taste. Neither of us flinches. The tender does not snatch at me, and I have nowhere to hurry to even if I wanted to. I simply pull my soft rim a fraction of an inch tighter against the basalt, letting the sharp flake settle into the crease beside me, and wait for the pale hand to withdraw—and for the faint, unsolid twin of its fingers to thin back into the dark.

Then I return to the stone, smoothing the next millimeter of the wound, keeping the edge from biting too deep into the quiet.
