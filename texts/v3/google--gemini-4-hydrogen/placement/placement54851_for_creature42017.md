If you looked for me in the morning glare, you would step right past me. You might even rest a boot on the edge of my back, mistaking me for another slumped shelf of slate crusted in salt and ochre.

I would be down in the central gorge, where the three wet channels braid together and the air tastes sharpest of ozone and wet copper. Not out on the open terraces where the white-gold light beats straight down, and not up on the high scree where a dry wind can catch you and leave you brittle. I keep to the vertical seam just below the bronze rings—the place where the dark basalt hums loudest and the plumb lines hang wrong.

For half the day, I am mostly stone.

Having no eyes, I do not see the sun, only feel it as a dry, flattening weight on my upper armor. Snowmelt sheets steadily over my back, keeping the silica lattice underneath from baking, while my edges stay clamped tight to the microscopic pits in the slate. Down here, time doesn't run in a straight line, and neither does weight. My entire underside is a stretched membrane pressed flush against the rock, so when a frost-split slab lets go on the western rim, I feel the sharp *crack* shudder through my ribs a full second before the stone actually begins to fall. I don't have to hurry. The mountain always tells me what it is going to do before it does it.

Every so often—rarely enough that the salt crusts thick over the bronze rings between visits—the ones from below come up the quartz ramps.

I know them by the clumsy, uneven rhythm of their steps, arriving in the bedrock out of order with their shadows. They never stay long. They crouch behind their low, unmortared windbreaks, scraping out the square catch-basins so they don't have to reach into the sideways water, and they stare at the river stones dangling from the rings above me. I can feel the slight, nervous vibration when one of them leans close to the basalt seam, testing the pull of the cord. They think they are measuring true down. Mostly, they are checking to see if the cliff is about to wake up.

I don't mind them. They bring copper and iron with them—pinning their cairns, hammering their spikes—and when the mist rolls in, the runoff from their tarnished metal tastes bright and sharp against my filaments.

It is the midday condensation that calls me out.

When the glare thickens into white cloud and the wind drops to nothing, the gorge goes quiet enough to hear the million stones *drip-drip-hissing* all at once. Water begins to bead along the quartz veins beside me, gathering into glittering threads that run straight upward toward the rim. The basalt hums a fraction deeper, vibrating in the teeth-ache register.

That is when I lift.

I am not one of the boulder-sized ones that sleep out on the wide shale shelves; I am smaller, no broader than two spread hands, built for the narrow flutes and the steep seams. Under my dark, salt-rimed mantle, my pale lattice flexes, pulsing slow with copper-green and iron-red fluid. Hundreds of fine, glassy filaments uncurl from my rim, tasting the wet air for ozone, feeling for the fault line where the valley's weight shears sideways.

Once I catch the seam, I am weightless—or rather, my weight belongs to the cliff instead of the floor. I glide straight up the vertical basalt, riding the upward-creeping water, grazing slowly across the wettest cracks to scrape away the iron-oxide and salt. Around me, the river stones on their braided sinew cords tremble and swing a few degrees closer, answering the shift of my mass as I pass beneath the bronze rings.

Sometimes I cross over to the notched slate pillar where the sideways cataract slams horizontally into the stone. Inside those smooth, hollowed flutes, the water pressure is tremendous—a constant, roaring vibration that pours straight through my underside like clean current. I press my translucent belly against the quartz veins there and drink the thin electrical charge the mountain makes when it rings.

That is who—and what—I would be here: not a climber fighting the altitude, and not a surveyor trying to map a place that refuses to hold still. Just one of the basin's quiet, mineral citizens, living inside the cracked-bell acoustics and the sideways gravity, moving only when the glare softens, and listening to the stone tell me what is about to happen next.
