You would not find me inside the bell-shaped hollow—not for long, anyway. My lungs are too small for that kind of cold, and my eyes are built for daylight and peat-smoke, not pewter twilight. 

You would find me half a mile back from the cleft, where the salt-marsh gives up and turns to heather and thin turf, or else out on the mudflats at low water with a woven basket slung across my back. I would be one of the people who live along the seam between the tide and the limestone: a gatherer of glasswort and mussels, a cutter of bog-myrtle and marsh-reed, someone with salt dried white in the creases of my coat and mud permanently under my fingernails.

My own shelter is low and crooked, built into a dry fold of gray rock where the wind passes over the roof instead of into the door. I keep a small fire there of driftwood and dried peat, which smells of brine and resin, and I spend the slack hours stripping willow rods for baskets or mending the hems of my woolens. It is a quiet life, full of small, sharp noises—the crack of a crab’s shell, the hiss of wet wood in the hearth, the rattle of dry sedge when the sea-breeze shifts.

But there are days when the wind off the water will not stop shifting, and the small noises pile up until my head feels like a jar full of dry peas. That is when I pull my boots on and walk down toward the marsh.

I do not look for the seam in the limestone with my eyes. The briars grow thick over it, and from ten paces away it looks like any other crack in the weathered rock. Instead, I walk slowly along the low shelf until I feel it—a faint, steady thrumming coming up through the leather of my soles, setting a tiny itch in the bones of my ankles. Then I know I am standing over the throat.

Squeezing past the threshold is always clumsy for me. My wool coat catches on the briars; my shoulders scrape the wet stone. The moment I drop into the hollow, the cold hits my face like a flat palm, and the sound takes my breath away. It is not something you hear with your ears. It is something that fills your chest the way water fills a bucket, pushing out whatever else was in there.

I always go straight to the leeward wall, stepping carefully over the slick, moss-padded boulders, watching the pale tips of the hart’s-tongue ferns blur and shiver in the gloom. Behind the crescent of stacked cobbles, the pressure breaks just enough for a person to sit without aching. I tuck my knees up, pull my collar high, and let my back rest against the cold limestone.

For a long time, I used to wonder about the people who built that low wall. I would look at the flat hearth-stone with its dead, cold charcoal, and run a thumb along the notched tally-marks scratched into the soft rock shelf—not counting them, just feeling the little interruptions under my skin. Whoever they were, they had been gone a long time. Their willow frame leaned against the wall, gray and salt-cured, and their flint scraper sat undisturbed in its dry crack. I never touched the scraper, and I never tried to light a fire on their hearth. A fire would have felt like an argument in a room where something much larger was already speaking.

It took me three winters to notice that the room was being kept.

After a hard December gale, when the sea outside had hammered the cliffs for days, I came down to the cleft expecting to find the cobble wall shaken loose. A few stones in the middle had always been prone to shifting; in high water, they used to chatter against each other—a dry, irritating *clack-clack* that cut right across the cavern’s deep drone.

When I sat down behind the wall, the chattering was gone. I reached out in the dimness and felt along the seams between the stones. Wedged tightly into the cracks were thick, damp plugs of dark-emerald feather moss, squeezed free of their water and packed down hard by blunt, heavy fingers.

I sat very still after that, letting my eyes strain into the pewter shadow along the far rim of the floor. I saw the slick boulders. I saw the sluggish gray salamanders waiting in their little cups of condensation. Once, a rock-wren dropped through the high skylight like a falling pebble and vanished into an alcove above my head. If anything else was there, it wore the color of the wet stone so well that I looked right through it.

I did not go looking for it. On the marsh, you learn early not to pry into every crevice you find; things that live in the dark have their own reasons for staying there, and most of them do not want company.

Still, I come back when the tide is rising. I sit behind the cobbles with my eyes closed, my hands flat on the trembling floor, and wait for the sea to push harder into the throat below. Sometimes, when the drone climbs to its purest, heaviest pitch, the vibration in the stone seems to double—thickening all at once, as if another throat somewhere in the dark has opened to match it. The air turns solid against my ribs. The rattle in my head smooths out, flat and quiet as a mudflat at dawn.

When the tide turns slack and the cold begins to bite too deep into my marrow, I stand up slowly, careful not to dislodge the moss between the stones. I squeeze back out through the briars into the smell of bog-myrtle and salt, and walk home across the marsh with the hum still fading from the soles of my boots.
