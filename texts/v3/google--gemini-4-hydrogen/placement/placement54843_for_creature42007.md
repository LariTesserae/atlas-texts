The first thing that happens—before I know what I am, or where my hands are—is that I try to breathe too fast, and the shelf punishes me for it.

It is an old habit from somewhere lower down, where air has weight and coldness to it. Here, pulling a quick breath only fills the throat with warm, wet glare. My vision grays at the corners; the sharp black planes of the cliff face tilt and swim. So I have to stop. I have to let my ribs drop and wait, the way the water-driven pistons wait at the bottom of their stroke before the pressure builds again.

*Shhh-thunk.*

Only when I match that pace—drawing the steamy haze down slow and deep, letting it sit in the chest until the oxygen peels loose from the water—does the world steady itself.

I am sitting, I realize, though not on the stone. The stone is alive with stray current; even through thick vulcanized rubber you can feel it tingling in the heels like pins and needles, and bare skin against the wet slate would make your teeth ache. No, I am wedged onto a dryish ledge of timber and gutta-percha scrap, high up on the flank of the third condenser tower, where the algae has been scraped back to the bare, warm iron.

From here, I can see the whole notch at once, though *see* is the wrong word when the noon sun is bouncing off the cloud-deck below. Without the smoked mica pulled down over my eyes, the open fourth side of the cliff is just a sheet of white fire. Everything inside the notch is either blindingly wet or velvet-dark under the fern-canopy.

I look down at my hands. They aren’t the hands of someone who just climbed up here. The skin is pale and puckered, soft as the aerial roots dangling from the overhangs, with a chalky line of mineral salt dried into the creases of the wrists. Under the nails, and packed into the cracks around the knuckles, is that stubborn, bruised green of tarnished copper.

So I have been here a while. Or I have always been here, and only just woke up enough to notice the shape of it.

Still, I am not one of the two down by the pressure-house. I can feel them through the iron of the condenser tower—not hear them, because the copper relief stack is screaming its steady, endless white noise into the sky—but feel them. A slow, rhythmic *tink, tink* travels up the riveted flank under my thighs: someone chipping mineral scale off a brass valve-stem, unhurried, economical. A minute later, the counterweight drops inside the pump-house with a dull, belly-deep *boom* that shakes the mist off the fern fronds.

If there are two down there, and there are never more than two or three, then I am the third.

It takes a little longer to figure out *which* third I am. The shelf doesn’t keep spare people; every pair of hands here is part of the balance, like the relief valves or the ferns that drink the runoff. If I am sitting up on the condenser flank with a rag of oiled hide tucked into my apron belt and a length of horn-handled wire-scraper resting across my knees, it’s because this is where the shelf needs weight.

I lean out—slowly, always slowly, so the thin air doesn’t take my head—and look at the porcelain insulators bolted into the sheer glassy-black rock beside the tower. They are glazed white, or they would be if the emerald moss and algae didn’t creep up them an inch a day. Below me, the wrist-thick insulated cables loop down through the wet green thicket, gleaming slick where the condensation beads on them, and then vanish straight over the lip of the precipice into the bright, bottomless cloud-tops.

That’s it, then. The ones below tend the heart—the boiler, the sight-gauges, the great oiled rim of the flywheel turning twice a minute in its bed of ferns. My work is the skin and the edge.

I unhook my scraper, reach out with one heavy, salt-crusted arm, and begin to pare the slick green film from the nearest porcelain bell. It comes away in wet, rubbery ribbons that fall into the moss below. The motion is small, almost lazy. If I rushed it, my heart would hammer against my ribs like a trapped bird, and in ten seconds I’d be dizzy enough to slip off the tower. Here, you don’t fight the algae; you simply outlast it, returning to the same insulator every few days with the patience of the mineral water itself.

Halfway through the third insulator, something shifts in the vibration under my boots.

It isn’t the *shhh-thunk* of the pistons, and it isn’t the chest-rattling hum of the transformer bank. It’s sharper—two quick, flat raps against a warm conduit, followed by a pause, then one long, heavy strike. Metal on metal, travelling clean beneath the roar of the steam-stack and straight up through the soles of my rubber boots into my shinbones.

*Bearing. Dry. Flywheel.*

I don’t call back; my mouth is busy with the slow work of breathing. Instead, I slide the horn handle of my scraper out of my belt and tap the condenser’s iron rib twice—short, dull blows that sink into the metal and carry down to the pressure-house. *Heard. Coming.*

I climb down the tower one rung at a time, letting my weight settle fully on each wet iron bar before I move the next foot. On the ground, the warm mist closes around my shoulders. The ferns brush my bare, scarred arms with broad leaves veined in faint copper-green. Near the flywheel pit, where the giant wheel groans around and sheds sheets of warm water from its rim, one of the other keepers is already standing motionless, one palm pressed flat against the bearing housing, eyes hidden behind strips of smoked mica.

Neither of us looks at the other. There’s no need. I crouch beside the tallow-pot and the oil-can, feeling the heat of the flywheel’s axle radiating against my face, and wait for the wheel to complete its half-turn so I can reach the grease-cup safely.

In the warm shadow right beside the iron housing, three pale salamanders are pressed flat against the rivets. Their skin is almost translucent, their tiny throats pulsing so slowly you’d think they were dead if you didn’t watch for a full minute. They haven’t scattered. They’re still soaking in the hum. Whatever is dry in the bearing hasn’t spiked the current yet; the station is still in balance.

I dip the wooden paddle into the thick, dark grease, and as the oiled rim glides past my shoulder—slow as a drifting cloud, smelling of hot iron and wet moss—I ease the paddle into the cup.

Afterward, we don’t linger by the pit. The sun has reached the dead center of the notch, turning the mist into a blinding white cauldron. My chest feels heavy and full, the way it does when the air is at its thinnest. Together, without a word, the two of us drift back toward the transformer bank, where the ground is paved with thick mats of woven rubber and the heat is dry enough to crust the sweat on our backs into fine white salt.

I sit down with my spine against the humming iron casing. The vibration enters straight between my shoulder blades, rattling gently through my ribs, steadying my pulse until it matches the deep, electrical thrum. Beside me, the other keeper’s barrel chest rises and falls—once, twice—in time with the great wheel’s turn.

I push my smoked mica up onto my forehead. The glare off the clouds is too bright to look at, so I let my eyelids droop half-shut, heavy and unblinking, watching the water bead and fall from the waxy vines, feeling the iron speak beneath my heels, content to be the part of the mountain that has hands.
