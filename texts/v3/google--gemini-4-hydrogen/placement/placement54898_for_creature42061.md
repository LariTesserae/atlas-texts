I would not be in the hollow itself. That room is already full—packed to its low ceiling with travertine and water and one long, patient body that has spent decades learning how to be stone. To climb down into it with lungs that want fresh air and knees that bruise on calcite would be to break the seal.

Instead, I think I would be just above it, on the slumped shoulder of the hillside where the grey-green twilight begins.

In the world above the cleft, there is wind, and seasons, and the dry rattle of oak leaves that never make it down through the wedged boulders. There are villages down in the valley, or scattered stone-walled folds where people burn wood and shape iron and argue over boundaries. I would belong to that world, loosely—enough to have calluses from digging, and a smudge of red earth under my fingernails—but I would be one of those who spends most of their days alone on the high limestone benches, where the soil is thin and the sheep won’t climb.

I think I would work with clay. Not fine, painted ware for tables, but heavy, thick-walled things—storage jars, water-crocks, salt-dishes—dug from the iron-rich pockets where the hillside washes out after autumn rain. When you spend your life with wet clay, you learn to trust what is slow and heavy and full of lime. You learn the smell of old water, and how a vessel changes when you leave it in the dark to dry.

That is how I would know the cleft. Not as a cave to be explored—my shoulders are too broad for the tightest pinch of the boulders now, and I have no wish to carry a tallow lamp into a place that clearly wants no fire—but as a warm mouth in the rock.

In winter, when frost stiffens the grass and the wind cuts across the ridge, you can find the spot by the moss. Everywhere else, the limestone is dry and brittle with lichen, but around those three wedged boulders, the stone is always faintly damp. If you kneel and put your face near the roots of the old ash tree that has gripped the crack for a hundred years, you feel it: a steady, barely-moving breath of air, warm as a kitchen after the hearth has been banked for the night, smelling of iron and wet chalk.

The older people in the valley still have a word for it—something that translates roughly to *the cistern that drinks itself*, or *the warm throat*. Nobody goes down there anymore. My grandfather told me once that his own grandfather knew a woman who went down with a pine-torch and three red bowls, back when the drought had dried every spring on the ridge, hoping to find a vein of sweet water. She came back up empty-handed, her shins scraped raw, and said the water down there was too thick and warm to quench a thirst, and that the dark had moved around her like grease. She left the bowls behind because she couldn't carry them and climb at the same time.

That is the story, anyway. People tell stories to explain why they stopped going somewhere, when the real reason is usually simpler: it was hard to reach, and there was nothing there you could sell or burn.

I would come up to the cleft in the late afternoons, when the work at the clay pits is done and my hands are still grey with slip. I wouldn't bring a lamp, or a chisel, or anything meant to leave a mark. I would just sit with my back against the ash-tree’s roots, where the ground is soft with old leaves, and let the warm, lime-heavy air rise past my ankles.

Sometimes, when the summer has been long and dry and the clay in the pits cracks before I can wedge it, I would lean over the gap between the boulders and peer down into the grey-green gloom. You can’t see much from above—just the wet gleam of a single travertine column, and a dull pewter glint where a pool catches the thin light. You can’t see the soot on the ceiling, or the notched bone, or the three bowls sitting on their dryish ledge. You certainly can’t see the thing curled in the back basin, its skin slick with condensation and its ribs rising once every few minutes.

If you listen very hard, holding your breath until the wind in the oak trees drops away, you might hear a single *plip*—muffled, far below, as if someone dropped a pebble into a full jar in another room.

And maybe, once in a great while, when I am rinsing the dried clay from my knuckles with water from my leather flask, a few drops spill down through the roots and hit the stones below. Or a small, blind beetle, startled from the bark of the ash tree by my elbow, loses its footing and tumbles into the dark.

I wouldn't know that anything down there turns toward the ripple. I wouldn't know that a long, mushroom-padded hand reaches up in the cold rain to rest against the rough rim of a clay bowl like the ones I make, or that the creature below knows the texture of unglazed terracotta better than it knows the sky.

I would only know that the place is still there, holding its own heat, keeping its own counsel. On the hillside above, everything is always trying to become something else—clay into pot, wood into ash, grass into winter. Sitting by the cleft, watching the faint mist of warm air vanish into the cold twilight, I would be the one person who comes here simply to let something stay as it is.
