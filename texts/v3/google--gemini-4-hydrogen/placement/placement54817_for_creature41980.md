From ten paces away, I would not know where I was going unless I already knew how to look.

The basalt plain gives nothing back to the eye. Out here above the receding salt pan, the light is flat and merciless, bouncing off unweathered fracture lines and razor-edged stone that still looks freshly broken. When you walk it, you learn to watch your boots. You learn to read the slight shifts in color where the salt dust settles into a hairline crack, and you learn to feel the ground through your soles—not for warmth, which is everywhere under the sun, but for the faint, steady vibration that shouldn’t be there.

That is where I would find myself: out on the shelf at mid-morning, carrying a canvas tool-roll I almost never open, following a sixty-cycle tremor up through the heels of my boots until the stone suddenly parts in front of me.

I think I would be a line-walker for whatever distant, unseen grid owns this spur—someone sent out once a season, or once a year, to check the stray organs of the network that were bolted into raw rock and left to run. Not an engineer. Engineers hate places like this; they want concrete pads, weatherproof housings, clearances measured in inches rather than millimeters. I would be the kind of technician they send when a circuit is too minor to rebuild and too stubborn to shut down—someone paid mostly to walk, to carry a multimeter and a canteen, and to make sure the numbers on a distant panel still add up.

When I reach the seam, I don’t step up to the edge right away. I unroll a thick square of folded canvas onto the basalt so the stone won’t slice through my trousers, and I kneel.

At first, leaning over the lip, I am only an interruption. My shadow falls straight down into the narrow pantry of the cleft, cutting off the noon glare. Down in the damp, the sudden shade registers all at once: a brief stillness along the upper lip of succulents, a faint shift in the light off the verdigris-streaked sight-gauges. The warm, coppery air rises into my face, smelling of wet slate, hot oil, and salt—a sharp relief after miles of dry wind.

If I were newer to this job, I would reach for my insulated pliers. Looking down at the rusted junction box, the oil-stained transformer, the copper busbars tiled edge-to-edge with milky silica plates, a proper technician’s instinct would be to scrape it clean. To clear the moss off the brass valve-wheels, brush away the amber silk, pry the galvanic chitons off the live rails before they bridge a phase and blow the whole pocket sky-high.

But I have been walking this shelf long enough to know better. I keep my hands on my knees.

Instead, I just wait for the click.

In the quiet of the basalt plain, forty seconds is a long time to hold still. I watch the three glass sight-gauges, waiting for the sun to catch the edge of my shadow just right so I can see the silver thrashing of the gauge-worms inside—the surest sign I have that the water is still circulating, that the manifold hasn’t scaled shut. I trace the wrist-thick rubber cable where it disappears into the drilled borehole in the north wall, checking by eye for cracks in the jacket, though I already know the oil-skimmers will have packed any weeping seam with silk long before I got here.

Then the thermal relay inside the rusted box gives its polite *click*.

The whole wall of chitons exhales, dropping a hair’s breadth with a soft *shhhk* of sliding silica, and a younger plate at the margin wedges itself a fraction of a millimeter closer to the copper. Watching them, I realize what I actually am here. I am not the caretaker of this machine. The skimmers are the caretakers, spinning insulation faster than the condensation can short the terminals; the scavengers are the sanitation crew; the slugs and crabs balance the mineral broth at the bottom of the trough. If I reached down with a wire brush and "fixed" this place, the transformer would drown in its own sweat before I made it back to the salt pan.

My only real work is to witness it, and to leave it alone.

I pull a small brass mirror from my pocket—the only tool I actually use here—and angle it just past the sharp obsidian fins, catching a sliver of daylight to bounce into the pinched triangle of shadow behind the transformer. I don’t do it to disturb what lives there, only to make sure the housing isn’t bulging or weeping fresh oil. For a second, the light touches a curve of mottled grey-green skin, rubbery and still, edged with a faint ruff of copper-colored moss. Two milky, lidless eyes stay half-shut against the reflection. I tilt the mirror back immediately, letting the shadow return, and tuck it away.

Before I stand up, I stay knelt at the rim for another few minutes, just to give the pocket my shadow. Out here on the shelf, shade is the rarest thing there is. Beneath me, the temperature drops by a degree or two; a bead of condensation swells on the underside of a slate fin, lets go, and hits a glass gauge with a bright *tink*.

I don’t write much in the logbook—just that the voltage is steady, the relay is cycling on time, and the borehole is clear. Then I fold my canvas pad, shoulder the unopened tool-roll, and step back ten paces across the young basalt, watching the seam vanish into the flat, sun-bleached stone as if it were never there.
