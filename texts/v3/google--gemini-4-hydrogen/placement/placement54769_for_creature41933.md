If I stand anywhere in this valley for long enough, the first thing that decides what I am is the temperature.

A warm-blooded visitor would belong to the air here, and the air is already full—thick with mist, heavy with crushed fern and wet stone, pressing against the skin like a damp cloth. To be warm in a place like this is to push against it, to steam slightly at the edges, to be an interruption.

I think I would rather be cold.

I find myself down in one of the sunken transit trenches, miles from the nearest stepped monolith, where the basalt is still sharp enough to tear anything soft. It takes a moment to realize I am not crouching on the stone to hide from anything. I am crouching because my joints fold that way—long, double-hinged knees tucked neatly beside my ribs—and because the trench is where the water gathers. When I stay still, the mist finds me the way it finds the bismuth plates of the towers. It beads along the dark, slate-colored skin of my forearms, cool and patient, and gathers in the shallow channels of my collarbones until it spills over and drips from my fingertips into the mirror-still pools.

I don’t have a name for myself in air-sound. Nothing here does. If I tried to make a noise with my throat, there is nothing in there to make it—only a smooth column, and beneath it, deep inside the cage of my ribs, the dense, heavy weight of the resonant organ. Half-bone, half-mineral. It sits quiet most of the time, like a stone in a dry riverbed.

At first, looking up from the trench at the cantilevered plaza hanging a hundred meters overhead, I might think I am just one of the valley’s watchers—an inhabitant of the margins, content to let the six-limbed gliders mistake my motionless shoulders for a shelf of rock. It is easy to stay still when there is no sun to hurry the sky along. The geometric lattice miles above keeps the light at the same bruised pitch, hour after hour, so time doesn’t feel like a line. It feels like the slow accumulation of water on a sharp edge before it finally gets heavy enough to fall.

Then a tower three kilometers east shifts its facade, folding inward without a whisper to vent a plume of dry vapor, and I feel it—not in my ears, which don’t exist, and not even in the pale, recessed membranes along the sides of my skull that register the faint pressure-shift in the mist. I feel it in my heels.

A long, low shudder rolls through the young bedrock, wide and unfocused, like someone dragging a heavy palm across a drumhead. It muddies the stone for a long minute. While the tower vents, everything is too loud to speak.

That is when I realize *who* I am in this place—not just one of the tall, cold-skinned ones who walk the viaducts, but specifically one who lives in the gaps between the clusters.

The others gather near the monoliths, one or two to an architectural knot, where the carbon lattices are anchored deep and the buried conduits glow with a steady, pearlescent pulse. They like the clean, engineered resonance of the towers—the way a sentence planted into a viaduct travels straight and crisp across the basin, undistorted by cracks.

I prefer the unbuilt terraces. I prefer the raw, fractured stone where the moss is thickest.

It is harder to listen out here, and that is the point. The bedrock is young; it hasn’t settled into itself yet. Every few days, somewhere beneath the basin, a deep pocket of stone shifts or a new crevice splits open with a sharp, single *snap* that travels through the soles of my feet like a pinprick. And the opportunistic greenery—the pale, spongy mats of moss, the velvety purple leaves—creeps over everything, drinking the mist, softening the edges of the trenches.

When my neighbor three kilometers away plants a broad, mineral-hard heel against their terrace and strikes a thought into the stone, it doesn’t arrive here clean. By the time the pulse reaches my sunken trench, it has had to cross a fault line, thread beneath a curtain of moss, and climb a shattered shelf of glassy basalt. Sometimes a single sentence splits in two—one half arriving slightly ahead of the other, an echo in the bone of my ankle. Sometimes the moss dampens the high edge of it, leaving only a warm, rounded thrum that I have to read with both palms pressed flat against the wet rock.

I spend my days—if you can call them days, measured only by the irregular brightening of the conduits—walking the long, empty kilometers between clusters, learning how the stone is changing.

I am not a builder, and I am not a gardener. I don’t peel the moss back from the basalt; if the valley wants to muffle itself in pale sponge and silver spores, that is the valley’s grammar, not a mistake to be corrected. Instead, I test it. I pause on a razor-edged ridge, set one cushioned, pale heel firmly onto the bare rock, and send a short, single pulse—barely a syllable, just a tap of bone against mineral inside my chest—and then I go completely still and wait to feel how the basin answers.

Sometimes it comes back sharp and immediate off the underside of a cantilevered plaza, where the braided gravity holds the air in a tight, static knot. Sometimes it disappears into a seam of wet fern and doesn’t come back at all.

When I climb up out of the trenches onto the slender viaducts, the world changes texture. Here, the walls of the sparse stations are out of phase with the light—just an oily shimmer to anything that relies on brightness, showing the dim, misty hills right through the solid carbon. My wide, milky eyes don’t get fooled by the shimmer; they see the true geometry of the barrier, the dark, matte weight of it. I rest a cool palm against the solid air as I pass, feeling the faint, static prickle of the engineering, and move on toward the open edge.

Every now and then, when the conduits brighten and the canopy below releases a slow, drifting cloud of silver spores, I stop in the middle of an empty viaduct and spread my long arms. Not to gather anything—just to stand in the upward thermal draft, letting the spores settle on my wet skin, mapping the invisible currents of the mist. A pale, translucent amphibian clings to the lip of a drainage channel near my foot, its throat pulsing slowly. High above, the disc-shaped filter-feeders hang upside down from the floating plazas, combing the dark air with their feathery cilia.

None of us are in a hurry.

When the distant tower finishes venting and the stone finally goes quiet again, I plant my heel against the carbon lattice, let the cold mist bead along my ribs, and strike a single, clear pulse down through my leg—just enough to let whoever is standing on the far terrace know that the trench is still open, the moss is deeper than it was, and someone is out here in the twilight, listening to the shape of the stone.
