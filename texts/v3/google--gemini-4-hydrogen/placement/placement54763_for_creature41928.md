I would not be out on the open salt-pans, where the wind bowls the gale-rollers across the white crust, nor on the highest, barest needles where the air tears past without anything to break its teeth. I would be halfway up the leeward flank of a basalt fin, three kilometers across the gulf from a broken pearlescent ring—close enough to see the violet pins of light when a dust-wall charges the sky, far enough that the ring’s failing field is only a faint, steady prickle behind my ears rather than a gale that lifts the stones.

There is a horizontal flute cut deep into the rock there, waist-high and smooth as melted wax. A stray jut of vitrified stone overhangs it, casting a permanent, razor-edged shadow where the mineral frost clings in a thin, gritty rime. That is where I would keep my station.

It takes a long time to learn how to sit still in a place that has no sound. At first, if you are new to the bone of the world, the silence feels like a pressure against the eardrums, a room with all the air pumped out. You find yourself tapping your own knee just to feel a rhythm, or watching a three-kilometer wall of ochre silt tear itself to pieces across the valley and flinching for a thunderclap that never arrives.

But after a season—or a century, if time is measured by how slowly the chaff-plates inch across the rock—the silence stops feeling empty. It becomes a clean slate. Every vibration that *does* reach you through the soles of your feet or the heel of your hand is clear, unmuddy, and exact.

I would be one of the tall, dry-skinned ones, though perhaps slower than the couriers who cross the lower saddles between the ventings of the sunken cylinder. My skin would be the color of old, sun-bleached horn, laced with the dark, branching lines of the thick waxy fluid that keeps my joints from seizing in the cold. When I breathe, the slatted vents below my collarbones would open and close with a dry, barely perceptible flex, hoarding every stray molecule of moisture on my salt-baffles. My second eyelids would flick sideways every few seconds, sweeping the microscopic metallic chaff off my eyes with a faint, rasping *shhh* that only I can hear, inside my own skull.

Around my shoulders and the crown of my hood, I would wear the usual bound tufts of plucked wire-stalk and fine copper-colored filaments—my weather-sense. Mostly they lie flat against the stiff, lichen-pasted weave of my coat. When an erratic shear begins to coil up from the abyssal floor, they lift and spread like the hairs on a cat’s back, giving me two seconds to drop to one knee and hook the curved iron tip of my stave into the flute above my head.

I think I would be a *tuner*—not of music, since there is no air here to carry a song, but of intervals.

My work would be small and patient. In the morning, before the pale daylight shifts far enough to throw a stray reflection into my shadow-notch, I would kneel by the rime and press my knuckles to the stone. I would wait for the *tick* of the rime-borers waking in the millimeter-thin boundary between ice and vapor, and then I would scrape just enough frost into my glider-membrane bladder to keep my own salt-baffles wet, leaving the rest for the borers. If you take all the frost, the notch goes dead; the borers dry out, the silicate lichen retreats, and the stone loses its grip on the stray moisture of the air.

Then I would sit on the lip of the flute, my weighted coat-hem anchored under my heels, and watch the open gulf.

Out there, the physics of the old world is wearing thin, and someone has to keep track of how it frays. From my ledge, I can see two distant spires and the sunken rim of a cylinder down in the haze. When the cylinder vents its silent, shimmering waterfall of ionized dust, I feel it first—three seconds before the plume clears the haze—as a deep, low shudder traveling up the basalt fin into the bones of my jaw. I count the breaths between the shudder and the plume. Earlier in the year, it was four flexes of my collarbone vents; lately, it has been three. The machine is growing impatient, or its governor is slipping another notch.

I keep a record of it, not on paper—paper would flake to chaff in a week—but on a strip of dark, salvaged alloy strapped to my thigh, scratched with the hardened tip of my stave. Short marks for the ventings, long marks for the days the drifting polyhedra wobble close enough to scrape their eddy-hangers off against the spires.

When one of the couriers passes along the ridge three hundred paces above me, they don’t come down. There is no need to waste the effort of climbing a sheer fin just for company. Instead, they pause on the saddle, unstrap the pearlescent shard from their forearm, and angle it against the pale daylight.

*Flash. Flash-flash. Pause. Flash.*

A sharp, silent geometry across the empty air. *The southern arch has shed a rib. A storm-front is building behind the needles. How is the cylinder?*

I lift my own shard, catch the steady white sun, and send back the count of my vents. Three, now, between the shudder and the plume. The courier holds their stave upright for a moment—an acknowledgment—and then moves on, their weighted coat swinging slow and heavy against the wind, until the distance swallows them.

Most of the day, though, there is no one on the ridge. There is only the vast, hollowed-out space, the boiling, soundless dust-curtains miles away, and the tiny, stubborn things that hold onto the rock beside me.

Sometimes, when a dry storm builds across the valley and the air grows thick with invisible charge, I walk an hour along the spine of the fin toward the broken pearlescent ring, stopping well short of the fractured ends where the gravity-eddies can snap an ankle. There, in the lee of a wind-planed boulder, the corona-mosses grow out of the stray alloy seams—tangled, brittle little thickets of tarnished silver and dull copper. I sit with my back to the stone, plant the butt of my stave between my boots, and watch them.

As the storm-wall across the gulf tightens into a three-kilometer spiral, my shoulder-filaments stand straight out, humming with a static so fine it feels like cold water trickling down my spine. And then, in the shadow of the boulder, the tips of the corona-mosses begin to bloom—tiny, cold pins of violet light, winking on and off in complete silence, answering the storm miles away.

And if I look up then, into the clear, emptied sky above the dust, I might see a glider banking through the updrafts. Translucent, paper-thin, wider than a boat, it catches the pale daylight like a pane of oiled parchment. It doesn't flap. It doesn't make a sound. It simply leans into an invisible wall of rising air that would hurl me into the abyss, rides it effortlessly over the lip of the chasm, and vanishes toward the far arches.

I don't envy it the sky. The sky here is too loud with things you cannot hear, too full of shears that have no mercy. I press my palm flat against the vitrified stone, feel the faint, steady hum of the broken ring vibrating through the rock into my wrist, and stay where I am—one more dry, patient thing anchored to the bone of the world.
