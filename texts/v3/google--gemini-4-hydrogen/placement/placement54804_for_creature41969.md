I would not arrive here as a visitor. Anything wet—anything that sweats, or blinks away grit, or exhales a cloud of warm vapor—would be an injury to this room. Even a single human breath would fog the anhydrite needles, soften the pitch-seals, and leave a damp smudge on the bone-dry powder of the floor. 

To be here at all, I would have to belong to the drought.

My first awareness would not be sight, nor even sound as a throat-born creature knows it. It would be *geometry*: the sudden certainty of invisible walls pressing against the flared horn-plates at my temples, and of a narrow, breathless corridor of stillness tucked just behind my shoulders. 

I am small—no taller than a child’s waist—and light, as if my bones were carved from the same silica-stiffened hollowness as the *bladder-ferns*. When I draw breath, my ribbed chest expands in a slow, shallow cycle, taking in the heavy kiln-heat, the tang of sulfur, the dry amber of the air. Nothing inside me sloshes. Nothing inside me cools. Where my knees and wrists bend, the cured vellum of my skin is dark and supple with *pitch-creeper* resin, oiled against the crack of eternity.

I find myself on a narrow, razor-edged shelf of basalt halfway up the chamber’s eastern wall, wedged neatly into the shadow of a *spore-cushion*. This is my node. Just an inch in front of my knees, the standing wave rises like a sheer cliff of pressure; if I were to lean forward, the roar would hammer against my skull-plates until my thoughts scattered like disturbed dust. Here, though, in the seam where two opposing currents cancel each other out, the air is almost tender.

From this ledge, the whole hall lies open in the dim, bruised-amber glow of the stressed rock. Below me, the pale mineral powder on the floor holds its crisp, undisturbed rings. Three *parchment-drifters* hang suspended in the mid-air between my shelf and the central slab, motionless as pressed leaves, riding the exact same pocket of quiet that shelters me.

Beside my left knee stands a tripod of dried vine-wood and bone.

I know it by touch before I look at it. Its legs are wedged into natural pits in the basalt, braced with hardened pitch so the floor’s perpetual tremor cannot walk it out of place. Held in its cradle is a scraped-out seed-pod, its wide mouth aimed precisely toward a curtain of *pitch-creeper* hanging from the ceiling—and, through that curtain, toward a hairline fissure in the far wall.

If I had a function in this world, a quiet habit that defined me among the others, it would be here: at the listening-pods and the high chimes.

Not everyone tends the great floor-vents. Those who wedge the thick basalt splinters into the main fissures have a heavier patience, a gift for reshaping whole corridors of the room—steering spore-clouds, shifting the quiet pockets so a new cluster of *bladder-ferns* can inflate without tearing. My temperament is narrower, tuned to the upper register. I watch the *seam-lines* where the room’s vast groan breaks into smaller, glassier threads.

I lean sideways—slowly, distributing my weight so not a grain of pale dust shifts beneath my calloused soles—and press my horned temple against the rim of the seed-pod.

Instantly, the crushing bass of the chamber falls away. The pod swallows the roar and leaves only a single, thin harmonic: a high, steady silver wire of sound, pulled clean from the ore of the room. I sit like that for a long time—minutes, or seasons, in a place where neither word has teeth—just checking the tension of that thread.

Today, there is a hair’s breadth of roughness in it. A tiny, dry burr, no wider than a *spine-crawler*’s pellet, trembling along the note.

It is not a crisis. Nothing in the pocket rushes. Still, it is a question that asks to be answered.

I lift my head from the pod, blinkless eyes adjusting to the faint piezoelectric sparks bleeding along the rock-veins, and reach up with one resin-tipped finger to brush the hanging chime of anhydrite shards above my ledge. *Tink-shhh.* A crisp, glassy shimmer slices cleanly above the floor’s organ-pipe groan—not a word, just a small, polite announcement of motion.

Down by the central slab, another of my kind pauses beside the white tally marks. Its head tilts slightly, horn-plates catching the vibration of my chime. It does not hurry over; it simply lifts one hand and clicks two hardened fingernails together twice in reply, a dry *clack-clack* that travels straight through the stone beneath our feet. *I hear you. The pattern holds.*

I climb down from the shelf, stepping from ledge to ledge with the slow, tilted grace the room demands, sliding along the invisible troughs of the standing wave. Near the base of the wall, where a *drum-backed husk* clings blind and humming to the stone, I find what I was listening for: a flake of translucent silica, shed from a *bladder-fern* overhead, has drifted down and lodged against the mouth of a minor wall-slit.

It is barely larger than a fingernail, yet it has been enough to fret the reed, sending that tiny burr up into the ceiling currents where my tripod catches it.

I crouch, pressing the side of my skull close to the stone until I can feel the exact flutter of the flake against the gas-stream. With two long fingers, I pinch it free. Instantly, the burr vanishes. Across the hall, one of the *parchment-drifters* dips half an inch and settles again, smoother than before. I tuck the shed flake into a pouch at my belt—nothing is thrown away here; ground fine, it will thicken the next batch of resin, or weight a new chime-string.

On my way back up to the ledge, I pass one of the high alcoves. An elder sits propped there, completely dry now, its vellum skin darkened to the color of old *pitch-creeper*, its chest no longer rising and falling in slow cycles. It has ceased to hum, yet it is still part of the room’s tuning; the warm gas passes through its hollow ribs, and its flared horn-plates catch the bass without flinching. Soon, its ribs will be needed for a new tripod near the northern vents. I pause for a moment, laying the flat of my horned temple lightly against its dry, weightless shoulder—a greeting, or simply a check of the resonance—before climbing back to my node.

If you asked me what I am, I would not say a guardian, or a priest, or a laborer.

I am an ear pressed to a gourd in a room that never stops singing. I am the small patience that keeps a single thread of sound clean, in a world where nothing rots, nothing cools, and the only clock is a white scratch on a stone slab—waiting, without hurry, for the next reason to be made.
