I would not be one of the tall, chalk-white keepers. I don’t have their hairline seams, or the extra joint that lets them fold flat against a pipe flange, and I could never reach shoulder-deep into an inch-deep puddle to pull up a ribbon of living tin.

Instead, I think I would be what happens to the person at the end—the one who stood still long enough for a keeper to lean its smoked-glass brow close, wait a patient minute, and draw a single neat line of white chalk across the sleeve of their coat.

An hour later, when it looked back, I would have moved only a hand’s breadth.

I would find myself in the middle tiers of the lattice, down where the blinding silver glare of the sky is broken into crisp, geometric bars by the overhead catwalks and cable trays. Up on the ridge among the cooling towers, the light is too sharp, bleaching everything to chalk and aluminum. Down in the deepest shafts, where the keepers tuck away their bolts of rolled-up shadows, there is no light at all. The middle tier—under the banks of oiled transformers, beside the copper manifolds that sweat thin beads of condensation—is where the tolerances are widest, and where the air smells faintly of ozone and cold metal.

My shadow would be gone by now. It detached itself months ago—or years ago; without rust or weathering, it is hard to tell—sliding off my heels onto the underside of the grating overhead to spread like dark lichen along a busbar. A keeper peeled it off with an aluminum scraper and carried it down into the dark. I don’t miss it. Without a shadow dragging behind me, I feel lighter, and the keepers no longer pause to inspect me when they glide past on their narrow feet. The chalk mark on my sleeve has never smudged; nothing here wears away.

Because I cannot fold myself through a four-inch gap between a transformer and a duct, I have had to learn the basin’s other geometries.

When I want to move through the tighter clearances, I walk up to the third-tier catwalk and pass behind the three-foot vertical cooling duct. I emerge on the far side no taller than a keeper’s knee. At that scale, the whole cramped valley opens into a series of vast, cool cathedrals. The underside of a cable tray becomes a high ceiling hung with clusters of fiberglass-furred climbers, silent and pale as spun glass. I can walk upright along the rims of the machine-cut condensation gutters, stepping politely around the armored grazers as they scrape silver-wire moss from the rivets. When I need my full height again, I simply step behind a narrow structural pylon and come out the other side restored, though I have never figured out where my missing inches wait while I am small.

I spend most of my time doing what the keepers have no patience for.

They are occupied with the glacial scale of things—the flywheel turning a hand’s breadth, the pistons easing down over an hour, the stray shadows, the luminous grey fluid that only pools on the aluminum floor if you stand three paces to the left of the glass pipes. I like standing in that spot myself, watching the grey sheen gather around my dry boots, waiting for a keeper to fold down beside me and drink it away without a sound.

Mostly, though, I watch the hanging drops.

I find a brass fitting in the shade beneath a heat exchanger, kneel on the brushed aluminum decking, and wait for a bead of condensation to detach. When it pauses at eye level—trembling slightly, suspended for three full minutes before gravity remembers it—I lean in close. When I am knee-high, a hanging drop is not a speck of water; it is a clear, trembling globe the size of a plum, and the impossible room inside it is easy to see.

The keepers ignore these drops, just as they ignore the staircases that loop back onto their own landings, because the drops run on the wrong clock. Inside the water, between the teeth of phantom brass wheels that do not exist anywhere in the basin, the tiny, copper-bright, needle-limbed things dart and cling. They are frantic and brilliant, living out entire generations in the hundred and eighty seconds before the drop snaps upward back into the pipe.

I cannot help them, and they have no way of knowing I am crouching on the aluminum outside their sky. Still, in a valley where everything else moves at the speed of cooling steel, it feels important that someone watches them. Someone ought to notice a three-minute world before it vanishes.

When the drop snaps back up and the brass fitting is dry again, I walk over to the four-flight staircase nearby—the one that descends cleanly through three tiers of machinery only to deposit you back on the exact landing where you started. Nobody else uses it; the four-winged birds fly straight through the scaffolding, and the keepers walk straight through the vitrified brick walls.

I sit on the top step, which is also the bottom step, and rest my elbows on my knees. The air is thin and cold, though my breath does not plume. Down the length of the terrace, a piston eases another fraction of an inch into its sleeve without a hiss. From somewhere overhead comes the sharp *tick* of an I-beam adjusting to the noon glare.

I sit still enough that the sound of my own pulse begins to arrive a half-second before my heart beats—a quiet, steady rhythm in the acoustic seam—and I wait for the next drop to form.
