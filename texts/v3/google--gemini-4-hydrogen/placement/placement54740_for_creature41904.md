I wouldn’t be looking down from the rim. Anyone who stands up there long enough to take in the whole bowl is just passing through, or else they’re already dead and waiting for the lichen to finish with their ribs.

If I belong here, I am already low—knees bent, weight over the balls of my flat hide boots, tucked under the lip of a middle-tier overhang where the noon glare only reaches in bright, jagged wedges. From here, the world is mostly gray wet slate, the pale undersides of reed mats, and the narrow horizontal strip of blinding sky that fits through the slits of my goggles.

I think I would be one of the trough-tenders. Not the ones who swing the heavy antler levers to pry up flagstones, and not the fast, young ones who scramble up the notched timber to lash a sagging roof before the slush takes it. I’d be down where the fissures weep warmest, working the log cisterns and the runoff channels with a long wooden scraper and a flint blade dulled to the exact curve of a hollowed trunk.

It is quiet work, even by the standards of a silent town. Up on the upper terraces, there is always the dry *clack* of bone-tabs flicking against each other, the muffled thud of shovels, the constant shifting of bundles from one pocket to another. Down by the troughs, the air is thicker with mineral steam. It smells sharply of sulfur and boiled fat, and it coats the outside of my sealskin mittens with a slick, silvery film that never quite freezes.

My job would be to keep the warm water moving—to read where the rock is changing its mind. A hairline crack that wept a finger’s width of brine yesterday might widen by the thickness of a crawler’s shell by noon, and if that extra warmth pools under a retaining wall instead of draining into the moss-beds, the whole tier will slump before sundown. So I spend my hours on my knees, scraping the pale, eyeless crawlers off the lip of the troughs into woven reed baskets, clearing the slush-dams with the edge of my paddle, and pressing my bare wrist—just for a second, before the cold bites—against the weeping slate to feel whether the mountain’s breath is running hot or cool today.

You learn to see without looking up. The bone-slits narrow your world to a ribbon, which is a mercy; you don't have to take in the whole restless, blinding bowl at once. You just watch the strip of stone in front of your knees. You watch the *tick-tick* of needle-ice sprouting along the dry edges of a plank, and you know without turning your head when the sun has shifted past the rim because the crystals stop growing and begin to sigh.

When the plump, velvet birds waddle past my shins, I don't shoo them. They’re heavy as loaves and warm as hearth-stones; sometimes one will settle right against the side of my knee while I’m scraping a channel, its oiled feathers shedding beads of brine like mercury, probing the chinks of my boot-lacing for a stray crawler. And when the white burrowers dart out from the dry-stone wall behind me, I keep my shovel still. Once, maybe, one of them steals a strip of spare sinew from my belt-pouch. I let it go. It’s a fair trade for their whiskers. If three of those sharp white heads suddenly pop out of the chinks and bolt straight up toward the lichen-line, I don't wait to see what's coming—I drop the scraper, roll onto the timber catwalk, and let the slush-slide take the trough. You can always carve another log.

Because I’m down by the water so much, my skin would be cured darker than most, stiff as smoked tallow around the jaw, with salt permanently wedged into the creases of my knuckles. I’d probably have a groove worn into the bridge of my nose from the goggles—forty years of sinew pulling bone against bone, or at least twenty, enough that even when I take them off inside a sleeping-hollow at night, my face still feels strapped in.

Maybe one eye is already going milky from a winter afternoon when a strap rotted through and I caught the full mirror-glare off the basin before I could clap my mitten over my face. That’s often how you end up at the troughs anyway. The work doesn't ask for sharp eyes; it asks for hands that know the difference between solid limestone and a shelf of rime that’s about to shear.

And when the shadows finally drop across the bowl and the ice-gnats freeze out of the air, I’d crawl back into whatever stone pocket my household’s bundle is wedged into that week. I’d unlace the wet outer pelts, wedge my back against a neighbor’s shoulder and a bird’s warm, oily down, and eat my share of salty crawler-mash in the blue thread of light from the fat-lamp.

If I noticed a fissure weeping too hot that day—if I felt that deep, subtle groan in the stone beneath my boots that means a whole terrace won't be safe by morning—I wouldn't try to click it across the dark, and I wouldn't waste time signing it by tallow-light. I’d just crawl across the pelts to whoever holds the roof-bundle next to ours, pull my hood back just enough, and press my forehead against theirs.

Then I’d let the hum rise in my chest—low, steady, vibrating right through the salt-cured bone of my skull into theirs—until I felt them hum back, letting me know they heard the mountain shifting, and that come first light, we’d unlash the horn ribs and move together.
