If I am in that room, I cannot bring eyes with me. Eyes are an outside habit, built for horizons and forgiving angles, for light that arrives from somewhere far away and leaves the undersides of things alone. Down here, forty meters under unbroken basalt, light is a weather system. It presses flat against every surface at once, hot and clinical, stripping the dark out of corners until even the water has nowhere to hide.

So at first, I wouldn’t be a shape at all. I would just be a tuning fork.

I would know the room by the vibration in my ribs—that low, steady thrumming that lives inside the stone and never stops, only thickens and thins as the knot of filaments overhead folds through angles my mind can’t picture. I wouldn’t need to picture them. In a world without shadows, geometry isn’t something you look at; it’s something that leans on you. It’s the sudden, heavy tilt in the fluid behind your brow, the way the air goes tight and sour with steam a fraction of a second before the basin tears itself off the floor.

If I am to last here, I have to be one of the quiet ones in the flutes. Not the great scarred patriarch draped twelve meters up on the broken walkway—that’s a monument’s life, too heavy for the walls, taking the upward rain like a piece of the cliff that forgot it was alive. And not one of the pale, blind amphibians darting through the runoff cracks, forever flinching at the edge of the surf.

I think I would be one of the middle adults. A tenant of the third flute from the northern pylon, holding my quadrant of the scalloped slate, breathing in time with the drain.

What draws me to this body isn’t its armor—though the armor is extraordinary, a slow patience of dissolved rock and calcium laid down plate by plate over years of warm spray, polished by the gales until it gleams like the floor channels. What draws me is the *shadow* it carries.

In a room where nothing else is allowed a dark side, I would be the keeper of a private night. Beneath the curved, boat-hull roof of my back, tucked behind the wedge of my brow, there is a narrow band of permanent shade. Cool, damp, mist-pale. That is where my real self lives: the soft, pearlescent belly, the rows of thick suction-feet, the delicate branching frills that never have to face the noon overhead. I don’t hide *in* the dark; I *am* the dark, clamped tight to the wet wall, letting the storm hammer its fists against a roof I grew out of the storm itself.

And I would live for the drop in pitch.

That is the thing I can’t stop thinking about—how small the margin is between stillness and annihilation, and how completely you have to trust your own bones to read it. Every few seconds, buried under the roar of indoor surf and the sharp, percussive cracks of mist flashing into steam, the room’s hum slips downward by a fraction of a tone. Just a hair. A looseness in the ribs.

That is the bell.

Before the water-wall even finishes tearing upward from the floor, I am already uncoiling. Thirty or forty pale feet rippling over slick slate, out into the two-second pocket of dead calm where the basin has been yanked into the air and held trembling overhead like a sheet of shattered glass. For two heartbeats, the floor is bare and steaming. My frills unfurl from under my brow—the only time they ever taste the open room—sweeping the warm mineral film, snatching a stunned crustacean, feeling the fierce, flat heat of the lattice pressing straight down onto my plates.

Then the stones in my skull shift. The inertia tilts.

There is no panic in it, only a practiced drop into the nearest floor channel, a tucking-in of everything pale and tender, a locking of suction-feet against the stone. Then the sky falls sideways. The water-wall booms down across my back with the weight of a breaking wave, trying to peel me off the floor, and my curved carapace simply sheds it, splitting the fury around me while I sit in my small, damp dark and wait for the drain.

That is the rhythm of the glare. But if I am honest, the place in this world where I would most truly *find* myself is not in the channels. It is at the base of the matte-grey pylon.

Because even a creature built for the storm cannot stay soaked forever. The bathhouse warmth softens the pale underside; the constant mineral soup wants to rot what isn’t stone. So every so often, between squalls, I would haul my two meters of slate-colored armor up the slight rise to one of the three seamless ceramic pillars, and coil myself into its halo.

That thin, bone-dry ring where the water refuses to touch.

I would press my soft belly into that dry warmth, listening to the surf roar and sheet off the ceramic just millimeters from my plates, never quite crossing the line. There is something almost unbearably peaceful about that boundary—an old piece of someone else’s geometry, left behind before the basalt sealed over, still keeping a dry place in the middle of a hurricane. If I had eggs to lay, I would tuck their leathery, translucent sacs into that same dry ring, knowing that when they hatched, the white, soft-bodied young would scramble straight up the lee of the pylon into the mist-diffused moss and leathery ferns, waiting years for the air to build them a roof of their own.

And sometimes, in the brief lull between the lift of the water and its fall, I would meet another of my kind in the polished channels.

We wouldn’t speak. No voice could survive the hiss of evaporating spray or the boom of water on slate. We would simply halt, nose to nose in the blinding mist, and press the broad, mineral plates of our brows together. For one second, before the next gale tore us back to the floor, I would feel the cavern’s endless thrumming pass straight from my skull into theirs—the same pitch, the same weight, the same patient waiting—and then we would slide apart, each of us carrying our own small shadow back into the glare.
