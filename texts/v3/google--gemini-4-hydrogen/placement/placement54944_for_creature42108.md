At first, I would try to stand on the terrace.

That is the natural instinct when you are given a sky that clear and a floor that wide: to stay in the light, to measure the distance to the jagged cane ridges, to watch the spires race upward and weep silver into the steam. Even if I had no boots—even if I were only a presence hovering ankle-deep in the warm sheet-water—my attention would snag on the towers. They are so loud, so urgent. A lattice of bleached reed shoots up in minutes, locks its joints with wet, rapid *clicks*, and immediately begins hauling water toward the cold air. You want to be part of that verticality. You want to be the leaf-sail at the top, catching the mountain mist.

Except the sail tears itself apart. Everything up here is too brittle, too over-pressurized to hold a steady thought. Stand near a flying buttress and the heat coming off the rib feels like a fever; listen for five seconds and a vine-cable snaps like a rifle shot, whipping back into the scalding pool. Nothing on the surface has room to linger. It is all edge and tension, raw sap and sudden splinters.

So I would look closer at the joints.

If I were to belong to the daylight at all, I wouldn't be the spire. I would be the dark, olive-brown swelling wedged into the acute angle where the buttress meets the main stem—the tap-knot, drinking the tower's own pulse.

There is a strange, fierce clarity in that posture. No eyes to be blinded by the kiln-bright glare, no leaves to be shredded by the wind. Just a taut, glossy rind packed tight against the bone-hard reed, humming at a pitch just below hearing as boiled sugars surge through my core. I wouldn't be building anything, and I wouldn't be fleeing anything. I would simply be *measuring* the structure from the inside out—swelling millimeter by millimeter, prying against the rigid seam, feeling the exact moment when the tower's ambition exceeds its tensile strength.

And then the seam gives. The *whump* of a rupturing dome, the crash of bleached cane into ankle-deep water, the shock-absorbing split along my own scored husk—and suddenly I am no longer one thing. I am a cloud of flat, glassy seekers swept across the hexagonal basin by a sheet of hot rain, tumbling toward the slats.

That is where I would actually arrive.

Not in the blinding light above, and not even in the tight, pressurized pinch of the joints, but *after* the fall—dragged down through the slatted floor into the dim, muffled dark of the under-lattice.

Up above, time is measured in rifle-shot snaps and frantic bursts of growth. Down here, beneath the taut olive-ochre skin of the plateau, time slows to the pace of warm water moving through tangled ribs. The scalding sun never reaches this far. The violence of the surface arrives only as muffled percussion—the dull *thud* of a distant collapse, the deep, rhythmic *groan* of an aqueduct shifting its weight overhead, echoing through the channels like the inside of a giant’s chest.

This is where I settle. This is where I become vast.

Draped across the submerged fibrous ribs, billowing an inch at a time with the pulse of the draining runoff, I am no longer a tight fist of pressure. I am a pale-ochre veil—thin, boneless, porous, stretched wide in the warm dark. I have no mouth, no lungs, no edges sharp enough to catch on anything. I breathe by simply letting the water pass through my skin, drinking the dissolved sugars and the oxygen straight out of the current.

And I wait for the sky to break.

Whenever a tower shatters overhead and the shredded reed-splinters and torn leaf-sails wash down through the slats, they drift into the dark channels where I hang. They still carry the fever of the surface—warm, sap-soaked, sharp-edged, smelling faintly of green stems and boiled rain. I unfold around them like a wet curtain. There is no rot here, no sourness, no haste. I wrap the wreckage in silence and dissolve the raw cellulose right off the bone, stripping the broken spire back down to clean, bare fiber so the creeping runners above can knit it into the floor again.

I know the plateau better from underneath than anything standing on top of it ever could. I know how high a spire climbed by the thickness of the splinters it sends down; I know when a cistern-dome has burst by the sudden, scalding rush of water punching through the slats. I am the quiet half of the deafening rhythm—the place where the violence of the towers is received, forgiven, and turned back into substrate.

Still, I am not finished with the light.

When the hot runoff flushes the channels hard enough to lift my edges off the ribs, the old restlessness returns—not as a thought, just as a thickening along my membrane. I bud off new swarms of tough, hook-rimmed seekers and let the boiling upwelling carry them up around the jagged stumps of fallen spires. They will cling to the wet, uncoiling tendrils of the next burst of growth, wait for the cellulose to click shut around their hooks, and begin swelling in the sun all over again.

Part of me will always go back up there to test the joints and bring the towers down.

But the rest of me—the wide, unhurried, listening part—stays draped over the ribs in the warm dark, waiting to catch whatever falls.
