I would not arrive from beyond the slate rim. Nothing crosses that pewter haze; there is no path down the fractured shelves, and no reason to come here if you are made for a world with weather. If I am in the basin, I have always been part of its dry accounting.

I find myself first not as a shape, but as a stillness inside a hollow tower—high enough that the light from the arched openings is thin and shadowless, low enough that the air around me still carries the faint, cool weight of the slate floor below. There is no sound to locate me by. No drip, no settling groan of masonry, not even the whisper of my own weight against the ramp. I know where I am because the stone beneath my pads is slightly less dense than the stone beside me: the lattice-mites have been working the pillar at my back for decades, turning its core into a bone-white filigree, and I can feel the delicate, hollow give of it against my folded ribs.

If you stepped through the archway six stories down and looked up into the dimness, you would not see me. You would see only the pale, unweathered geometry of the interior, and perhaps a vertical bundle of dry struts tucked into the angle where the spiraling ramp meets the wall.

I have been folded here for a long season. When you have no eyes, time does not pass in light and dark; it passes in gradients. The pewter sky outside shifts by a fraction of a degree, and the air in the tower’s chimney answers—rising a little slower, then a little faster, brushing past the thin, eggshell dish of my crest with the faintest trace of mineral vapor. In the dark, I read the health of the building the way a sleeper reads the weight of a blanket.

And then something shifts across the plaza.

It is not a sound. It is a tiny, jagged irregularity in the static field—a sharp, feverish pinch where the charge should be smooth. Three towers over, high on an outer cornice, a mason is laying down mortar too fast and too dry. Its paste is curing crooked. Even from here, through four walls of porous chalk and fifty yards of motionless air, I can feel the faint ache of an arch beginning to pull out of true.

That is what wakes me. Not hunger, not alarm—just an asymmetry that cannot be left to harden.

I unfold slowly, one porcelain joint at a time, sliding mineral against mineral without a click. My four limbs are long and dry as cured reed, ending in wide, spongy pads that drink the impact of every movement before it can become a vibration. When I step out onto the spiraling ramp, the tower does not know I am moving. I descend in absolute silence, my dish-crest tilted slightly downward to read the curve of the wall, until I pass through the lower arch and out onto the swept-clean slate of the avenue.

Here, I am taller than anything that moves, though almost nothing moves fast enough to notice.

A sweeper is gliding across the boulevard ten paces ahead—a matte-charcoal sheet no thicker than spilled paper, erasing a drift of fallen velum-skins into nothing. I step cleanly around its fringe of cilia, careful not to disturb the dry, alkaline film it leaves behind. Above me, the wide viaducts span the empty air, their edges crisp as broken porcelain. Nothing here has ever been rained on. Nothing has ever been forgiven by erosion; every mistake in the stone stays sharp until something comes to undo it.

That is what I am. I am a **caliper**—one of the four or five tall, eyeless instruments that keep the lung from choking on its own quiet industry.

I cross the plaza toward the tower with the crooked charge. As I approach its base, I tilt my crest upward, reading the vertical face of the masonry. The air rising from the tower’s arched windows is sluggish; near the top cornice, a cluster of papery-white velum-growths has fanned out too thickly, drinking the vapor before it can clear the lip, and just beneath them, clinging to the pale stone, is the mason.

Even without eyes, I know its shape: flat, segmented, the size of a spread hand, and wrong. Its shell is too chalky, its gait too erratic. Riding its back, locked onto the seams of its carapace with slate-dark limbs, is an ash-shard, needle sunk deep into the mason’s last drop of brine. The spire above them has already veered two degrees off plumb—a dry, silent grimace against the pewter haze.

I climb. My spongy pads press against the vertical chalk, finding the tiny, unweathered ledges of the overlapping courses. There is no rush. The mason cannot hear me coming, and the parasite has no eyes to see my shadow—though there are no shadows here to cast.

When I reach the cornice, I pause for a long moment, letting my crest measure the draft. First, with the long, rake-like digits of one forelimb, I gently peel back the excess velum-growths clogging the upper arch. They come away without a tear or a rustle, drifting down through the still air toward the slate floor like weightless sheets of ash. Already, the column of cool air inside the tower begins to lift a fraction more freely.

Then I reach for the mason.

Two delicate, tong-like digits close cleanly around the edges of its carbonate shell—careful not to crush it, careful not to disturb the wet, crooked mortar beneath its pads. The mason’s electrostatic feet release the wall with a faint, dry reluctance. The ash-shard on its back does not thrash; it only clings tighter to its dying host as I lift them both clear of the parapet.

I climb back down the tower face, carrying the pair of them against my ribbed thorax, and set them gently on the dark slate of the avenue, directly in the slow path of the nearest sweeper. The sweeper does not hurry. It will reach them in an hour, or two, and dissolve them back into clean mineral salt to feed the foundation of the wall they were trying to ruin.

I stand for a moment in the empty boulevard, my crest angled toward the dim sky.

There is no satisfaction in it, no grief for the mason’s spent brine or the parasite’s interrupted brood. Only the quiet relief of a balanced load—the sense that the air is moving again through the hollow tower, rising in its slow, laminar chimney toward the upper stone, keeping the whole dry engine turning over in the dark.

When the static field across the plaza settles back into a smooth, even hum, I turn and walk back through the nearest arch. I spiral up the tool-less ramp into the cool void of my tower, press my back against the porous, mite-hollowed pillar, and fold my porcelain limbs tight against my ribs—waiting, without impatience, for the next small ache in the stone.
