I wouldn’t be at the bottom, down where the floor-grates shudder over the impellers and the heat comes up sharpest, stinging with sulfur and scorched grease. That is a place for the light-boned and the quick—the young ones whose backs are still mostly skin and thin, flexible scale, who can dart into the thermal plumes to tear bacteria-hair from the seams and crack chalk-white crabs against the iron without being torn loose by the blast.

Nor would I be out on the snapped ends of the I-beams anymore, waiting with my rear claws locked into the pitted steel for the quarter-hour stall.

I think I would find myself higher up, on the fourth tier, where the current is a little less frantic and the amber light from the nearest caged lamp barely reaches—just a dull, silty smudge against the dark. I would be one of the older ones, heavy enough now that the Sump has begun to win the argument with my joints. Decade by decade, the water has laid its pale travertine across my shoulders in thick, overlapping tiles, and dusted my flanks with velvety black manganese. Where the eddies scour me, my edges are smooth as river pebbles; in the sheltered creases between the plates, my gill-fronds still flare and flutter, tangled with the same colorless threads of bacteria that grow on the floor-seams. I carry a small, warm pasture on my back, and it is getting too heavy to lift into the void.

So I don’t leap anymore. When the pitch of the lower grates drops—when the fluid-filled chambers along my ribs tell me the sluice-gate is about to turn—I don’t scramble out toward the empty center to snatch at loaches in the three-second hush. Instead, I brace my six splayed limbs into the diamond-mesh of the fourth-tier catwalk, or wedge myself inside the rusted doorframe of a maintenance shack where the steel door fell off a century ago.

Inside the shack, out of the worst of the cavitation’s metallic hailstorm, it is almost quiet—if you can call a world quiet when a tectonic *thrum* lives permanently inside your sternum.

I would be a listener. A keeper of the iron.

Because I am blind—my face only a broad, blunt dome of tallow-pale skin pitted with sensory pores—the cracked porthole window of my shack means nothing to my eyes. Still, I like to press my snout close to the glass when the boiler scale drifts past, tasting the water that seeps around the rotten gasket. Everything that happens in the vertical town arrives eventually as a flavor or a tremor.

Three tiers down, the corroded busbar in the half-collapsed junction box arcs—a brief, silent snap that turns the water sharp as copper. Even up here, tucked beneath a dead control console, I taste the bloom of it rising on the thermal plume, and I feel the scatter of claws on the pipework below as the younger ones flinch back into the alleys.

And I hear them talking. Not into the water, which is useless, chewed to pieces by the roar of the impellers, but into the bolted skeleton of the Sump itself.

*Tap—tap.*

A slow, double chime travels up through the flanged risers, bypassing the current, vibrating straight through the grated floor and into the notched, iron-stained claws of my forelimbs. Someone has cornered a loach in a dead-end manifold on the second tier. A moment later, a heavier, three-beat rhythm answers from the pump-house across the void: a pressure-relief valve is about to blow, or the eddy behind the east flange is too crowded. I don’t answer often—my forelimbs are thick with mineral crust now, my strikes duller—but sometimes, when the silt hangs thick and the water tastes unusually sharp of fresh rust, I tap once against the dead console above me. Just a single, heavy *tock* into the iron, to let the lower tiers know the fourth is still holding.

Most of all, though, I am here for the bell.

Just outside my shack, bolted to a stanchion that still remembers the surface world’s geometry, hangs the muffled bronze warning bell. Every so often, tripped by a float-switch buried somewhere in the silt, it rings out its slow, underwater *dong—dong—dong*.

Nobody is coming to answer it. There is nothing above the welded bulkhead to hear it, and if there were, the sound would never make it through three stories of bedrock. To the younger ones, it is only a landmark—a steady pulse in the ironwork to orient by when the quarter-hour reversal turns the chamber into a blinding blizzard of rust.

For me, it is company.

When the current stalls for its three breathless seconds, and the whole roaring, oily lung of the chamber goes slack, I sometimes crawl out onto the grating and haul my heavy, terraced bulk up beside the stanchion. I rest my chin against the cold-turned-warm bronze. When the clapper strikes, the *dong* doesn’t travel through the water at all—it pours straight through my jaw, rattling my teeth, humming down my spine, shaking the travertine plates along my back until the Sump and I vibrate at the exact same pitch.

One day, I know, the mineral crust will fuse the last gap between my shoulder plates. I won’t be able to crawl out to the bell anymore, or wedge myself under the console. I will find a quiet leeward eddy behind the fourth-tier pump-house, lock my six claws into the grating one last time, and let the heat and sulfur keep me whole while the Sump finishes plating me over.

Until then, I stay here in the warm, deafening dark—tasting the copper from the third tier, reading the tremors in the pipes, and resting my chin against a warning that has outlived the world it was meant to save.
