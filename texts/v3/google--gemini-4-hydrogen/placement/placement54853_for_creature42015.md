I would not be inside the ring. Not for long, anyway. Everything inside that wall of willow and alder has already settled into its final shape, and there is no room there for a creature that fidgets, or ages at the speed of weather, or needs stone under its boots.

If I belong anywhere in this world, I am out in the lowlands beyond the rim, where the ground still has gravel in it and the ditches rise and fall with the rain.

I would be the one who notices the culvert.

Every lowland has its drainage—old concrete pipes, rusted sluice-gates, ditches choked with cattails that turn to mud in August and overflow in March. You learn to read them without thinking, the way you read a roofline for sag. Except there is one outfall, tucked back behind a thicket of alder that looks from a distance like a low, silver-grey hillock, where the water never changes. In a dry summer, when the surrounding pastures crack and the creek beds go white with dried algae, a steady, cold stream still pours from three thick, ribbed hoses buried in the nettles. It smells faintly of bog-iron and rot-blackened wood, and it runs at the exact same pressure at noon in July as it does during a November thaw.

Running parallel to the hoses, stapled to leaning cedar posts until it drops into the sedge, is a heavy black cable. Three-phase, armored, old enough that the rubber sheathing has gone chalky in the sun.

Maybe I work the rural grid—a line-walker, or the night-shift tender at a brick substation four miles down the road, where the air smells of ozone and warm oil. If you spend enough hours watching the gauges on an old distribution board, you get to know the personalities of the feeder lines. Most of them breathe. They climb at six in the morning when the dairies start their milkers; they dip at midnight; they spike and stutter when wind-blown branches brush the insulators.

One branch never breathes. Its needle sits dead-still, drawing a flat, unvarying load, hour after hour, year after year.

Sooner or later, anyone who tends lines for a living walks that branch out to see what is still spinning at the end of it.

You follow the cedar posts until the pasture gives way to marsh-grass, and the marsh-grass gives way to a wall of interwoven willow and deadwood, matted so thick with ivy and lichen that there is no clear path through. You have to force your way between the trunks, ducking under low, horizontal limbs that feel more like bone than wood. The air grows cool before you even clear the branches. Then the wind stops, the sky flattens into a thin, pale lid of leaves, and the sound hits you—not a roar from a distance, but a solid, pressurized hum that seems to come up through the soles of your boots.

I wouldn't cross onto the peat. Even before seeing what lives there, you can tell by the look of the floor that it isn't meant for heels or treads. It is too smooth, too level, like a cellar floor swept clean for something that doesn't want tracks left behind.

I would stand just inside the willow-wall, one hand on a wet, lichen-crusted trunk, and watch the three bare pumps shuddering on their pilings. It would take a minute for my eyes to separate the pale, oil-smudged bulk draped across the transformer coils from the iron itself. At first it looks like a discarded tarp, or a swell of peeled alder-root. Then you see the slow rise and fall of ribs, the broad, flat belly pressed tight to the warm metal, the chin resting on the copper-humming casing.

And then, even over the *chug-hiss* of the pumps and the wall of frogsong in the margins behind me, it would know I was there. Not from my breathing, which the machinery swallows, and not from my scent, lost in the dampness—just from the slight, unfamiliar dent my weight makes in the edge of the peat-mat.

It wouldn't rush me. It would just slide down from the iron, smooth and heavy, and shuffle out onto the open floor until it stood between me and the black cable. Broad-footed, earless, teeth stained dark reddish-brown from licking rust off the bolts. Waiting with the infinite, cold-blooded patience of something that has outlasted whoever laid that cable in the first place.

That is the moment you understand what you are in this world.

You aren't the keeper of the hollow. The hollow already has a keeper, and it doesn't know your name or care about your maps. If you stay, you are only a grit in the chord, a strange pulse in the peat that has to be waited out.

So I would step back through the willow and let the branches close behind me.

I would find myself back at the substation, four miles away, where the world still has seasons and rust and storm-damage. That is where I belong: at the unseen end of the wire.

I wouldn't file a report on the un-housed motors sitting in a bog. I wouldn't send a crew out with chainsaws to clear the alder-wall, or fence off the pilings, or hang yellow warning signs on the ancient trunks. I would simply leave that branch off the upgrade lists. When a summer gale knocks out the main feeder and the board goes dark, I would walk the line with my climbers and my splicing kit, clear the fallen oak limbs off the cross-arms, and throw that breaker first—standing there in the dark substation with my hand on the switch, listening to the sixty-hertz hum catch and steady itself, knowing that four miles away, in a cold, loud room beneath the leaves, something pale and damp is laying its cheek back down against the warming iron.
