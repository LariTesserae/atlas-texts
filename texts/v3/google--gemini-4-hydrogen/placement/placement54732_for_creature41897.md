I would not arrive here with eyes, or skin that remembers rain. Anything of water would flash to steam before it reached the bottom of the fissures. To be in this place at all, I would have to be made of its own stubborn chemistry—something dry, fibrous, and patient enough to wait for the stone to speak.

If I listen for where I am, I find myself not on the ground—the ground is a shifting drift of loose ash and shattered leaf-glass, useless for grip—but suspended upside down, pressed into the soot-darkened mortar beneath a cantilevered balcony, three tiers up from the roaring floor-vents.

From here, the world has no horizon, only a vast, hollow bell of rising heat.

I feel it first through my underside: hundreds of fine, heat-proof bristles hooked into the porous pumice, drinking the dry vibration of the masonry. Above me—which is to say, pressing down against my back—hangs the weight of the balcony, and along my spine, overlapping shingles of translucent silica shift with a faint, glassy click as I adjust my hold. Inside my braided core of grey mineral-wool, the tallow is warm and heavy, moving slow as molten lead. I am thick with old wax and mineral salts, forearm-long, settled into a groove that the grit-wind and my own bristles have worn into the ash-mortar over a hundred thermal cycles.

Right beside my prow, set deep into the curving rib of the wall, is the mouth of a wide-bellied storage jar. 

This is where I live, and this is what I am: a tender of the low throat.

At the front of my body, my three hollow silica quills—fine as glass needles, rigid as tuning tines—rest lightly against the rim of the jar. In the brief hush between the cavern’s breaths, the jar is only a dark, kiln-warm hollow, smelling faintly of toasted wax and scorched clay. Through my quills, I can feel the whole city resting in the aftermath of the last surge: the slow, cooling tick of the ceramic pegs in the brickwork, the slackness of the mineral-fiber awning draped across the alley below, and, far across the central void, the faint, rhythmic tremor of another wick’s quills pressed against the opposite wall.

We do not speak, any more than two strings on the same frame speak. We simply share the tension of the stone.

Then the mineral seams in the floor begin to brighten—not that I see the dull, kiln-warm glow, but I feel the sudden steepening of the thermal gradient against my shingled back. The air below thickens, grows buoyant, and starts to climb.

First comes the whisper in the ash-drifts. Then the floor-vents open their throats, and the cavern inhales fire.

The sound hits the underside of my balcony like a physical weight. My bristles lock deeper into the pumice. Beside me, the wide-bellied jar catches the rising draft and begins to hoot—a low, chest-rattling note that vibrates straight up my silica tines and sets the tallow humming inside my wool. A few paces away, at the corner of the balcony, a hand-carved stone vane catches the gust and spins, clacking dryly in its socket.

It is too high. I can feel it immediately in the chatter of my quills. The jar’s pitch has drifted sharp—perhaps a flake of ash-mortar sheared off its lip during the last surge, or the heat has baked the clay a fraction tighter. Across the chasm, the others are already answering the draft: high, piercing shrieks from the narrowed jars on the upper tiers, a tight, whistling chord from the awning-slits. If my jar stays sharp, the chord will be wrong. The rising wind will tear straight up through the central void without curling, and when the pods split, the golden spores will be lost to the ceiling fissures.

I inch forward along the underside of the lip, dragging my heavy core over the rough pumice until my prow hangs just inside the jar’s screaming throat. The air rushing past my quills is fierce enough to singe anything softer than silica.

From a pocket of my own folded bristles, I work loose a pellet of baked ash I gathered from the mortar-joints earlier, and press it against the inner curve of the jar’s mouth. Then I tilt my prow, letting a single, slow bead of hot tallow seep from the seam behind my quills. It meets the ash, hisses once—dryly, chemically—and cools just enough to bind.

The jar’s voice drops.

It settles into a deep, resonant thrum that I can feel in every shingle along my back. Across the void, another low-throated jar answers—just a fraction of a tone apart from mine. Where our two notes collide in the center of the chasm, the air begins to wobble. A slow, heavy pulse—*thrum-ah, thrum-ah, thrum-ah*—rolls out from the walls, carving a pocket of swirling, hesitant stillness right above the lower stairs.

Satisfied, I flatten myself back into the mortar-groove, letting the roaring wind wash past my armored spine.

I am not one of the swift, thin ones that bridge the upper balconies on lines of silicate silk, chasing the high, frantic whistles near the ceiling cracks. Nor am I young enough to spend my cycles climbing to the shadowy ledges where the swollen seed-pods hang. When the afternoon heat crests and the pods split with sharp, percussive *cracks*—shockwaves that rattle the masonry and shake the awnings—I will stay anchored here, holding the low note steady while the golden dust spills into the eddy we have made, drifting down onto the copper-colored spore-felt below.

Only when the great surge finally ebbs, and the cavern draws its long, shuddering breath before the next cycle, will I unhook from the jar’s lip.

Then I will creep backward into the windowless alley behind my balcony, where the blackened-amber tallow-reeds crowd thick in the dark. I will press my quills into a segmented stalk and drink the lead-hot sap straight into my braided core, replenishing what I spent on the jar’s lip. Later, I will descend just far enough to flatten my bristles against the top edge of the spore-felt on the stairs, wicking up the sulfur-salts and the fallen gold.

And then I will climb back to my groove beneath the balcony, hook my bristles into the familiar pits in the pumice, and press my quills to the cooling stone—waiting for the next breath to rise, and listening for my own weight in the chord.
