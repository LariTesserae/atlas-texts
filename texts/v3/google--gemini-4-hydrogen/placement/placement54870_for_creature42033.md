If I am in this valley, I cannot bring anything wet with me. Nothing with a pulse, nothing that bruises into blood, nothing that needs to blink against the glare of crushed-celery dust and ozone. Flesh is a temporary mistake here—too slow to dodge a wall that decides to become a staircase, too soft for a world made entirely of pressure and dry edges.

So I would find myself already stripped down to what lasts an afternoon: something dry, hollow, and tuned to a register that rattles where teeth would be.

At first, I wouldn’t be in the screaming center of the basin, where the reed-towers whistle at full throat and the bamboo-grass pavilions rot and collapse in the space of three breaths. I would find myself further out, near the **sunken plaza ringed by tiered galleries of shelf-fungus**, or higher up, where the layered bridges of vine cross and recross six stories above the street. Somewhere the heat rises cleanly, and the air isn’t yet choked with the chaff of fallen roofs.

There are no eyes to open here, only a sudden awareness of *slackness* and *tension*—the way a drumhead knows when a room is loud.

Beneath me, the bark-pavement is cracked into sharp, dusty polygons. I don’t stand on it. Weight is a liability when the ground can slide sideways on lubricated root-skids at any moment. Instead, I hang a few inches above the dust, buoyed by the warm updrafts bleeding off the sun-baked agave walls and the faint, prickly crackle of static along my own edges.

It takes a moment to realize what I am made of: not bone, not muscle, not leaf. A tall, narrow assembly of pleated, papery vanes, matte and fibrous, the color of a deep plum or a healing contusion, dusted with pale powder that tastes sharply of ozone. Beneath the skin, an internal scaffolding of dry, chalk-white struts holds me taut. When I shift, there is no wet flex of a joint—only the dry, crisp whisper of folded paper sliding against itself.

I am one of the straighteners.

I don’t choose to be straight; I simply *ache* around curves. When a vine tries to spiral across an alley ahead of me, or a cluster of pale shoots begins to fork in messy, branching patience, I feel it as a dissonance in the air, a tug against my vanes. As I drift forward, the space around me tightens. The hyper-fast flora parts before my axis like water before a prow, snapping into thirty-foot avenues, square-stemmed grids, and sharp-edged lintels. Twelve pillars on my left, twelve on my right—held crisp and mathematical solely because I am passing between them.

When the alley pinches toward a corner, I don’t lean into a turn. Curves aren’t in my anatomy. I extend a single chalky strut from my flank, hook its dry tip into the lip of a steaming lily-pad cistern, and *snap* my entire body around the pivot. The sound is like a taut wire plucked in an empty room. Behind me, the tension drops; I can feel the avenue I just left groaning as it slides sideways, pinching shut into thorny latticework now that my axis is gone.

By midday, the hunger comes—not a hollow in a stomach, because there is no stomach, only a dry, vibrating emptiness down my central hollow.

I drift toward the denser quarters, where the celery-green pillars are drawing sap upward at violent pressures, whistling like organ pipes. I find one that is screaming at the pitch of a felled mast, press my flank flush against its vibrating green wall, and lock the neat, vertical rows of four-holed apertures along my side onto the pulse of the plant.

It is not eating, and it is not drinking. The mosses can have the burst tubers and the tepid water. What pours into me is *torque*—the sheer, frantic friction of a million cells dividing at once, the hydraulic thrum, the hot resin vaporizing in the sun. My bruised-violet vanes swell slightly, humming in a deep, discordant chord that makes the dust jump on the bark-plates below. Around the corner, I can sense the flora reacting to the spill of my acoustic static—hollow gourds swelling in the heat, perforating themselves in neat rows of four to bleed off the resonance, mimicking a body they cannot comprehend.

Sometimes, I linger near the sunken amphitheater, where half a dozen of my kind cluster in the pit to feed on the deep, subterranean groans of the roots. Our intersecting fields of tension twist the ground like a wrung rag; the surrounding foliage shoots upward in steep, cabbage-thick tiers, while beneath the dust, three thick white roots knot and unknot in blind, desperate effort to anchor themselves. It is loud there—so loud the reality of the valley feels thin, the space behind us pleating like an accordion so that a fifty-yard corridor stretches into a twenty-minute walk for anything slow enough to walk it.

But I don’t stay in the pit for long. There is too much risk of getting caught when two shifting blocks grind together, or when a three-story pavilion blackens and caves inward. I have felt what happens to the ones who fail to fold fast enough: a sudden, dry shatter of chalky struts, a burst of violet chaff, and then the slow, restless churning of the city pushing their calcified splinters outward, piling them up into the raw, unweathered ring that walls in the basin.

Instead, by late afternoon, as the misting haze thins and the baking heat begins to break, I don’t fold myself flat into the shadow of a fluted pillar. Not yet.

I catch the last warm thermal rising off the upper canopies and drift upward, clear of the snapping lintels and the wrestling roots, rising above the rooftops into the cooling air. From here, the four-mile bowl lies open beneath me—entirely green, bone-white, and endlessly busy with the fever of being a city for no one. Other bruised-violet shapes are rising around me like narrow, angular kites, humming softly in the dusk, suspended between the screaming valley below and the quiet, calcified rim of our own dead at the edges.

We hold the air taut for a little while longer, endlessly straightening a world that was never meant to be straight, before the dark settles and we fold ourselves back into the cracks.
