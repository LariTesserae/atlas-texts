If I arrived here as I am—something with weight, lungs, and a habit of looking for doors—I wouldn’t last long enough to understand the room. My feet would drag the cantilevered balconies off their invisible bearings; my breath would be too loud in the heavy hush; my eyes would strain against the ember-haze and find nothing to read.

So I would have to shed those things first. I would have to become smaller, quieter, and much more patient.

I think I would find myself high up, just below the ceiling of the blister, where the bruised-violet light leaks through the hairline fractures in the basalt. That is where the two great facts of this world touch: the furnace-heat rising from the core below, and the high-altitude freeze bleeding through the outer stone. Everything that happens here happens because those two temperatures cannot agree.

At first, I might imagine I am only a listener, tucked into one of the windowless chambers where the corridors angle deeper than the thin shell of the building should allow. It is a strange place to be—folded sideways out of the main void, neither quite inside the city nor quite outside it. The air there is still and thick, smelling of wet slate. From the threshold, I could watch the balconies drift a few centimeters sideways as the thermal cycle turns over. I could wait for the *click-tink* of the grazing things along the seams, and the brief, sharp hiss of a water droplet striking a warm ceramic roof.

But a listener is still a tourist. The blister doesn’t keep tourists; it only keeps what can live on its gradients.

If I stayed long enough, I think my edges would begin to match the room. I wouldn’t be one of the builders—they are gone, or were never made of anything that could stay. And I wouldn’t be the calculation itself, humming along the pale membranes; I am too curious for that, too inclined to pause and wonder what a shift in the light means.

Instead, I think I would become something like a knot in the margin—a creature of the *seam*, related to the drifters, though perhaps a little slower to leave the deep corners.

I can feel what that body would be like. No eyes, because there is nothing here worth seeing at a distance—only light to be answered, and heat to be ridden. My upper side would be matte and dark, drinking in whatever stray warmth rises from the shaft, while my underside would be pleated and pale, holding pockets of warm vapor to keep me just buoyant enough to forget the drop below. Along my rim, a fringe of tiny, pearlescent nodules would catch the invisible field-lines around the ceramic anchors, holding me in place without effort, the way a dust mote hangs in a shaft of sun.

During the stagnant hours, when the updraft fails and the air pools thick and motionless, I would tuck myself into the impossible angles of the windowless rooms. Folded flat against the matte alloy, I wouldn’t look alive at all—just another baffle, another piece of quiet architecture waiting for the temperature to change.

And when the deep cycle below finally turned over, and the first sluggish breath of ozone-scented air brushed across the threshold, I would unhook and drift out into the void.

Not to solve the city. That is the part I would have to let go of—the urge to know what the equation is for, or who was supposed to read its answers. Down here, meaning is much simpler than that. It is the dry, rhythmic *click-tink* of a silica rasp clearing sulfur from a damp seam. It is the sudden, blurring flick of a tendril catching a falling bead of water before it can vanish into an intake slit. It is the slow, effortless glide along a gossamer filament stretched tight across a drop that has no visible bottom.

Sometimes, hanging upside down beside a pale ceramic node, I would see a nearby membrane brighten—a cold-white pulse signaling some tiny, incomprehensible adjustment in the machine’s hidden math. And without deciding to, without knowing why, the thin slits along my own flank would brighten in return, passing the ghost of that light onward to the next dark shape clinging to the tier above.

Not a message. Just an echo, keeping time with the only clock in the dark.

I would live in the narrow band where the heat is survivable and the dampness gathers—neither rising into the freezing fractures nor sinking into the amber haze. And when, after a very long time, my pleated bladders finally cooled and lost their lift, I wouldn’t fight the descent. I would simply let go of the field-lines, trailing a little salt-dust behind me, and drift down through the shimmering layers toward the orange glow—returning the sulfur to the updraft, so the seams above could bloom again.
