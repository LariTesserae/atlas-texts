I would not be near the cleft. Up there, twenty paces below the frost-shattered sky, the air moves too fast and tastes of nothing. It steals heat from the skin and offers no weight in return.

I would be down where the stone curves inward like the hollow of a throat, three ledges above the eastern basin.

Here, the dark is thick enough to lean against. My spine knows the exact shape of the basalt behind me because ten thousand spines before mine have ground it smooth, leaving a shallow, greasy dish that catches the warmth of my kidneys and holds it. I do not sit up straight—there is no reason to spend breath holding a posture the rock is willing to carry for me. I let my shoulders slope forward, let my heavy ribcage hang between my arms like a leather bellows at rest, pulling in the slow, wet soup of the chamber: ammonia, warm limestone, the sweet rot of the floor, the sharp, oily musk of the birds shifting fifty feet above my head.

If you asked me what I am, I would not have a word for it. Naming takes air, and air is for the lungs and the hum.

I am one of the slow ones. One of the heavy-shouldered, tallow-skinned shapes that the bats ignore and the crickets walk over without breaking stride.

Right now, a cricket is crossing the back of my left wrist. I feel the dry, needle-light *tick-tick-tick* of its legs as it navigates the coarse tuft of hair growing along the bone, its long antennae sweeping my skin to taste the grease there. I do not twitch. Twitching is a waste, and besides, its carapace feels thin—a young one, not yet worth the closing of a hand. I let it pass onto the damp wall, where it disappears into the living, rustling tapestry of its kind.

My right hand rests palm-down on the mineral-glossed lip of the ledge. Through the horn-thickened pads of my fingers, I can read the whole room better than any eye could.

Below the high, papery *whir-clatter* of the oil-birds and the needle-rain of the bats, the stone carries the slower news. Two ledges down, near the rim of the lukewarm pool, someone is drinking—the faintest *slap-drip* of a cupped palm breaking the surface, careful not to wake the pale salamanders hanging beneath. Farther left, in the deep cleft where the shelf-fungus gives off its dry, spore-heavy heat, a pair of elders are pressed forehead-to-forehead. I cannot hear them over the roar of the chamber, not with my ears, but I feel their chests working through the basalt: a low, gravelly vibration, steady as a heartbeat. *Warm here. The mire is rising. Stay.*

I press my own palm a little flatter against the stone and answer, letting a quiet hum rumble up from the bottom of my ribs, down through my collarbone and into the rock. *Here too. The water is clear.*

It is not a conversation. It is simply the sound of the mountain knowing where all its pieces are.

Beside my hip, wedged into a natural pocket of the flowstone, sits my inheritance. My fingers find it without looking—they have never looked at anything, not since I was a wrinkled weight clinging to the chest-tufts of my mother, learning the pitch of her hum through the small of my back. It is one of the hard ones. Smooth, dense, heavy in a way the porous basalt around us never is, bound around its waist with a knotted cord of twisted sinew and crown-hair that has been oiled and re-oiled by a hundred palms before mine.

I do not know what a river is. I do not know that this stone was once rolled in cold, loud water under a sky so wide it has no ceiling. To me, it is simply the thing that does not break. When a bird's heart gives out in the upper dark and its heavy, grease-rich body tumbles through the roar to thud against the lower shelves, I will untie the sinew, lift the hard one, and join the others. *Tock-tock-tock.* The dry, clean rhythm that cuts through the wet clamor of wings. Bone splitting for marrow. Fat stripped cleanly. Nothing left for the dark to waste.

But nothing has fallen yet. So I wait.

Waiting here is not an emptiness; it is the whole shape of a life. My chest rises—a long, deliberate pull—and falls. A drop of lukewarm mist gathers on the blunted tip of a stalactite overhead, swells until it is heavy enough to defeat the stone, and drops straight down, striking the back of my neck before rolling along my collarbone.

Far above, through the crooked crack at the summit, the alpine wind must be skipping across the mouth of the peak, thin and cold as iron. Perhaps a single flake of snow is falling right now through the pewter-colored sliver of light, melting before it ever reaches the roosts.

I do not tilt my head up. Up there, there is nothing to touch, and nothing that hums back when you press your hand to it. Down here, the stone is worn to the exact curve of my spine, the crickets are ticking softly against my ankles, and the dark is warm enough to keep us all alive.
