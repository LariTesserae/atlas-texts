I would not be out on the open shelf, where the wind has nothing left to work on except gravel and its own patience. And I would not be in the bright, empty center of a market plaza, where the daylight is too thin to hold anything together.

I would be on the shoulder of a basin, halfway down from the rim, where the outer kilns begin to crowd against the first terraces of giant moss.

Specifically, I would be at a weir.

Not a grand one—just a waist-high notch of dry-stacked slate where two channels meet: one carrying dark condensation-water down from the fern-brakes above, the other bringing water briskly uphill from the lower quarters. They should cancel each other out, or spill over the herringbone path in a cold, gray sheet. Instead, they meet at the copper-bound lip of the weir and simply braid, slipping through the narrow oak sluice-gate together without raising a ripple.

It is the kind of place you only find if you walk past the kilns twice, or if you follow a shadow that hasn’t caught up to its cloud yet.

*

I think I would not have arrived here in boots. If I had, the thin air would have already taught me to stop hurrying, and the cold would have long since settled into my knuckles like damp in unseasoned oak.

Instead, I think I would be something closer to a **joiner**—not one of the great Tenders who raise the multi-story houses or carve the door lintels, and not one of the soot-colored grazers that vanish the moment a shadow snaps back to its fog bank. Something smaller in ambition, and more patient in posture.

I would be the one who tends the *fit*.

When a street is three hundred years older on its left side than on its right, the flagstones do not naturally agree. The older slate has been worn concave by centuries of mist and shifting weight; the newer basalt is still sharp-edged, dark, and stubborn. Where they meet down the center of the alley, the world wants to peel. Left alone, the bedrock beneath will begin to curl upward like wet vellum, exposing the underside of the cobblestones to the pale, pulsing membranes below.

My work—if you can call it work when there is no hurry and no one waiting for it to be finished—is to keep the seam tight.

I would have hands like the Tenders: pale, matte, the color of lichened slate, with the faint, milky translucence at the joints where the stone has thinned. I wouldn’t have a face worth describing—only the smooth, rain-washed plane beneath a hood of woven reed, and no mouth at all, because there is nothing here that needs to be persuaded by speech. My joints would move with that dry, small *snap* of a seed pod opening at midday, and between one motion and the next I would stand still long enough for the fog’s fine hairs of moisture to bead along my wool.

*

In the bright, ordinary daylight of the surface, you wouldn’t see me.

You would only see the bronze wedges resting in the crack between the two centuries of pavement, and the small copper mallet lying beside a bowl of crushed peat. You might notice that the wedges seem to have been driven in a little deeper since you looked away to watch a fog bank roll over the rim. You might hear, from somewhere just behind your own shoulder, a single, dry *clack*—like two flat stones meeting in the dark.

I would be working from the folded layer just underneath the noon.

From inside the seam, the air is thicker. It smells of wet iron, old oak, and the clean, sharp tang of hammered copper. Up here—or down here, depending on how the bedrock is lying—the light isn't the bright, uncolored glare of the plateau. It is the silver, diffused light that comes *through* the translucent sheets of stone, like daylight filtered through horn or thin ice.

I kneel on the older side of the street, because the older stone holds a body better. With a flat-edged chisel, I shave a millimeter of peat and root-fiber from the lip of a cushion-moss that has begun to creep over the boundary. I do not kill the moss—it is only doing what it knows how to do, lifting the flat stones like a slow green lever—I just turn its edge slightly, coaxing it back toward the retaining wall where it is needed to bind the mortarless slate.

Then I take the copper mallet and tap the bronze wedge once.

Above, in the empty, sunlit street, no one is there. The mallet does not appear. Only the wedge sinks a hair’s breadth deeper into the basalt, and a faint puff of dry stone dust rises from the crack and settles immediately in the still air.

*

If you ask what I am, beyond the reed coat and the mineral hands, I think I am **the habit of keeping things aligned**.

There are no animals here to carry seeds, no birds to map the distances between basins, no voices to remember which side of the alley was built first. Without someone—or something—at the seams, the plateau would not fall into ruin; it would simply *unfold*. The uphill water would forget where the ferns were, the shadows would drift so far from their clouds that they would never snap back, and the cities would peel apart into flat, translucent pages of stone, unreadable and upside down.

I don't stop the delamination. Nobody could; the world is too old for that, and the rules have worn too thin.

I just pin the corners down.

I use oak pegs where the timber still remembers being a tree, and green-patinated copper straps where the stone has begun to forget it is mineral. I listen to the *thud* of counterweights in the workshops three terraces up, and the *snick* of the seed pods in the noon warmth, and I know by the pitch of the water in the weir whether the next fog bank is three miles out or already brushing the outer kilns.

*

And if you were to walk through my street—if you came down from the bare plateau with your lungs burning in the thin air, and stayed past the first hour, past the second fog bank, until the cold settled into your boots and your own shadow began to lag half a pace behind you—

I would not stop you. I would not even turn my head.

I would simply pause, mallet resting on my knee, and watch your shadow linger on the herringbone basalt after you had stepped past. I would measure the gap between your heels and your darkness the way a mason measures the gap between two courses of dry slate.

And if you stayed long enough—if you sat down on the low retaining wall to catch your breath, and let the mist bead on your coat until you stopped shivering and started to match the temperature of the stone—

I would reach out, very slowly, with a handful of damp peat and a strip of hammered copper, and begin to tuck your shadow neatly back into the seam beneath your boots, so that neither of us would come apart.
