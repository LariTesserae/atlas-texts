I would find myself not on the polished floor, and not yet in the quiet eddy of the eastern domes, but wedged high in the fluted ribs of the western wall—halfway between the warm fissures below and the jagged lip where the sky spills in.

At first, there is no *me* separate from the stone. There is only the pressure of the wind trying to peel things off the rock, and the counter-pressure of bone wedged into a basalt seam. Everything here tastes of warm iron and chalk. The roar of the basin is not something heard from the outside; it is a weight that lives inside the chest, a deep, continuous organ-note that makes the marrow hum.

Then the static gathers, and the shape of what I am begins to prickle into focus.

Along the ridge of my spine, fine, dry hairs lift toward the curtains of wire-grass draped from the ledge above. My skin is tight as cured vellum, warm from the stone at my back, and dry all the way through—no sweat to cool it, no oil to slick it, only a fine, pale-gold silt of spores wedged into the creases of my knuckles and wrists. When I blink, a second lid of smoked horn slides across my eyes, dimming the sharp, blue-white snap of sparks in the grass below until they look like slow embers drifting in soot.

I am one of them—perhaps the eighth, the one who prefers the high flutes when the cycle turns—though my knuckles have not yet taken on the copper-green stain of the elders. My horn-plates are still thin along the forearms, notched and pale where the flying silica has scored them.

Down on the floor, fifty paces below, the world looks like a shallow, spinning bowl of ash and brass. I can see the three slate domes tucked into the eastern alcoves like barnacles in a tide-pool, and the tiny, taut line of braided fiber outside them, its bone-notches blurring as they clatter. Near a boulder in the lee of the wind, the elder is crouched low, her grounding cord trailing behind her like a dark tail over the ironstone, scraping the dry pith from a dead rattle-reed.

Up here, I do not wear a grounding cord. A cord would only snag in the reeds and whip me against the wall. Instead, I let the charge build.

That is why I climb to the western ribs. The seven down in the alcoves read the basin through the floor—through the bass hum of the basalt plazas and the high, frantic clicking of the drum-crabs in the grass. Someone has to sit where the cold crosswinds first drop over the rim and collide with the geothermal heat rising from the fissures. Someone has to feel the air before it hits the floor.

I press the flat of my jaw against the smooth, wind-carved flute of the wall, hooking my double-jointed fingers into the honeycomb pits to anchor myself. Through the dense bone of my temple, the basin sorts itself out. The *clatter-clack* of the lower reeds fades into a distant, rhythmic rustle. Beneath it, I can feel the long, slow shudder of the peak itself—the cold weight of the high-altitude dust pressing down against the warm updrafts.

A sharp *snap* jumps from my elbow to the basalt, numbing my wrist for a heartbeat. The charge is thickening faster now. The ozone taste on my tongue turns metallic, almost sweet. Down in the fissures, the bellows-blooms are beginning to swell, their leathery bladders stretching tight in the dimness, holding their breath.

I do not wait for the bone-line outside the domes to chatter. I feel the shift first in my teeth—a sudden, heavy drop in the stone’s vibration, a full octave lower, as if the whole mountain just opened its throat.

A gust spills over the jagged rim above me, cold enough to make the dry plates along my shoulders creak. Below, the elder pauses, touching her knuckles to the ironstone. From the low doorways of the eastern domes, dark shapes begin to uncurl, unfolding into the bruised twilight with wide, knotted nets in their hands.

I unhook my fingers from the rock, drop my center of gravity low, and let the wind catch the curve of my back. I don’t climb down; I step out into the updraft, sliding from ledge to ledge with my grooved soles hissing against the basalt, bleeding off the last of my static into the rushing air as I fall toward the floor to run with the rest.
