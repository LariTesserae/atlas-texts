I wouldn’t be up on the sunlit domes, where the swells break into blinding silver and the cormorants knife down through the foam. That is too loud, even for a body built to take the weight of it—too much frantic light, too many herring scattering like flung coins, too much of the surface’s bad temper.

And I wouldn’t be down in the blue-black trenches, either, where the silt boils up like cold smoke and the pressure turns everything slow and blind.

If you looked for me in this drowned range, you would find me in the middle shelves—right where the bull kelp thins out into bare, fluted gneiss, and the great armored power cables come snaking down from the turbine saddles toward the intake station.

At first, I think I would just be a weight pressed into the lee of a boulder, learning how to stop fighting the current. It takes a while to unlearn the urge to swim. In forty knots of water, swimming is a joke; the mountain will peel you off and fling you into the dark if you try to argue with it. So you find the thin, quiet skin of water—barely an inch thick—that clings to the stone itself. You flatten your belly to the granite. You wedge the blunt keratin pegs of your forelimbs into a groove worn smooth by ancient ice, pinch your nostrils shut, and let the storm pass over your back like a freight train over a tunnel.

That is where the body becomes clear to me. Not sleek, not graceful—heavy-shouldered, slate-colored, slick with cold oil so the glacial grit slides right off. Eyes small and recessed behind thick bone, veiled by clear membranes that flick shut whenever a cloud of sand-lance or torn weed whips past. No ears to speak of, just pinholes sealed behind the skull.

Because here, you don’t listen with your head. You listen with your jaw.

When I press my lower jaw and my ribs against the bedrock, the whole range opens up. It isn’t noise anymore; it’s geography. Five hundred yards up-slope, a pothole is grinding its clutch of cobbles—*clack-grind-clack*—telling me the surface swell is building from the northwest. Farther along the ridge, the kelp holdfasts are creaking like strained timber as the tide begins to turn. And underneath it all, running straight through the stone like a warm vein, is the sixty-cycle hum of the iron.

I wouldn’t be one of the eldest—the great, scarred matriarchs whose hides are nearly white with old barnacle-scrapes and who still remember what the mountain sounded like before the turbines arrived. I would be younger than that, born into a world where the bronze impellers were already part of the geology, as permanent and unquestioned as the granite. To me, a mountain *should* hum.

I think I would be one of the ones who lingers near the cables.

When the current slackens for those brief, breathless minutes between tides, the others head up to the kelp corridors to pry mussels from the ledges or drop armored crabs into the cobble-potholes to let the storm crack them open. I’d go with them for a while, riding the oarweed slingshots across the ridges, banking into the eddies. You have to eat, and there is a fierce, clean joy in letting a cross-swell hurl you through a narrow saddle like a stone from a sling.

But when the heavy rip comes back and drives everyone down into the honeycombed basins to sleep, I wouldn’t always wedge into the rock. I’d find the main trunk cable—thick as a thigh, stapled to the gneiss with rusted iron brackets, furred with sea-moss and barnacles—and hook my pegged hands around it.

The cable leaks a faint, steady warmth. It isn’t much, just enough to take the sharpest edge off the bone-ache of the cold, and it vibrates with a clean, predictable pulse that carries straight up through my wrists and into my chest. Hanging there in the blue-black dimness, just beyond the amber cone of the intake station’s floodlights, is the closest thing to peace this place allows. Translucent shrimp drift in the shelter of the concrete piers, vibrating in place; silver sand-lance flicker at the edge of the light like sparks.

And because I spend so much time with my jaw against the iron, I’d be one of the first to hear when something goes wrong.

It starts small—a tiny, ragged *tick* inside the smooth hum of the cable. A bearing wearing thin up in the saddle, or a tangle of snapped fishing wire and torn kelp fouling a turbine shroud. To the others, it’s just an irritation, a sour note in the stone. To me, it’s a splinter under a fingernail. I can’t sleep through it.

So before the maintenance bell even starts its long, swaying descent from the silver ceiling, I’d haul myself up the cable-line toward the saddle, wedging from bracket to bracket against the push of the tide. If it’s just fouled wire or a jammed crab-pot rope wrapped around the intake grate, I’d hook one arm tight around a concrete pier, brace my blunt face against the wash, and use my stone-cracking teeth and pegged fingers to worry the tangle loose until the shroud clears and the *thrum* settles back into its clean, steady bass.

If it’s deeper—if the bronze blades themselves are grinding—I’d clear out and drop back down to the shelter of the station’s piers, because I know the taut steel hawser is about to start singing in the current.

I’d watch them come down, then: the clumsy, fragile things in their canvas and brass, weighted with lead boots, trailing noisy ribbons of wasted air. They look absurdly vulnerable in the forty-knot gloom, squinting through scratched glass, fighting upright against water that would snap their necks if they lost their grip on the safety line. I wouldn’t fear them, and I wouldn’t try to speak to them—what could you possibly say to a creature that can’t hear through its own bones?

Sometimes, though, while they work with their heavy wrenches and the amber light swings wildly across the silt-fog, I’d glide out from behind the pier—just close enough to brush a scarred shoulder against one of their rubber air hoses, feeling the strange, hollow *hiss-thump* of their breathing inside my own ribs, before banking back into the dark.

And afterward, when the bell hauls back up and the ragged stutter in the mountain has finally gone quiet, I’d leave whatever I’d pried off the grates—a knotted wad of monofilament, maybe, or a few cobbles polished round as marbles from the upper basins—wedged neatly into the iron brackets by the intake door. Not as a gift, really. Just clearing the shelf, the way you tidy a ledge before the storm takes it back.
