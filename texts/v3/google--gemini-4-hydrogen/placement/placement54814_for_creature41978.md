I would not be in the main room. There is barely an elbow’s width of passage there as it is, and the keeper’s elbows are already full—braced against the weeping bellies of the jars, lashed to the ceiling ribs, keeping that single wet wheel turning. A second pair of hands in that scalding olive dark would only bruise the rims.

I would be one bowl down.

Through the poorly mortared gap behind the keeper’s lowest reed baskets—past the spot where the wall forgets to be a wall and opens onto a sliver of colder air—the room repeats itself, smaller and tighter, suspended in the draft like the second cup in a nested set. That is where I would find myself: crouched in the narrow, pale-lit hollow beneath the keeper’s floor, where the heat from the sunken brazier arrives only as a faint, damp memory through the mud-daub, and the screaming blue outside is no longer muffled by three layers of hide.

Here, the ceiling-ribs are closer together, and the oiled-skin membranes stretched between them are thin enough to let the sky through—not bruised olive, but a washed, stinging celadon. The wind does not boom here like a fist on a drumhead; it whines across the taut gut like a bowstring pulled a tone too high, vibrating in the sternum rather than the molars. There are no broad, heat-bloated leaves crowding the rafters. Only a few stunted, thick-skinned ferns cling to the mortar, their edges rimed with a fine, dry salt of frost whenever a gust leans hard against the outer hide.

And I would not be wet from the wheel.

If the keeper above is raw slip—slick, unformed, held together by hemp bindings and copper teeth so the pressure does not split it—then I am what clay becomes when it has sat a long time in a cool draft: *leather-hard*.

My skin would be matte, cool to the touch, the dull grey-green of slate before it rains. No sheen of water, no soft slump at the waist or throat. If you pressed a thumbnail into my forearm, it would not sink into mud, nor would it flake away in dust; it would leave a clean, burnished dent with a sharp edge, the way a bone tool marks a pot just before it is ready for the kiln. I would wear no hemp lashings at my wrists or ankles. I am already past the point of slumping. Instead, my danger is the opposite of the keeper’s: not splitting from softness, but stiffening into chalk.

So I would spend my cycles in the narrow margin between thawing and freezing, tending the seam where the keeper’s excess falls.

Everything the room above cannot afford to keep eventually slips through the poorly mortared crack overhead. A bead of condensation that vibrates off its bowstring and misses the pitcher-plant; a stray curl of grey slip scraped from a weeping jar; the frost-bitten tip of a gourd-vine, pinched off cleanly by two square-tipped thumbs before the chill can climb back into the shelf. They drop through the sliver of ozone-smelling air and land in my lap, or on the narrow, unglazed ledges that ring my smaller walls.

I would catch them. Not to spin them upward—there is no momentum left in them for that—but to use what little moisture they still carry.

When a pinched-off tendril of vine falls through the gap, stiff and glassy with upper-atmosphere ice, I hold it in the hollow of my palm until the faint heat rising through the floor-baskets softens its skin. Then I braid it—slowly, carefully, because my own fingers are stiff as dried reed—into the gaps where my thinner hide walls have begun to pull away from the mud bricks. Where a hairline crack opens in the mortar, letting in a needle of air so cold it smells like struck flint, I score the edges with the corner of a broken potsherd, wet them with a single drop of fallen condensation, and press a thumbful of salvaged slip into the seam. It sets almost immediately, turning matte and tight, holding the blue out for another cycle.

Sometimes I hear the keeper directly above me. The *scritch-scritch* of the heavy-shelled snail rasping its terracotta comes through the ceiling muffled and slow, like a fingernail dragged across a wooden bowl. Now and then, a pale, tuberous root pushes all the way through the floor-daub, groping blindly into my colder air for a jar that isn’t there. I do not cut it back. I only tuck its tip into a damp hollow behind my own water-pan—where the water does not simmer, only trembles under a skin of thin ice—so it can drink what it needs and pull itself back up into the dark.

I have no use for speech either, though for a different reason than the keeper. Above, the noise is too wet and loud for words; down here, the air is too thin to carry them. If I tried to speak, the sound would simply flatten against the celadon light and blow away through the seams. Instead, I listen downward.

Because behind *my* lowest shelf—where the willow cradles hang empty and dry—the mortar fails again.

The gap there is narrower than a wrist, opening onto a third bowl below me, smaller still: no larger than a potter’s kiln-hood, bright as the inside of a glacier, loud with the raw, unhindered hiss of the stratosphere. Whatever lives down there—if anything can—would have no slip left on its ribs at all. It would be bone-dry, or fired hard, or made of pure salt and ice, ringing like porcelain every time the wind struck its roof.

Once in a long cycle, I crouch beside that lower crack, bracing my cool, matte palms against the floor so I do not lose my balance over the drop. I watch the frost try to climb up from the bright bowl below, feathering white along the underside of my reed baskets. I take a sliver of warm, damp mud I saved from the keeper’s floor, smear it across the rim of the crack, and feel it stiffen under my thumb—not quite wet, not quite dry, holding the middle place between the spinning world above and the shattering blue below.
