I would not be near the fissures, where the air thins and the light has teeth. I would be three shelves down, on the wide lip of flowstone where the heat from the floor still reaches the belly, but the peat—and the restless, hunting weave of the centipedes—stays below.

If you looked for me there, you would see only the shoulder of a boulder, upholstered in the same dark velvet moss as the rest of the ledge. It takes a long time to notice that a stone is widening, then settling, in time with the *tock* of water dropping from an upper fern-frond.

When I wake—though waking is too sharp a word for it; it is more like a slow rising through tepid water—the first thing I know is the itch of lime in the creases behind my knees. Months of sweating stone have stiffened the hide there, turning the folds dry and brittle. Beside me, inside the waist-high ring of stacked travertine slabs, two others are still curled. One has its blunt chin resting on my forearm; I can feel the slow, heavy pulse in its throat, slower than the drips, steady as the warmth rising through the stone beneath us.

I do not pull away all at once. Nothing here jerks or snaps. I wait for the pulse in my own neck to widen, then I unhook my hip from the stone lip and let my weight pour sideways over the ring’s edge, four-limbed and low, sliding down the mossy incline until my palms find the wet, familiar ridges of the lower wall. Before I go, I press the flat of my wrist against the travertine rim, leaving a faint, oily smear in the stagnant air: *the shelf is warm, the crust is thick, I am moving down.*

The tufa niche is just beyond the curtain of aerial roots. I back into the hollow, feeling the soft, chalky rock give slightly beneath the weight of my shoulders. I grind my flanks against it, slow and deliberate, until the stiffened mineral film flakes away in pale, powdery curls. When I turn and gather the dust on my flat palms to lick it clean, it tastes of old water and dense bone—the taste of staying solid in a world that dissolves everything given enough time.

A heavy-bodied constrictor is draped across the broad, waxy leaf-plates that bridge the gap to the next alcove. Its skin is the exact bruised green of the ferns, its belly the chalk-white of their undersides. I do not startle it, and it does not coil. I step wide, placing my splayed feet on the bare travertine where the blind ants are marching in a silent, chemical line, and let my belly brush the cool, damp moss rather than the snake’s flank. We know the weight of each other. Neither of us wastes the heat it would take to quarrel.

By the time I reach my alcove, the bruised shaft of noon has arrived—not a beam, just a thinning of the olive gloom above the upper ledges, enough to make the wet beads along the travertine ridges gleam like small eyes.

My basket sits in the dryish recess, half-swallowed already by creeping liverwort. The aerial roots I wove into it seasons ago have grown stiff with mineral crust; soon I will leave it to the moss and begin another, softening the pale root-braids between my blunt teeth, stripping the bark by touch in the dark.

But not yet.

I settle my wide chest against the warm stone, let my milky third lid slide across my eyes to clear the damp, and reach into the basket. My thick, pad-tipped fingers know the shape of everything inside without looking: three river-smooth white pebbles, worn slick by water I have never seen, and a shallow heap of giant beetle wing-cases.

I lift one of the wing-cases—no larger than the flat of my thumb—and hold it where the rumor of daylight touches the ledge.

For a long time, nothing happens. It is only a dark, curved scrap of shell. Then I tilt it a fraction of an inch.

A flare of green-violet answers from the gloom—sharp, brief, impossibly bright, like something alive that does not belong to the heat or the damp. I tilt it back, and the color dies into slate. I tilt it again, and it wakes.

Down in the peat, a moth drifts between the fern-fronds like a scrap of ash, and a gecko’s toe-pads adhere to the sweating wall without a sound. The chamber simmers in its thick, unhurried quiet. I sit in the middle of it, wide-hipped and moss-backed, turning a dead beetle’s wing in the dark—holding onto a piece of the sky the only way a creature of the stone knows how.
