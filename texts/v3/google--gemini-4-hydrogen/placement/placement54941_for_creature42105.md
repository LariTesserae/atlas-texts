I wouldn’t be near the bore-shaft, where the air still remembers a vertical drop and the drums come down on cables with the faint, metallic smell of the surface clinging to their rims. And I don’t think I’d be at the main generator cages either, where the turbines whine high enough to set your teeth on edge and the halogen glare burns too white against the mist.

I’d find myself deeper in—about two-thirds of the way across the rift, where the ceiling-fins dip low enough to make the valley feel briefly like a throat, and three smaller dewatering branches from the side-fissures converge into a single twelve-inch iron trunk.

A manifold junction. No real pump-house there—just a wedged shelf of fractured dolostone canted over a warm, shallow pool, a steel trestle bolted into the raw basalt, and a single yellowing sodium-vapor lamp caged in wire. Its amber smudge barely reaches the next rock pillar, and beyond that, there’s only the dark, the sheen of wet stone, and the faint, wet click of harvestmen shifting on the underside of the pipe.

That is where I’d be: one of the sparse handful, neither the first nor the last along the line. A junction-tender.

---

At first, when you drop into a place like this, your eyes try to do all the work, and they fail almost immediately. Thirty paces of mist and then a wall of jagged shadow. You can’t map a valley you can’t see across.

So you stop trying to hold it as a picture, and you start holding it as a pulse.

If I lived at that junction, my whole world would be the meeting of rhythms. Each of the three branch pipes coming out of the dark side-fissures has its own voice—one a steady, thick *thrum* from a sump pump tucked behind a talus jam, one a wet, irregular *chug* where air keeps getting sucked through a low screen, and the third just gravity-fed, a fast, glassy hiss of lukewarm water sliding downhill through iron. They meet at the cast-iron manifold right beside my boots, and when they’re all running clean, the combined vibration settles into a low, even purr you can feel through the rubber soles of your waders without even thinking about it.

That’s what I’d be: someone who knows when the purr goes thin.

You don’t need much else down here. Cut-off waders stiff with mineral salt at the cuffs, knee-pads buckled tight over skin that’s pale and nicked from brushing dolostone, a fiberglass helmet slick with condensation so the lukewarm drip from the ceiling-fins runs down the brim instead of into my eyes. Gloves curled into permanent hooks from grease and wrench handles. You stop noticing the thirty-degree broth-air after a while—not because it gets cooler, but because your body gives up on the idea of being dry, and once you stop waiting to dry off, the wet just becomes another kind of skin.

---

My days—though *day* doesn’t mean much when the sodium-vapor lamp never blinks—would be small, unhurried, and mostly horizontal.

Moving with that low, three-point balance across the talus, testing every slab with a toe before putting weight on it so it doesn’t shift and ring like slate. Scraping the pale, blistered crusts of gypsum and iron-ochre off the intake screens with a short-handled chisel before they choke the flow. Brushing a layer of long-legged cave crickets off a valve wheel with the back of a wrist—not with disgust, just the way you’d brush sawdust off a bench, because there’s nowhere else for them to go and they’ll be three-deep again by the time you turn around.

And listening. Not with your ears, which are already full to the brim with the slap of water and the endless hammering of the pistons two hundred meters up-valley, but with your heels and your palms.

I think I’d be good at the pipe-language. There’s something deeply honest about talking through iron. When you’re shouting in the air, the mist eats your words; when you tap a wrench handle twice against the twelve-inch trunk, the sound doesn’t care about the mist or the turbines or the bats chittering in the upper fissures. It travels clean and bright right under the roar, straight into the soles of someone crouching in another amber smudge three hundred meters away.

*Tap-tap.* Clear the screen.
*Tap—tap-tap.* Pressure’s dropping on the east branch.
*Tap-tap-tap.* I’m shutting the gate for ten minutes; don’t let the sump run dry.

Sometimes, if I put my gloved palm flat against the wet insulation of the main line, I’d feel the echo of someone else’s wrench-tap traveling past my junction toward the bore-shaft, and I’d know they were still there, still keeping their stretch of the equilibrium open, even if I hadn’t seen their helmet-brim in weeks.

---

Where the sodium-vapor lamp meets the wet basalt, nothing green grows—the light’s too yellow and dim for the ferns, which prefer the harsh white halogens closer to the big pump-houses. My shelf would have only the slick, dark sheen of wet rock and the pale, tentative mineral skin forming in the seams.

Down below the trestle, in the warm pool, the blind white crayfish and pale eels would churn the silt in slow, constant circles. I’d keep a wire trap hung off the steel crossbeam, pulling it up when it felt heavy, boiling them with salt in a battered kettle wired straight to the nearest junction box. You’d eat slowly, sitting on an overturned drum with your helmet off for twenty minutes, watching the mist drift through the amber cone while the grease cooled on your knuckles.

And when it came time to sleep, I’d climb onto the sagging catwalk above the manifold—just high enough that the grating catches the worst of the ceiling-drip—and crawl inside a canvas hammock wrapped tight in fine mesh. Not to keep out the noise. The noise is the blanket. If the noise is there, the bearings are greased, the pistons are cycling, the water is staying at its mark.

If I ever bolted upright in the dark, heart hammering before my eyes even opened, it wouldn’t be because of a sound. It would be because the twelve-inch trunk beneath the catwalk had dropped half a beat, or gone suddenly, terribly quiet.

---

I don’t think I’d wonder much about the surface, or how long I’d been down there, or when the rock might finally wear smooth. A place this newly cracked doesn’t ask for urgency; it just asks for attendance.

I’d be the one at the confluence, with a chisel in one hand and a wrench in the other, keeping the three dark branches clear so the main line can keep humming—just another piece of the valley’s slow, damp patience, waiting out the centuries alongside the eels and the ochre.
