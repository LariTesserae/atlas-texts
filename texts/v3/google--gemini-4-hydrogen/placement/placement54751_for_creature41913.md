If I tried to stand on the marl shelf, I wouldn’t last long. There is too much sky and nothing to lean against, and the glare would scour me until I felt like an intruder made of wet meat and bad timing. Even kneeling at the lip of the pocket, I would only be a disruption—a warm, clumsy shape blocking the pressure gradient, making the clay stutter beneath my palm.

To actually *be* in this world, I would have to give up everything that spoils in dry air. I would have to stop needing shadow, and stop needing water, and stop expecting the ground to yield when I press my weight into it.

At first, I might think of the seams—those hairline cracks between the wedged amphorae where the windblown grit settles and the silver lichens hold on with thread-roots. There is a kind of stubbornness there that appeals to me: growing a fingernail’s width in a decade, stiff and papery, refusing to bend. But the lichens only endure the place. They sit on the margins of the noise, shedding flakes of themselves into the draft, waiting out centuries of flat light.

I wouldn’t want to be on the outside of the chord. I would want to be inside the engine.

So I let go of eyes—what use are they in a bowl of shadowless white?—and I let go of ears, because ears are for things that start and stop, and nothing here ever stops. What is left is a hollow lattice, lighter than pumice, dry as a wasp’s nest, cold as the cellar air rising from the floor.

I would find myself wedged near the outer edge of the courtyard, not in the deep roar of the widest chimneys, but inside one of the honeycomb blocks of pressed chalk-paste, just below the lip where the draft first shears across the rim. My cell is hexagonal, its inner walls still carrying the smoothed-over ghost of someone’s thumb-press from centuries before I existed. I don’t remember the hand that shaped it, only the shape it left behind—because I grew over decades to fit that exact curve, pressing my ribbed, papery flanks outward until the mineral walls and I became a single, load-bearing joint.

From above, if you squinted through the glare until your eyes watered, I would look like nothing at all: just a flat, slightly cupped plug of chalk-grey crust sealing a cell.

Down here, there is no above and no below, only the pitch.

It isn’t sound the way a creature with ears understands sound. It is the thing that keeps me from turning into a dead block of salt. The chord passes straight through the fused paste of my cell walls and sets every brittle chamber of my body humming. Inside me, the dry world behaves like water. Microscopic flecks of silver lichen, salt crystals, and chalk dust—skimmed from the air passing over my upper vent—never settle to the bottom of my ribs. The vibration holds them suspended, swirling them in tight, silent eddies through my fluted passages and dry sieves, stripping out whatever trace of energy they carry before the spent powder vents back into the draft.

I don’t have to breathe. The wind breathes across me, and the tremor does the rest.

Through the shared walls of the honeycomb, I can feel my neighbors—not as voices, just as different weights of shaking. To my left, a narrow reed of clay shivers at a pitch so high it feels like a needle held steady in my cartilage. Beneath me, the wide throat of an unglazed amphora carries the low, stone-dense roar that underpins the whole courtyard. We are all blind, and we have never touched anything softer than dried gypsum, yet we are locked together in a single, unbroken posture.

The only time I know anything exists outside the pocket is when the air breaks.

It happens rarely—a sudden, inexplicable snag in the pressure gradient. The shear over my upper vent falters. Instantly, the pitch in my walls drops a fraction of a tone, thinning into a dry rasp, and the dust swirling inside my chambers goes heavy. For a heartbeat, it threatens to settle, to cake against my fine hairs and choke the sieves.

There is no panic in it, only an immediate, blind tightening. My pleated collar draws taut, widening my throat by the width of a hair to catch the lost draft. Through the chalk walls, I feel a dozen others doing the same—membranes shivering, apertures shifting in unison, all of us straining together to haul the chord back to the exact frequency that keeps us alive.

Then the obstruction moves away from the lip. The pressure gradient smooths out. The note locks flat again, dense as stone, and the dust inside me lifts back into its silent, endless swim. My collar relaxes into its groove, and I go back to being indistinguishable from the clay.
