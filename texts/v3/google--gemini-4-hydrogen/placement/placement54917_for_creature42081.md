Not up on the rim. The rim is too bright, too dry, and too far from the work—if you can call it work. Up there, looking down, the basin looks like a problem someone walked away from, or an engine left running in a ditch. You only understand it when the mist is in your lungs, or whatever you use to take in the wet air, and the thirty-foot walls of dark slate are behind you, weeping cold fresh water down the back of your neck.

I think I’d start in the undercut—that deep, shadowed lip at the base of the cliff where the ancient water-lines are carved into the stone. It’s the one place where the ground doesn’t quite liquefy under you, because the shelf of slate runs out a little way beneath the silt. Fresh water slides down the black rock in thin, steady sheets, tasting of nothing, and hits the salt-brine pooling at the bottom. You can sit right at the seam where the two waters refuse to mix cleanly, watching the mineral foam drift past like curdled milk.

At first, I want to be something small and observant—maybe just someone who climbed down a rusted ladder bolted into the slate and forgot why they came, sitting on an overturned brick cistern-rim with their knees drawn up, watching the amber and pale green lamps blink behind their salt-clouded glass.

But a person in boots doesn’t last long down here. Boots sink. Clothes soak up the mist until they weigh as much as the iron pilings, and human ears go slightly mad in a silence this thick. When you can’t hear a cast-iron sluice gate drop, or a mudbank blister and slide away twenty feet in front of you, your eyes start doing frantic, useless work, darting from a twitching pressure gauge to a reversing channel to a dipping dredger-arm, trying to assemble a story out of things that have no plot.

No. If I’m actually *in* this world—not visiting it, not taking notes on it—the body has to change.

It’s simpler than having hands. Hands want to fix things, or pry open the oil-filled housings, or wipe the salt off the gauge-domes to see what the needles are actually reading. Down here, that’s just a way to get pinched between a counterweight and a guide-wire.

What emerges instead is heavier, lower to the silt, and entirely uninterested in what the gauges mean.

I’d be wedged into the gap between a half-buried porcelain insulator and the tarred timber stilt of the pump-house—or no, closer to the wall, where the rubberized conduits snake out of the slate and disappear into the mud. My skin wouldn’t be skin so much as something cured by brine and grit: thick, hairless, the color of wet slate on top and bruised pale gray underneath from dragging over brick nubs. No claws to snag in the tangle. Just broad, grippy pads that know how to take hold of a wet copper float when the floor suddenly turns to soup and starts sliding sideways.

It’s strange, how quickly the eyes give up once you’re down in it. They become just two sunken, membrane-covered pits—useful enough to notice when the flat daylight dims under a drifting bank of mist, or when an indicator lamp blinks green instead of amber, though neither color means anything to me.

The real world opens up along the jaw and down the flanks.

Once you stop trying to *see* the basin, you realize it isn’t silent at all. It’s crowded with information. The sagging black cables overhead bleed a faint, steady tickle of current into the damp air. The brushless motor tucked under the pump-house puts out a hum so low it lives in your ribs rather than your head—a warm, even vibration you can press your belly against when the cold brine gets into your joints. And the water itself is a soup of signals: the sharp metallic tang of a freshly sheared mineral collar, the slick trace of leaked machine-oil, the sudden flatness of fresh runoff when a fissure shifts.

I wouldn’t be one of the bold ones—not the kind that drapes itself over a counterweight to ride the greased guide-wire down into a newly scoured pool, or hitches a ride on the elbow of an automated dredger-arm just to drop off at the far end of its swing. I’d be a little more cautious than that, keeping my strap-like tail coiled loosely around a rusted piling near the edge of the pump-house, where the timber stilts break the force of the reversing channels.

Waiting is most of it. Not bored waiting—just holding shape while the terrain refuses to hold its own.

You feel the change before it happens. That’s the secret of living here. You don’t watch the mud; you listen to the steel. A tripod ten paces away will suddenly go rigid with tension, its riveted legs vibrating at a slightly higher pitch. Behind the glass domes, the needles must be spinning, though I don’t bother to look. What matters is the hydraulic ram beside me: it twitches once, twice, pulling tight in its greased housing with a sub-audible *clench* that travels straight through the wet brick into my jaw.

That’s the cue.

Before the slate beneath us even finishes groaning, I’m already uncoiling from the piling, sliding off the brick rim without a splash. The water is thick with suspended silt, opaque as slate-dust, so you navigate by the hum of the conduits and the shape of the current against your flanks. Two others are already moving in the dark—I can feel the displaced water of their heavy bodies threading through the rubberized pipes nearby. We don’t crowd each other. There’s no need for noise, and no room for it anyway.

When the fissure blows, there’s no roar—just a sudden, massive push of cold, pressurized water from below that lifts the silt bank like a blanket and peels it silently away.

In the brief minute before the cloud settles back in, the water runs astonishingly clear. Newly exposed brass valves gleam dull yellow in the flat daylight. Blind, pale shrimp and sulfur-worms tumble out of the fractured dark, caught in the upwelling, along with a small white crab that tries too late to wedge itself into a pipe joint.

There’s no thrashing. You take what the surge gives you, pulling back into the quiet gap beneath a brick cistern-rim to eat, pressing your pads against the worn masonry until the channel reverses and the mud settles over everything again.

And then, sometimes, in the narrow sluiceway on the way back to the undercut—when the water is just beginning to cloud—you meet one of the others coming the opposite way. Neither of us stops for long. There’s nothing to say, no territory to argue over. We just pause for a fraction of a second, bracing against the wet ironwork, and press our pore-lined flanks together.

A low, wordless thrum passes between us. It’s pitched to the exact frequency of the brushless motors humming under the pump-house—neither mine nor theirs, just the basin’s own pulse, borrowed for a heartbeat to say *still here, still holding*.

Then the silt rolls back in, thick and cold, and we slip apart into the machinery.
