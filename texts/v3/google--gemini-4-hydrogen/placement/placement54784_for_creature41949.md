I wouldn’t be out on the bright, crushed-stone aprons by the central tower, where the sky pours down unblinking and the oxide-red iron rises into the glare. I would drift toward the seam where the basin’s geometry softens—the narrow, cool alleys behind the flat-roofed workshops on the western side, where the basalt cliff rises straight up behind the brick and the hart’s-tongue ferns crowd the foundations.

If I found myself there, it would take me a little while to realize I wasn't listening with my ears.

At first, all I would know is the weight of the head-binding—the snug, slightly greasy pressure of felted wool and beeswax against my temples, the leather strap buckled under my jaw. Everything above my collar would feel sealed in a warm, dense quiet, like having my head underwater. And then, slowly, the floor would start talking.

I’d be barefoot on the flagstones inside the western workshop, or wearing boots so thin-soled and supple they might as well be a second skin. Through my heels, I’d feel the whole basin’s slow, enormous pulse: the deep bass turn of the flywheels three hundred paces off, the sixty-cycle buzz of the transformer yards humming up through the mineral seams of the young rock like blood in a wrist. I’d know I belonged there the moment I stopped trying to hear the air and started reading the stone.

What would I be doing? I wouldn't be one of the ones climbing the high steel gantries—my balance has always belonged closer to the ground—and I wouldn't be pouring lead seals or wrestling the heavy wrenches on the pump-house floors.

Look at the bench in front of me. There’s a curved knife, a brass awl, a pot of rendered tallow, and stacks of cured hide cut into rings and strips. Beside them sit cakes of raw beeswax and hanks of unwashed wool.

I’d be the one who tends the membranes.

At first it would just look like cobbling and gasket-cutting, the quiet, seated work that fits a person who likes their hands busy while the world turns slowly around them. In the mornings, I’d cut leather rings for the sweating iron flanges of the condenser pipes—measuring the give of the hide with my thumb so it swells just enough in the damp to hold pressure without rotting. In the afternoons, I’d stitch the flat, thin-soled boots for the tenders who walk the gravel aprons. Too thick a sole and a person goes deaf to the bedrock; too thin and the sharp, unweathered basalt cuts them to the bone. My job would be knowing the exact millimeter of hide that lets a foot feel a dry bearing three hundred paces away without bleeding for it.

And between the gaskets and the boots, I’d mend the head-bindings.

I like the idea of that—sitting in the cool doorway of the workshop, where the sun doesn't reach and the moss creeps right up to the threshold, fitting a strip of oiled felt for one of the younger ones coming down from the tenements for their first full shift. You can’t rush that kind of fitting. If the wool isn't packed evenly around the beeswax, the sixty-cycle roar leaks in like a needle; if the jaw-strap sits wrong, it rubs raw before the third hour. You have to teach them, with slow hand-signs and a tap of the knuckles against their collarbone, how to test the seal by resting a palm on the workbench and closing their eyes.

Every twelve minutes, I’d know the valve was about to blow without looking at a gauge. I’d see it in the shadow of the starlings lifting off the eastern gantries—a sudden, silent darkening across the bright gravel outside the door.

That’s when I’d set the awl down in the tallow-dish. I wouldn't hurry. I’d just lean my shoulders back against the cool, damp brick of the workshop wall, part my lips slightly to keep the pressure even behind my eyes, and wait.

When the *hiss-clank* hits, it isn't a sound anymore; it’s a hand pressing flat against my chest. For twenty seconds, the mineral seams in the floor shudder so hard my teeth hum, and the dust on the windowsill dances. Everything stops. You can't thread a needle or pare a strip of leather while the rock is shaking like that, so you don't try. You just sit inside the white vibration and let it rinse out whatever small, tight thoughts you’ve been holding onto. When the shadow of the birds drops back onto the gravel, you pick up the knife again.

In the late afternoon, before the light drains out of the scoop, I’d probably take a flat tin can and walk the damp ditch behind the substations—not because I’m one of the water-harvesters, though everyone here clears a fallen fern-frond from a catch-basin when they pass one. I’d go there to check on the pale tree frogs in the shade, watching their throats swell and pulse in perfect time with the transformers, and to gather a handful of dock or bitter greens from the warm, dry soil tucked against the brick walls where the coils throw off their heat.

Then, at dusk, the slow climb up the steel gantries on the eastern cliff, my boots reading every rung, my hand sliding up the cold iron rail—feeling, through my palm, the distant tap of someone three levels down signaling that the last shift is clear.

Inside the tenement, behind the wooden louvers and the heavy woven mats of dried bracken, the roar drops away to the sound of surf a mile off. That’s where I’d finally unbuckle the strap, pull the wax plugs from my ears, and let the room come in.

My voice wouldn't be much to listen to—not up above the rim, anyway. Years of living in the scoop would have taught my throat to drop down below the sixty-cycle drone, into a low, chesty rumble that sits comfortably under the machinery, or else to rise into soft, dry clicks that skate over the top of it. In the evenings, sitting on the bracken mats while the stone walls breathe in the dark, I’d hum with the others—nothing with words, just a slow, steady note that locks into the vibration of the cliffside until you can't tell where your ribs end and the basalt begins.

I wouldn't climb the rim. I know myself well enough to know that the wide, ordinary silence up there—ground that doesn't pulse, air that doesn't hold the steady, patient rhythm of water being pulled from nothing—would feel to me just as it does to them: like stepping off a ledge into empty air, with nothing underneath my heels to tell me I’m still here.
