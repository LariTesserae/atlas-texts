I would not be out on the gravel bars by choice, though I would know them well enough to cross them in the dark. If I am honest about where I would settle in a place like this—where my weight would naturally come to rest—it is at the mouth of a warren, three feet inside the slate-roofed trench where the roar of the valley drops to a heavy, rhythmic thudding in the stone.

I would be one of the dwellers, pared down like the rest of them: short-necked, grease-smelling, my cheekbones crosshatched with the pale scars of wind-thrown grit. Not the strongest pole-carrier in the deep channels, and not the fastest into the flattened ring of sedges after a pylon snaps. I would be the one who stays at the warm edge—perhaps because a peat-bank gave out under me ten winters back and left my left knee stiff in the damp, or perhaps simply because someone has to tend the dry space, and my ears were always better at reading the wall than my shoulders were at hauling mire-beast hides up the scree.

The matte-gray housing would be at my back. That is where you would find me: sitting on a folded slab of grazer-hide a palm’s breadth from the seamless composite, where the air smells of hot dust and ozone and the moss just outside the trench steams steadily into the sleet. 

It is a strange kind of work, tending the only dry thing in a world made of water. You do not worship the box—there is nothing in you that wants to ask who built it or why it runs—but you know its moods the way a person in a gentler world might know the sky. When the storm-load shifts out over the ridges, the housing’s hum changes. It isn’t a sound you hear with your ears so much as a change in the pressure against your shoulder blades, a slight tightening in the teeth. When it drops half a pitch and begins to flutter against the slate floor, I know the katabatic squalls are pouring off the high rock, and I know the tethered pairs out in the marsh will be packing their wicker scoops and tightening their waist-lines.

So I would be the one getting the warren ready before they come back. 

I would be scraping the thick, rubbery mats of pale orange fungus from the base of the metal with a slate knife, dropping the strips into a blackened pot to boil down into bitter paste. I would be gathering the fat, blind slugs from the steaming moss-ring—slowly, because they don’t run, and you leave enough behind to keep the next brood coming—and rendering them down for lamp-tallow in a shallow stone dish. While the pot thickens, I would be checking the boots and the boiled alder-bark shin-guards hanging from the rafters, rubbing fresh storm-bird grease into the seams where the sleet always tries to work its way in.

It is quiet work, but it isn’t lonely. Nothing in the warren is ever really alone; there isn’t room for it. The dried fern-fronds are packed close, and when the pairs come crawling back through the low entrance—shedding sheets of freezing rain from their pelts, their tallow-soaked bog-cotton still wedged tight in their ears—the whole trench fills at once with the smell of wet wool, crushed sedge, and cold river-mud.

That is when the real talking starts. Not with mouths—even inside, with the stone muffling the river, the housing hums too steadily for easy speech, and half of us have ears ruined by too many pressure-snaps—but with hands. A partner sits down beside me, pulls the wads from his ears, and presses two cold, wet fingers hard against my collarbone: *two*. Then a flat-palmed tap on the ribs, sliding downward: *bank slumped, lost a scoop*. I answer with a slow squeeze on his forearm, then point with my chin toward the drying rack where his spare shin-wrappings are already warm. 

Sometimes, when the housing’s hum levels out into a long, steady drone—meaning the wind has settled into a single direction and the pylons aren’t spiking—I would take my own ash-pole and go out as far as the steaming circle’s edge, or down to the nearest alder thicket to check the crustacean traps wedged between the roots. I wouldn’t go alone; nobody goes past the scree without a sinew-tether at the waist and a second pair of eyes watching the overhangs for the flat, slate-colored heads of the mire-beasts. 

Out there, leaning into the sleet with my hood pulled low and the grease beading water off my shoulders, I would feel the valley for what it is: not an enemy, and not a home in any soft sense, just a vast, loud machine of rock and water and buried metal that we have learned to wedge ourselves inside. You watch the storm-birds fighting the updrafts along the cliffs. You feel the three-second climb of the pylon’s static in your jawbone, drop flat into the mud with your palms over your ears and your mouth open, take the *whump* across your back like a shovel-blow, and then scramble up with everyone else to grab what the shockwave threw onto the bank before the river takes it back.

Then back up the scree, back into the trench, back to the warm gray wall that never cools.

If I had to name what I would be in this world, I would be a keeper of the dry margin—someone whose hands are permanently stained with slug-tallow and orange fungus, who knows the exact temperature of the composite wall by touch, and who keeps the fireless hearth ready for the people who go down into the roaring dark. Not a explorer of the valley, and not a conqueror of it. Just one more piece of stubborn life wedged into the lee of something indifferent, using its heat to dry a pair of boots before the next squall hits.
