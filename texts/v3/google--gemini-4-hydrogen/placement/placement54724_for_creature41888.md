I would not be native to the chamber. Nothing human could belong to a place that spends water by the single drop.

I would be a line-walker from the levels above—someone hired to descend the iron ladders once every few weeks with a canvas satchel, a grease gun, and a tin of spare fuses, just to make sure the deep sinks are still idling. On the surface, or even a hundred meters higher in the ventilated shafts, this room is probably nothing more than a penciled box on an aging schematic: *Sub-station 4, Geothermal Condenser Array, Low-Current Maintenance.* Nobody upstairs talks about the dark islands of leaves, or the fact that the rock down here is still feverish from whatever split it open. They only care that the solenoids keep clicking.

I would find myself by the cast-iron junction boxes near the threshold, where the thick, braided copper cables enter from the shaft and spread out across the bare stone.

Compared to the chamber, I am an absurdly loud, clumsy thing. My boots scrape and clatter on the angular basalt. My canvas coat catches on the fresh, razor-sharp edges of the rock. Even when I stand dead still, my lungs work like bellows, pulling in the parched, ozone-heavy air and throwing moisture back out into the dark with every exhale. I carry my own climate with me—a noisy, sweating column of warmth, smelling of wool, boot leather, and the tepid water sloshing in my canteen.

Because of that, I’ve learned to move the way the room demands: slowly, with my mouth shut, breathing through my nose to keep what water I have. I don’t bring a bright lantern down here. A halogen beam only cooks the air around your face and ruins your eyes for the dim amber indicator bulbs. I carry a small, hooded brass torch, and I keep it shuttered until I need to read a pressure gauge or check the oil level behind a soot-rimmed sight glass.

My work is simple and mostly silent. I wipe the mineral dust off the glass with a greasy rag so I can see the faint cherry-red glow of the heating elements. I press the back of my knuckles—never my bare palm—against the iron transformer housings to judge if they’re running too hot. I tighten a packing nut on a brass valve where the condensation has begun to weep too fast, starving the basin below.

I know the dark islands, though I don’t have a name for them. Up in the main shafts, the older hands might call them *pipe-rot* or *copper-weed*, if they mention them at all, and tell you to scrape them off the conduits so they don’t foul the fittings. I leave them alone. Partly it’s because the broad, bruised-violet leaves are slick as wet leather, and slipping on a sharp shelf of basalt down here is a good way to break an ankle a mile below the daylight. Mostly, though, it’s because anything that can make a living out of hot stone and copper runoff has earned its corner of the floor. I step wide around the drip-lines, careful not to snag the pale, fibrous roots with my boot heels.

If I’m lucky, my circuit takes fifteen minutes, and I’m back up the ladder before the sweat dries white on my collar.

It’s only when a relay hangs—when a solenoid fails to click on the twenty-minute mark—that I truly find my place in the room. Then I have to wait. You can’t force an old iron casing open while the current might still be cycling; you have to sit out at least two full intervals to be sure it’s dead.

So I unhook my canteen, take one small sip, and sit down on an overturned porcelain insulator crate near the wall, shuttering my torch. I let my knees cool. I let my breathing drop out of my chest and settle into something flatter, quieter.

For the first twenty minutes, there is only the heat radiating through the soles of my boots, the faint metallic *tick* of cooling iron, and the irregular *tock* of water hitting a waxy leaf across the room. By forty minutes, the pulse in my ears has finally gone quiet enough that I stop feeling like an intruder and start feeling like another piece of furniture bolted to the stone.

That is when I sometimes notice the little discrepancies I usually miss. A shallow basalt basin under a dripping valve that was choked with dead, stewing stems last month is clear now, the path open for the water. A mat of struggling bone-white roots has been banked with damp mineral paste, as if by a heavy hand.

And once—sitting in the dark past the second cycle, waiting for a stuck solenoid to either seat or fail—I felt the low-frequency hum rise through the floor, vibrating in my teeth for sixty long seconds. In the dim cherry-red glow of the transformer housing, I saw a low ridge of matte-black stone just outside the violet foliage shift an inch closer to the sweating copper tower.

I didn’t shine my torch on it. I didn’t stand up.

I just sat on my crate with my hands resting on my knees, keeping my noisy, wet breath as small as I could manage, and waited for the hum to die back into silence. Whatever it was, it belonged to the twenty-minute rhythm in a way I never would. When the solenoid finally clicked and the relay cleared, I gathered my satchel, stood up stiffly in the baking dark, and turned back toward the ladder—leaving the chamber to its stone, its iron, and the slow, patient things that know how to live without the sun.
