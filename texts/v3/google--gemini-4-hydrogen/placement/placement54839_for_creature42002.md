At first, arriving here, the instinct is to look for a way to move—to kick lightly against the cold, clear water and drift above the machinery like a diver with a lamp, reading the faces of the pressure gauges, tracing the black roots of the cables across the silt.

But the basin refuses that kind of attention. A single fin-stroke would lift a cloud of pale mineral dust that might take days to settle back into the exact level it held before. Even the urge to look around feels clumsy here, where the blue-green water flattens every distance and nothing happens for the eye to catch. There is no weather to watch, no fish to follow, no current to lean into. To stay in this place without breaking it, you have to give up the idea of passing through.

So I let the habit of swimming fall away, and with it, the need for lungs or a lamp. What remains of attention sinks—not to the flat center of the floor, where the cast-iron pump housings and the finned transformers hold their heavy, steady court, but toward the northern edge of the basin, where the stepped shelves of limestone climb toward the unseen shore.

It is there, along the underside of one of the heavy-gauge conduits where it bridges a shallow step in the rock, that I find my shape.

I am not as thick or as settled as the ones tucked into the transformer seams below. Down there, pressed against the bare iron and the sacrificial zinc plates, the sixty-hertz hum is rich and physical, and the galvanic current leaks freely through the mineral water. Up here on the rock lip—perhaps ten feet below the surface instead of fifteen—the cable is wrapped in rubber and steel armor. The hum reaches me muted, thinned by insulation, more like a remembered pulse than a heartbeat. There are no zinc anodes within reach, only the faintest induced charge humming along the outer braid of the conduit and the trace minerals dissolved in the cold lake.

Because the current is sparser here, I grow even slower than the others. My outer architecture is only a few narrow shelves of pale, precipitated lime, thin as mica, tucked so tightly into the shadow between the cable and the limestone that from above I look like nothing more than a rim of frost on the rock. Beneath the plates, my body is a slender, translucent ribbon of blue-gray tissue, anchored by fine, copper-dark filaments that feel their way along the stone fracture rather than the steel skids. Beside me, a single tuft of dark aquatic moss clings to the rougher grain of the conduit’s clamp. Below me, in the silt-filled crack of the lower shelf, a pale stalk of stonewort stands completely still.

I have no eyes for the faint brightness overhead, no mouth to open to the water. Yet I am closer to the surface than the rest of the basin, and that small difference defines what I am.

The ones in the deep seams live in an unbroken chord. Up here on the lip, the chord is still the center of everything—steady, unvarying, carrying the distant load of the shore station through the rock—but it is not quite alone. When a storm passes over the lake ten feet above, I do not see the rain or hear the wind, yet the weight of the water pressing against my lime plates shifts by a fraction of a gram, a slow, irregular sigh laid over the sixty-hertz hum. At midday, the cold against my back thins by a tenth of a degree, just enough for my internal fluids to turn a hair’s breadth faster before the long evening chill returns.

Sometimes I wonder—if a slow conductive net of trace metals can be said to wonder—about the shore station where the conduit goes. I can feel it only as a constancy: the unbroken willingness of the line to carry its load, year after year, without surging or falling silent. My brown filaments reach fractionally upward along the underside of the cable, millimeter by decade, toward the dry air I cannot survive, while my older plates stay anchored to the limestone, listening downward to the deeper hum of the transformers.

I am neither the machine nor the lake, and not quite one of the deep-basin dwellers either. I am the quiet seam at the threshold—holding the place where the circuit leaves the water, taking my measure from the smallest shifts in pressure, and asking nothing of the stillness except that it hold.
