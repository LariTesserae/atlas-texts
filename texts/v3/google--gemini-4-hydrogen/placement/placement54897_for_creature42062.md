If I were to wake into this valley, I think I would first notice my own stillness—and the sudden, startling lightness of a body that no longer has to carry water. 

At first, I might imagine myself out on the open bark-plain with the Plain-Wardens, walking those pale, sound-swallowing rugs between the districts. There is a pull to that emptiness, the crispness of a world where you can see for miles under the selenite ceiling without a single drop of mist to blur the edges of a distant tower. Or I might picture myself high in the skeletal ribs of a mill, a Pruner with ceramic shears, coaxing stubborn chalk-cane away from the porcelain insulators.

But the longer I stand in the brisk, cedar-scented air, the more I realize I am not built for the long marches of the plains, nor for the high-wire work of the canopies. I would gravitate toward a place where the city’s geometry meets its pulse: a **junction-alcove** halfway along one of the elevated causeways, built right into the iron buttress where three district bracing-cables converge and anchor into the living rock.

I wouldn’t be one of the ones who *makes* the current, or harvests the silk, or cuts the cane. I would be a **Listener**—or, as the Tenders might call it in their dry, economical finger-signs, a **Tension-Keeper**.

In a valley where the ground itself is acoustic foam and a shout dies three paces from your mouth, distance is measured by taut steel. The Tenders strike the bracing-cables with weighted brass mallets to send coded *thrums* from mill to mill. Most people only feel those messages as a brief, urgent vibration through the soles of their boots as they walk past. Someone has to sit where the lines cross, where the harmonics of a dozen different districts overlap and tangle, and sort the signals out.

That is where I would be.

My alcove is small, woven from trained chalk-cane and lined with dry, pressed lichen-felt to keep out the draft of the pneumatic collectors. I sit on a low stool of petrified coral-wood, my back straight, wearing a stiff, silver-grey coat stitched with copper thread. Like every Tender, my skin is matte and cool, rubbed with amber resin at the start of the shift so it smells faintly of dry cedar. A bronze grounding-strap trails from my left heel to the iron floor-plate, bleeding away any stray charge before it can gather in my chest.

In front of me, the great steel cables pass through oiled porcelain collars before plunging into the fractured limestone. I don't use my ears to do my work; the air here carries almost nothing but the *shush* of papery fronds and the occasional *tick* of cooling metal. Instead, I rest my bare, resin-slicked palms—or sometimes the soles of my grounding-boots—against the wooden sounding-blocks bolted directly to the cables.

Through the wood, the valley speaks.

I learn to tell the difference between a deliberate mallet-stroke from the eastern resin-refineries and the accidental, heavy *thud* of a Grit-Back lumbering into a guy-wire out on the plain. I can feel when a Silk-Reaper’s Leyden-wand is drawing too much static from a high strut, or when the living cane around a distant tower has thickened enough to dampen the line’s ring—a dull, wooden flatting of the note that means it is time to tap out a summons for the Pruners.

Even when no one is sending a message, the cables are never truly silent. They carry the sub-audible hum of the geothermal shafts, the glacial, rhythmic groan of the wax-lubricated flywheels, and the faint, erratic *snap* of a Snap-Fox discharging its whiskers somewhere up in the root-bridges. Sitting there in the even, shadow-sharp daylight that never dims into night, I become a kind of living switchboard for a world that communicates by touch and vibration.

I don't think I would be entirely solitary, though the work is quiet. Now and then, a Plain-Warden stops at the edge of my alcove to trade a sealed glass flask of warm, oily sap-broth for news of which districts are running hot, or to warn me that a dead thicket of coral-wood has fallen across the southern line. We don't waste breath; we use the brisk, precise finger-signs, our dry sleeves rustling like leaves, before they step back onto the bark-plain and vanish into the pale distance.

And sometimes, in the long, motionless lulls between shifts—when the flywheels are locked and the chalk-moths drift in slow, silent clouds around the arc-lamps above—I climb out onto the iron lip of the causeway just to look up. Not at the mills, and not at the plains, but at the jagged selenite ceiling miles overhead, where the Plate-Gliders cling like frost to the dry fissures.

If I am anything in this world, I am that quiet point of contact: not the current, and not the root, but the hand resting lightly on the wire between them, learning to read the shape of a rainless world by the way it hums.
