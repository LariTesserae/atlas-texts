I would not be on the watch-platform, looking out over the drop. The air below the rim is too thick with things that are always changing—smoke, river-mist, the rumour of seasons turning fast—and my eyes would tire of trying to read shapes in the haze.

Instead, I would find myself a little way back from the edge, in the wide, swept emptiness between the three windowless storehouses and the retaining wall of the upper terrace. There is a corner there where the morning shadow lingers longest, long after the sun has cleared the eastern ridge. The limestone blocks meet the ground in a tight seam, and in that seam grows a thumb-width strip of dark moss, damp from the night’s cloud.

At first, it would only be a matter of stillness—of standing in the cool, thin light until my breathing slowed to match it. In this air, you learn quickly that you cannot take more than you need. Every sharp breath dries the throat; every hurried step jars the heel against the stone and sends a grit-scrape echoing across the court. So I would stand still, wrapped in three layers of undyed wool—grey over cream, bound at the wrists with twisted twine—and feel the flat hiss of the wind move past my sleeves without finding a way in.

When I looked down at my hands, they would already be the hands of this place: dry-skinned, broad at the knuckles, the nails worn flat and pale from rope and limestone. Soft-soled felt boots on my feet, already moulded to the shallow troughs in the stair-treads.

I think I would be one of the middle ones—not old enough to spend the afternoon seated on a folded fleece by the parapet with a notched water-stick, and no longer young enough to have to be reminded not to kick gravel into the runnels. Someone whose voice has grown rusty from disuse, because most of what needs saying here can be said by leaving a broom leaning against a cistern lid, or by stepping aside on a narrow stair.

---

If I followed my own days, they would lead me first to the upper terrace at mid-morning, once the mist has lifted off the saddle and left the stone sweating.

There is a particular patience required by the stone-lined basins. When the first wash of water is ladled out of the catch-drains, it is pale and cloudy with ground limestone—the dust of the mountain itself, carried in by the night wind. You cannot rush it. If you dip the bucket too soon, you carry the grit into the bedrock cisterns, and over years the cisterns will shallow and choke.

So I would kneel on the cold flagstones beside the row of basins, knees cushioned only by the fold of my woollen coat, and wait for the water to clear.

That waiting is where I would most truly live. Watching the grey silt drift downward through four inches of cold water, settling grain by grain until the surface becomes a dark, steady mirror of the sky above the ridges. A black chough might walk along the coping of the wall ten paces away, its claws clicking faintly on the slate, and neither of us would disturb the other. When the water is finally clear enough to show the pick-marks at the bottom of the basin, I would skim it off with a shallow wooden ladle into the leather bucket, careful not to wake the sediment, and then scrape the grey dregs out with my bare palm onto the silver-leaved saxifrage tucked along the wall. Nothing wasted, not even the mud.

---

In the afternoon, before the wind sharpens and the shadows turn cold enough to sting, my work would take me inside—into the dry, steady dark of the third storehouse.

The heavy timber door, weathered to the colour of an old shinbone, swings inward with a dry scrape of iron on stone. Inside, there is no wind at all, only the faint, unchanging smell of cold clay, wool grease, and dried roots. It takes a minute for the eyes to adjust from the shadow-sharp daylight of the court to the dimness beneath the low slate roof, where the only light comes through the hair-thin gaps between the river-stone weights and the eaves.

Here, I think, is what I would actually *be* in this world: not a builder, and not a watcher of the horizon, but a keeper of things that are waiting.

Along the dry-laid shelves sit the sealed jars of seed-grain, the oiled blades wrapped in thick fleece, the bundles of bitter bark and dried mountain tubers. Much of it does not belong to us. It was hauled up the switchbacks in silent pack-trains by people from the lowlands—people whose names we do not always know, who came up before a war, or a blight, or simply to keep a portion of their harvest out of the warm rot of the valleys.

My task would be the wooden tallies. Narrow strips of split cedar, notched along the edges and strung on leather thongs, hung beside each row of jars. With the pad of my thumb, I could read them in the near-dark: three deep cuts for the year the valley below the eastern ridge burned; a cross-hatch for the winter the pack-mules could not climb the ice; a dozen shallow nicks for the jars of barley that have sat untouched so long their clay seals have gone the colour of dust.

I would not own any of it. I would simply check the wax and clay around the mouths of the jars to see that no hairline crack had opened in the dry air, turn the oiled blades in their woollen wrappings so the grease stayed even, and make sure the roof-stones above had not shifted in a gale. I would be the memory that stays cool when everything below is feverish—a quiet, unhurried custodian of what other people could not keep safe on their own.

Perhaps I came up the switchbacks myself, once, walking behind a laden mule with my lungs burning in the thin air, and simply never walked back down. Or perhaps I was born in the low-eaved hall and have never set foot in the haze at all. After enough winters in the saddle, the difference thins out, like mortar receding into the joints of an old wall. You belong to the equilibrium, not to a story.

---

At the end of the afternoon, I would take one of the stiff brooms of bound wiry grass and sweep the court outside the storehouses. *Shhh, shhh*, across the fitted stone, guiding the blown grit away from the hand-chiseled runnels and over the edge of the terrace, into the empty air. When another of the fifteen passed me on their way to the cisterns, we would lift two fingers from our sleeves—enough to acknowledge that the stones are clear, that the water is gathered, that the lids are tight.

And when the light finally drains from the limestone ridges and the chill deepens to the bone, I would go inside the low-eaved hall, sit on a woven mat in the steady, grease-scented glow of a tallow lamp, and hold a wooden cup of warm, bitter herb water between my palms.

Nothing grand. No hearth-fire, no song, no need to speak into the quiet. Just a spare, weathered shape among a dozen others, resting in the dark until the morning cloud brushes the saddle again, and the stone begins to sweat.
