If I am honest about where I would end up in a place like this, I wouldn’t be up in the unseen boats, lowering bone hooks through an ice-hole with stiff fingers. I wouldn’t even be the octopus, clever enough to wedge a flake of flint across its door and make a fortress out of a crack.

I would be down in the silt, trying to figure out how to stay put.

At first, I think I would be small—smaller than I expect, and far too soft. That’s how it feels when you first drop into a place with no edges to hold onto: everything in you is too light, too easily lifted by the first cold draft spilling off the shelf. If you try to look around, the water just fills your eyes with grey flour and takes your balance. So you stop looking. You let the dark close over those useless pits in your head until they are nothing more than a dull gauge for whether the ceiling miles above is bruised grey or pitch-black.

Instead, you learn to read with your jaw.

That’s where I find myself first: pressed flat into the cold, tight seam *underneath* the stones, where the valley doesn’t bother to look for you. Up above, the boulders are knocking together in a squall, rolling an inch and locking back into place, and the water is a roaring, muffled wall of mud. Down here, in the coin-thin labyrinth between the bedrock and the cobbles, it is only cold and still and tastes of old shell-dust.

I think I would spend a long time there—longer than feels reasonable—just threading through the dark, scraping bacterial film off the undersides of stones, listening to the ceiling shift. There’s a strange comfort in being too small for the current to care about. You don’t have to brace against anything. You just fit into the gaps that the big things leave behind.

But you can’t stay soft forever. Even in the seams, you feel the hunger for weight.

It starts with a grain of gravel. Then another. Not because you’re starving, though you’re always grazing, always gulping the thin grey broth and straining it out through the bristles in your throat—but because something in your gut knows that one day the crack you’re hiding in is going to be too narrow for your shoulders. You’re going to have to push up between two boulders and meet the water head-on. And if you go up there hollow, the first eddy will peel you off the floor like a strip of dead kelp and tumble you until there’s nothing left of you.

So you swallow the floor.

You find the smooth ones—egg-rounded basalt, heavy little knots of iron—and you take them down into the dark of your own belly. They sit there, cold and reassuring, clicking faintly against one another when you turn. Season by season, the salt in the water settles into your hide, stiffening the skin across your back until it stops feeling like skin and starts feeling like something the valley made on purpose.

I know the exact moment I’d finally come up.

It wouldn’t be in the middle of a storm. It would be in one of those long, flat intervals when the silt settles just enough to turn the dark into slate-grey, and the only sound is that dry *shhh* of grit sliding down a boulder’s flank. I’d wedge my blunt snout into the gap between two stones, brace my stubby, thick-clawed forelimbs against the bedrock below, and heave.

The first thing that hits you on the open floor isn’t the cold—you’ve been cold your whole life. It’s the *push*. Even on a quiet day, the water out here has a shoulder to it. It leans against your plates, testing every seam, looking for a loose edge to get under.

You learn very quickly how to answer it: you don’t fight it, and you don’t run from it. You just jam a wedge-shaped claw into the lock between two cobbles, pull your pale belly flush against the curve of a stone to seal out the grit, and wait.

I don’t think I would be one of the great, scarred patriarchs with whole gardens of dark-red seaweed rooted in their backs. I’d be somewhere in the middle—heavy enough to hold my trough when a squall hits, still small enough to remember what the underside of the stones felt like. My plates would be dull iron-black, already scratched pale along the ridges from the time I misjudged a rolling boulder in the murk and took it across the shoulder.

And inside me—among the smooth basalt cobbles grinding away at blind clams and sea-slaters—I think I’d have one odd thing. A notched river-stone, maybe, or a bit of snapped bone hook that fell out of the ceiling years ago. Something from that other world I have no name for, turning slowly in the dark of my gut, being polished smoother than the current ever managed. I wouldn’t know it was a tool. To me, it’s just good ballast. It keeps me down.

Mostly, though, I think I would be someone who listens.

It’s easy to think of a place like this as lonely, because you can’t see three feet in front of your face and any scent you follow gets shredded to nothing by the next eddy. When a squall comes down from the shelf, you tuck your throat into your collar, wedge into the deepest dish you can find, and let the black silt bury you halfway. You become a stone among stones. You cough out the mud that packs your gills, and you wait.

But then the storm passes. The wall of grit thins out. And across the wide, crowded dark of the trough, something else answers.

*Tock.*

A pause.

*Tock-tock.*

It isn’t a boulder slipping in the gravel. A slipping boulder doesn’t wait to be heard. That’s someone else, half a trench away, braced in their own dish, lifting a cobble in the crook of a forelimb and striking the bedrock. Saying: *Here. The seam is still here. I am still heavy enough to stay.*

So I’d shift my weight. I’d hook my claws a little deeper into the lock, feel the cold stone under my chest, and strike back.

*Tock.*

Just to send something straight through the murk. Just to prove that even down here, where everything sharp is ground to dust and nothing built lasts, there is still a rhythm that belongs to us, and not to the current.
