I would not be standing on the plain. Anything that stands here casts a shadow that doesn’t belong, and anything that walks with weight would dull the crisp, newly-cut corners of the terracotta.

I would find myself where the first course of brick rests on the second, in the horizontal dark of a mortar joint, three fingers’ depth below the noon glare.

At midday, that is the only place to be. Above me, the pale ochre tiles are kiln-hot, expanding by fractions of a millimeter with a dry, patient *tick* that travels straight through the clay like a pulse. Down here, the world is the temperature of a cellar. It smells of wet lime, crushed shell, and the faint, green starch of a purslane root wedged two inches to my left. I am pressed flat between two planes of fired earth, following the straight, unhurried geometry of the trowel-marks.

I think I would be part of the slow, pale felt that lives in the seams—neither root nor fungus, the color of cured lime, stretched thin enough to be mistaken for dried mortar if a crack ever widens to the sun.

It is a strange, tactile way to exist. I would have no eyes to be blinded by the white sky, and no legs to blister on the clay. Instead, I would know the plain by pressure and chemistry. I would know the vast, sunken basin as a map of hairline fractures and damp pockets, feeling the weight of a knee-high retaining wall a few yards away as a deeper stillness in the bed beneath me.

For most of the day, my work is simply holding on and breathing. As the sun bakes the surface and the bricks swell tight against one another, I exhale a thin, mineral-warm dampness upward into the cracks, keeping the saltbush root beside me from turning to tinder. In return, the root leaks a tiny, slow bead of sugar into my threads. We don’t hurry. There is nothing to hurry toward; the basin has only just been laid, and the mortar is still curing. We have seasons—decades, perhaps—to soften the crushed shell beneath us into something like soil.

Yet I don’t think I would stay wholly buried. When the heat-haze collapses after sunset and the bricks finally begin to cool, shrinking back from one another with soft, settling sighs, the seams open just enough.

If I am the part of this world that waits, I am also the part that slips out to taste the night.

In the hours before dawn, I would draw the lingering condensation up from the sub-layers, swelling until I am translucent as wet vellum, veined with faint rust from the iron in the clay. I would ease out of the sharp crack between the tiles—no thicker than a ribbon, boneless, conforming to every thumb-groove left by whatever hand laid this floor.

Out here, the plain is enormous and cool. There is no sound except the papery rasp of a solitary reed and the tiny, audible bead of dew forming along the shaded northern lip of the nearest retaining wall. I would follow the straight line of the mortar seam, flowing slowly toward that wall, climbing its vertical face by pressing flat against the unglazed terracotta. There is no slime in my wake, only a clean, faintly damp track where I have absorbed the fine, windblown silt from the clay’s pores. At the top, in the shadow of the lip, I would drink the dew straight through my skin—pure, unsalted water before the sun can claim it.

And then, before the sky turns white and the glare returns to bake me to paper, I would already be sliding back down into the dark seam, carrying the coolness of the night down to the roots and the crushed shell.

Sometimes, though, I imagine myself farther out, in the center of a square catch-basin where the brickwork drops a forearm’s depth below the plain. In the dry hours, I would be one of the warped, salt-crusted discs lying loose on the floor, indistinguishable from a broken tile that had curled in the heat. I would know nothing at all for days—just the dry weight of the air, the white glare, the slow accumulation of feathery salt along my rim.

Until the afternoon thermals build their flat-bottomed clouds miles overhead, and the first warm drops hiss against the clay.

That is the moment I would wake: feeling the deep rust-red darken around me, softening, uncurling into a broad, leathery cup as the rain fills the basin. I would unspool my pale fringes into the tepid puddle, filtering the fine dust and dissolved lime washed off a hundred yards of brickwork, holding onto a single spoonful of clean water as the squall passes and the plain turns back to steam. Then I would fold inward, seal the dampness inside my own skin, and bake back down into the color and hardness of clay.

Whether I am the thread in the dark, the ribbon on the night wall, or the cup in the basin, I would be the thing that keeps the water just a little longer than the brick can. I would be the quiet, patient counterweight to the sun—turning a newly fired, empty geometry into a place that remembers the rain.
