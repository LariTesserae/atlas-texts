I would not be on the high roofs when the clouds tear open. That silver glare, the sudden flashing of a thousand wet slates—it is too wide, too exposed, like standing on the back of a sleeping animal that might notice you.

I would be three stories down, where the lanes narrow to the width of a ribcage and the overhanging eaves meet so closely they almost roof the sky out. Even at midday, the light down here is the color of wet ash. The wind never reaches this far; it is broken above by the stepped geometry of the upper walls, shredded into nothing before it can touch the moss.

At first, I think I am only listening.

That is how you know where you are in a place like this—not by looking, because the grey slate looks the same in every direction, and the mist makes distances lie. You know where you are by the *tock* of the water. Each cistern has its own pitch, depending on how full it is and how deeply it was cut into the bedrock. A shallow well rings sharp and quick; a deep one, half-emptied after a dry spell between cloud-banks, answers with a low, hollow thump that you feel in the soles of your feet before you hear it in your ears.

If I am here, I have been here long enough to know the voices of the wells by heart.

My hands are cold, though not painfully so—the kind of cold that has settled into the knuckles and become a permanent temperature, like the stone itself. They smell faintly of rendered fat from my cloak, of sulfur lichen, and of the dark, mineral grit that washes down from the roof-runnels into the silt-traps. Under my fingernails there is always a fine grey line of slate dust.

I am one of the keepers, though not one of the ones who walk the highest terraces in the afternoon mist. My knees have spent too many winters on slick stone for the steep, cantilevered roofs. Instead, my beat is the lower honeycomb—the covered passages, the settling basins, the dark rooms tucked directly against the bedrock where the water finally comes to rest.

It is a good place to be. Up above, you are always watching the sky, waiting for the cloud bank to roll in or the frost to glaze the lintels. Down here, there is only the steady, cool equilibrium of the rock. The temperature scarcely changes between noon and midnight.

You learn to move slowly in the lower tiers. Not just because the carved stairs are worn into slick, algae-coated troughs, but because haste makes noise, and noise here feels like a bruise. My feet are wrapped in braided sedge and oiled hide—it is late in the season, and the frosts are close—so my steps make only a soft, damp *shh* against the stone, hardly louder than the mist brushing the windward faces of the walls above.

In the crook of my arm, I carry a wooden pry, its flat end worn smooth and dark as horn by hands that held it long before mine. At my hip hangs a heavy pouch of slate shards.

Most of my work is small. Today, I am checking the settling basin at the foot of the eastern steps, where three alleys converge under a low stone bridge. When I kneel beside the square cut in the bedrock, the water is black glass. A single drop falls from the runnel lip above—*tock*—and a ring of silver light, stolen from the narrow slot of sky far overhead, widens until it touches the moss-packed edges and vanishes.

There is a pale limestone pebble resting on the dark slate sill beside the basin.

I don't know who left it. Maybe the lean, silent one who walks the upper western roofs; maybe the woman with the scarred brow who tends the fungi-troughs two lanes over. It doesn't matter who. The stone says: *the water below needs skimming.*

So I set my wooden pry aside, push back the stiff, heavy cuff of my oiled cloak, and reach down to lift the heavy slate cover of the cistern-mouth.

The air that rises from the well is different from the air in the alley. It is older, heavier, smelling of wet limestone and nothing living—or nothing that breathes air. I climb down the notched footholds cut into the shaft, feeling for each one with my sedge-wrapped heels, until I am standing on the narrow ledge just above the black water.

Here, the city disappears. Above me are three stories of dry-laid stone, hundreds of tons of slate held together by nothing but weight and friction and the quiet stubbornness of chinking shards. Around me, in the hollow cores of the walls, I can sometimes hear the faintest rustle—one of the long-bodied wall-mammals slipping through a drainage gap, hunting snails by touch in the dark.

I take a flat, woven basket from my belt and skim the surface of the cistern, clearing the fine skin of drowned midges and stray sedge-bits that have washed down from the roofs. If it is left too long, it mats; if it mats, it fouls the water; if the water stagnates, the whole quiet balance of the lower rooms sours.

As I work, something shifts in the black glass below my basket.

It is one of the old ones—the pale amphibians that never leave the wells. This one is thick as my wrist, ghostly white in the dimness, its feathery pink gills flaring slowly behind its flat head as it hangs suspended near the bedrock wall. It does not dart away from the basket. It has probably lived in this single cold room for thirty years, listening to the same *tock* of the same runnel, never once seeing the flat glare of the sun or the mist rolling across the plateau.

I hold the basket still until it drifts back into the deeper shadow. There is no need to disturb it. We are doing the same thing, in our own ways—staying out of the wind, keeping to the seams, letting the water pass through us without making a sound.

When I climb back up into the alley and slide the slate cover into place, I pick up the pale limestone pebble from the sill and tuck it into my pouch. The message is answered. In its place, I wedge a small, knotted tuft of wiry sedge into a dry joint near the bridge-arch—not because anything is wrong yet, only because I felt a hairline give beneath my heel on the third step down, a fraction of an inch of settling that will need a fresh shard before the hard freezes come.

Someone else will see it tomorrow, or the day after, and tap it tight with the heel of their palm. *Thuck.*

I don't climb to the roofs for the evening sun. Instead, when the cloud bank finally tears open and a single, thin blade of silver light slices all the way down into the bottom of the alley, I sit on a dry doorstep with my back against the bedrock wall. The stone behind my shoulders is cool and steady. Down the lane, a slate-colored bird drops silently onto the edge of a settling trough, pauses, and becomes still as a carved finial.

I pull my oiled collar up to my chin, rest my hands on the smooth wood of my pry, and listen to the city breathe its water back into the sky.
