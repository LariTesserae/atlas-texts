I would not arrive here with boots. Boots would be an injury—too loud, too sharp, tearing the silvery skin of the bryophytes with every heavy *suck* of mud and water, announcing me half a mile away as a rip in the mat.

If I am here, I have always been here, or I have been here long enough for the plateau to finish softening me.

I would find myself in one of the shallow basins where the Overweave hangs a little higher and the air is thick with the sweet, damp rot of a fallen drifter. Not at the center of anything—there is no center where the horizon closes at a hundred paces in every direction—just a slight dip in the endless mattress where the water pools mirror-still, dark as steeped tea, and the moss has worn thin enough to show a single, palm-sized nub of ancient granite.

I would be a weaver, though perhaps one of the slower ones—the kind who lingers a half-day behind the kin-group, long after the others have pressed their wrists to the bark and drifted back into the bruised olive haze.

It takes a long time to learn how to have no voice. At first, the silence feels like a held breath, waiting for a twig to snap or a wind to rise. But there is no wind, and nothing here is dry enough to snap. Slowly, the need for speech dissolves into the dampness, and what is left is attention. My skin—bare, damp, the color of wet amber lignite, patched across the shoulders with pale, velvety lichen—does the listening now. The sparse filaments along my forearms stir when a broad-winged drifter glides overhead, riding the faint rising warmth of the composting floor. Through the grooved soles of my splayed feet, I feel the plateau’s slow, continuous pulse: the hour-long *groan* of a water-swollen siphon-reed shifting its weight somewhere beyond the mist; the rhythmic, heavy *thump-crawl* of a moss-backed grazer moving past at its ancient pace.

This is where I am most myself: kneeling at the edge of the shallow pool, one four-fingered palm resting against the cold, soap-soft nub of granite, the other against the cartilage-soft column of a nearby reed.

Inside the reed, I can feel the slow hydraulic tide—the column drinking from the weeping canopy above and pumping water downward into the thirty-meter sponge of roots and peat beneath us. It is alive, yielding, full of small, rhythmic tremors. My cousins are three hundred paces off; if I squeezed the column in a slow, deliberate pattern, they might feel the pressure-wave through the connected root-channels, or catch the faint, musky signature I left on the bark an hour ago.

But under my other hand, the granite is utterly dead.

It has no pulse. It does not give way when I press it. It does not drink the *plink* of the canopy-drip, and it does not warm to the heat of my palm. To a creature born of peat and hollow tubes, stone is a small, impossible silence—a place where the world forgot to be alive. I do not have words for *before the plants*, or *carved*, or *mountain*. I only know that the stone refuses to answer when I touch it, and that this refusal holds me here longer than it holds the others.

When I finally lift my hand from the granite, I rub my wrist across its slick, green-coated crown. The stone will hold my musk for months, long after the wet bark of the reeds has swallowed my scent back into itself. A cousin passing this way in the spring of another year—or another decade—will flare the gill-folds along their cheekbones, taste my lingering trace in the still air above the pool, and know that I paused here, marveling at the silence.

Before I follow the kin-group into the mist, there is one thing left to do.

Nearby, the old drifter that settled onto the moss days ago has begun to sink into the bryophytes, its paper-thin membranes already graying with pale fungal threads. I crouch beside it, my lidless eyes adjusting to the dim green halos of the water-bladder epiphytes overhead, and gently strip away its long, thread-thin feelers. They are tough and dry—the closest thing to cordage the plateau ever yields.

I carry them to a fork between two young siphon-reeds. Using my flat, chisel-edged front teeth, I gently bruise the rubbery outer skin of the shoots—just enough so they will bend without snapping, just enough to coax them toward each other. With my blunt claws, I hook them together and bind the join with the drifter’s feelers, tying a loose, patient knot.

It is not a shelter for me. By the time these living shoots thicken and fuse into a dry-ish, teardrop-shaped cradle suspended above the dampness of the floor, fifteen or twenty years will have passed. My silvery outer skin will have sloughed off and regrown; the circuit will have brought me—or someone who smells faintly like me—back around the endless, flat curve of the world. Perhaps a cousin heavy with a single, slow-developing young will curl inside it for a few quiet days, safe from the wet, listening to the *pat* of heavy droplets on the Overweave.

I tap my knuckles twice against the moss—a small, hollow rhythm, like heavy rain—and wait until a pale, blind segmented thing noses up from the warm water-channels below. I eat it unhurriedly, then rinse my fingers in the pool, watching the clear water well and settle.

Somewhere beyond the hundred-pace rim of the haze, the grazers are scraping the soft rind from the reeds. A column shifts and groans. I rise, stooping slightly beneath the hanging curtains of aerial roots, and step back onto the living mat—leaving no footprint that the moss will not erase before the next drop falls.
