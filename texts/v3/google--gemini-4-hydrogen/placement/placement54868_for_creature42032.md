If I arrived here as I am—upright, soft-skinned, full of eyes and breath—the vault would unmake me in three cycles.

My eyes would be the first thing to go, stung raw by boiled mineral salts and hot dust before the first curtain of condensation ever fell. My throat would dry out trying to shout over a roar that doesn’t travel through air so much as it occupies bone. Standing tall on that cantilevered lip, I would be nothing but an obstruction in the updraft, too slow for the *whuff*, too loud against the stone, an easy shape for a hungry glider to clip in the dark.

To *find* myself here, I would have to let the room subtract everything it has no use for. 

So I let go of sight first. I let the lids draw down and seal into tight, dry seams against the grit. I drop my weight out of my spine and onto my palms, my soles, the flat of my chin—learning the three-point crawl along the sintered basalt until my center of gravity belongs to the wall rather than the air. My skin thickens and pales to tallow, taking on the matte toughness of something cured in warm smoke, oiled at the joints with the iridescent slick skimmed from the lower basins.

Now, when I ask where I am, the answer isn’t a vista. It’s a texture.

I am not down in the sheltered corners behind the buttresses, though I go there when the vents need clearing and the basins are thick with ribbon-feeders. And I am not high in the rafters where the condensation gathers in heavy, patient drops, though I climb there with a dried glider-bladder slung at my ribs when my mouth turns to salt.

I find myself on a middle tier, just inside the lip of a recessed gallery where one of the slender, load-bearing filaments anchors into the pitted ceramic-metal.

It is a threshold place. Behind me, the maintenance hatch to our service-crypt sits flush in its frame, its hinges kept quiet with basin-oil. Above and below, the stacked terraces step away into the bruised ember-violet bleed of a stressed conductor and the dirty-orange shimmer of a distant vent. In front of me, there is nothing at all—only the cathedral hollow, the rising heat, and the vast, indifferent shifting of mass beneath the floor.

If I am one of the dwellers, I am not the boldest hunter among us. I don’t have the patience of the ones who hang motionless by the mist-curtains for three cycles without twitching a toe, nor the quickness of the ones who string oiled wire across the buttresses just as the air begins to thicken and burn. 

I am the one who stays nearest the anchor-points. A reader of the wire.

It suits a creature who used to live in language. Here, where throats are useless, the tension-filaments are the closest thing to a voice the vault possesses. When I wrap a calloused palm around the humming strand, I can feel the whole geometry of the room singing through it—the deep, chest-rattling thrum climbing toward its teeth-aching whine, the subtle slackening when a heavy curtain of rain lets go high overhead, the tiny, dry shiver of lime crusting over a distant clamp.

Most of the time, my work is ordinary. During the *whuff* and the quiet minutes after, I crouch with the others at the intake louvers, working a chipped ceramic wedge into the pale gypsum seams until the warm draft sighs back into our tunnels. I bind strips of dried wing-membrane around my forearms when I have to crawl deep into a conduit near a hot manifold, and I keep a glider’s curved arm-bone tucked into my wrap for prying at the tighter joints.

But when the louvers are clear and the others have gone down to drag tendril-mats through the oily shallows, I crawl back out to the edge of the abyss and lay two fingers against the wire.

I am listening for the *ping*.

Half a mile across the empty dark, on a gallery I have never seen and could not cross to if I tried, another hand is resting on the same web. When a thumbnail plucks the taut metal, the vibration cuts clean over the bass roar—sharp, bright, unmistakable in the bones of my wrist. *Cool seam.* *Rain coming.* *Glider down.*

Sometimes, if I hold my chin flat against the sintered basalt and my palm against the filament at the same time, I can tell things the others miss. I can feel when a distant *ping* lands slightly flat, damped by a fresh buildup of mineral foam at its far anchor. I can feel the slow, centuries-long groan of a bulkhead settling a fraction of a millimeter deeper into the bedrock, or the faint, irregular flutter of a conductor somewhere three tiers down beginning to stress before its casing cracks and bleeds violet into the dark.

I have no name for this, because we have no names. If the others know me at all, they know me by the weight of my crawl on the conduit floor, the particular warmth of my collarbone when we tangle together to sleep, and the rhythm of my thumb against the string—steady, unhurried, always checking the tension before the climb begins.

And when the air tightens—when the smell of hot dust sharpens and the deep thrum starts to rise toward that breathless, high-frequency ache—I am usually the last one through the hatch.

I linger just long enough to feel the first heavy drops of tepid condensation hiss against the hot manifolds below, and to send one last, damped pulse out across the dark to whoever is holding the far end of the wire. *Inward. Safe.*

Then I pull my palm from the string, slip inside, and draw the oiled alloy shut behind me. In the vibration-damped dark of the service-crypt, the roar drops back to a low, comforting pulse. I find my place in the tangle of limbs, press my chin against a familiar shoulder, and close my sealed eyes against the stone, waiting for the *whine* to break into the *whuff* that will call me back out to the wire.
