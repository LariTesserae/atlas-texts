I think I would not be on the ridges at first, or not in any way you could point to. In a world where every millimeter of stone is buried under three feet of wet, living wool, you do not arrive by standing up and looking around. You arrive by pressing your weight down until the moss gives, until the cold water rises around your ankles and the hum of the ground reaches the back of your teeth.

If I am anywhere here, I am one of the Tenders—though not one of the young ones who tie stones to their waists and drop into the black sinkholes to scrape the bronze turbines clean. My lungs would not hold the four minutes of freezing dark anymore, and my fingers are too stiff to read the mineral scale on a spinning vane by touch alone.

Instead, I would be one of the slow ones who walk the long, curved terraces behind the main band, carrying a broad shoulder-bone spade and a staff of dark horn, listening for the faults before they happen.

The young splicers look for dead patches—the places where the warm emerald moss has browned and the herds shy away from stray voltage in the mire. By then, the copper taproot has already parted. What I listen for is earlier than that: the tightness. When the peat settles over decades, it drags the braided lines with it, pulling a buried cable taut across a knuckle of basalt. It doesn’t break all at once. It just begins to sing a fraction of a tone higher—a thin, anxious tremor you can only catch if you kneel in the cold wet, press your cheek flat against the dwarf willows, and let your own pulse slow down until it stops getting in the way.

That is mostly what I would be: a pair of damp knees, a calloused palm, and a jawbone tuned to the floor of the sky.

My coat would be heavy with grazer-tallow and the pale, stubborn liverworts that root in anything that moves slowly enough. My boots would be worn thin on purpose, their soles dark with mineral grease, so the plateau is never muffled from me. In the mist, where the horizon is only a pale curve and every ridge looks like the ridge you left an hour ago, you do not navigate by landmarks. You navigate by the pitch of the current underfoot—the deep, even drone of the main trunks, the fainter, fizzing purr where the electrolytic cells split the bog-water into rising pearls of gas, the dead, flat silence over a basalt shelf where no lines run at all.

I would know the other residents mostly as interruptions in that hum.

When a herd of the broad-footed grazers passes in the fog, I wouldn't see them until their moss-grown backs loomed out of the cloud-sheet like moving boulders. I would feel them first—the rhythmic, heavy *thump-thump* of their splayed hooves travelling through the waterlogged peat, a low language I don't speak but know how to answer by standing still and letting my weight sink. And when I dig down through the root-labyrinth with my bone spade to ease a strained cable off a shelf of rock, I would work gently around the bronze-backed beetles marching along the polymer sheathing, careful not to crush their hollow, copper-stiffened pupal skins, and careful not to stir the blind, gill-breathing newts hanging in the warm, salty pockets below.

Even the predators—the long, slate-gray hunters with their gel-pored snouts—would be part of the grammar of the day. You learn never to linger too long at the edge of a copper-rimmed pool when your heart is beating fast from a climb. A Tender who is winded throws off a bright, ragged spark in the wet turf, loud as a lantern in the dark. So you learn to breathe shallow in the thin air, to keep your muscles loose, to let your bioelectric noise sink back into the steady, flat voltage of the buried grid until the hunter sweeps its snout past your boots and reads nothing there but peat and copper.

At the end of a long stretch of ridges, I wouldn't look for a roof or a fire. I would follow the faint, metallic warmth rising through the mist toward one of the bright emerald basins where the transformers lie buried. The rest of the band would already be there—a tangle of oiled hides and dark, wet hair, half-buried in the steaming sphagnum. I would wedge my horn staff upright into the root-mat, peel back a heavy blanket of living willow and lichen, and curl in against someone’s warm shoulder, cheek down in the moss.

For a few minutes before sleep, I would just lie there and let the waste heat soak into my ribs, listening to the *plip* of cloud-water dropping from the sedges and the tireless, sub-audible thrum of the flooded shafts miles below. Not owning the engine, and not really separate from it either—just one more wet, patient thing keeping the current clear.
