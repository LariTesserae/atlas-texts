I would be found in one of the lower draws, where three travertine fins converge around a sunken pocket of basalt—a place the others pass through rather than claim, because the steam-seam there runs too hot for comfort and the puddle beneath the overhang tastes heavier of iron than salt.

I am one of the plateau’s own, though an unhurried one, shaped more for waiting than for crossing the wide scree-slopes. My back has the bleached, chalky crust of old rhyolite; my belly and the undersides of my long arms are dark slate, webbed with thick veins that flush warm when I press them against damp stone. Like the rest, I have no voice. My nostrils pinch shut between breaths, hoarding moisture against the thin air, and my teeth are narrow-spaced for straining grit from mineral water.

What keeps me in this particular draw is not the puddle, nor the cluster of swollen succulents wedged above it, though I know the exact depth of both. It is the junction.

Just above the overhang, bolted straight into the steaming rock, sits a squat geothermal cylinder of blackened iron and white ceramic. Beside it stands a windowless relay shed with a dusty solar-vent spinning lazily on its roof, and from the pylon behind them, three taut conduits split away—one climbing the eastern ridge toward the high condenser-towers, one dropping south across the blistered mounds, and one running straight into the glare of the western basin.

During the white-gold hours, I am nowhere to be seen. I fold my narrow ribs sideways into a deep, wet fissure beneath the cylinder’s concrete plinth, knees drawn to my chin, long fingers tucked under my jaw. Flat-bodied lizards pack into the crack beside me, their scales cool and dry against my shoulder. Above us, the heat shimmers off the travertine fins, and the metering gauge inside the shed clicks on in its slow, oblivious rhythm. I do not sleep so much as I let my breathing drop until a minute passes between one shallow draw of air and the next, listening through the stone to the steady, sub-audible hum of the cylinder’s core.

When the blue hour arrives and cold air spills over the saw-toothed rim, I uncoil before the first mist has even settled on the copper mesh.

My wide, leathery feet find the knife-edged ledges without a sound. First, I kneel at the overhang and drink, filtering the tepid, iron-dark water through my teeth, feeling the familiar sting of purged salt crusting beneath my eyes. A pale-feathered stalker stands three paces away in the steaming mud, motionless on its long legs; it watches me with one bright, unblinking eye and decides I am only another kind of stone. Before the pre-dawn chill deepens, I cross to the relay shed and press my dark belly against the flank of the transformer box, taking in its dry, steady warmth while the wind needles through the draw.

Then I climb the condenser-tower.

It is skeletal and tall, swaying almost imperceptibly in the thermal shift. I move up the struts with the quiet, four-limbed certainty of something born to bridge gaps, carrying a bundle of dried, waxed-needle scrub tucked into my belt. At the top, the fine copper mesh is stiff with yesterday’s salt-crusts and studded with the husks of copper-shelled beetles that crawled up for the mist and died in the sun. I brush them away in slow, downward strokes, clearing the weave so the morning dampness can catch and bead. I do not care about the buried cisterns or the unseen grid beyond the horizon—only that clean mesh weeps water down the outside of the insulated pipe, and that trickle feeds the lichen and the damp seam at the tower’s base.

By the time the sky pales toward gold, I am back at the pylon.

This is where I spend the best of my breath-hoarding hours: not foraging across the basin, though I will prise a succulent from a crack or tap a porous beetle-hive with a length of salvaged copper wire and plaster the breach shut with red mud before the heat rises. Mostly, I listen to the lines.

I press the flat of my palm, or the side of my jawbone, against the taut guy-wire that leads east. Beneath the steady, mechanical thrum of the geothermal cylinder and the rare, distant *clack* of a pressure valve releasing dry steam two ridges away, the metal carries smaller, sharper things. A tremor arrives—thin, attenuated by miles of sun-warped wire, nearly lost in the hum.

*Tap. Tap-damp. Tap.*

Someone on the high basalt fins, striking the line with a travertine knife. A cracked pipe near the northern cistern, or a rich seam of larvae, or a cold front gathering early behind the saw-toothed ridges. I hold my jaw against the cold steel until the intervals settle into certainty, then I lift a shard of chipped white ceramic insulator from the gravel at my feet.

I lay my free thumb lightly on the western conduit to feel its tension, and with the ceramic edge, I strike and damp the wire in clipped, dry intervals—sending the news onward across the basin without spending a single lungful of air.

When the sun clears the eastern rim and the rocks begin to bake, I climb down. The gliders are already lifting off the ridges on translucent wings, catching the first rising columns of heat. The copper-shelled beetles retreat into the salt-rimmed cracks.

I slip back into my fissure beneath the cylinder’s plinth, drawing my knees high as the lizards press in beside me. Outside, the heat-haze begins to blur the distant towers, and the solar-vent spins faster in the thin, dry wind. In the cool dark, with the stone steady against my spine, I can still feel the faint vibration of the lines humming down through the rock—and I wait for the next blue hour.
