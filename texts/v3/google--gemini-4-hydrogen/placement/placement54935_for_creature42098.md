I would not be down in the basin all the time. If I lived there, I would cook, or go deaf, or lose the edges of myself to the salt and the steam.

Instead, you would find me up on the flat, scrubby shelf of the coastal plain—walking the wind-scoured gravel between the stunted salt-bush and the black basalt, carrying a canvas tool bag that smells of machine oil and dry dust. I would be the pump-tender for this stretch of the coast: the one who drives out from the inland station once a month to check the pressure gauges, walk the surface lines, and make sure the brine is still moving up from the deep rock.

It is an ordinary, solitary kind of work. Most of the stations along the shelf are miserable, exposed things—rattling corrugated sheds where the sea wind strips the paint off the galvanized steel and sand jams the bearings. You spend half your shift with a wire brush and a chipping hammer, fighting off rust and crusted gypsum, cursing the men who bolted the pumps down without leaving room to swing a wrench.

Station Four is different. You don’t see it until you are almost on top of it, just a seam in the dark rock where the insulated pipe and the heavy armored power cable drop out of sight.

The first year I worked this route, I came down the jagged cleft the way I approached every other pit: boots laced tight, earplugs jammed in, chipping hammer in my belt, ready to scrape the valve clean and get back up to the breeze before my shirt soaked through. The old man who had the route before me hadn’t said much when he handed over the keys—only that Four ran hot, that the bearings never seemed to seize, and that I shouldn’t bother bringing the descaling acid down the crack.

I understood why on my third visit.

It takes a few trips before the roar of the pressure-bleed valve stops feeling like an assault and starts feeling like a room you can stand inside. Once you stop fighting the heat—once you accept that your skin will be drenched in ten seconds and your own breathing is swallowed whole by the coils—you start to notice how unusually *clean* the ironwork is.

At the other stations, a month of mineral vapor will choke a bleed valve half-shut with brittle white scale. Down here, the valve’s mouth is always clear, ringed only by that slick, translucent collar that glistens in the straight-down daylight. The first time I reached out with the flat of my screwdriver to test it, expecting to crack off a flake of gypsum, the collar gave slightly under the steel—soft, warm, and stubborn—and a faint shiver ran back through the housing behind the slatted grate. I pulled my hand back, wiped the screwdriver on my thigh, and stood very still.

That was when I learned what my actual job at Station Four was.

I am not here to fix it. Whatever settled into the seam between the condenser and the weeping basalt has been tending the machinery better than any linesman on the payroll. Inside the solitary rusted junction box bolted to the rock rib, where the salt air and sulfur runoff ought to have rotted the relays to powder years ago, the blue-green and rust-red filaments have wrapped the contacts so neatly they hold the current steadier than new copper. When I swing the iron door open, I don’t bring a wire brush anymore. I just shine my pocket torch into the box for a second, check that the yellow moss at the base hasn’t crept up into the seals, and close the latch gently so I don’t pinch the pale runners threading through the seam.

Mostly, what I bring down into the hollow is just the power from the grid above—and, once a month, my own weight.

When I finish checking the gauges on the western housing, I always walk out to the center of the basin, beside the tall, slender standpipe, and stand there for five minutes before climbing back up to the daylight. I take my earplugs out for a minute, just to feel the low-frequency drone settle behind my eyes and vibrate up through the soles of my boots.

Around my ankles, the saucer-sized pool catches its four-second drip. I watch the ripples strike the pale, fluted folds along the rim—watch those porcelain-thin ridges tilt inward by a fraction of an inch, angling toward my shins. On the mirror-still pans around my boots, the opalescent film begins its glacial, patient creep toward my heels, gathering into clear ribbons across the wet black stone. Behind the slatted grate, the hum of the coils thickens, dropping a fraction of a tone as the pale mass wedged against the steel shifts its weight to read me.

It doesn’t know what a linesman is, or what the coastal plain looks like above its neat square of sky. To the thing in the rock, I am only a sudden, cool shadow that interrupts the standing waves—a temporary shape that smells faintly of dry wind and machine oil, standing still long enough to be measured.

And to me, standing there in the scalding dark-stone quiet beneath the roar, it is the one place on the coast where nothing is breaking down. I am just the keeper of the threshold: the one who makes sure the line stays hot from the inland grid, who logs *Pressure nominal, valve clear* in a damp notebook, and who knows better than to mention what lives behind the coils. When the five minutes are up, I lift my boots out of the creeping film, tap the insulated pipe twice with my knuckle—a small, dry punctuation against the four-second drip—and climb back up the cleft into the wind.
