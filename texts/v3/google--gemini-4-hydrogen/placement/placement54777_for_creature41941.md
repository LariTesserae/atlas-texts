I would not be down in the sump, where the air is thick as cold broth and the newts hang motionless under the shelves. And I would not yet be up in the dry, high ribs where the elders go to let the mist glaze them shut.

I would find myself in the middle tiers—somewhere in the dense, cantilevered tangle where the city hangs out over the central drop, three or four spans above the loudest catch-basins. That is where the struts are most crowded, where the clay conduits cross the bone scaffolding and the sinew bridges pull tight against the rock. It is the loudest part of the pocket, which means it is also the clearest.

I would be one of the builders, though not one who herds the snails or scrapes the iron-slime beds. If I follow the pull of this place, I find my hands already holding a splinter of dark flint and a small, curved awl of split horn. I am one of the ones who keeps the edges sharp.

Because the cave is always trying to forgive itself. Every drop of water that drifts through the mist carries a memory of limestone, and where it settles, it softens things. A sharp notch cut into a waist-high parapet—three deep bites to mean *broken flagstone ahead, step wide*—will begin to fill within a few spans. The pale, waxy sinter lays itself down in the groove, layer by patient layer, until the warning feels no different under a passing thumb than the smooth curve of a safe handhold. The braided sinew cables stiffen with mineral crust until a double-knot feels like a simple, meaningless lump. Left alone, the city would not crumble; it would smooth over, becoming once more a single, illegible wall of rock.

So I move slowly along the two-finger alleys, my spine curved to fit the low slate lintels, my stiff newt-hide cloak creaking faintly against the damp stone.

Before I step onto any bridge, I pause and press the flat of my cheekbone—or the bare, chalk-pale skin of my wrist—directly against the nearest wall-strut. Instantly, the blinding white pressure in the air resolves. Through the bone of my jaw, I can feel the whole tier breathing: the deep, steady *thrummm* of the main conduit two levels down; the high, nervous chatter of a clay weir beginning to choke with lime-grit; the faint, rhythmic *tock-tock* of someone three alleys over chipping the glaze off their own doorway. 

Satisfied that the struts beneath me are still carrying their weight without a hollow rattle, I reach out with a horn-hardened thumb and find the bridge cable.

This is where I belong: in the quiet, unhurried work of re-cutting the language. I crouch in the pewter dimness, hook a heel into a floor-groove so the mist-slicked slate won't take me over the edge, and test the parapet with my fingertips. Here, the notch for a shared cistern has grown shallow and slick, blurred by half a lifetime of passing grease-stained palms and mineral spray. I brace the flint splinter against the groove and work it back and forth, feeling the vibration of the scrape travel up my forearm, clearing away the pale, waxy crust until the stone edge bites my skin again. Clean. Legible. 

When another builder approaches from the opposite direction in the narrow dark, there is no startlement. I feel the slight shift in the wall’s vibration ten paces before our knuckles meet. We pause, chest to chest in the cool mist, and touch palms and shoulders—a brief, silent weighing of balance and load. They smell of wet chalk and old sump-fat; I smell of flint-dust and salt. One of us hooks an elbow around a protruding horn lintel and flattens against the weeping rock; the other edges past, trailing a hand along the newly sharpened parapet, pausing for half a heartbeat at the fresh notch to acknowledge the work before moving on.

Sometimes, tucked into a dry pouch beneath my stiffened cloak, I carry something rare: a needle whittled from a drowned cave-cricket’s leg, or a pale splinter of bat-bone brought down from the high granaries. It is too fragile for stone, of course. I use it only on the sinew cables, to pick the stubborn, calcified grit out of the tightest knots without fraying the hide. Working with something that light—something that grew in the rumored air above, where there is no ceiling—feels strange in fingers made for heavy slate. It has no mineral weight to it. It barely carries a vibration at all.

When the work of a tier is done, I do not rest in silence, because there is no silence here. I sit in a shallow overhang near one of the tiny grease saucers—not for the pinprick of blue-white light, which barely reaches my knees, but for the faint, dry warmth that keeps the mist from settling quite so thickly on my cloak. I chew slowly on a dense wafer of dried slime-curd, tasting cold iron and salt, and let my back rest against the masonry. 

Through my shoulder blades, the cavern plays its single, unbroken chord. Nothing is changing. The aquifer above is feeding the rock at the exact rate it always has; the snails are grazing the shelves; the newts are waiting in the dark. 

I know how it ends, and there is no dread in it. One day, the cold will settle too deeply into my knuckles to hold the flint steady. My knees will stiffen until I can no longer hook a heel into the alley grooves. When that happens, I will climb up to the high ribs, pull my lime-crusted hide tight around me, and press my jaw one last time against the humming stone. And while I go still, someone else—curved, pale, and unhurried—will be down in the middle tiers with a horn awl, keeping the edges sharp until the wall takes me in.
