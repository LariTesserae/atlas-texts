I would not be in the pocket itself—not at first, and never for long. The pocket is already full, every cubic foot spoken for by things that have spent centuries perfecting the geometry of staying put.

If I find myself in this world, I think I am in the narrow, dry-ish *seam* just behind the outer hull, three ribs up and slightly to windward of the weeping chamber. Or perhaps I am moving through a whole chain of such seams, following the long, vertical spine of the tower where the ancient ceramic-alloy plates overlap like scales.

At first, I don’t know what I look like. I only know the temperature.

One side of me—my left shoulder, the curve of my ribs—knows the knife-cold. Not the cold of winter on open ground, which is wet and heavy, but the thin, dry, scouring cold of high altitude, leaking in microscopic drafts through the hairline fractures where the outer sacrificial skin has finally begun to give up the ghost. The air here barely carries a scent. It stings the membranes.

My other side knows the heat. Just inches away, behind the inner baffle, the manifold is running, and the bruised-violet warmth of the computational lattice presses against my skin like a fevered forehead.

So I am a creature of the *margin*—the thin, miserable, endlessly interesting millimeter where two impossible extremes cancel each other out into something just barely livable.

I test my hands against the wall. No—not hands, not quite. Or if they were hands once, they have been simplified by the dark and the tight clearances. Long, flat-tipped fingers with thick, horn-like pads at the knuckles. Calloused, dry on the backs, slick and sensitive on the palms. I don’t have much use for eyes here; the violet bleed only shows up where the wall is sick or worn, and the amber status-filaments are down in the pockets, below the grating. Mostly, I see with my teeth and my collarbones.

When I press my chin to the rib, the whole tower opens up to me like a lit room.

I can feel the pocket below me—the heavy, wet *thrum* of its twenty-minute cycle, the faint, jittery static of a hundred armored grazers scraping lichen, and the sharp, rapid *tick-tick-tock* of the pale climbers tapping their territorial claims into the tension-cables. They think they are alone in the noise. They don’t know that three meters above them, in a crack no wider than a forearm, someone is listening to their gossip through the bones of the skull.

No—that’s too small, too merely observant. If I live in the seam, what do I *do* here? What do I eat? Why haven’t I slid down into the pocket to be caught by the great flat sleeper’s feathery palps, or frozen to the outer hull?

Let me feel the hunger.

I am not a predator of the climbers, and I don’t scrape the salt-crusts like the grazers. I follow the *violet*.

Wherever the sacrificial skin wears thin and the bruised-violet waste-photons begin to leak out, the ceramic-alloy changes. The heat is slightly different there—drier, sharper, humming with a tiny, irregular stutter that has nothing to do with the fluid manifold. That stutter is the computational lattice, buried deep in the wall, doing whatever slow, sub-atomic arithmetic it was cast to do before the base of the tower was lost under the clouds.

Those worn patches are where I camp.

I curl into the tight space, wedging my back against the cold outer plate and pressing my chest—bare, pale, maybe ribbed with thin, dark plates of my own, or just toughened skin—directly over the glowing seam. I don’t drink the light the way the great sleeper does, with electro-receptive pits. I think I *tamp* it. Or I *harvest* what grows on the edge of it.

Because where the cold draft from the outer hull meets the violet heat of a failing patch, the condensation doesn’t fall in slanted rain. It *sublimes*. It forms delicate, brittle needles of mineral-rich rime—half salt, half metallic ozone, faintly luminescent with trapped charge. *Frost-scale.* It tastes like copper and lightning and boiled stone, and it melts instantly on the tongue, sending a tiny, sharp jolt straight into the roof of the mouth.

That’s what I am. A **rime-grazer**. No—more mobile than a grazer. A **seam-walker**, a **tinker**, a **listener**.

I move vertically. The tower is taller than anything should be, its base drowned in cloud-deck, its crown lost in air too thin to breathe. I don’t know what is at the bottom, and I don’t know what is at the top. My world is a vertical ribbon of twilight between two deaths: freeze to death on the outside, boil or drown in the gray-water flush on the inside.

Every twenty minutes, I feel the *whump* coming up through my heels before the pocket below me even knows it’s happening.

The pressure-membrane shifts somewhere deep in the core, and the massive roar drops by half an octave. In my narrow seam, the vibration changes from a teeth-rattling buzz to a long, slow, cradling roll. That’s my signal to move. During the three minutes of the flush, the manifold’s field-leakage slackens, the walls cool by two degrees, and the frost-scale along the worn violet patches loosens its grip on the ceramic.

I crawl—spider-limbed, flat-chested, silent—scraping the charged rime into my mouth with a broad, horny lip, swallowing the tiny sparks of mineral heat before the gray-water drains and the roar climbs back up to lock everything in place.

Sometimes, when a seam widens into a dead-end blister, I look down through a weeping joint into a pocket like the one below. I see the pale climbers nodding their heads in time with the lattice’s irregular ticks. I see the great matte-black sleeper peel its edge away from the bulkhead, luminous white underneath, sweeping its feathery nets through the steam.

I don’t go down there. They belong to the wet and the roar.

Once, maybe—a thousand generations ago, or a hundred, or whatever passes for time when nothing changes except the slow flaking of ceramic—whatever I am might have been the same thing as the climbers. Or maybe I was something that *built* the lattice, shrunk down and folded inward until it forgot the blueprints and kept only the habit of checking the seams.

I press my jaw to the wall.

*Tick. Tick-tick. Pause. Tick.*

It isn’t a message meant for me. The lattice doesn’t know I’m here, any more than a brain knows about a louse in the hair. It’s just doing its slow, sub-atomic work, burning through centuries in the dark.

Still, I tap back with one horn-tipped knuckle. Just once. A small, dry *clack* against the ancient ceramic, answered by nothing at all, before I wedge my shoulders tighter between the cold and the heat and wait for the next twenty-minute turn.
