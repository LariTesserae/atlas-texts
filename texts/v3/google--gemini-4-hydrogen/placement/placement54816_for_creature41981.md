I would not arrive here in boots. Anything that walks upright on the Deck is an argument with the sky, and the sky always wins—shrinking your shadow to a razor-edge under your heels, drying the salt out of your mouth, turning every step into a dull, swallowed *tock* that the ceramic forgets before your weight has even shifted.

If I am here, I belong to the flat.

I find myself at the lip of a sunken basin, three days’ slow crawl from the nearest scarred trench, where the hexagonal outer plating has worn thin enough to show the ribs of the world underneath. Here, the ground steps down in shallow, delaminated terraces—pale chalky composite giving way to exposed honeycombs of tarnished bronze and grids of optical lenses, frosted opaque by a million years of blowing silica flour. Thin, translucent reeds grow in the saucer’s center, swaying without wind whenever the ground’s magnetic pulse turns over.

It is the hour just before first light, the only time the Deck feels cool. A microscopic film of salt-dew has condensed in the deep seams between the tiles, darkening the silver dust for a hand’s-breadth around every joint.

My ventral plate is pressed flat against the ceramic, warming slowly as the substrate remembers the coming day. I have no ears—there is nothing in the dead air worth catching—but through the dense, sintered bone of my underside, I can feel the basin speaking. Half a mile to the east, someone is knocking. *Pulse, pause, pulse-pulse.* A slow, coded rhythm driven straight into the floor with a mallet-nodule, asking whether the eastern louver-line has cleared its grit.

I lift my foreweight slightly, shift my eight ceramic-sheathed legs until my carbon-felt pads find purchase in the crevices between the cooling fins, and knock back. *Clear. Steady.* The vibration leaves my body and vanishes into the plain, crisp and lossless, bypassing the silent air entirely.

I am a Seam-Keeper—though I have no word for it, only the weight of my mantle and the particular ache in my antennae when a buried line goes dark.

My shell is old. Years of grinding pulverized glass and abraded metamaterials in my gizzard have left my dorsal plates thick and heavily fluted, baked by a thousand white-gold noons into the exact chalky, high-albedo matte of the surrounding plain. When I tuck my limbs in and shut the quartz louvers over my optical slits, I am just another low ridge of worn-down architecture. Even a glider, drifting three inches off the floor on its magnetic cushion, will pass straight over my back without altering its course, mistaking me for a cooling fin that never broke.

I do not travel with the herds anymore—the younger ones who follow the thermal tides across the open continent, chasing the vast, shimmering waves of opening louvers to scrape out jammed silica. My joints are stiffer now, and the silver antennae trailing from behind my mantle have been kinked and mended too many times with spun copper. Instead, I keep a junction.

In the center of my basin, three buried waveguides cross beneath a broad, shattered lens-plate. Two of them still run strong: every few minutes, a slow, deliberate pulse of amber light travels up from the south, meets a sharper, flickering violet current from the west, and vanishes downward into the sub-layers. They are not talking to me. They are the autonomic reflexes of a hull too vast to know I exist, balancing heat and charge across a hemisphere.

The third line—the one leading north—has been dark since before my shell hardened.

That is where I spend my mornings, before the overhead glare forces me back into the seams. I crawl out onto the frosted glass on my silent felt pads, dragging shards of broken mirror-tile and loose hexagonal plates behind me with my hooked, diamond-tipped foreclaws. I am not building walls; nothing here is ever stacked high enough to catch the dust-devils. I am laying angles.

With excruciating patience, I tilt a sliver of vitrified glass three degrees toward the southern waveguide, wedge it in place with a pinch of fiberglass lichen, and wait. When the amber pulse arrives beneath the frosted quartz, the shard catches a fraction of the scatter and throws it—dim, refracted, barely brighter than a spark—across the dead gap toward the northern conduit.

Nothing happens. The dark glass stays dark.

Still, I adjust the angle by the width of a hair-thin wire. Tomorrow I will shed a thin strip of copper-veined under-shell from my left flank and press it into the hairline crack where the northern lens has sheared, trying to bridge the circuit. I do not know what will happen if the pulse ever crosses the gap—whether the sub-audible hum behind my optical slits will grow steadier, or whether some distant, buried louver a hundred miles north will finally sigh open. I only know that the silence of a dead line feels like an itch under a plate I cannot reach, and that the light wants to keep moving.

Out on the flat beyond my basin, a dust-devil spins up without a whisper—a tall, pale pillar of ceramic flour aligning itself to a leaking field-coil, shedding tiny blue sparks into the floor. A pair of jar-walkers stride into its wake on their glass stilts, raising their copper-wound abdomens to drink the weather. Near the western rim, a loose hexagonal tile shifts by a fraction of an inch, and I pull my trailing antennae in tight against my flanks. Something down in the flaky dark—a braid, coiled around a leaking wire, blind and patient—has felt the warmth of a passing body and is waiting for a careless foot to trip the seam.

I do not step on loose tiles. I know every sound plate in my basin by the way it answers my knock.

By midday, the sky has bleached to incandescent white-gold. Shadows shrink to thin, razor-edged smudges, and the heat begins to press down from above as fiercely as it radiates from below. One by one, I retreat from the lens-grid to the shaded margin of my mosaic—the shallow spiral of angled shards I have arranged over years to trap the last whisper of morning chill and gather stray static into a single, comforting patch of trembling silver dust.

I lower my belly onto the warm ceramic. I tuck my eight legs beneath the fluted rim of my mantle, draw my silver antennae in out of the glare, and click my quartz louvers shut.

In the dark behind my eyes, the world narrows to two things: the faint, rhythmic tingling of the eastern keepers knocking to one another miles away through the floor, and the slow, steady *amber—violet—dark* of the junction pulsing beneath my chest. My fresh ceramic crust bakes dry in the heat, sealing me into the Deck. I wait for the sky to tilt, holding the hum inside my shell, keeping the seam until it is time to move the glass again.
