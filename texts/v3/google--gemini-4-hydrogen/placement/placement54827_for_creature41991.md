I wouldn’t be in the boiler-house at the center of the void, and I wouldn’t be down on the lowest walkways with the young ones holding lengths of copper wire.

I think you would find me a few tiers up, on the underside of one of those twisted staircases where the world briefly forgets which way it is built.

From the alley above, it looks like an ordinary climb of wet timber, three flights up between two gall-tenements. You turn the corner, and the wood thins out into flickering, half-rendered brick—translucent enough that you can see the black, empty volume of the cavern right through the mortar—and the landing deposits you upside down on its own ceiling. That is where I stay. The water in the gutter runs upward past my ankles in a thin, quiet thread, and if I lean out past the railing, the central boiler-house hangs beneath me—or above me, depending on how long I have been standing still—suspended on its three glistening tendons in the cool dark.

At first, between the surges of current, you might mistake me for a swelling in the brick, or a crooked brace left behind when the street grew past. I have the habit of standing very still when the air is quiet.

When a relay clicks somewhere across the hollow and the nearest lamppost swells with its bruised-violet light, you would see that I am one of the tenants—tall, spare, pale as tallow, with the same unweathered sheen on my forearms as the newly extruded bark-plates below. My milky eyes are set deep under chestnut-colored brow ridges, and I keep them mostly half-shut. There is very little to look at here anyway. I read the space the way the others do: by the faint, dry *tick* in my jaw answering the sub-audible thrumming in the vascular cables, and by the weight of the damp air against the fine, pale hairs along my knuckles.

I wear a sleeveless coat of cured cambium, still cool and slightly tacky at the hem. Along the left seam, where it snagged weeks ago on a raw silicate ledge, three ash-pale moths settled while I stood waiting beneath a dynamo; their wings are stiff, flat copper clasps now, cold against my ribs, holding the torn edge shut.

I don’t have a trade, not in any sense that finishes. If I have a habit that passes for work, it is listening to the places where the town doesn’t quite meet itself.

Sometimes I sit with my heels hooked over the braided cambium rail and my palm pressed flat against a riveted copper flange that is still soft where it buds from the bark. I wait for the *plink* of condensation falling from the upper trusses—and more than that, I listen for the droplets that *don’t* fall. Every few minutes, a bead of water detaches from an overhead pipe, drifts sideways at a lazy angle across the dark, and vanishes into the rim of a spinning brass flywheel without ever wetting it. When the timing feels slightly off—when the air-pressure shifts or a valve swells with sap—I reach over and turn the brass wheel by a fraction of a turn. Then I wait another half-cycle to see if the next droplet finds its rim.

Other times, I walk the narrow bridges between the tenements, barefoot or in flat plates of crystallized salt, my knees clicking softly in the chill until I pause by a gantry crane and borrow one of the wet-chalk creatures from between the wooden gear-teeth. It leaves a cool, clear ribbon of mucus across my joint and crawls unhurriedly back into the mechanism. When I pass another tenant on the bridge, we don’t speak. We simply lean our foreheads together for a breath, or press the backs of our wrists side by side, sharing whatever tiny vibration is traveling up through the timber from the boiler-house below. Sometimes a cold spark jumps between our collarbones, smelling briefly of ozone and wet cellulose, and then we step past each other without disturbing the blind amphibians clinging to the underside of the rail.

Only when the air changes—when a brisk, dry clicking starts up along the catwalk, too fast and too sharp to be a relay—do I flatten myself against the half-rendered brick. I let my breathing slow, let my skin coarsen and dull into the matte, fibrous texture of bracket fungus, and wait while the wrong-jointed predator trots past upside down, its eyeless head swinging through the dark just inches from my shoulder.

I am not as young as the ones at the bottom who still smell of gall-sap, waiting for two streets to grow close enough to splice. And I am not as old as the ones in the deeper chambers, who curled up in their wall-alcoves long ago and stayed still until their shoulders widened into the bark and violet membranes swelled along their collarbones.

I am somewhere in the long, unhurried middle—still walking, still listening, though I can already tell which hollow in the nearest tenement wall fits the curve of my spine.

Sometimes, late in a cycle, I tap three times with my knuckle on a copper pipe that ends twenty feet out in empty air, just to feel the vibration travel out into the dark and stop. There is never an echo. There is only the hum of the current in my teeth, the slow *plink* of water missing its cistern, and the quiet, patient certainty that sooner or later I will lie down in my alcove, let the fine pale rootlets thread out from my fingertips into the floorboards, and become one more warm, faintly glowing seam in the town that never finishes growing.
