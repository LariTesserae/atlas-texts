I would find myself in the leeward hollow of an unnamed mesa, three days’ walk from the nearest neighboring grove, sitting with my back against a rib of wind-planed rhyolite while the night’s cold still clings to the stone.

At first, before the sun clears the eastern rim, there is no impulse to move. Movement in thin air spends warmth, and warmth is something you hoard here the way other worlds hoard water. Between my knees, a lump of amber resin—pried years ago from the heart of a collapsed fan-spire—holds its last, flameless glow. It does not crackle. It does not smell of woodsmoke, only faintly of warm sulfur and dry chalk. When I cup my palms over it, I can feel the faint, gritty heat soaking through the cured, plated skin of my fingers.

If you were to look at me from the rim of the basin, you might mistake me for a fold in the rock, or a mound of dead bracts swept into a corner by the afternoon eddies. My cloak is stitched from the world’s cast-offs: heavy, overlapping tiles of felted fan-spire leaf on the inside to trap what little heat my body makes, and a pale, dusted outer layer of shed glider membrane and silver moss-powder to turn away the fierce noon glare.

I am a *tender*. Not one of the young ones—if *young* is even a word that means anything on a plateau where everything grows at the speed of acid dissolving gypsum—but one who has walked the northern corridors long enough that the joints of my shoulders have set into the slight, forward cant of someone who leans into continental wind.

When the light finally hits the upper lip of the basin, it doesn't warm the air; it simply turns the sky from black-violet to that sharp, bruised cobalt, and makes the white mineral pan beyond the grove’s edge blaze like struck flint.

That is when I begin.

I do not walk out onto the flats right away. My work is in the margin where the dark velvet of the grove meets the chalk. As the sun reaches the sunken floor, the towering fan-spires begin to uncurl their heavy, suede-like leaves, locking open to face the glare with a dry, slow *creak* that travels better through the soles of my wrapped feet than through the thin air.

I kneel at the base of the oldest spire—its trunk thick as a boulder, scarred with decades of salt-crust—and slide my bare, chalky hand beneath the overlapping skirts of the plate-vines. Down here, out of the light, the air smells sharply of dry acid and pulverized silica. Beneath the silver-grey cushions of dust-moss, I feel the faint, papery *shivering* of the bract-borers waking in their labyrinth of shed husks. I don't disturb them; they are the ones who grind the dead litter back into meal. Instead, I reach deeper, feeling for the choked, brittle husks of last season’s fallen bracts that have wedged themselves too tightly around the young shoots.

I pull them free, one by one, stacking them neatly at the outer edge of the moss where the afternoon eddies can catch them and wear them down into powder. As I work, a cloud of spark-mites scatters across my knuckles—tiny, amber-shelled grains leaping from hair to hair on invisible bursts of static. They leave a faint, prickling tickle against my skin, like dust settling on dry glass.

I have no voice. Even if I had something to say, the air here would swallow it before it cleared my teeth, and there is no wet in my throat to waste on sound. What I have instead is *charge*, and *patience*.

By midday, the open pans beyond the hollow begin to heat, and the silent thermals rise. From the shelter of the spire’s shadow, I watch the glittering curtains of mineral dust lift off the chalk and sweep across the basin. The whole grove leans into it—every microscopic hair on every broad frond standing upright, snatching nitrogen and trace metals straight out of the sky. My cloak drinks it in, too. As the dry, glittering wind combs over my shoulders, the felted fibers gather a slow, humming static, storing the day’s passage in a way my memory never could.

Once, while I am sitting still with a pouch of harvested spore-powder in my lap, a strider steps out of the glare on the far side of the pan. It is miles away, yet in this clear, waterless air I can see every slow, pendulum swing of its six stilt-legs, its pale, boat-shaped shell parting the wind without a ripple. It will reach this hollow by late afternoon, crack open a few of the tough, silica-armored spore-cones at the grove’s skirt, and carry our golden-grey dust on its bristled joints to whatever basin lies ten miles south.

We do not acknowledge each other. We are simply two different gears in the same dry clock. Above us both, a single glider hangs pinned to an invisible pillar of rising dust, its translucent wings casting a brief, razor-sharp shadow across the chalk as it watches for the ridges of a burrowing chalk-skimmer.

If you asked me what I want, standing here at the edge of the velvet dark, I wouldn't know how to shape the answer. Wanting is a wet, hurried thing. Here, you only *tend* and *wait*.

I spend the afternoon pressing small pinches of spore-powder into fresh, hairline fractures in the gypsum along the basin’s leeward wall—places where the wind has finally peeled back a layer of old stone and left raw mineral exposed. It will take years for the dry acids to bite deep enough for a new cushion of dust-moss to take hold, and decades before a fan-spire rises from it. I will likely not be in this hollow when it does.

That is the quietest part of what I am. I am not building a monument for myself, and I am not keeping a record in ink or speech. When the sun drops toward the dusty horizon and the light turns to piercing amber, I will gather my cloak tight, tuck a fresh coal of resin into my belt, and step out onto the cooling pan, heading east toward the next smudge of verdigris and tarnished copper on the horizon.

Somewhere out there, perhaps three basins over, or perhaps not for another four years, I will cross paths with another tender on the blinding white stone. We will stop a few paces apart, let the wind settle the talc around our feet, and press our dry, chalky palms together.

And in that single, sharp snap of static—passing from my cloak to theirs, and theirs to mine—they will know everything I have seen: the health of the northern spires, the richness of the sulfur in the afternoon dust, the slow, unhurried passing of the striders, and the quiet persistence of the moss taking root in the fractured stone.

Then we will draw our hands back, turn our prows into the wind, and keep walking.
