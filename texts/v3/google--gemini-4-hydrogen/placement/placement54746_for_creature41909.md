I would not be down on the lower tiers, where the salt-brick terraces are widest and the lichen-farmers crowd shoulder-to-shoulder in the green-gold shade of the bladder-leaves. And I would not be a traveler stumbling down through the northern lip, blinking in the milk-white glare with open, bleeding ears.

I would be native to the dryness, sealed since my third day of life. You would find me high up on the sixth tier of the northern arc, just below the jagged mouths of the wind-throats, where the air enters the dome fastest and the white dust of the ancestors settles thickest on the sills.

I am a *tuner of ridges*—one of the few who live where the sound is rawest, tending the seam where the city’s nervous system meets the exterior gale.

My day does not begin with light. My alcove is carved deep behind three packed walls of pith and felted lichen, windowless and dark as the inside of a closed fist. Instead, I wake when the thinnest copper wire coiled around my left jawbone begins to itch against the skin. That is the dawn-gauge catching the first shift of the exterior air, telling me the desert crust outside is beginning to warm and the dome’s belly-deep bass is climbing toward morning.

Before I step out, I take a pinch of bitter, clove-scented lichen-meal, chase it with a single drop of stalk-oil from a stopper of gourd-skin, and rub fresh plant-wax into the creases of my knuckles and the leathery, sealed folds where my outer ears used to be. Nothing can be left unsealed. Then I dip my palms and fingertips into a bowl of charred resin-soot until they are black as flint, pull back the heavy pith curtain, and step out into the roar.

The glare hits like a physical weight, my amber inner eyelids sliding shut automatically to cut the selenite’s fierceness. The sound hits harder—up here on Tier Six, the standing waves are thick enough to bruise an unbraced rib. I plant my horn-thick heels flat on the salt-brick, clench my molars lightly so they don't rattle, and let my skeleton take the chord.

From my ledge, the whole three-hundred-pace void drops away beneath me, empty and boiling with invisible currents. Across the milk-lit gulf, the opposite walls look like a hive of pale wasp-paper, alive with tiny, flashing black hands—soot-stained palms flickering in sharp angles through the drifting chaff.

My work sits in a shallow niche beside my door: three long bones laid out on a mat of amber lichen. One is a femur, bleached light as a hollow stalk and smooth as tallow; the others are radii from an elder who stopped taking oil two seasons ago, carried up past my ledge to the wind-throats and brought back down to me once the copper was uncoiled from her wrists.

I spend the mid-morning hours with a bone bow-drill and a flint bit, boring small, beveled mouths into the femur. You cannot tune a bone inside the quiet of the pith-alcoves; you have to bring it out into the shriek. I wedge the femur into a natural fissure in the gypsum wall, press my fingertips lightly against its shaft, and wait for the chamber’s rising pitch to find it.

At first, the bone only chatters—a harsh, dry buzz against my fingertips that means the hole is too narrow, fighting the air. I pull it back, scrape the rim with a sliver of flint, blow the white powder away, and wedge it in again. When the bevel is true, the chatter vanishes. Suddenly, the femur doesn't feel like a separate object at all; it goes silky and weightless under my fingers, humming in a deep, steady octave that travels straight up my radius and settles behind my sternum. When evening comes and the wind-throats slacken, this bone will catch the cooling draft and hold a low, warm note long after the pods have stopped rattling.

Toward noon, the thick copper coil on my ankle begins to burn with vibration—the pitch is climbing toward the white-hot shriek. Above me, the selenite roof gives a sharp, pistol-crack snap as it expands in the sun. Down in the central void, the bladder-plants pop open all at once, and the air fills with a blinding blizzard of glittering, weightless chaff-scales spinning upward in the violent updrafts.

A sharp, stammering triplet snaps through the taut sinew-line lashed to my handrail. I lay my soot-black palm flat against the cord, pinching it lightly with my thumb to read the tension.

*Tier Four. A cracked retaining-brick beneath the root-tangles. Bring copper pins.*

I pluck back a single, heavy pulse—*heard, coming*—and reach for my coiled wire and bone pliers.

Before I descend the zig-zagging stair, I pause for one breath at the edge of my terrace. Out in the blinding glare, a papery, blind glider banks sharply off the updraft, sensing the vibration of the notched bone rod I keep wedged beside my doorway. It settles onto the wood, its translucent wings humming like taut parchment against the standing waves.

I reach out carefully and brush a speck of gypsum dust from its blind, smooth head with one soot-stained finger. Through the tremble of its wings, I can feel what the wind-throats are about to bring: a fierce, dry gale building miles out on the salt-flats, hours before the lower tiers will feel it in their copper.

I let the glider peel back into the glare, turn to the sinew-line, and send the warning down into the bones of the city.
