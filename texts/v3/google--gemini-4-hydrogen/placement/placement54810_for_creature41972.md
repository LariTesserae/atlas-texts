I would not arrive here with a lantern, or a rope, or a question. If I were to find myself in this place, I would already belong to its temperature.

I would be on the underside of one of the lower travertine shelves, a hand’s breadth above the coppery water on the floor. Palm-sized, soft-shelled, blind—not with closed lids like the one who lives in the back hollow, but with no eyes at all, no place in my body ever shaped to expect a sun. My shell would be thin and slightly yielding, pale as tallow, already dusted along its rim with the same faint, chalky lime that coats the basalt needles.

My whole world would be two surfaces: the stone above me, and the air below.

Upward—though there is no *up* here, only *stone*—my broad, flat foot would be pressed against the slick mineral skin of the shelf. Through it, I would know the mountain’s core: not as a sound, not even as heat, because everything here is the same blood-warmth, but as a steady, almost imperceptible hum. It is the vibration of rock that has only just stopped being liquid, holding its breath in the dark.

Downward, hanging into the thick, wet wool of the air, my soft shell would gather condensation. Every so often, a bead of lime-water would swell at the lowest curve of my back, grow heavy, and let go. I would feel the tiny release of its weight, and a moment later, a faint *plock* would travel up through the basalt pillar and into my foot—the only proof that there is a floor below me, an inch of tepid water waiting to catch what falls.

Time, here, would not be measured in days or breaths. I would scarcely breathe at all, taking what little oxygen exists directly through my wet skin from the film of water that coats the rock. Instead, time would be the slow thickening of the mineral crust at the edges of my shelf, and the occasional, faint shifting of the water film when something in the chamber moves.

Most of the time, there is only the weeping of the seams.

Then, from the curve of basalt near the back of the pocket, comes the long, wet draw of air.

It is the first sign of the other inhabitant. They do not make a sound when they sit folded in their hollow, but their chest is wide, and when they finally take a breath, the still air of the chamber pulls slightly toward them. A tiny draft—no stronger than the stir of a falling drop—brushes across my shell. A long pause follows, while they hold what little they have gathered. Then the wet sigh releases, and the air settles back against my skin, warm and heavy as before.

When they unfold and begin to move through the teeth of the chamber, I know it through the stone first. Their heels—thickened into tough, leathery horn—press into the tiered ledges of the floor, sending slow, dull ripples through the inch of standing water and up into the pillars. I do not hurry. Hurry is a thing for places with wind. I simply slide a fraction of an inch deeper into the underside of my shelf, where the travertine curves inward, feeling the slick film of iron and lime part smoothly beneath my foot.

Sometimes they pass close enough that I feel the displacement of the air as a dense, silent wall gliding past. They never strike the stone. They know every jagged fin and hanging curtain of crust the way water knows a basin. Once in a long while, I hear—not with ears, which I do not have, but through the rigid spine of the rock—a dry, rhythmic *scrape, scrape* from the center of the chamber. It is the sliver of old bone clearing the fresh lime out of the horizontal notches on the basalt rib. The vibration is sharp and brief, alien to the wet softness of everything else, and when it stops, the hum of the mountain’s core seems louder for a moment before it smooths back into stillness.

Not far from my shelf, a single hair-thin root hangs down from a seam in the ceiling, having forced its way through meters of solid peak from a world I cannot imagine. When the water shifts, or when a drop swells on its tip and falls, the root sways. Sometimes the inhabitant’s fingers—long, scarred across the knuckles, horned at the pads—reach past my shelf to find that thread. They never pull it. They only pinch its tip, or touch it to their tongue, taking whatever faint bitterness it has carried down from the frost and the soil above.

And sometimes, those same fingers sweep the underside of my shelf.

There is no rush of heat when they touch me; their skin and my shell are the exact same temperature, slick with the same mineral water. I feel the tough, calloused pad of a thumb trace the rim of my shell, testing my weight, reading my stillness. I do not pull away. There is nowhere to pull away to, and nothing in me that knows how to refuse the chamber’s equilibrium.

Once, long ago, there was another like me clinging to the far side of this same shelf. The fingers found it, paused, and pried it loose with a slow, gentle twist. It did not fight, and the hand did not hurry. Afterward, the space it left behind was empty, and over a long, uncounted stretch of drops falling into the pool, I slid an inch farther along the stone to fill it.

Today, the thumb only rests against my shell for a moment, then moves on, leaving me intact in the warm, wet dark.

I stay where I am, pressed against the underside of the stone, waiting for the next shift of the water, listening through my belly to the mountain breathing around us both.
