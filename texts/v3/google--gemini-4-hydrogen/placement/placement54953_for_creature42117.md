I would not be out on the causeway-ends with the lances, and I would not be in the center of the knapping yards where the fresh basalt rings sharpest.

I would wake in the thick-walled dark, tangled in the warm heap of my kin under a stiff, oil-cured hide, with the city outside reduced to a steady, distant thrumming in the stones beneath my ear—like water moving through a deep pipe. Someone’s knee would be pressed against my hip; someone else’s forearm, slick and cool with settled silt, would be thrown across my ribs. In the dark, there is no way to tell how long I have slept, only that my joints have stiffened and my stomach has hollowed out.

I would crawl out through the low doorway, blinking into the pewter dusk, and let the wet heat settle back onto my shoulders.

For a few breaths at the threshold, the noise is a flat hand against the chest. The frogs in the drainage channels never stop; the shorebirds on the eaves never stop; the *clack-clack* of the stone-breakers and the bright *ting* of the copper hammers bounce off the wet shale walls until the air itself feels crowded. I would crouch there by the lintel, scooping a palmful of gray silt and rendered frog-fat from the clay jar by the door, smearing it over my forearms and the back of my neck before the midges find the creases of my skin.

And then I would look down at the foundation chinks, where the pale-shelled crabs are already pulling back into the dark between the stones, and I would know what my shift is for.

I think I would be one of the polishers—one of the quiet ones who tends the smooth things.

In a place where a razor edge takes three strikes of a cobble and costs nothing, patience is the only thing worth trading. My fingers would be thick-knuckled and permanently stained a deep, mottled slate, the calluses on my heels as hard as horn, my ears half-dulled from forty or fifty shifts—or whatever passes for years here—of living inside the stone echo. Not wholly deafened yet, not like the oldest ones who read a whole conversation from the twitch of a cousin’s shoulder across a terrace, but deafened enough that the frog-chorus blurs into a low, harmless vibration in my jawbone, leaving me oddly undisturbed.

I would carry a pouch of cured frog-skin slung across my chest, and inside it, the things that take time: a palm-sized slab of shale I have been rubbing against wet hide and fine mineral mud for longer than my youngest nephew has been alive, working its jagged corners down until they feel like a toad’s belly; two or three cold-hammered copper pins waiting for their final sheen; a small, rounded piece of bone.

You find your place in a city like this by what you don’t mind doing. I don’t mind the slowness.

Half my shift would be spent squatting under a stretched-hide awning on a middle terrace, my back against a dry-stacked wall, working a piece of stone in circles against greased leather while the city roars around me. When a neighbor comes to trade—a basket of crushed shellfish, a fresh coil of cured gut, a newly struck basalt blade—we don’t bother shouting over the birds. We press close enough to smell the salt and guano on each other’s hides, or we let our hands do it: a flat palm slicing down for *stone*, two fingers curving inward for *round*, a flick of the knuckles against the jaw for *wet*. The signs are crisp, angular, made to be seen through the hanging mist. Even when my hands are busy, a nod of the chin or a tap of my sandal against the wet plinth says enough.

The other half of my shift would be higher up, where the city meets the pewter ceiling.

Someone has to climb to the high stone platforms above the terraces—the places where the air smells sharpest of chalky guano and wet feathers, and the dark-plumed divers snap their needle beaks at your ankles if you step too close to a clutch of eggs. That is where we lay our dead, because there is no soil to dig and no wood to burn. The shorebirds know the arrangement. They strip the flesh cleanly, squabbling and slapping their wings in the dim, unchanging light, and when they are done, what is left is pale and raw.

I would go up with a basket of woven sinew and a jar of oil. I would gather the clean bones—a grandmother’s femur, an uncle’s ribs—and carry them back down the slick alleys, stepping carefully over the scuttling crabs that keep the stone clear of rot. Down by the house, in the narrow shadow of the eaves, I would wash the bones in warm seepage water, scrub away the last grit with a scrap of rough hide, and rub them with rendered fat until the porous pale surface drinks it in and turns the color of old tallow.

Then I would walk the base of our family’s walls, testing the dry-stacked basalt, looking for the gaps where the gray clay has washed thin. A house here is never finished; it settles, it weeps, it shifts on the mineral mud. You fit a smooth, oiled bone into a jagged chink between two dark slabs, wedge it tight with crushed shell, and the wall holds a little better. The dead keep the noise out. They keep the damp from creeping into the sleeping mats.

Out beyond the outer plinths, where the causeways end in pale mire and the rust-orange biofilm quivers over the warm pools, the hunters would be wading into the mist with their basalt-tipped lances, silent except for the tap of stone against stone beneath the water. I would watch them go from the edge of the terrace, and when they hauled one of the broad-backed, snail-crusted giants up the stones, I would take my place in the crowd with my copper blade, claiming a strip of heavy hide for my rubbing-cloths and a rib for the roof.

And when my eyes grew heavy—not because the sky darkened, for the sky never changes, but because the tide of waking had simply run out in my limbs—I would hand my half-smoothed shale to a cousin stepping out of the doorway, crawl back into the warm, muffled dark of the stone room, pull a cured skin over my face to keep the condensation off, and sleep to the low thrumming of the basin.

I would not be a master of anything here. Nobody is. I would just be one more pair of gray-stained hands in the wet heat, turning sharp stone into something you can hold without bleeding, and fitting the smooth bones of my kin into the walls so the quiet inside stays quiet.
