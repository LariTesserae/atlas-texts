At first, there is only the white—not a color, but a pressure against the eyelids, warm and wet as a wrung cloth. I would not know if I were standing or lying down until I felt the film of water along my shins, and beneath it, the razor-sharp seam of a mortar joint pressing into the arch of my bare foot.

That is where I would find myself: balanced on a ridge between two tiles, already knowing how to keep my heels off the flats without thinking about it.

If I looked down, I wouldn’t see a shadow to tell me my own shape. Just pale, matte skin flushed a faint terracotta along the forearms where the floor-heat rises, slick with a sheen that never beads and never cools. At my hip, the knot of twisted reed cordage would be damp and heavy, holding a polished bone scraper against my thigh. No chert leaf—or perhaps it is there, and my fingers simply haven’t reached for it. My chest would rise and fall in the slow, shallow cadence of the padded air, while the pulse in my throat kept its own quiet, stubborn time, two beats behind, or three ahead.

I wouldn’t walk toward the gardens first, though the emerald moss and the heavy, leather-petaled lotuses pull at the eye in all that glare. Something in the whiteness makes you want to look for edges, for the places where one thing stops being another. I would follow the narrow mortar ridges out past the shallow cisterns, stepping lightly so the water on the flats stays unbroken.

Beside one of the rectangular basins, I would stop—not to dip a gourd, not to touch the wading birds that stand like carved chalk in the warm shallows, but to look down at the water around their legs.

The birds’ reflections would be facing the wrong way, turned toward the far rim. And mine would be there too, pale against the dark tile bottom, though I wouldn’t be kneeling. The figure in the water would be standing just behind my own shoulder, its head tilted slightly, looking past the basin toward the distant, reed-thatched sheds. Not with alarm. Just with the patient attention of someone waiting for a sound in a place where sound does not travel.

When I looked up, the sheds would be sitting in the exact same white-hot afternoon they had occupied a moment ago, or an hour ago.

So I would go there—not into the center of the blinding flats where the runnels tremble at their clay lips, and not under the shield-sized leaves where the pale frogs pulse in the green dark, but to the edge where the glare softens under the reed thatch.

Inside, the dimness smells of wet clay and dark pitch, thick and grounding after the bright emptiness outside. Four or five tenders sit around a damp mound, their wet hair plastered to their shoulders, passing a bone scraper from palm to palm without looking up. They do not notice me, or rather, they notice me the way they notice the thatch overhead or the warm water in the cauldrons—as something already accounted for, already part of the room’s stillness.

I wouldn’t sit at the mound. My hands wouldn’t want the clean, flat slabs of the tiles, or the rhythmic arcs of the wooden floats.

Instead, I think I would find myself at the back of the shed, where the reed wall thins out and the unlit kilns stand.

They are quiet, domed things of low-fired brick, their mouths open to the dimness, their floors swept clean of ash—if there was ever ash to sweep. No wood is stacked beside them. No heat lingers in their bellies, only the same uniform, radiant warmth that rises from every tile in the valley.

I would kneel at the mouth of one of the kilns, my knees resting on the unweathered brick, and I would be the one who tends the *unfired* edge of things.

Not with frustration. There is no room for frustration in air this heavy. I would take a small lump of clay—not sliced clean with cordage, not pressed into a wooden mold, just gathered from the trimmings that fall away when the others scrape their tiles mirror-flat—and I would roll it slowly between my damp palms. Rolling it until it warms to the exact temperature of my skin. Pressing my thumb into the side of it, not to leave a crisp, bevelled mark like the tiles in the floor, but just to feel the soft resistance give way.

Sometimes, I would set the small, thumb-marked lump inside the cold mouth of the kiln, in the deepest part of the dimness where the glare cannot reach at all.

It wouldn’t dry there. The wet air wouldn’t let it crack or harden, and no fire would ever come to turn it into terracotta. It would simply sit on the swept brick, pale and damp and unfinished, holding the shape of my palm. And when enough of them had gathered along the inner curve of the kiln—a neat, silent row of things that were never meant to be stacked—someone from the clay mound would eventually reach in without looking, gather them up in two wet hands, and drop them into the pitch-sealed cauldron of warm water to be kneaded back into the earth of the shed.

Or perhaps I would do it myself.

That is who I would be, I think: not a maker of the sharp, endless floor, and not a keeper of the brimming runnels, but the one at the threshold of the unlit kiln. A tender of the pause between the molding and the fire—living in the soft, unhurried breath before anything hardens, shaping small, nameless things out of wet clay just to feel them give, and then giving them back to the water before they can ever be finished.
