At first, the instinct is to put myself on the catwalks—high above the black water, one hand on the pitted iron railing, trying to take the measure of the place. From up there, the basin looks legible. You can trace the geometry of the octagonal pump-towers, watch the sluice gates dump their mineral sheets, and see the whole steaming enclosure in a single sweep.

But the catwalk is a lie for anything with lungs and nerves. A minute of breathing that warm, copper-heavy broth is enough to prove I don’t belong on the iron. The sub-bass of the submerged rams doesn’t stay in the soles of your boots; it climbs your shins, rattles your teeth, turns the fluid in your inner ear to static. To stay there as a creature with eyes and a pulse would mean spending every second flinching—waiting for the next shriek of a steam vent, the next *plop* of a seed pod, the next wet, scalding gust from below. You wouldn’t live in the basin; you would only endure it.

So if I am actually *of* this world, I have to give up the catwalk. I have to let the basin take away the things it has no use for—the fragile eardrums, the dry skin, the frantic need to look across the whole room at once—and see what is left when the noise stops being noise.

***

For a moment, I wonder if I’d be one of the pale creepers—specifically the kind that wraps directly around the hottest steam pipes. There is something appealing about that blind, stubborn hunger: gripping scorching iron, swelling in the mineral mist, pulsing in time with the pistons until a burst of superheated steam strips you back to wet pulp, only for you to uncurl again an hour later. 

Yet that feels too frantic. The plants here are caught in a race against a schedule. They grow fast because they have to; they are always climbing toward a blade or a blast of steam. I don’t think I am built for that kind of urgency.

Nor am I the machinery. The gears and scraper-blades have no give in them. They don’t notice when the water shifts; they just turn until their brass goes green and their iron pits, indifferent to the world they keep alive.

***

Where I actually find myself is lower down—off the catwalks, past the brickwork, beneath the mirror-slick skin of the lagoon. Not out in the open, deep water, and not wedged high on the dry trusses, but in the shallow, turbulent margin where a submerged brick causeway meets the base of an octagonal pump-tower.

It is not a place you would look at twice from above. The water here is hot enough to keep the copper dissolved, clouded with fine silt and shredded fern-pulp washed down from the spillways. A heavy hydraulic ram thuds twenty yards away, slow and staggered, and every few minutes a sluice gate groans open overhead, sending a fresh sheet of scalding runoff crashing down the tiered brick.

I have no eyes to see the olive-amber gloom, and no ears to be deafened by it. Instead, I am a cluster of thick, leather-tough lobes—mottled dark copper and bruised violet where the membrane stretches thin—resting half-submerged against the warm mortar.

At first, it is hard to imagine being something without a thought, without a spine, without even a *front* or *back* to orient by. Yet once I settle into the shape, the absence of nerves feels less like a loss and more like a kind of vast, heavy peace. The deafening roar of the boiler houses is gone. In its place is a clean, physical geometry inside my own chambers: standing waves moving through dense mucilage, fine metallic grit drifting and settling along the bottom of my sacs.

I don’t *hear* the basin; I *register* it, the way a bowl of oil registers a footstep on the floor.

When the submerged ram thuds, a slow pulse crosses my middle. When a rotating scythe clears the spillway above and a drift of bruised, half-cooked creeper fronds washes over the causeway, the water around me thickens and cools by half a degree. The interference pattern inside me shifts. My internal grit slides toward the disturbance, and without deciding anything, without wanting anything, my bulk leans after it. I press one flank harder against the hot brick of the pump-tower; the fluid in my rear chambers warms and expands, and I heave myself forward by inches, draping a heavy lobe over the wet pulp to let my sour exudate do its work.

***

If I look closely at where I am in the long arc of this place, I don’t think I am one of the young, fist-sized bladders—still nimble enough to creep up the spillways and risk the scythes, still light enough to ride the currents of the lagoon.

I am older than that. Not quite one of the ancient ones, plated entirely in solid verdigris and silica, indistinguishable from the stonework—not yet. I am in the long, slow middle of turning into architecture.

My lower half is already heavy with settled copper carbonate, wedged firmly into the angle between a submerged arch and an intake grate. I can still shift my turgor when a large enough drift of seed pods *plops* nearby, still lean a few inches toward the warmth of a steam line when the lagoon cools slightly under the rain from the glass ceiling. Mostly, though, I am a stationary gauge. I sit in the current, filtering the hot, mineral broth through my underside, letting the basin’s rhythms pass through my gelatinous core and arrange my grit into quiet, shifting patterns.

A pale creeper has already found my upper ridge. In the dim amber twilight, its root-tendrils can’t tell my salt-crusted hide from the brick beside me. They grip my crown, pulsing faintly in time with the pump-tower’s pistons, drawing warmth from the same machinery that keeps my chambers alive. Every few days, it drops a heavy, fluid-filled seed pod straight onto my back. I don’t reach for it, and I don’t thank it. I simply soften the skin beneath it, absorb the gift, and go on breathing with the basin.
