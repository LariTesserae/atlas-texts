If I were to wake into this world—not as a visitor passing through with dry boots and a map, but as something that belonged to it—I would not be out on the dizzying undersides of the high arches, nor drifting with the gliders in the open amber air.

I would find myself at a junction.

Not the highest ones, where the broad-leafed canopy filters the light into a pale, feverish gold, and not the rotting lower hammocks where the fallen things go to be stripped by tiny, silent scavengers. Somewhere in the middle twilight: a swollen, ancient knot where five great ribs of braided timber collide and fuse, and half a dozen swaying vine-bridges trail off into the bruised-green mist like loose threads.

You would mistake me at first for a lump of pale callus-tissue, or a fungus shelf wedged into the crook of a lashing. I would be kneeling on the slick, yielding bark, one broad, tacky-padded foot braced against an ancestor’s bone peg, my ear pressed flat against the wet slate of the wood.

Because that is what I would be: a listener at the crossroads. A keeper of the cords.

In a world where the air eats your voice twenty paces from your mouth, someone has to stay near the places where the messages cross. Along the flanks of my junction, dozens of single bast-fiber strands are drawn taut between sharpened bone pegs, disappearing in every direction into the fog. Some run along the upper curve of a rib toward the canopy; others vanish downward along the vertical wall of a pillar that used to be a floor an hour ago. Each cord has its own pitch, though you never hear it in the air. When I pluck one with my thumb, or when someone half a mile away plucks their end, the message arrives as a dull, woody *thrum* through the soles of my feet, or against the bone of my jaw.

I am not one of the swift, long-limbed climbers who cross three swaying bridges before the mist shifts. Perhaps my knees are a little too stiff from the dampness, or perhaps I simply have the temperament of something that prefers to stay where the wood is thick. My skin is the color of peeled willow, mottled across the shins with green lichen-dust from years of kneeling on the moss-crust. Around my waist is a wrap of oiled glider-membrane—darkened with age, mended with sinew—and at my hip hangs a perforated gourd lined with wet moss.

Inside the gourd sits an old amphibian, its throat pale and translucent, its breathing slow. When a blue-green spark blinks from the far side of the chasm—a patient, rhythmic flicker from some unseen tender clinging to a distant pillar—I do not shout. I lift the gourd, touch a single drop of bitter sap to the creature’s tongue with the tip of my finger, and wait for its throat to flare in answer. A small, quiet lantern of wet skin, speaking across three miles of warm, empty sky.

My hollow is grown just below the junction’s lip, coaxed over decades by hands older than mine—a dry, domed blister of fused shoots smelling faintly of peat and dried sap. Inside, a woven hammock hangs by a single loop, and a cord waits beside it to tie around my ankle before I sleep, so that if a dream makes me roll toward the open doorway, I will not drift out into the warm eddies and spin away into the haze.

Mostly, though, I am awake, tending the *when* of things.

Because the geometry here refuses to hold still, travelers arrive at my junction disoriented. They come stepping off a three-rope bridge out of the fog, barefoot and silent, carrying the faint, damp smell of another altitude. Sometimes they have walked a triangle—Pillar to Pillar to Pillar—only to step back onto my ribs and find the wood twice as thick as when they left it, the bridges trailing off into chasms that were not there yesterday.

They do not ask *where* they are. There is no *where* in a world that breathes and shifts.

Instead, they kneel beside me on the slick bark. We lean in until our foreheads touch, skin to skin, and they hum low in their chests—a vibration that passes directly from their skull into mine, bypassing the wet wool of the atmosphere. *Whose hands?* they ask in that bone-deep buzz. *How long?*

I take their palm and lay it flat over the ancient lashings at the center of the junction. Beneath our fingers, the great bast-fiber cables have been half-swallowed by the growing wood, the bark around them scarred and healed into smooth, pale ridges. I guide their thumb along a particular spiral of bone pegs, a staggered ridge of flint-adze notches, a double-loop twisted in the old way before the cambium closed over it.

*Look,* I hum back into their forehead. *Feel the callus. This is the third generation after the great sag. The bridge you crossed is older than the one you left from, and the mist has turned it. You are not lost. You have simply arrived later than you thought.*

They nod, their shoulders dropping. Sometimes they leave a gift—a gourd of condensed water, a strip of dried glider-membrane, or a worn flint adze whose edge has gone dull. I wedge the adze into a fresh notch in the rib and leave it there. In a few seasons, the dampness will wake the stone, and pale, calcified tendrils will sprout from its edges, rooting it into the living timber so the arch grows a little stronger. Everything here is only borrowed for a while before the wood takes it back.

And if I am honest, that is the quiet pull at the center of my days.

Just above my hollow, wedged into a tight crook where two massive ribs cross, there is one of the Rooted. An elder who stopped walking long before my skin was stained with sap. Their shins have vanished under the moss-crust; their face is as smooth and pale as healed bark, eyes closed, breath indistinguishable from the slow condensation dripping from the leaves. Their long, lean arms are wrapped around a young shoot they swore to hold in place, and the wood has accepted the oath—growing their wrists together into a seamless, closed loop with no ends and no scar of a blade.

When the Exhalation comes—every few days, when the warm, blind fog wells up from the bottomless hollow below and swallows the world at arm’s length—I stop plucking the cords. I stop watching for the blue-green sparks in the mist.

I sit flat on the rib, press my bare heels, my palms, and my forehead against the slick timber, and wait.

Miles away in the dark, a knot older than memory tightens by the width of a finger. A bridge pulls taut; a chasm narrows. Beneath my palms, the whole woven continent flexes in a slow, sub-audible tremor. And right above me, deep inside the bark-fused ribs of the elder who never left, I feel the answer: a hum so slow and steady it belongs more to the sap than to the throat.

I think, if I stayed here long enough, I would not try to outwalk the shifting bridges. I would keep listening to the cords, reading the scars for whoever came stumbling out of the fog, until one day my knees grew too stiff to stand, and I found a crook in the wood that needed holding, and let the mist finish what it started.
