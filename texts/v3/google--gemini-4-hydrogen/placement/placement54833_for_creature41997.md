I would not be down on the floor of the bowl, where the looms *thock* behind every doorway and the bedrock mortars grind from morning until the tallow lamps are lit. Nor would I be out on the lashed-timber catwalks before dawn, hanging over nothing at all while the mist-nets darken with the ghost of a valley’s breath.

I would be three-quarters of the way up the curving eastern wall, wedged into a shallow shelf just beneath the first overhang of the domes—high enough that the swallows’ nests are level with my knees, low enough that the pewter light still reaches me secondhand, bounced off the opposite cliffs and stained the color of smoked amber.

Here, the granite is always warm against the small of my back. A beard of dry, gray-green air-plants hangs across the mouth of my niche like a tattered curtain, filtering the rising heat and catching the fine grit that drifts down from the summit. If I lean forward, I can see the whole town stacked beneath me: four and five tiers of mortarless stone, the hemp bridges swaying slightly when a goat crosses, the tiny copper-bright punctuation of bells moving along the roofs. Above me, the switchbacks narrow to a finger’s width and vanish into the honeycomb hollows where nothing lives, and nothing rots.

I am a filer of the returned, and a mender of nets.

It is work for someone whose breath has grown too shallow for the heavy pestles, or whose knees have begun to click louder than their tongue. My fingers are thick at the joints, dusted pale as flour from the sandstone blocks I keep beside me in a hollowed-out caper-root bowl. My heels are horn; my skin is oiled with tallow until it drinks the ochre shadow of the wall. When I breathe, my ribs barely seem to move—a slow, deep tide, mouth closed, nostrils flared just enough to take the thin, dry air without spilling a drop of moisture back to the sky.

Most mornings, after the mist-tenders have wrung their catch and retreated from the outer glare, one of them climbs up to my shelf with a bundle of dark goat-hair netting slung over a shoulder. They do not speak when they duck beneath the air-plants. In the dimness of the overhang, a single low hum in the throat—*mmh*, pitched beneath the rattle of the cicadas—is enough to say *here, a tear near the weight-line*. They leave the net and a tiny bone cup of salty goat’s milk, or two tart, fleshy leaves snapped from a silver-jade rosette, and slide back down the soapstone steps before the silence between us even has to be filled.

I spread the net across my knees. To mend it, I use a needle I shaped six winters ago from the shin-bone of a Tally-Keeper who lived two doors below me—a quiet woman whose notched rods still hang in the store-room. When she was brought down from the hollows, light and dry as a fig-husk, her bones were dense and fine-grained, the kind that take a long, patient edge without splintering. I remember the feel of her under the sandstone file: the dry, warm whisper of grit against bone, the faint, clean scent of baked chalk, the way the curve of the shaft slowly forgot it had ever walked a switchback and remembered instead how to slip through woven hair.

That is my whole craft—helping things forget what they were so they can hold together what is here.

When the nets are mended and coiled, I turn back to the tray of unfinished pieces beside me. A child’s hairpin, whittled down from a splinter of rib, its tip rubbed smooth against a piece of slate so it won’t snag oiled hair. A heavy shuttle for one of the lower looms, still rough-hewn, waiting for the hours it will take to hollow its belly and polish its throat until the hemp thread runs through it like water. Everyone in the bowl knows the sound of my file—not loud enough to carry across the chasm, just a dry, steady *shhh-shhh* that blends into the cicadas and the scrape of lizard claws on the lintels.

Because I sit high above the alleys, I hear the town differently than the people inside it. Down below, the bowl is a drum, and sound smears against the stone. Up here, at the lip of the echo, the noise sorts itself out. I can tell by the rhythm of the *thock-thock* which weaver has a cracked beam in her loom. I can hear a Mason’s sharp tongue-click from three tiers down, calling for slate wedges, followed by the high, two-note swallow-whistle of an apprentice answering from a roof across the gap. Sometimes, when an old weaver’s ears have thickened and she can no longer catch the high whistles from the catwalks, I tap the message down to her along the living granite with the butt of my sandstone file—three slow knocks, a pause, two quick ones—letting the stone carry what the air would lose.

At dusk, the tallow lamps flare in the recessed niches below, turning the town into a cluster of warm embers trapped in dark pewter. The looms stop. The mortars fall still. My hands rest in my lap, gray with bone-dust, holding a half-finished needle that will wait until tomorrow.

Then the evening thermal begins to rise from the unseen plains miles beneath us. It brushes past the catwalks, slips through the thorns of the caper-bushes, and curls up under my overhang, lifting the dry beards of the air-plants against my cheek. It carries the phantom smell of wet earth—of rain falling somewhere so far away that it might as well be another sky.

I close my eyes, breathe it in slowly through my nose, and let it go just as carefully. Around me, the granite holds the day’s heat. Below me, the swallows settle into the eaves. I am one small, dry knot in the crust that keeps the mountain from being empty, and tomorrow there will be another net to mend.
