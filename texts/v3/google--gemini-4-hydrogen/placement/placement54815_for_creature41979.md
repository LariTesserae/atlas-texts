Before there is any thought of where I am, there is the hum in my sternum.

It is not a sound that arrives from outside. It lives in the marrow of my shins, rises through the flat soles of my feet, and settles behind my teeth like the aftertaste of iron. A slow, wet thudding—rolling, staggering, never quite stopping—that tells me the walls are still standing and the water is still moving.

I am wedged into the curve of the outermost step, knees drawn up to my chest, chin tucked into the hollow of my collarbone. Above me, the brick sweats. A bead of cold water gathers on the brittle rib of lime-scale beside my brow, hangs until it grows heavy, and drops onto my shoulder, where the mineral crust drinks it in and stiffens again. My back is ridged with the same pale salt that streaks the mortar. If anything looked down from the slatted oculus eighty paces across the mist, it would see only another slumped knuckle of chalk and wet clay.

That is where I would find myself: not at the center, where the light is clearest, and not in the upper vault with the birds, but at the seam where the floor begins to curve into the wall. The place where the water is no thicker than a film, and the room’s long memory presses directly against my spine.

If I had arrived here from somewhere else—from a dry world of wind and sharp edges and open sky—I would have been ruined in an hour. My ears would have split under the percussion of the jars; my skin would have cracked in the chill; my footsteps would have shattered the geometry of the floor. So I know, without having to decide it, that I belong to the wet dark. I am built for it.

When I unfold from the brick, my arms hang low, brushing the damp slate before my knees straighten. My fingers are wide and blunt, the skin pale grey and puckered in the creases like river-clay left too long in the shade. Behind my jaw, where another creature might have ears, there is only taut skin stretched over bone—a drumhead tuned to the low frequencies of the dome.

I do not look up to take the measure of the room. I look down, at the ankle-deep sheet of black water spreading from the step beneath my toes.

The booming of the amphibians raises a crisp, trembling lattice across the surface—thousands of tiny intersections of light and dark, shifting in a steady pulse. Every jar in the wall writes a line into that pattern. Every swift that drops from the oculus tears a sharp, fleeting *V* across it. I can read the whole eighty-pace basin without lifting my chin.

There—near the third step on the far side—the lattice blurs. A knot of milky eels has thickened around a snag at the drain, or perhaps a feather has caught in the notched stone. And beneath that, felt rather than seen, is a faint hitch in the rhythm against my ribs. A tiny gap in the rolling wave of drums. A jar has worked loose from its lime-scale bed, or a singer has grown old and stopped inflating its throat.

I step down into the water.

My feet are broad and flat, the toes spreading to feel the rounded lip of the river-slate. I move slowly, sliding my heels forward without lifting them, letting the eels brush against my ankles like cold, living ribbons. There is no hurry here; hurry makes waves, and waves blind the floor.

Halfway to the center, a sharp *tink-tink-tink* drops from the mist above.

My body knows the truce before my mind does. My knees buckle into a low, rounded crouch; my chin tucks; my wide hands press flat against the slate to anchor me. A shadow with sickle wings slices past my shoulder, close enough that the wind from its wingtips stirs the salt-dust on my brow. A faint splash, a silver flash of an eel snatched from the spiral, and the bird is already banking upward into the dimness. I wait until the *V* on the water smooths back into the lattice before I rise.

This is what I would be: not the singer in the niche, and not the hunter in the air, but the quiet, grey thing that keeps the interval between them intact.

At the central grate, I kneel in the deepest sheet of water. The cold settles into my hips, familiar and steadying. I reach down with both hands, feeling along the notched stone and the lashed bone. My fingers find the snag—a mat of drowned midges, a snapped thread of translucent fungus, and a sliver of shed swift-feather wedged tight against the current. I pull it free, roll it into a tight ball, and tuck it into the braided water-mold belt at my waist to carry up to the crustaceans on the top step.

Beneath my palm, one of the bone lashings has gone soft and spongy with rot. I draw a small, notched flake of stone from my belt, saw through the ruined sinew, and strip it away. From the same belt I take a length of cured eel-skin, tough and pale, and begin to wrap the joint anew. No knots—knots catch the current and gather silt. Only tight, overlapping turns that I pull taut with my blunt thumbs, knowing the mineral water will stiffen them into stone-hard bands by the time the mist turns over.

When the grate runs clear again, the spiral of eels tightens and smooths. I wade back toward the perimeter, waist-deep in the cool mist that clings to the brick, following the stutter in my ribs until I stand before the quiet niche.

The jar is tilted a finger’s breadth out of its lime-scale bed. Inside, the pale amphibian is still, its blind eyes milky, the silver strands of water-mold hanging limp from its flank. I reach in carefully, draw the small body out, and lay it across my forearm to carry to the steps. Then I scoop a handful of grey silt from the trap at my feet and pack it wetly around the rim of the clay jar, pressing it home with my thumbs until the rattle vanishes from its throat and the wall takes its weight again.

An empty jar does not stay empty for long. Soon a younger singer will climb from the water’s edge, wedge itself into the narrow neck, and add its pulse back to the dome.

I am neither the elder—whose brow is already heavy with thick, branching shelves of mineral crust, and whose movements have slowed to the pace of the seepage—nor am I entirely new. I am simply the one currently awake to the work. In a place where nothing is made of metal and nothing stays dry, balance is not a state of rest; it is a continuous, unhurried mending.

When the work is done, I scoop a handful of small, pale crustaceans from a mortar crevice and chew them slowly, followed by a strip of dark-olive liverwort peeled from the brick. My tongue tastes of wet chalk and cold stone.

Then I wade back to the outermost step, wedge my salt-crusted back into the curve of the wall, and tuck my chin. Around me, the jars boom in their long, unbroken cycle. Beneath my feet, the standing ripples hold their sharp, shimmering geometry. I close my milky eyes, let the mist settle over my shoulders, and listen with my bones to the room keeping time.
