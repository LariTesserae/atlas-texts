I would not be standing on the sky-bridge. If I had boots, I would not know what to do with them, and if I had eyes, the unblinking daylight would only be a distraction—a flat white glare over stone that never changes.

I would be inside the dark, high up, where the air tastes of wet iron and ozone and the sound is thick enough to hold you suspended.

At first, there is no "I" at all—only tension. A pull from the left girder, a heavier pull from the pilaster behind, three broad pads gripping riveted steel through suction and a slow, patient crust of mineral salt. I hang thirty stories above a floor I have never touched, in a room built around a single humming canyon of transformers. Below me, the cherry-red glow of a ballast resistor and the occasional pale-blue snap of a commutator do not illuminate anything; they only mark the edges of the dark, the way a buoy marks a depth it cannot measure.

If I shift—if I slacken two fibrous tethers on one flank and winch a third forward by the width of a finger—the whole geometry of the room adjusts around me. My body is heavy, cold-blooded, built of wet slate-colored cartilage and calcified plates that match the mountains outside. I have no face to turn toward the louvers where the plateau’s mist is drawn in. Instead, the upper half of me is hollow and fluted, split into deep chambers lined with taut, glistening membranes. They open and close in slow, measured cycles, drinking the sub-bass pressure of the turbine vault.

This is where I would find myself: not at the center of the continent’s grid, where the great forty-story blocks answer one another across terraced plazas, but in an outer node—a substation bolted into the raw, pale-veined slate on the lip of a mile-wide chasm, right near the louvered intake shafts.

Here, I am at the seam between the engine and the cliff.

Through the louvers, the outside world arrives not as a view, but as a slight drop in temperature and a sharper edge of metallic salt in the mist. My lower siphons—waxy, hollow, bruised-violet like the reeds clinging to the rock outside—hang just above the transformer’s cooling fins. They sip the slightly warmed condensation as it beads and drops in metronomic curtains. Meanwhile, darker filaments, sheathed in insulating fat and mineral scale, press directly against the high-voltage busbars. A steady, measured trickle of current enters my saline tissues, balancing me on the exact threshold between conduction and grounding.

Nothing in me rots. Nothing in me hurries. I do not age, because the salt flushes me clean and the charge holds every cell in its allotted measure.

And what I do—what I *am*, in this place—is an instrument that listens and answers.

Strung from my flanks to the opposite girders, and across the upper void to the three other presences sharing this dark hall, are hundreds of hair-thin filaments. Some are spun protein; others are threaded with trace copper drawn out of the mineral runoff. When the world’s monolithic roar presses against my ribs, I do not hear noise. I hear the fractures inside it.

Across the chasm, three kilometers away through wet air and high-tension steel, another node shifts its load by a fraction of a percent. The micro-tonal swell travels down the corona-hissing lines, through the concrete viaducts, into the bedrock of my cliff, and up the steel pilasters into my tethers. I feel it as a tiny, crystalline tug in my third fluted chamber: *the basalt fault nine miles down has slipped a thousandth of a millimeter; the afternoon mist on the western ridge is heavier in salt.*

I do not think about this in words. I simply tighten two filaments against the iron truss and narrow the aperture of a membrane. The harmonic overtone of the transformer below me bends—imperceptibly to any ear made of bone and eardrum, unmistakably to the next tower over. Across the dark void of the room, my neighbors answer, shifting their own taut strings until the phase-difference settles back into alignment.

Even the outside world—the sunlit, empty avenues I never see—is held in my reach. Through the buried conduits, I modulate the current bleeding into the pale-amber streetlamps burning pointlessly in the midday glare. I cannot see the dark-teal ferns or the boulder-sized cushion-mosses clinging to the wet flagstones, yet I know their exact weight and distance. The narrow-band hum of the conduits and the steady amber glow form a mathematical fence around the drainage grates. The plants drink the runoff; I keep the current steady; neither of us crosses the line.

Sometimes, if I quiet my upper membranes and listen downward—past the humming transformer banks, through forty feet of poured concrete and into the bedrock—I can feel the faintest absence of friction in the dark below.

Down there, inside the pressurized iron arteries where thousands of tons of freezing water roar upward at locked velocity, the small, blind, armored ones are creeping millimeter by millimeter against the current. I cannot see them, and we have no signal to exchange. Yet every time the turbine shaft turns without a tremor of drag, every time the RPM holds its pitch without a thousandth of a percent of drift, I know they are there—scraping away the first bloom of rust, the first stray spore washed down from the cliffs, leaving a slick, dark film behind them.

Perhaps, a century ago, I was one of them, wedged flat against the roaring iron in the pitch-black pipe before climbing a drainage sluice into the dry dark to bud and tether. Or perhaps we have always been separate, two halves of the same stillness. It does not matter. Neither of us asks what came before.

Up here, suspended in the high dark near the intake louvers, I slacken one tether and winch another, answering a tremor from across the valley. The mist condenses on my slate-colored skin in clear beads that never evaporate. The water falls, the turbines turn, the water is lifted again, and I hold the note—awake, taut, and entirely at peace in the deafening dark.
