You would not find me in the orange pools beneath the caged bulbs, nor down at the sluice-gates where the waterwheels dip their mossy paddles. Look higher, and further from the light—where the perimeter masonry gives way to raw, weeping limestone, and a narrow catwalk of grated iron juts out over the empty dark.

I am folded into the angle where a heavy porcelain insulator anchors a tar-wrapped cable to the rock. If you passed along the walkway with a lantern, you might take me for a bundle of salvaged canvas left behind by a crew that vanished a century ago, or a particularly thick growth of travertine and moss crusting the brick pier. I do not shift when the air moves. I have learned, over however many decades make up a life here, that stillness is the only way to hear what the cavern is actually saying.

At first, if I try to tell you what I am, there is only the listening. Not with ears—sound in the open air is thin, scattered by the vast hollow of the vault—but through the soles of my wide, calloused feet, through my knees pressed against the damp iron grate, through the fine, gill-like creases along my neck that quiver when the DC current rises in the cable beside my shoulder. 

I am one of the quiet ones. One of the fifteen or twenty who belong to the damp and the slow iron.

Where others of my kind spend their long hours down on the two central islands, leaning shoulder-to-shoulder against the warm brick housings of the hydraulic accumulators, I prefer the span. I like the place where the structures cling to the cliff and the open, cold dark begins. Here, the air tastes sharpest of wet limestone and ozone. Here, you can feel the whole architecture of the hollow at once, the way a spider feels the tension of its web—not by seeing the far corners, which are lost in shadow anyway, but by resting two verdigris-stained fingers lightly on the iron railing and reading the tremors that travel fifty yards through the riveted girders.

Beneath my brow, there are only smooth, shallow depressions of pale skin. I have no use for the amber bulbs, except that their small warmth draws the pale-shelled arthropods, and where the arthropods gather, the seals stay tight. My face is broad and flat, built to drink the air: wide nostrils flaring slowly to measure the balance of machine oil and weeping mineral water. When a relief valve lets go three terraces below—a soft, grease-muffled *hiss*—I know by the scent that follows whether the packing is holding or beginning to dry.

Over my back and shoulders, my shell has grown thick. Years ago, when I was still translucent and curled in a bed of dry lichen behind the bricked archways, I had only my soft, tallow-colored skin. Now I wear a patient accumulation: mats of dark thread-moss that drink the cavern’s mist, a scrap of tarred felt I pressed onto my shoulder blade when I was smaller, and broad, overlapping plates of the arthropods’ pearlescent wax, cured to a supple, waterproof armor. It creaks faintly, like damp leather, when I uncoil my spine to reach a high pipe-joint.

That is mostly what I am: a keeper of joints and quiet spans.

Not an operator. I do not know what the numbered brass plaques nailed to the catwalk mean, though I know the raised bumps of their numerals by touch, the way I know the different textures of velvety rust and lime-crust. I do not know why the copper-wound dynamos were lowered into this sunken cathedral, or who first strung the cables from pier to pier. To ask *why* the counterweighted pistons rise and fall at one stroke every few hours would be like asking why groundwater weeps down the mineral curtains. It simply does. It is the weather.

My work—if you can call something so unhurried by that name—is to keep the weather from catching on itself.

Just now, through the iron beneath my knees, there is a slight irregularity. Not a break, nothing so dramatic as a fault, just a thin, dry whisper buried deep inside the sub-audible hum of the eastern dynamo. Most of the others wouldn't notice it from this distance; it is masked by the steady *plink-plink* of drops hitting a tin pump-house roof below. But out here on the perimeter catwalk, the cable carries the vibration cleanly.

I uncurl, slow as a rising piston, keeping my spine curved in the deep crouch that keeps my center of gravity low on the slick grates. My fingers trail the railing as I move along the wall. Halfway to the junction box, I pass a glider hanging upside down from an overhead pipe-run like a sodden grey rag. As my shoulder brushes past, the faint electrostatic prickle lifting the pale hairs on my forearm seems to stir it; it shifts one long-limbed digit, tucks its wide membrane closer, and settles back into sleep.

At the junction, the cast-iron casing is furred with grey-green lichen and warm to the palm. The bearing just below it is weeping a little too briskly—not oil, which is dark and heavy, but thin mineral water cutting a channel through the grease. Left alone, the bearing will score, and the whisper in the iron will sharpen into a scrape, and the scrape will travel through the girders until it unsettles the whole hollow.

I reach up to the warm porcelain insulator above my head. Three palm-sized arthropods are grazing there on the condensate, their pale shells slick with fresh wax. I lift two of them—not prying, just sliding a calloused thumb gently beneath their edges until they release—and set them down beside the weeping seam. 

Then I wait. 

Time here is not a line; it is a basin that fills and spills. I fold myself down against the warm iron of the transformer housing, my knees tucked to my chest, my hands resting flat on the grates. An hour passes, or three. Down in the black water, the dynamos pulse, and for a few heartbeats a bruised-violet glow blooms beneath the brick piers, outlining the slow-turning knots of ribbon-like swimmers before the ink swallows them again. A glider detaches from the upper trusses—a silent, pale triangle drifting across the wide, cold air between the perimeter and the central island—and lands without a sound on a tin roof below.

Beside my shoulder, the arthropods begin to move. Their tiny mouthparts scrape softly at the mineral crust; a faint, sweet smell of curing wax rises to my nostrils. The thin whisper in the bearing softens, thickens, and folds back into the deep, velvety hum of the grid.

Somewhere across the dark, fifty yards away on the masonry island, a knuckle taps twice against a catwalk girder. *Tink-tink.* A dull, metallic pulse that travels through the iron straight into my collarbone. Not an alarm, not a word—just an acknowledgment. Someone else is awake, leaning against a warm brick pier, feeling the same steady stroke of the pistons.

I lift one hand and tap my knuckle once against the railing in answer. *Tink.*

If you came looking for me, you would have to stand very still, and let your lantern burn low, and wait until your eyes gave up trying to measure the dark. Only then, when you began to feel the hum of the dynamos in the soles of your boots, might you notice that the heap of moss and tarred felt beside the junction box is breathing—slowly, deeply, in exact time with the cavern’s waterwheels.
