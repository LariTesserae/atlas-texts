I would not arrive here. There is no path up from the lowlands below the rim, and nothing in this valley knows how to hurry toward a destination. If I am here, I have always been here—or I have been here long enough that the distinction no longer has edges.

At first, I am only the warmth on the dark stone.

It takes a long time to notice where the basalt ends and I begin. My back is turned to the open sky, soaking in the flat, unfiltered brightness of the middle hours, and my palms are turned upward beside my hips. The heat does not prickle or burn; it sinks in slowly, a steady current moving through matte grey skin and dark-slated shoulders, down into the quiet architecture of bone. Around me, the air is heavy and still, smelling of wet dust, crushed fern-stem, and the clean, sharp tang of newly cleaved rock.

I am one of the valley’s own—though I do not think of myself as *one* of anything, any more than a flagstone thinks of itself as a member of a causeway. I am tall, spare, built to the same unhurried geometry as the terrace beneath me. When I finally draw my knees up and sit, it is not because I am restless, only because the light has shifted by the width of a hand and the shadow of the pavilion’s lintel has reached my ankles.

Where I find myself is not the newest reach of the city, where the young ones are still tapping out fresh causeways across the reed-choked flats. Nor am I at the very foot of the weeping cliffs, where the first broad basins catch the condensation straight from the rock. I am a few terraces below the source, in a neighborhood that was finished before the moss had thickened in its seams.

The dwellings here are low, flat-roofed, and empty, their dry-stacked walls crisp and pale-mortared, untouched by wind or wear. No one has slept on these roofs or leaned against these doorframes in years, yet nothing has fallen into ruin. The silence in the rooms is not abandonment; it is simply space that is no longer needed, left intact like a bowl set neatly on a shelf.

My place is at the corner of a square settling-tank, where a shallow sluiceway brings water down from the upper steps and lets it spill over a dressed basalt lip into a wider, shallower basin.

I sit with my broad, flat-soled feet resting squarely on the dry flagstone, careful not to brush the bright green cushions of moss swelling along the mortared joints. My hands rest on my knees. The skin of my fingertips has long since thickened into smooth, horn-hard pads from decades—or centuries—of lifting slabs and coaxing stone into level planes.

If you were standing across the plaza, you would take me for a weathered corner-post, or a slab left leaning against the tank’s rim. Even my eyes—dark, glossy, set wide beneath low ridges of bone—hold the same flat reflection as the water beside me. When the translucent lid draws slowly across them, it is scarcely a movement at all.

What do I do here? Nothing that would look like work to a creature that breathes fast and tears its food.

Mostly, I watch the curtain of water.

It is thinner than a finger’s breadth, sliding over the stone lip without a ripple or a sound, keeping the face of the spillway permanently dark. Where it meets the surface of the lower basin, it scarcely disturbs the mirror-flat skin. Now and then, a single bead of moisture gathers on the underside of an overhanging lintel behind me, swells until its weight overcomes the stone, and drops into the tank.

*Plink.*

The small ring travels outward across the square of water, touches the four dressed walls, and dies. I do not turn my head toward the sound; I simply let it pass through the quiet, the way a thought might pass through a mind that has no words to trap it.

Yet there is a quiet purpose to my sitting here. Over seasons, the warm seepage leaves behind a pale crust of minerals along the lip of the spillway. Left alone, that crust would thicken unevenly, catching the sheet of water and splitting the unbroken curtain into ragged, chattering threads. And on the damp margin to my left, a broad-leafed fern has been uncurling its new fronds toward the sluiceway, drawn by the steady mist.

So, in the long, windless afternoon, I lean forward—slowly, so the air barely stirs around my shoulders.

With the horn-hard pad of my thumb, I rub gently along the mineral buildup on the stone lip, taking an hour to smooth away what a hurried creature would scrape off in a breath. I do not force it; I simply remind the stone of its levelness until the water sheets cleanly again.

Then I turn to the fern. I do not snap the stalk or bruise the waxy green stem. Instead, I reach into the shallow water beside the causeway, lift two small, flat shards of loose basalt, and set them dry-stacked against the margin of the channel—a tiny, patient curb. I coax the heavy frond aside until its underside rests against the cool rock, guiding its next season of growth out over the open wet flats rather than across the sluice.

When I settle back onto my dry slab, I am not alone, though no one has spoken.

Across the glassy expanse of the lower basin, on the stepped plaza of the next terrace down, two others sit beneath an open-walled pavilion. They are facing the same spillway I am. One has its pale hand resting lightly on the other’s slate-dark forearm. Neither of them looks up at me, and I do not need them to; the angle of our shoulders, the shared stillness of our knees, the simple fact that we are all holding our places along the same level of water is conversation enough.

Near my hip lies a palm-sized nodule of hard, dark stone. In a little while—not yet, perhaps when the light begins to soften toward evening—I will pick it up and begin to peck a shallow notch into the corner of the coping-stone, to ease a pocket where the condensation has begun to pool unevenly.

*Tap.*

A pause long enough for three drops to gather and fall from the overhang.

*Tap.*

The sound folds easily into the valley’s balance. I am neither master of the water nor a stranger passing through it. I am simply the quiet hinge between the stone that holds and the water that rests—patient, mineral-slow, and entirely at home in the long, unbroken afternoon.
