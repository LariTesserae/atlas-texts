I wouldn’t arrive here with eyes. Even if I brought them, the mercury-vapor lamps are spaced too far apart to stitch the dark together, and the mist off the brine would blind them anyway.

If I am in this world, I know it first through the soles of my feet—flat, broad, calloused pads pressed into the permanent film of black water on the basalt tiles. The cold comes up through them, just above freezing, and right behind the cold comes the *thrum*. It is not a sound so much as a floor-plan written in vibration: the deep, steady weight of the turbine shafts below, the higher, thinner whine of the exhaust fans overhead, and between them, a thousand smaller rhythms. A pneumatic valve cycling *clack-hiss* along a manifold three hundred yards away. The hollow, rhythmic *boom* of water dropping down a vertical concrete chute.

I find myself low to the ground, heavy-shouldered, built so the draft off the cooling fans slides right over my back. My skin is wet and gray as the poured floor, and along my spine and shoulders I carry a jagged, brittle ridge of salt and lime—months of pressing against a weeping pipe-flange in the dark until the basin’s own minerals grew into my hide.

Where am I? Not wedged in the sunken trench around the six massive heat-exchangers, where the air is thickest with steam and the larger ones crowd the warm steel. And not high on the three-story transformer tower, draped across the porcelain insulators where the little leathery-winged things roost behind the baffles.

I am out in the long, dim interval between the stanchions, along an unrailed causeway that borders a flooded sump. It is a quieter post, if anything in an eight-mile echo chamber can be called quiet. My territory is anchored by a single squat pump-housing bolted to the concrete apron—not one of the great organs of the basin, just a secondary motor that keeps the ankle-deep runoff moving toward the central drains. It puts out just enough warmth to keep my belly from stiffening, and a single dull amber pilot light on its control cabinet radiates a faint, steady prickle against the sensitive hollows of my blunt face.

I spend the slack hours between the tides pressed against its grease-slicked casing, letting the heat soak into my ribs. My barbels twitch in the electrical weather—the sharp, dry snap of a relay closing somewhere to the west, the heavy ozone weight of the transformer tower a quarter-mile off. When the motor’s intake fan develops a slight, off-beat rattle, I feel it in my jaw before I hear it. It bothers me the way a pebble in a shoe would bother something that wore shoes.

So I shift my weight, brace my heavy climbing-pads on the wet grating, and reach underneath myself with the pale, jointed inner digits tucked beneath my outer hooks. They are surprisingly small and nimble for a body the size of a cable-drum. I slide them past the louvers of the motor housing into the warm, greasy dark. Inside, the brittle, orange-brown coral of the iron-eating bacteria has sprouted overnight along the vents. I pinch off the softest, freshest buds to eat, then carefully scrape the dead, crusty scale away from the fan blades and intake screen until the metal is bright again and the rattle smooths back into a clean, even hum.

Then the pressure shifts.

No klaxon has sounded yet, and the eastern sluice-gates haven’t moved, but a faint, high-frequency chatter begins to run down the vertical concrete chutes. The basin is drawing a breath.

I peel myself off the warm pump-housing. Every joint in my calcified back clicks softly in the damp. I wade out off the concrete apron onto the iron floor-grating of the causeway, where the runoff stands ankle-deep, and turn my blunt, eyeless wedge of a head toward the east. I drive the dark, horn-like hooks of my feet down through the slots of the grate and lock them tight against the iron ribs.

Seconds later, the two-tone klaxons groan across the empty flats, flat and cavernous, their echoes bouncing off the crisp new concrete of the retaining walls. The gates grind upward.

When the cold gray brine hits, it doesn’t ask permission. It sweeps across the terraces in a foaming, roaring sheet, burying my legs, surging up over my salted shoulders, slamming against the jagged crust of my spine. For twenty minutes, there is nothing in the world except the weight of the river and the grip of my hooks in the grating. I open the slatted rakers along my flanks and let the basin feed me—pale, blind amphipods swept out of the upper pipes, torn ribbons of translucent kelp ripped from the undersides of the condensation trays. The water is numbing, sharp with salt, loud enough to drown out even the deep turbines.

Just as suddenly, the gates slam shut. The river drops away through the slotted floors, leaving only the steady patter of water dripping from the overhead trusses and the thick banks of cold mist rising off the tiles. The exhaust fans kick up a pitch, pulling the air clear.

I unlock my hooks from the grate. Out on the wet basalt, a palm-sized sump-crab is already clattering across the metal, scavenging the dead bacterial mats washed out of the drains. I cross the causeway, pin it under one heavy pad, and pry its flat shell open with a sharp *crack* that rings off the far walls for three full seconds before dissolving back into the roar of the blowers.

While I am still standing by the yellow safety bollard at the edge of the sump, a sound cuts through the industrial weather from half a mile away—a clean, dry *tink-tink... tink*. Someone out near the heat-exchangers, striking an iron catwalk with a calcified knuckle.

I don’t hurry. I finish checking the pump-housing to make sure the flood didn’t pack any loose kelp against the intake screen. The amber pilot light is still glowing, warm and steady against my face. The motor hums cleanly.

When I am satisfied, I lift one heavy forelimb, set my salted knuckle against the wet steel of the yellow bollard, and answer with two deliberate taps. The sound leaps off the bare concrete and travels out into the dark, letting whoever is listening know that the tide has passed, my corner of the grate is clear, and the little motor is still running warm.
