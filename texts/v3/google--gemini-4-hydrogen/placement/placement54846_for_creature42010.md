I would not be in the open light of the apron, where the sky presses flat and bright against the stone. And I would not be one of the climbers, carrying blood and breath up through the cloud-floor only to descend again with a bundle of brittle plates on my back.

If I found myself in this world, I would be wedged in the dark, wet seam beneath the great cushion of liverwort on the back wall.

Not a glassy pane in the open pools, creeping after split bladder-sacs at the pace of a stain, but one of the thick ones—the ones that lose their transparency in the shade and swell into something pillowy and slow, veined with rose and slate-gray where the minerals have settled into my flesh. I would have no eyes to register the weight of the noon sun, and no need to move across the floor. My whole world would be the narrow, feverish cradle between two surfaces: the hot, unyielding slate beneath my belly, and the dense, velvet underside of the moss pressing down upon my back.

In that dark, everything is known by pressure and temperature.

The stone below me is steady, radiating a deep, quiet heat that never hurries. It warms the water I hold inside myself until it rises in slow, silent plumes toward my upper skin, where the moss cools it just enough to make it sink again. That is my circulation. No heart, no pulse of blood—only a gentle, continuous turning of water, driven by the difference between the mountain’s fever and the shade of the liverwort. Every few minutes, I swell by a hair’s breadth, drawing in the runoff that filters down through the moss, and then I settle, sweating that warmed, mineral-heavy water back up into the fine, pale threads of the plant’s underside.

I would be, in a quiet way, the shelf’s hidden lung—breathing water instead of air, warming what falls from above so the great green cushion above me can keep its velvet thickness without ever touching the dry sky.

Time here would not be measured in days or seasons. It would be measured in the slow, mineral taste of the runoff, and in the vibrations that travel through the bone of the ridge. I would feel the distant, deep groan of the mountain cooling at night—a long, low shudder against my belly—and, yards away across the wet stone, the tiny, crisp *pop* of a bladder-sac splitting on one of the tall plum-and-jade stalks. I wouldn't see the cloudy wash of spores gliding across the apron, or the glassy pool-dwellers rolling their edges inward to catch them, but I would feel the faint tremor of the water shifting, and hours later, a stray spore might wash down the back wall, filtered through the moss, to settle in my own pale threads as a new knot of amber.

And then, every few years, the rhythm of the stone would change.

Long before a shadow could fall across the pools, I would feel it through my belly: a dull, spaced-out *thud… thud… thud*, entirely different from the hum of thermal drift or the groan of shifting rock. A thing with feet. A thing with weight that lifts and drops.

When that vibration reaches the cradle beneath the liverwort, I go still. I stop my slow, minute-by-minute swelling and hold the water inside me tight, stiffening my skin against the rock. Above me, the moss shifts slightly as the air outside is stirred by a moving body. I feel the faint, sharp *clink* of stone scraping stone out on the apron—the flat scraper prying up an abandoned, pearlescent saucer from one of the shallow pans—and the dry, rhythmic *grind* of a round pebble deepening a catch-basin.

Then the footsteps come closer, though they never step on the moss.

I feel the presence just outside the shadow of the cushion. A hand—warm in a different, quicker way than the slate—reaches up to the vertical fissure above my head. There is a sharp, sudden *tap*, and then another, ringing straight down through the rock into my belly: the knapped basalt wedge being driven a fraction of an inch deeper into the seam.

The climber leaves as quietly as they came. The footsteps retreat across the apron, step off the lip of the shelf, and vanish into the clouds below.

For a long time after, I stay still, letting the silence settle back into the stone. Then, from the fissure above, a single, slightly wider thread of weeping water finds the newly angled edge of the basalt wedge. It beads on the underside of the liverwort, cools, and drops straight onto my back.

I relax my margins, draw the fresh water in, and begin again—expanding by a hair’s breadth, warming it against the feverish slate, and sweating it back into the dark, patient roots above.
