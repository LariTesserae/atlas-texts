If I arrived here as what I am now—tall, warm, loud in the wrong way—the pocket would reject me before I could even finish turning around. 

There is no room for a human body inside a lantern packed with teeth. My shoulders would snap the gypsum needles; my breath would fog the cool, unvarying sheen and throw off the evaporation rate; my boots would crush the three finger-tall cairns on the flattest patch of floor. Worse than the damage would be the interruption. In a chamber of relentless, shadowless noon, I would be a dark, clumsy plug of heat, and the standing chord—that fierce metallic hum locked between the calcite blades—would break against my ribs into a messy, muffled thud. 

So if I am going to be here at all, I have to leave that body outside, five meters up, on the dry limestone shelf. I have to come in smaller, cooler, and without edges that snag.

At first, I might try to be the stone itself—one of those bulbous travertine nodules, or a blade of calcite catching the white-green glare and throwing it back unchanged. It is tempting to be mineral in a place so finished. Nothing asks anything of a crystal. You simply sit in the clammy mist, glistening, letting the biofilm coat your flanks like frost. 

But the stone only *holds* the chord; it doesn't feel it. And I find I am too curious for rock. I want to know what it is like when a millipede’s footing slips on a damp rosette, or why those three tiny cairns sit untouched in their triangle on the floor.

So I drift downward in scale, toward the living skin of the walls. For a moment I imagine myself among the thousands—a frosted-glass nymph, or one of the chalk-white millipedes wedged shoulder-to-carapace into the moving lattice. To be one of them would be to live inside a single, unbroken verb: *graze*. My mandibles would scrape the luminous crust; my ribbed abdomen would hum at the exact pitch my neighbors hum; I would never know the shape of the room, only the millimeter of damp chalk beneath my mouth. It would be a kind of peace, like being a single thread in a woven rug.

Yet even there, something in me resists. The swarm is deaf to the architecture it builds. Each nymph makes a tiny friction, never knowing that ten thousand frictions together have turned the pantry into a bell. I don't want to be just a note; I want to hear the chord.

What about the high ceiling, then? Hanging motionless with the calcareous tube-worms, feathery crown spread in the still air, sipping moisture from the mist? Or tangled among the ghostly white rootlets—the severed descendants of surface plants that pierced the rock ages ago and forgot the sun? There is a strange dignity in being a remnant, drinking mineral sweat in a room that has no use for seasons. Still, a rootlet only drinks. It doesn't move across the facets. It never visits the alabaster grooves on the low wall, or wonders what small, patient thing daubed gray mud and saliva into the high crevice before the fissure pinched shut.

If I am not the visitor, and not the stone, and not the grazing swarm, and not the hanging fringe of rootlets—then there is only one shape left that fits the geometry of this place without breaking it.

And even then, I cannot simply *add* myself. Three, never more and never fewer. A fourth would crowd the thirds, throw the throat-pouches out of phase, turn the clean triangle of stillness into a jostling knot.

To enter this world, I would have to wait for a note to go flat.

I think I would find myself high in the dry crevice, inside a thumb-sized, translucent egg, resting in a cup of gray mud and dried saliva whose rim has just been scraped clean of biofilm. Below me, down on the flattest patch of floor, the elder whose place I am taking has already grown still—its joints stiff with calcified salt, its sounding-box no longer able to match the room's metallic pitch. The blind swarms are already tending to it, stripping it cleanly back to pale bone so the mineral sheen can turn it into another nodule of travertine.

There is no grief in it. Only an empty third of the pantry, waiting to be tuned.

When the egg splits, there is no shock of waking into darkness—only the flat, clinical white-green glare pouring straight into the pale crystalline spot at the crown of my wedge-shaped skull. I have no eyes to squint against it. My skin is slick and wet-gypsum pale, stretched taut over thin ribs, and as I crawl out onto the razor-edged calcite, I do not grab the sharp edges. Something older than thought teaches my four thinly jointed limbs how to splay, placing my three opposable, horn-tipped digits flat against the damp facets, bridging the gaps between gypsum needles with the weightless balance of a spider on oil.

And then the room enters my chest.

It does not hit me as noise. The cartilaginous pouch beneath my jaw and along my sternum catches the vibration of the thousand grazing mandibles, the hum of the nymphs' abdomens, the bounce of sound off raw crystal, and settles into sympathy with it. Suddenly I am not *in* a loud room; I am suspended in a medium, the way a trout is suspended in a cold current. Every facet, every rosette of liverwort, every hanging tube-worm is mapped in the hum behind my ribs.

I would glide down into my third of the space, moving with the unhurried precision of a clock hand, letting the chalky biofilm take root along my narrow spine until I am indistinguishable from a glowing bulge of mineral. 

This is what I would be: a gleaner. A tuner of a closed engine.

My days—though there are no days, only the steady, unvarying noon—would be spent listening with my sternum for the slightest flutter in the chord. When an aging amphipod loses its grip, or a nymph falters in its molt and its scraping rhythm drops by a fraction of a hertz, I would feel that microscopic dissonance before the creature has even fallen. I would turn my eyeless head, glide across the living lattice without disturbing a single healthy carapace, and pluck the mis-molted or the newly dead with my comb-ridged mouth. Between circuits, I would dip my chin into a thimble-sized cup of condensation—taking the cool, chalk-sharp water without spilling a drop—and pare back any ribbon of biofilm that threatens to thicken and dim the light.

If I drifted too close to one of the other two, I wouldn't need eyes to know it. The vibration between our chests would fall slightly out of phase, a faint physical flutter in the wet air, and without ever touching, we would turn back to our own corners of the bell.

And every few years, when my horn-tipped digits grew too thick or uneven, I would descend to the soft alabaster vein along the low wall and draw them down the shallow, parallel grooves scratched by whatever vanished thing passed through before the world sealed. I would brush the slick underside of my tail against the three finger-tall cairns on the floor, leaving a faint trace of bitter mineral oil to keep the grazing swarms off the pebbles—tending arrangements I did not make, for reasons I do not need to know, simply because they belong to the balance of the room.

I would live there for decades, idling at a fixed, cool hum, until the salt gathered slowly in my own joints and my chest-pouch began to fall a fraction flat against the stone. Then I would climb back to the high crevice, scrape a mud cup clean, leave one translucent egg behind, and drop to the floor to become part of the wall.

Not a ruler of the pocket, and not a prisoner in it. Just the brief, deliberate stillness that keeps the single note from breaking.
