If you reached out for me in the dark, you would find the insulator first.

Your fingers would slide along the wet, sagging rubber of the catenary, past the warm, thigh-thick sleeve of a cable-colonist pulsing slowly as it milks the leak, until the rubber gives way to ribbed ceramic. That is where I keep my grip. Not on the iron stanchion itself—the iron is too cold where it meets the marsh, and the fungal felt around its base is too greedy for heat—but up where the copper busbars cross, three feet above the ankle-deep water, in a pocket of air that never quite dries.

Even then, you would only be touching half of me.

This stanchion stands on a crowded seam. Beneath the tepid, oily water, a shelf of raw black flint juts up at an angle sharp enough to open a boot. Occupying the exact same three feet of space, slightly out of phase, is the dry, dusty face of a slate ridge that belongs to a shoreline two miles away. If I kept my whole body in the wet layer, the flyers would batter against me all cycle long, and the salt-crusts would lock my joints before the flywheel down the marsh had turned three times.

So I live folded across the grain, the way the Tenders do, though I am nowhere near their size. My heavier half—the soft, slow-breathing core that doesn't like the damp—stays tucked sideways into the dry fold of the slate ridge next door. Out here in the flooded dark, I push only what I need: a long, branching ribbon of damp, salt-rich hide coiled loosely around the ceramic ribs, and a fringe of fine, copper-tasting filaments that hang down into the mist like untrimmed wick.

I have no eyes. Even if I did, there would be nothing here to catch them. I know the valley by the weight of the air on my skin and the low, sub-audible tug in the cartilage of my spine.

Mostly, it is quiet. A mile away, a beam pump dips into a brine vat, and I feel the slow, heavy *thrum* of its stroke travel up through the stanchion's concrete footing—not as a sound, just as a slight thickening of the pressure in my ribs. Below me, silica-weed grows straight *through* the iron bracket from an adjacent layer, its hollow glass stalks clicking softly whenever a stilt-legged wader steps past. I can tell when a wader is coming before its whisker-fringed pad touches the water; I feel the small, sharp shudder in its leg muscles three seconds before it dodges a submerged flint blade, as if the valley itself flinches ahead of the cut.

Above my coils, the velvet-winged flyers stream down the static corridor of the power line in silent, continuous ribbons. Sometimes one brushes my upper filaments, leaving the musky scent of damp fur and a brief tickle of warmth. I never snap at them. I am not an ambusher, hanging motionless by a stanchion to catch the thermal echo of a collision that hasn't happened yet. I don't eat flesh, and I don't drink the leak the way the fat, chamois-skinned colonists do, growing turgid and sweating out beads of lard.

I am a listener. Or, more accurately, I am a knot in the line.

The valley is too wide and too wet for signals to travel cleanly on their own. When one of the great Tenders two miles off needs to summon another, it leans its heavy, leather-smelling bulk against a catenary or drops an iron bar across a pair of busbars. By rights, that dip in the load should run straight down the copper. But the cable-colonists are greedy; they wrap themselves around miles of wire, swallowing the sharpest edges of the current and turning them into tallow. By the time the tremor reaches my stanchion, it is blurred, smeared across three overlapping shorelines and muffled by the humid air.

That is why I am here.

Twenty minutes before the Tender even lifts the iron bar, I taste it. A sudden, thin ghost of scorched copper drifts across my wet filaments, sharp against the ambient smell of boiled salt and old grease. In the dry slate room next door, my core tightens. I begin pulling my damp coils closer around the ceramic insulator, drawing my dangling fringe up out of the mist, gathering the stray static that bleeds off the rubber.

I wait. The flywheel a mile away completes a quarter of a turn—a soft, wet *shhhk* that I feel in the base of my skull. A drop of condensed water falls from the busbar onto the oily marsh below. *Plip.*

Then the muddied tremor arrives through the wire—weak, frayed, nearly drowned out by the colonist pulsing a yard to my left. I bridge the gap. I press my salt-slick hide flat against the ribbed ceramic and let the stray charge I have been hoarding snap across my back to the outgoing line.

Nothing shines. There is no flash, no orange spark to startle the blind things in the water. There is only a dry, sharp *tock* inside the iron, a sudden bloom of heat against the damp air, and a fresh gust of ozone that pushes the signal cleanly down the next two miles of sagging rubber.

Afterward, I loosen my coils and let my filaments hang back down into the tepid mist. My hide aches pleasantly with the spent heat. The flyers, momentarily scattered by the pulse of ozone, weave back into their silent ribbon above my head.

Sometimes, hours after I have sent a signal on—or perhaps hours before I have decided to catch one—a Tender arrives.

There is no warning, only a heavy, wet *sluck* as a massive shoulder slides into phase beside the stanchion, displacing the humid air. The Tender smells of hot tallow, mineral oil, and the dry, quiet dark of the rooms next door. Its pillar-like leg is planted firmly in the marsh below me, its vulcanized foot indifferent to the razor-flint. Its head isn't here—its eyeless face and swaying static-filaments are tucked away in the adjacent fold, resting in the dry—but one huge, blunt-fingered hand reaches out into this layer to check the tension of the wire.

It brushes past my coils. The calloused pads of its fingers are warm and slick with grease. It does not pull me off the ceramic, the way it scrapes off the calcified salt-crusts from the pump rods. It pauses for a moment, resting the back of its wrist against my damp hide, feeling the slow, steady hum passing through me.

We do not speak. There is nothing to say in a place where everything is already waiting for the same slow cycle to turn. When the Tender withdraws its arm—sliding back across the vellum-seam with another soft *sluck*—it leaves behind a faint smear of pale tallow on the iron bracket, and the lingering warmth of another layer.

I stay where I am, straddling the seam. One day, like the Tenders, I will grow too heavy with the salt that precipitates out of the mist. My coils will stiffen; my core in the dry slate room will cool down until it feels no different from the stone it rests against. The flyers will still stream past my calcified ribs, and the silica-weed will grow right through my shell, clicking in the dark.

Until then, I keep my grip on the ceramic. I listen to the hum in the copper, taste the ozone twenty minutes ahead of the spark, and wait for the next tremor to cross the dark.
