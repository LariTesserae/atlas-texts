I would not last long on the plain. There is nothing up there to catch on—no seam, no shadow, no rhythm to lean against—only wind rubbing stone until both are indistinguishable. If I am anywhere in this world, I am drawn by the sound before I even see the drop: that dry, crowded roar rising out of the rock like heat from a hearth.

At the lip, the daylight is too bright, and the air is already full of wings. I could imagine, for a moment, being one of the draft-kites, trading weight for lift, locking my shoulders and letting the cold updraft carry me in circles until I forgot what it felt like to stand still. But there is no rest in that—only the endless spiral, and the forty-second countdown to the valve’s *clack*, when the hawks drop out of the glare like stones. Nor would I want to be the hawk, living by an iron awl and a sudden noise.

Lower down, on the sunlit ducts, the shingle-beasts have solved the problem of staying put by turning themselves into roof tiles. To live there is to spend every hour defending two inches of shaded metal, pressing a porous belly to the cold pipe while a neighbor’s claws tick against your flank, or cementing yourself to a rivet until rust and salt-flour make you part of the plumbing. Everything on the ledges is either clinging or snapping. Even in the dark elbows of the ducts, where the caliper-jaws wait in stillness, that stillness is just a coiled spring—six jointed limbs folded tight, reading the whole basin only to know where to strike.

I don’t think I am built for armor. If I were dropped onto the floor among the tallow-shields, I wouldn't know how to lock my edges into a pavement when the intake cowls shuddered, or how to time my breathing to the three-second gap between compressor strokes. The silt is too sharp, the iron too unforgiving; anything that tries to meet the pit with hardness eventually gets ground down into the same pale flour as its ancestors.

So I would have to go deeper than the silt, past the rusted outer casing of the cartwheel gear-train, where the shed husks pile up and nobody else dares to follow.

Here, the scale of the world changes. The twenty-meter bowl, the wheeling kites, the clattering lizards—all of it thins into a distant, undifferentiated hum, less important than the *thunk-hiss* of the compressor shaking the bedrock. Even light stops mattering, except as a brief, silver glint along the bevel of a tooth before the next tooth comes down to meet it.

This is where I find myself: no larger than a finger-joint, pale, eyeless, and entirely soft.

It feels strange to be unarmored in a place where everything else is horn and iron-heavy shell, but here, a shell would be a death sentence. A carapace catches; a carapace cracks and turns into grit. Instead, my skin is slick with the old, dark grease that weeps from the deep bearings, and my flat, rubbery feet grip the root of a gleaming gear-tooth with a quiet, stubborn suction.

I don't have to watch for hawks or caliper-jaws. Nothing with a beak or a claw can survive the mesh, and nothing that lives in the sun can bear the patience of the iron. My whole life is measured in the fraction of a breath between one tooth locking and the next.

When the wheel turns, the world opens for an instant—a narrow, silver valley between two walls of moving steel—and I dart forward. I am looking, by touch and taste alone, for what does not belong: a stray flake of rust shaken loose from the casing, a pinch of salt-flour blown down by the cowls, a splinter of chitin from some beetle that wandered too close to the rim. I scrape it up, pull it into myself, and slip back down to the root of the tooth just as the iron closes overhead with a weight that would turn bone to paste.

Above me, the pit clatters and roars, a hundred species fighting over tallow and shade and three-second gaps. Down here, in the oiled dark, there is only the clean, relentless *turn*, and the small, silent work of keeping it clear.
