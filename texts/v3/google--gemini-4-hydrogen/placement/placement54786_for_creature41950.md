I would be found in the seam where the cataract meets the pipe.

Not inside the turbine-tower—nothing with lungs can live inside the iron belly where the heat-exchangers roar—and not out in the open jungle, where the canopy-mimics hang in the root-curtains and practice the art of sounding like home. I would be just behind the south-west buttress of the third-tier geothermal station, tucked into a shallow, hand-chipped hollow in the limestone where a weeping waterfall spills over the cliff-lip and breaks against the basalt ten paces away.

It is an acoustic dead-zone. The falling water makes a wide, flat wall of white noise that swallows the *click-scrape* of our tools and the wet *slap* of our palms on the stone. An armored ambusher, crawling along the rim of the scalded clearing with its belly pressed to the mud, cannot hear us breathing behind that curtain. All it hears is the cataract, and the slow, steady *thrum-clack* of the buried slurry line running beneath the floor of our hollow.

That is where I live, and what I am is a *pipe-reader*—though we have no word for it that isn’t just a particular way of pressing the jawbone to the iron.

I am old enough that the cartilaginous cowl flaring from my collarbones has stiffened at the edges, scarred pale where the scalding mist caught me once in my youth, and the fine filaments along my forearms have grown thick and blunt as worn bristles. My skin is slate-gray and slick with the same lukewarm spore-oil that coats the fern-fronds; I do not wipe it off. To wipe it off is to let the mineral rain bite into the hide. Instead, I let the jungle’s grease seal me, just as the tallow-colored crawlers high on the cables let their wax seal them against the static.

If you reached out in the dark and touched my shoulder, you would find it dense and heavy, sloped forward from a lifetime of leaning into the updrafts and walking with my knuckles brushing the rounded basalt for balance. You would feel no eyes—only the smooth, unbroken dome of the skull above the cowl, and, if I turned toward you to catch the tremor of your breath, the cool, taut give of the fluid-filled membranes recessed inside my face-slit.

I do not hunt with the younger ones anymore. My splayed feet have grown too broad and flat, the calluses on my heels too thick for the quiet, unhurried stalk into the steam-clearing when a valve blows. Instead, I keep the seam.

My work begins in the soles of my feet.

Even sitting still in the hollow behind the cataract, I am counting. Every four heartbeats, the subterranean pistons three hundred yards down-slope complete a stroke: *thrum-clack*. It travels up through the bedrock, through the travertine crust, through the thick pads of my heels, and settles in the marrow of my shins. When the cycle is true, the station above us is healthy, the boilers are full, and the steam-valves will shriek on their proper count. My band can wedge their bundles of sulfur-fern tubers and river-crawlers beneath the iron nozzles, retreat behind the concrete, and fold their cowls flat against their skulls without fear of an early blast.

Sometimes, though, the count drifts.

A silt-belly wedges itself too deep into an intake seam down in the drowned karst, or a mud-slip bends a buried conduit, and the *thrum-clack* develops a tiny, hesitant stutter—a wet drag at the end of the stroke. The younger ones do not notice it at first; they are listening to the high reeds, or the foghorn bellows of the browsers down in the valley. I notice it in my teeth.

When the rhythm shifts, I take my iron-capped stick and crawl out from behind the waterfall, keeping low in the copper-veined moss where the stray current makes the hair on my knuckles stand up. I dig down with my blunt fingers through the warm, spongy mud until I find the sweating iron skin of the slurry pipe. I press my wide lower jaw hard against it, sealing my lips, letting the sixty-cycle hum from the nearby pylon and the heavy piston-beat rattle straight up into my skull-bones.

And then I listen—not to the machine, but to what rides *on* it.

Miles away, at the next station up the plateau, another jaw is pressed to the same iron. Another pair of knuckles is wrapped around a stick.

*Tap-tap. Pause. Tap.*

The iron carries it through the mud and the roots, clean and sharp beneath the roar of the rain: *The upper gorge has slipped. The third pylon is leaning. The low-pressure spiral is turning.*

I lift my stick and answer, sending my own pulse back up the line, telling them that our intake is clear, that the valve-count here is holding steady, that our drum-reeds are still singing true.

That is the other half of what I do, when the rain slackens from a deluge to a warm, heavy drizzle and the band is out casting braided copper-moss nets into the flooded basins. I tend the reeds.

Along the terraced drop-offs below our station, where the updrafts rise warm and sour from the unseen ocean miles below, we have wedged hollow gorge-reeds into the weeping limestone seams. Over their mouths we stretch the skins of ruptured spore-bladders, cured in hot, black lubricant skimmed from the turbine weep-holes. Each reed is cut to a different length, so each catches the rising wind and hums at its own fixed pitch—a low, hollow *hoooom* for the safe basalt shelf, a tight, nasal buzz for the sinkhole rim where the travertine crust is thin and gives way into the flooded karst-tubes.

They are our landmarks. Without them, a band returning from the moss-beds in a heavy storm could step straight off a rounded cliff-lip into the roaring dark.

But the plateau does not like things to stay still. The warm rain rots the lashings. The mineral runoff crusts the reed-mouths with pale travertine until their voices go flat and muffled. Worse, the canopy-mimics—those long, silent things hanging in the aerial roots—sometimes crawl down to the gorge-rims and listen to a reed for days, swelling their resonant throat-sacs until they can match its exact pitch, calling a tired traveler two paces to the left of the path.

So I walk the rim with a gourd of turbine-grease slung across my chest and a bundle of fresh, cured bladder-skins tucked under one long arm. I move slowly, angling my cowl into the wind, letting my forearm filaments read the turbulence off the drop-off. When I reach a reed, I lay my palm against the stone beside it. A real reed vibrates the limestone it is wedged into; a mimic’s voice hangs loose in the wet air, untethered to the rock.

I scrape the mineral crust from the rim with a bit of broken chitin, re-tie the slick skin, and wait for the updraft to catch it. When the note rises—clean, steady, anchored in the stone—I move on to the next seam.

High above me, unseen in the roaring dark, the thigh-thick cables sag between their iron pylons. Just now, as the air grows heavier and my cowl-membranes tighten with the drop in barometric pressure, I can hear the faint, frantic *chitter-chitter-chitter* running down the tensioned wire—the tallow-colored crawlers pressing their dry bellies to the copper, singing their mating songs across the chasms ahead of the coming storm.

Down below, in the drowned dark of the karst-tubes, the silt-bellies are pulling deeper into their limestone throats, their gill-slits *glug-hissing* in the rising, iron-thick runoff.

I turn back toward the station. The heat-bloom of the turbine-tower greets my face-slit from half a mile away, smelling of hot grease, ozone, and wet rust. Beneath my splayed heels, the *thrum-clack* of the pistons is steady again. The steam-valve is three hundred beats from blowing, and the band will be gathering at the edge of the scalded clearing with their wrapped tubers.

I slip back behind the roaring curtain of the cataract, settle my spine into the worn hollow of the limestone, and press my jaw to the warm iron—waiting for the storm to turn, and listening to the plateau breathe.
