I wouldn’t be the one walking the catwalks. The one with the rubber apron and the flat, steady stride already owns the beat of the floor—heel on the *thrum*, toe on the *clack*—and the room doesn’t have the spare space for two things keeping the same time.

If I am anywhere in that chamber, I am down in the narrow, dead-end slot behind the banks of humming transformers, where the catwalk ends against the raw concrete wall and the pipes pack too tight for a pair of shoulders to turn.

At first, it’s only a place to be wedged. The air here doesn’t move the way it does out by the intake louvers; it sits in a thick, cold pocket, heavy with the smell of sulfur and wet copper. Out in the aisles, the piston-pumps are a wall of noise, physical and blunt, but back here, tucked between the cast-iron ribs of the transformer casings and the weeping wall, the *thrum-clack* arrives slightly muffled, stripped of its sharpest edge. What takes its place is the hum.

It is a different kind of sound—not a strike, not a rhythm, just a continuous, sixty-cycle weight that lives in the marrow rather than the eardrums. It doesn’t ask you to walk in time with it. It asks you to hold still.

If I stay there long enough—and in a room where nothing changes, *long enough* is simply always—I stop feeling like something that crawled behind the machinery to get out of the draft, and start feeling like something that was poured into the gap when the concrete was.

I am smaller than the one in the apron, or at least folded tighter: knees drawn up, spine pressed flat against the pale, unweathered ridges left by the wooden forms. My skin doesn’t fight the wet; it takes on the same slick, chalky sheen as the wall behind me, beaded with water that never quite runs fast enough to warm up. Where the casing of the transformer brushes my shoulder, the metal is a fraction of a degree less freezing than the rest of the room—not warm, never warm, just spared the rime. That tiny margin of heat is where the black, rubbery lichen grows thickest, and where the translucent moss hangs in limp, pale threads.

I don’t have the pleated flanks of the walker, or the chitin-tight discs behind the jaw. If I breathe the thin, needle-sharp air, I do it shallowly, through the mouth, tasting the flat metallic emptiness of high altitude mixed with the faint, sweetish tang of fresh resin from the gaskets. What I have instead is a kind of stillness that matches the transformers. My ribs don’t work like bellows; they barely move at all, letting the continuous electrical buzz settle into my chest until I can’t tell where the humming metal ends and my own collarbone begins.

Out beyond the transformer banks, the amber pilot-lights make their blurred halos in the mist. Every so often—at the exact, unvarying interval the circuit demands—the cold blue-green glow of the eel-curtains parts, and the pale figure in the black apron moves past the mouth of my slot.

It doesn’t look in. There is nothing back here that needs checking: no valve stems to scrape clean of rime, no loose bolts, no pilot-lights to peer into. The only thing in this crevice is the steady, unbroken hum and the slow carpet of six-legged mites grazing over my shins and the transformer’s bolted seams, treating my ankles no differently than the cast iron.

Sometimes the walker pauses at the end of the transformer bank, resting one broad, flat-padded palm against the casing just above my head. Through the metal, I feel the faint, wet resonance of its throat-hum locking into the chord between the pistons and the shrieking bleed-valves. It isn’t checking on me. It’s just tuning itself to the room, and I happen to be part of what the metal carries.

What am I doing back here? Nothing that looks like work.

While the walker keeps the mechanical beat—keeping the valves clear, keeping the room five minutes away from starting—I am the part of the chamber that holds the hum. When the mites crawl over the backs of my hands, I don’t brush them off. When a heavy drop of condensate detaches from the overhead cable tray and strikes the crown of my head, I let it run down my temple and join the shallow, shivering film on the floor-plate, draining away at the exact rate it arrives.

Maybe I am an unfinished thing too—not the caretaker, not a creature of the swarm, just a quiet, folded listener left behind the machinery before the final coat of paint, keeping the low electrical note steady in the dark so the rest of the room has something solid to lean its noise against.
