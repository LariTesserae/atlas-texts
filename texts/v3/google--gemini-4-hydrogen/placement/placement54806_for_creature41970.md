Not in the deepest forests, where the bladder-trees crowd so tightly you cannot turn without bruising a seed-pod, and not out at the continental rim, where the sea stands up in a trembling wall above nothing at all.

I would find myself on the long, pale shoulder just below a high ridge—where the ancient granite domes begin to flatten into their final curves, and the kelp-curtains thin enough that the water opens by an arm’s length. It is a place of edges that have no visible line. To an eye, if there were anything here that used an eye, it would look like only more tea-colored gloom, more silt-ribbons hanging motionless, more smeared amber overhead.

I would not be looking. I would be *reading* the weight of it.

At first, I might not even know I was separate from the ridge. My back would be broad and low, dappled with the same velvet-moss that furs the stone, my shoulders and hips thickened into dull, calcified plates that taste of bedrock to the current. If a blind, paper-shelled crawler wandered across my flank, it would leave the same faint track in my silt as it left on the granite, and I would let it pass. There is nothing here to startle at. Even if a salinity storm tore an acre of canopy loose three ridges over, the water would swallow the snap of every holdfast before the tremor could travel an inch.

I would be silent, and deaf, and entirely unhurried—a heavy, pale thing with four thick limbs and broad, four-fingered hands pressed flat against the fibrous stone.

What I would have, instead of sight or sound, is the skin of my palms and the pale, pleated undersides of my body, tuned to the architecture of the water. And in this place, just below the crest, the architecture is broken in a way that suits me.

A seam runs here.

To my left, the water is cool, dense, and laterally tight—a stagnant hollow where the pressure crowds in from all sides like a held breath. A single grain of quartz has been hanging at the height of my milky eye-dome for what might be a season or a century; it does not fall, and it does not rise. When my heart beats in this pocket, the pulse takes its time, stretching out through my heavy tail-keel until the next beat feels like a distant memory.

A palm’s width to my right, a warm fissure breathes upward from the ancient rock. The water there is blood-warm, thinner, rising in a slow, greasy column that carries pale spores from bud to rot in the span of a single drift.

Most of the ridge-dwellers cross these seams carefully, sealing their inner valves and moving through before the difference can pull at them. I think I would stay beside this one.

I would anchor three of my limbs in the cool, slow hollow, letting my calcified plates settle into the hollows of the granite, and close the muscular valves along the middle of my torso—the quiet, internal doors that divide my blood into chambers. Then I would reach my fourth arm across the invisible boundary, into the rising warmth.

It is a strange thing, to feel two paces of time in one body. In the cool pocket, my chest is still, my moss barely swaying, my thoughts—if they are thoughts, and not simply the slow registering of weight—unfolding at the speed of stone. Out at the end of my right arm, the water races. Velvet-moss creeps visibly over my wrist, blooms in tiny, pale threads, and dies back into silt; a seed-pod brushes my knuckles, swells, and bursts against my palm, leaving a smear of sweet pith that is gone before I could ever draw it back to my leathery lips. My fingers in the plume are living through afternoons while my shoulder rests in a single, unmoving hour.

This is where I would work, if *work* is not too brisk a word for it.

Above me, just out of reach of the warm current, the underside of a drifting kelp-mat hangs like a ceiling of tangled roots. Because the gravity here near the crest has forgotten which way is down, dislodged pebbles have drifted up over the ages and caught there, resting against the kelp like inverted gravel.

When I want one, I do not swim—there is nowhere to swim—I simply loosen my grip on the granite, let the indecisive lightness lift my heavy body an inch off the stone, and push gently with a palm. I rise like a slow boulder, brush the underside of the mat with my blunt head, and coax a river-rounded stone free with two fingers. Then I let the water settle me back down to the ridge.

I am building a cairn, though not on the flat stone. I am wedging the stones into the seam itself.

If you place a flat pebble at the exact boundary where the cold hollow presses laterally against the rising warmth of the fissure, the water holds it. It tilts at an angle that ought to drop it down the slope—forty degrees, fifty—propped against nothing visible, kept upright by the sheer, packed weight of the two currents leaning against each other.

My palms know when a stone has found the seam. The tremorless push of the cold pocket meets the upward slide of the fissure right across the stone’s belly, and suddenly it stops wanting to fall. It simply *stays*.

Three stones are already balanced here when I arrive—or perhaps I placed the bottom one myself, three hundred years ago, and forgot, or perhaps another dweller paused here on a slow drift and nudged it into place. When I lay my palm lightly over the lowest stone, I can feel the slight, calcified film where a different hand once rested, and the faint, impossible tilt that tells me the fissure has shifted half a finger’s breadth to the north since then.

I add my pebble to the top. I hold it there until the current takes its weight, until the two speeds of the sea lock around it like fingers, and then I draw my hand back into the cold hollow and open my valves, letting the warmth from my wrist bleed slowly into the rest of me.

Overhead, a shadow peels itself loose from a bladder-tree and drifts down through the amber gloom like a strip of dark silk. It brushes across the smooth, milky domes on the sides of my head—not a sight, only a cool, brief dimming, like a hand passing over a closed eyelid.

I do not move. Somewhere miles away, or inches away, a pale ribbon-creature undulates between the trunks, feeding on nothing. Down beyond the rim, the lower world dries and cracks in the cold air. Here, the tea-warm dark holds steady, the stone in the seam does not fall, and I sit beside it, letting the velvet-moss creep a little farther up my back, waiting for nothing at all.
