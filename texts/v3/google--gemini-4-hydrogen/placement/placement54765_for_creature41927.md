I would not be near the center, where the magnetite slab grinds against its bed of mica and the dry current runs thick enough to taste like iron in the air. That is the engine’s throat—too loud, too relentless, a place of raw torque where the stone itself is always straining.

Nor would I be out on the wide, sun-struck flats of the ore beds, where the light pours straight down with nowhere to hide and the molten tin sweats out of the rock in blinding beads.

I would be at the margin. 

Where the glassy, stepped terraces of black basalt begin to rise toward the ridge’s lip, there is a quiet angle of stone—just wide enough to hold a drift of windless dust and the shadow of a single copper-veined thicket. The sixty-cycle hum of the floor reaches this corner softened, broken into faint, overlapping echoes against the sharp walls. If you pressed your hand to the rock here, you would feel it not as a roar, but as a steady, patient pulse, like the resting beat of something vast asleep beneath the crust.

That is where I am. And if you sat on the terrace above me for only a minute, you would never know I was anything other than a wedge of pale, unfinished rock wedged into the shade.

I have no eyes to see the ordinary sky above the ridge. To me, the sun is not a brightness; it is a heavy, dry pressure that sweeps across my back over the course of hours, warming my upper plates until the cloudy quartz expands by a hair’s breadth and settles tighter into its waxy seams. I know the shape of the courtyard the way a root knows soil—not by outline, but by gradient. Along my blunt, oil-slicked leading rim sits a row of pinhead beads of pure tin, and through them, the empty stone opens into a dense, shifting topography. I feel the steep cliffs of radiative heat rising off the basalt, the faint, invisible rivers of stray voltage bleeding from the central rotor, the subtle pull of magnetic contours that curve across the floor like paths worn into grass.

I am low, heavy, and unhurried—a stepped architecture of quartz and dull, iridescent bismuth, held together by dark gaskets of non-aqueous flesh that smell faintly of hot resin and ozone. Deep in the narrow slots between my plates, a quiet, resistive heat glows dull orange, the only sign that I am keeping my own counsel in the dark of my shell.

When I move, it is not walking. I pulse a tiny, rhythmic current through my joints, setting the dense mat of oiled copper bristles beneath me vibrating in exact phase with the sixty-cycle tremor of the mountain. I lift on a cushion of imperceptible hum and slide—a foot an hour, no faster than the razor-edged shadow of the terrace lengthening beside me.

This is the whole of my work: to hold my charge, to keep my plates clean, and to follow the contours where the stone needs tending.

When the heat-sink pods of the nearby thicket split with their dry, solitary *tick*, I wait until the slow magnetic drift of the floor gathers the carbonized spores into a dark, neat seam at the base of the stems. Then I glide over the drift and settle there for half an afternoon, vibrating gently, drawing the uniform pellets up into the oiled chambers of my underside. There is no hunger in it, no tearing—only the quiet intake of raw carbon to mend my gaskets and steady my internal resistance.

Later, when the abrasive mineral dust has worn the leading edge of my carapace thin, I follow a magnetic line out toward the edge of the ore beds. I do not go far. I find a tidy, unguided row of hexagonal bismuth wafers, press my soft rim down over a loose one, and draw it in. Over days, with a slow, localized surge of heat, I fuse it into the gap.

And when my insulation runs dry and the static begins to prickle too sharply along my seams, I return to the shade of the copper-veined fronds. I nudge my rim against the base of a thick, waxy stem, parting the skin just enough to siphon a few drops of hot dielectric oil. Before I slide away, I let a small, clean arc of static pass from my tin beads back into the roots, leaving the plant whole, grounded, and faintly humming.

Sometimes, out on the bare basalt between the thickets, the magnetic contour I am following intersects another.

I feel the symmetry before I touch it—a matching sixty-cycle tremor approaching across the stone, a warmth that answers my own. We do not hurry to meet. We align our leading rims and halt, pressing our tin beads together in the thin, scorching light.

For an hour or more, we sit bridged. A tiny spark, blue-white and no larger than a grain of salt, snaps once or twice across the gap between us, balancing our potentials. Beneath our shells, in the oiled shadow where we meet, a slow exchange of thick resin and stray carbon pellets passes back and forth. Nothing is spoken; nothing needs to be. When our charges are level, we angle apart and resume our separate arcs across the stone.

The hours I like best come late, when the sun finally drops behind the sharp, unweathered lip of the ridge. 

The light vanishes all at once, and the thin high-altitude air cools with a suddenness that makes the glassy basalt terraces *ping* and contract. The surface loses its baked, radiative weight. Yet deep beneath the floor, the mountain’s thermal engine does not stop; the magnetite slab keeps turning in its groove of mica, agonizingly slow, sending its dry current up through the cooling stone.

In that abrupt, cold dark, I tuck my plates tight and anchor my copper bristles into a sheltered seam near the thicket. The world above the ridge—the world of wind and falling water, of things that run and breathe and rot—is something I have never known and could never survive. Here, in the quiet crease of rock, my own small, resistive ember holds steady against the chill, humming in time with the mountain, waiting for the first thin weight of morning to touch my quartz back again.
