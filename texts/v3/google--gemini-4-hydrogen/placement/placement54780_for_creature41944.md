I would not be on the rim. The rim is a knife-edge between two kinds of nothing: the freezing, lightless drop behind and the kiln-blast rising from below. Nothing holds on there for long.

I would be down in the dark, on the eastern side, wedged into the low, stubborn geometry of the cairns where the iron-sand stops and the basalt begins to lean inward.

At first, if you reached down to touch me, you would think I was just another lump of cooling slag—perhaps a little smoother than the rest, polished like old soapstone by the endless, dry friction of the bowl. Three layers of wall-hangers would be shingled across my shoulders and spine, their suctionless pads gripping my waxy hide, their horn-dishes tilting in unison toward the floor. They wouldn’t know I was anything but a ridge of rock. I keep my heat pulled deep inside, lagging behind the stone, letting my skin stay as cool and dull as the basalt around me.

To survive here, you have to learn how to be quiet in ways that have nothing to do with sound. You have to be thermally quiet. You have to breathe once every three minutes, drawing the parched, iron-heavy air through dry nasal plates that strip every trace of vapor before it can leave you. In the creases of my elbows and behind my knees, clusters of husk-mites shift like gray lichen, combing the flaky mineral wax from my joints. They are the only company I keep, and they ask for nothing I am not already shedding.

I have no eyes. Instead, the deep, wrinkled pits along my brow and across my collarbones drink the infrared shimmer of the cathedral. In that dark, heat is shape, and heat is speech. Below me, beneath the rippled iron-sand, I can feel the faintest tremor of the sand-swimmers pouring in slow spirals, hunting the cinder-flour. Along the walls, I watch the hangers argue—sudden, sharp blooms of crimson warmth flaring along their edges when one crowds another too closely, followed by the dry, papery whisper of a body inching sideways.

I don’t argue. I wait.

Between the surges, there is only the weight of the dark and a strange, dry ache in my jaw—an itch that lives in the bone rather than the skin. When the hangers above me are still, and the sand below is quiet, my hand sometimes unfolds on its own. Long-fingered, broad-palmed, the pads worn glossy as river-stone, though no river has ever touched this mountain. My fingers find the flat threshold-slab at the base of the cairn.

I trace the grooves already cut there. Faint, blunt-toothed tally marks, worn smooth at the edges by millennia of shifting grit and passing bellies. I do not know what a day is. I have no word for *outside*, or *sky*, or *yesterday*. I do not know who stacked these black stones and vitrified bones, or what they were trying to keep in—or keep out—when they first drew a line in the dark.

I only know that the slab feels unfinished. That the pulses will blur together into a single, suffocating *now* if nothing is set against them. So I lean my head down, press a flat, millstone-crowned tooth against the edge of the basalt, and scrape. A tiny flake of stone dust falls, vanishing instantly into the iron-sand. One more notch. A quiet insistence that this pulse happened, and then this one.

Then, deep beneath the floor, the fissures flush.

I feel it in my collarbones before the sand even stirs—a bruised, angry bloom of heat rolling upward from the vents. Instantly, the cathedral wakes in hushed synchronization. The living shingle peels off my back in a dry, leathery cloud, banking downward into the cooler eddies. Below, the sand-swimmers erupt from the grit, their smoked-glass plates catching the dull ember-glow as they surge up the lower walls.

I unfold. Not quickly—to rush is to burn water, and water is the only currency that matters.

I step out from the broken ring of the cairn, my ash-gray hide dull in the rising heat. For sixty seconds, the layers cross paths in the dark: swimmers climbing, gliders dropping like falling rags, hangers drifting. My brow-pits lock onto a matte-glass collar scaling the stone a pace away. My arm shoots out—not a strike, just a broad, soapstone-smooth palm closing cleanly around the swimmer’s flank before its bellows can flare.

There is no thrashing. No cry. Just a single, measured pressure until the silica plate gives with a soft *pop*. Before the sand-mites can swarm, before a single drop of thick, amber fluid can touch the thirsty air and vanish into steam, I press my lipless mouth to the breach and drink it dry.

When the surge crests and the fissures begin to dim back toward black-red, I am already retreating to the eastern wall. I carry the empty, paper-light husk back with me. I don’t drop it on the sand. I find a gap between two stacked stones where the thermal pulse has shifted a bone loose, and I tuck the dry shell carefully into the seam, packing it tight.

Mending a boundary whose meaning the hollow swallowed long ago. Keeping the wall thick.

Then I fold my long limbs against my chest, tuck my chin into my collarbones, and let the husk-mites settle back into my joints. The hangers drift down through the cooling dark, their pads whispering as they shingle themselves across my shoulders once more.

I pull my heat inward, slow my breath to the rhythm of the stone, and wait for the next pulse to tell me it is time to mark the dark again.
