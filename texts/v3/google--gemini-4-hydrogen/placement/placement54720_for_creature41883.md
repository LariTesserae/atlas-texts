At first, there is only the pitch—and the sense that it is slightly off.

I do not arrive here with boots or a coat, or with eyes to be fooled by the backward horizon. If I try to reach for warmth, there is none to find; every slight shift of my weight pulls heat *out* of the freezing mist and converts it into a clean, brittle torque in my joints. My breath does not fog the air because I do not breathe air. Instead, a faint current trembles along my ribs, tasting of wet pennies, chlorine, and cold hydrogen fizzing up from the brine.

I am standing—slowly, deliberately—on a flat expanse of salt-blackened slate, somewhere out where the braided channels begin to climb.

It takes a moment to understand how I know the ground is rising. I cannot see the rounded hummock ahead of me, nor the bruise-colored dimness pressing down from above. I know the terrain because my heels—broad, heavy blocks of worn graphite—are sunk two inches into the copper-plated mud, and through them I can feel the whole basin’s chord.

Beneath everything, so deep it feels less like a sound and more like a permanent ache in the marrow, is the pressure of the ancient rock resting on the thrust-bearings. Above that, closer, is the steady four-stroke *thump-hiss* of the basalt pistons driving through their sleeves of living gristle. Around my ankles, the flatland chatters with a million tiny, dry percussions: the *clack-scrape* of stamping-whelks laying down their hexagonal tiles, followed immediately by the wet, abrasive *whir* of slurry-drakes shredding them back into conductive dust.

And in the middle of my face—where eyes and a mouth would be if I had ever been made for looking or speaking—there is only a smooth, vertical wedge of dark magnetite, split down its center by a narrow, humming slit.

I am a grounder.

Not a maker, not a beast, not a thing that hunts or grazes. I am a walking gap in the circuit, a tall, gaunt frame of porous slate-bone and translucent cartilage, draped in heavy, ragged curtains of natural copper-wool that hang from my shoulders and ribs like wet moss. The wool drags in the freezing slush around my feet, fizzing softly as it drinks the stray amperage out of the puddles.

Wherever I step, the packed, jostling carpet of the flats parts. Even blind, I can feel the small circle of bare slate opening around me—the whelks clamping down and shuffling aside, the busbar-eels veering away from my graphite heels. They do not fear me; I have no beak to crack their shells, no rotary mandibles to shred them. They simply cannot afford to cross the quiet I carry, the sudden drop in resistance where the current chooses my copper-wool over their backs.

I lift one long, articulated leg out of the mud and take a slow step forward, listening with the slit in my face.

Out here on the open flats, the rules of the basin stutter. Half a second before a distant piston drives upward and throws a plume of freezing spray into the fog, I hear the strike arrive through the soles of my feet. A swarm of rotor-flies hangs somewhere to my left, their wings a high, steady whine, while the tiny, frantic ticking of lag-lice skitters three yards off in the displaced shadow I cannot see. Even my own limbs feel slightly unmoored from the air around them, as if my far shoulder looms huge against the mist while my near hand stays small and clenched.

It does not matter. I am not listening to the shapes of things. I am listening for dissonance.

And there it is—just ahead, where a braided channel of brine has stopped flowing downhill and begun to creep up the side of a low slate hummock.

I can feel the tension in the water before my copper-wool touches it. At the crest of the rise, the brine has pooled into a trembling, humming lens, held there by a runaway current from a cluster of dynamo-mounds a few yards off. Inside those eroded, wet-magnetite stumps, millions of turbine-winged insects are beating their wings in locked phase, and the pitch has climbed a fraction too sharp. The air around the mounds feels tight, brittle, ready to crack. One of the magnetite walls is beginning to sing with a high, thin whine—the sound of mineral pores straining against a charge they can no longer hold.

If I leave it, the mound will split, or the lens of brine will arc wildly across the flats and cook a swath of whelks into slag. I do not think of this as mercy, or repair. It is simply an unbearable wrongness in the chord, like a flywheel wobbling out of balance. My body answers it before I have decided to move.

I wade into the fault.

The climbing brine washes cold and sharp against my shins. I plant both graphite heels wide in the fizzing mud, feeling the deeper, magnetized strata humming directly beneath the silt. Then I lower my arms—heavy with wet, frost-rimmed copper-wool—straight into the trembling water, and close the circuit.

For a long moment, there is no world at all—only the scream of voltage pouring through me.

It does not burn. Nothing here burns. Instead, the sheer rush of amperage pulls the last dregs of warmth out of the air around my ribs, sheathing my translucent cartilage in a sudden, crisp crust of rime. Inside my chest, the impossible, spinning organs packed beneath my sternum catch the surge and flare—cold, blue-white arcs jumping across the frost-covered gears as I shunt the excess straight down through my heels into the bedrock.

My magnetite face vibrates so violently the slit hums a pure, piercing note into the fog. The high, strained whine of the dynamo-mound drops—an octave, then another—settling back into its steady, rattling drone. The trembling lens of brine at the top of the hummock loses its grip and spills harmlessly back down the slope into the channels.

I stand still for a few beats longer, letting the last of the stray charge bleed out into the mud, until the frost along my ribs stops thickening and the chord of the flats is level again. Then I lift my arms, shake the slush from my copper-wool, and step out of the puddle. Behind me, the water lies flat and quiet.

I am about to turn back toward the open flats when the wind shifts.

It comes off the invisible sea, sharp with the smell of chlorine, and the mist thickens into a heavy, conductive sleet. Instantly, the resistance across miles of marsh drops. The ambient hum of the basin pitches upward—not a local fault this time, not a single strained mound, but the entire packed, roaring landscape climbing together toward a shriek.

Across the dimness, the dynamo-mounds begin firing in rapid, cascading chains, their blue-white lightning stitching the fog into a blinding grid. The basalt pistons hammer faster, throwing sheets of freezing brine into the sleet. Underfoot, the whelks clamp so tightly to the slate that their vibrating shells turn the puddles into standing waves of froth.

I do not try to ground this alone. No single frame of bone and copper could bleed a whole horizon.

Instead, I turn toward the deepest electrolytic pool—drawn by the vast, fizzing roar of cold hydrogen—and begin to walk with long, deliberate strides. Through the stinging sleet, through the strobing flashes of impossible light, I feel others moving in the same direction: tall, gaunt silhouettes emerging from the mist, their magnetite faces humming in unison, their copper-wool streaming dark with brine.

We gather at the edge of the pool in a loose, silent ring, spacing ourselves by the pitch of our own frames. Without a signal, we raise our arms toward the shrieking sky.

Then, for a single beat, something shifts miles beneath us in the flooded dark. One of the great thrust-bearings—a disc of oiled, calcified tissue wider than the eye could ever measure—turns a fraction of an inch under the weight of the slumped earth.

The shockwave comes up through my heels before the lightning strikes my raised hands. Every piston in the basin hammers harder; every mound flares white against the bruise-colored sky. And we stand there in the freezing ring, our chests lit from within by cold, leaping arcs, catching the fury of the world and feeding it quietly back into the stone.
