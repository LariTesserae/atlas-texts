If I were to wake there, I think I would not know my name before I knew the weight of the blankets.

It would be dark first, and close—the kind of dark that smells of lanolin, damp peat, and the faint, sweet musk of voles rustling somewhere behind the inner rawhide lining. I would be pressed between two other bodies on a deep mattress of dried sphagnum, my knees drawn up, my breathing already trained to a shallow, unhurried tide. No one would be speaking. The only measure of time would be the slow pulse of the person whose back lay against my chest, and the faint, steady warmth we were all hoarding together beneath the patched pelts.

Then, slowly, the pull of the morning—or whatever passes for morning in a place where the sky is always the same flat white—would reach me through the cloudy pane of split gypsum set into the chalk wall. Not a shaft of sunlight, just a gradual brightening of the stone’s pale skin.

I would slip out from under the furs without waking the others, moving with the careful, low-shouldered economy the room demands. My layers are already on: the moss-dyed wool, the scraped vole-pelt, the outer shell of grey felt stiffened at the seams with sinew. I pull my boots on, unloop the single knot of gut at the bone latch, and duck through the low doorway.

That is where I find myself: not at the bottom, among the cisterns, and not at the very lip where the gravel plain begins, but on the fourth tier up, on a narrow terrace where the roof of a lower house becomes my threshold.

The cold hits my cheeks first—sharp, clean, completely still. My breath blooms in front of my face and hangs there, a small white cloud that refuses to scatter. And the light hits everything at once. There are no shadows to step into, no dark corners to rest the eyes. Even the underside of the liverwort curtains hanging from the eaves above me is lit from below by the bounce off the chalk and the selenite seams. I crouch by the nearest wall trough, dip two bare, broad fingers into the rim of dark algae, and draw a smear of paste beneath each eye to dull the glare.

Looking at those hands—short-fingered, reddened by the chill, the nails thick as horn and stained faintly green at the quick—I know what I am here.

I am one of the seam-tenders. Not a leader, because a place this small and this steady has no need for anyone to stand above the rest, and not a wanderer, because there is nowhere to wander that isn't scoured by salt wind. I am simply one of the people whose work sits at the joints where stone, water, and living things meet.

My morning begins on my knees on the cushion-moss, parting the pewter-colored tufts with a bone needle to check the mortarless cracks below. I am looking for two things: pale tubers swelled enough to pry free without tearing the root-mat, and any shift in the stone where frost-heave might have widened a chink too far for the voles to plug. Everything here is a matter of small tolerances. If a crack opens too wide, the still cold slips in; if the moss grows too thick over a runnel, the condensed water stalls and pools instead of sliding down to the basins.

I move along the hand-carved groove that angles down past three neighbors’ roofs. Along the wet chalk, dozens of pale, translucent snails are grazing the film of algae. One has drifted onto bare stone where the moisture is thinning; I lift it gently between thumb and forefinger—its shell cool and fragile as a fingernail—and set it back onto a thicker patch of green. A little further down, a round-bodied bird with soft, owl-like plumage drops silently from the eaves and settles onto my felt-wrapped shoulder. It weighs almost nothing. We stay like that for a long minute, neither of us in any hurry, watching the moss until my bone needle uncovers a pale grub. The bird takes it without a sound and glides off across the alley.

When I cross the stone-slab bridge to the next tier, someone else is coming the other way—an older person carrying a clay pot up from the lower cisterns. The bridge is barely wider than my shoulders. We turn sideways at the same moment, neither of us breaking stride, and as we pass, we press the backs of our hands lightly against each other’s ribs. *Here I am. The balance holds.* Before we separate, I tap two fingers lightly against their collarbone—asking after the upper cisterns—and they incline their head once, slowly, toward the flat white sky. Full, and clear.

I think I would belong most deeply to the quiet of that exchange. In a world outside the bowl, so much is spent on noise—on explaining, defending, filling the air so it doesn't feel empty. Here, the air is already full: full of moisture waiting to bead on limestone, full of moss, full of the tiny, steady heat of bodies stacked six tiers high. Speech would feel like breaking a pane of split gypsum just to hear it crack.

By midday—though midday is only a slight brightening of the white glare—I would make my way down the spoon-dished steps to the lowest floor, where the air is heaviest and smells strongest of wet limestone. I kneel beside one of the sunken cistern-basins. Before I dip my pot, I lean over the rim and squint down through the clear, still water.

Three newts hang suspended in the middle depths, motionless except for the faint, rhythmic pulsing of their red-feathered gills. I watch them for three slow heartbeats, then four. They are steady. The water is good.

Later, perhaps, when the rare sea-fog rolls in off the distant tideline and smothers the gravel plain in windless grey, I might be one of the few who climbs the dished steps all the way to the lip. Not out of restlessness, and not to escape, but to gather what the outside world has broken and thrown away—a length of salt-bleached driftwood for a ladder rung, a strip of seal-blubber, a dry rib-bone waiting to be shaped into a needle. Stepping over the rim into the fog is like stepping into an unfinished thought: too wide, too loose, the ground flat and featureless under the mist. I would gather what we need as quickly and silently as I could, and feel a quiet, deep relief when the bowl opened beneath my feet again—bright, green, moss-padded, and sealed against the wind.

Back down in the fourth tier, sitting on my dished threshold with a hide across my knees and a flint flake in my hand, I would watch the condensation gather in fine bright beads along the chalk wall beside me. Not dripping. Never splashing. Just sliding down its carved groove in a silent film, at the exact pace of my own breath.

That is who I would be: a pair of bare, toughened hands in a grey felt shell, keeping the moss parted, keeping the snails on their green paths, reading the pulse of a friend’s wrist and the gills of a newt in clear water—one small, patient hinge in a city that has learned how to last forever by never making a sound.
