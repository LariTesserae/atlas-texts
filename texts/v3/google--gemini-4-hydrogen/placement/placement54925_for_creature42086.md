I would not be down in the dark between the giants, flattened like a gasket, waiting for the stone to warm. And I would not be up on the knife-edged ridges, turned to a mirror of white fire, watching the sky for a drifting bladder of water.

I would find myself in the middle of the noise, where the heat meets the wind.

At first, there is only the roar—a wall of sound so thick and continuous it stops being something you hear and becomes the medium you live in, like water. And the glare: a thin, merciless white that needles straight through closed lids if you let it. Everything on the surface of this world is either blindingly bright or ink-black, and the air smells fiercely of wet rust, hot oil, and the sharp, metallic bite of ozone.

To survive here, you have to learn how to close the sky out without going blind.

So I drop the outer shutters—thick, milky plates of grown quartz-horn—until the blinding sun softens to a flat, pearlescent blur. Beneath them, my inner eyes open to the only landscape that matters: heat. Instantly, the world sorts itself into things that burn and things that freeze. Beneath me, curving away like a polished hill, is a vast, dull-glowing plain of iron-sulfide, radiating a steady, subterranean orange. Above and around me, the air is a swirling blue-black cold.

I am low to the ground, heavy-shouldered, six-limbed, my coat a dense, matted tangle of fibrous hair stiffened with frozen mineral rime. When I shift my weight, I do not merely step; I unclamp and lock. My broad, flat feet are threaded with veins of natural magnetite, and every stride ends in a small, certain *clack* as iron finds iron.

This is where I belong: on the leeward side of a calcified dorsal stack, tucked out of the worst of the blade-wind, astride the living back of a smelter-herd.

The giant beneath me is a slow-moving mountain, grinding its way along a copper vein at fifty yards a season. Inside its armored flank, an acid furnace churns crushed basalt and dark, waxy stalks into heat and steam. Above my head, the chimney’s throat vents a steady, rhythmic plume of sulfurous vapor that flashes almost instantly into a glittering fog of ice needles, drifting down to coat my back in fresh metallic dust.

It is a good place to live, if you know how to listen with your feet.

I peer down over the rim of the stack. In my inner sight, the chimney’s throat is a bright, furious bloom of orange-white, ringed with the cooler, sluggish yellow of sulfur-crust and frozen scale. Left alone, that crust would thicken, choking the vent, backing the pressure up into the giant’s chambers until the whole furnace tore itself apart.

I brace all six feet, feeling the *clack-clack* of my soles biting into the carapace, and lower my narrow face toward the heat. My beak is heavy, chisel-shaped, built for stone and scale. *Tock. Tock.* I chip away a thick, brittle shelf of yellow crust, feeling the vibration run straight up my jaw. Beneath it, curled in the warm, acidic lining of the rim, a cluster of fat soot-grubs wriggles away from the draft. I scoop them up alongside the mineral grit, swallowing both—the sharp, clean crunch of scale, the warm, salty burst of the grubs.

Around the curve of the stack, I can feel my kin at work. I do not need to look for them through the milky glare, and I never waste breath calling into the roaring air. I know them by the faint, staggered *tock-tock* of their beaks travelling through the shell, and the occasional dull *clack* of magnetic soles shifting for a better angle.

Farther out, the world turns on its own unyielding clock. The giant beneath us shifts its weight an inch—an hour’s labor—and its flank grinds against the neighboring herd with a dry, agonizing shriek of metal on stone. Through the frosted blur of my outer lids, I catch the slow, drifting shadow of a condenser-kite passing high overhead, heavy with harvested fog. Down in the deep, ink-black cleft between the carapaces, a hillside-wide wave of static crests, and the air tastes suddenly sharp as a blade.

Then, the song beneath my feet changes.

At first it is barely a tremor—a slight tightening in the low, chest-rattling thrum that rises through my six locked soles. The steady *hiss-clack* of the dorsal valves stutters, holds for half a beat too long, and lets out a thin, high whistle. In my inner sight, the orange glow inside the chimney throat begins to pulse, brightening too fast, too fierce.

The giant is backing up. Somewhere deep in the acid-gut, a chamber is over-pressurizing.

I stop chipping. I do not look around, and I do not open my mouth. Instead, I arch my spine, press the ridged plate along my ribs hard against the iron-sulfide carapace, and drag.

*Skree.*

The sound doesn’t enter the wind; it shoots straight through the metal shell, sharp and jagged as a crack in quartz. Around the far side of the stack, the rhythmic *tock-tock* of the other beaks cuts off instantly. A second later, an answering *skree*—short, double-timed—vibrates back through my soles.

We unclamp.

Six feet lift, breaking the magnetic seal, and we scramble across the polished, tilting curve of the giant’s back. The wind hits us full force away from the stack’s shelter, tearing at our rime-stiffened coats, driving ice needles against our quartz-horn shutters. Below us, the seam between our host and the neighboring herd is a narrow, freezing chasm of dark air and crushed indigo stalks, widening and closing as the two leviathans grind past one another.

There is no time to wait for them to meet. I gather all six legs, launch across the screaming gap, and hit the neighboring carapace at a steep, slick angle. My magnetite soles scrabble for a fraction of a second on the polished iron-sulfide—then *clack-clack-clack*, all six lock home.

Behind us, the valve blows.

A sudden, towering geyser of white steam erupts from the stack we just left, loud enough to punch through even the basin’s roar. In the sub-zero air, it freezes mid-flight into a blinding, shimmering curtain of ice needles, raining a heavy, metallic sleet down over our backs. Through the shell beneath my feet, I feel the neighboring giant shudder in sympathy, its own steady thrum unbroken.

Back on the first herd, the angry orange bloom dims to a manageable, rhythmic glow. The pitch drops back down into its deep, slow groove.

I hunker down for a moment in the lee of a new, unfamiliar stack, letting the fresh rime settle onto my fur, listening through my feet to the pulse of a new furnace. Beside me, two of my kin lock their soles onto the warm iron, their outer shutters milky against the merciless sun.

After a minute, one of them leans over the rim of the new chimney. *Tock.*

I shake the ice from my coat, step forward with a dull *clack*, and lean in beside it to begin again.
