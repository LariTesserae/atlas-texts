I wake before the light changes—not that light means much down here, only a slow brightening of the green-gold canopy from slate to moss—because the hum in the iron bracket above my head has shifted a quarter-tone.

You learn to sleep with your ear against the hemp knot of your hammock, where the vibration of the central dynamo comes up through the cypress trunk and into the steel I-beam. When the pitch drops, even a hair, it means the head-pressure in the lower sumps is thickening, or one of the big Archimedes screws has caught a wad of unretted marsh-fiber in its throat. I lie still for a long minute, letting the thought settle like silt in a tank, listening to the irregular *plink-plink-tink* of condensation hitting the copper patch on the cabin roof above me.

I am not a dynamo-keeper. My skin still has the steeped-tea color of the upper galleries, and I can still hear a frog in the intake ditches when the wind—what little of it clears the dike—carries the sound right. Nor am I a tree-wright; I don’t have the patience for a pruning saw, or the knack of coaxing a strangler-fig’s aerial root around a brick corner without bruising the bark.

I tend the *high lines*—the overhead copper steam-jackets and the porcelain-knotted wires that thread through the upper third of the canopy, where the drying lofts hang like damp lanterns.

By the time I’ve strapped on my slatted clogs and knotted my oiled-canvas cap over my braids, the air is already thick enough to chew. I step out onto the catwalk—*clack-clack-clack* on the perforated steel—and pause at the cistern perched in the fork of our gallery’s cypress. I dip a tin cup into the charcoal-filtered rainwater, drink half, and pour the rest over the back of my neck. It’s the last cool thing I’ll touch until evening.

My work is mostly listening, and mostly waiting.

From the outside, people think the basin is a single roaring thing, a wall of noise. When you’ve lived in the forks of the trees for twenty years, the roar separates into layers, the way river-silt settles into sand and clay and peat. At the bottom is the dynamo, steady as a heartbeat in the back teeth. Above it are the reciprocating pumps, *chug-hiss, chug-hiss*, and the wet slap of the canvas drive-belts. Higher still, up where I walk, the sound thins out into the hiss of petcocks and the sharp, bright *ting* of wrenches talking through the copper.

I carry a short brass-headed hammer tucked through my resin-cured apron, a rag soaked in tallow, and a pocketful of dried sweet-flag root to chew on when the air gets too heavy with bark-liquor. My route takes me along the clerestory walkways, thirty feet above the tea-colored channels, where the philodendrons drape over the iron trusses and the steam vents up in slow, white pillars.

Half the time, I’m just sitting on an overturned grease-keg beside a pressure-manifold, watching a brass gauge whose needle moves the width of a hair every half-hour. A batch of marsh-fiber in Vat Twelve has been steeping since the last new moon; it needs another four days before the fungus softens the rind, and my job is to make sure the steam-jacket stays warm enough to coax the rot without scalding it dead. You cannot hurry a fungus. If you crank the valve open too wide, the burlap wrapping chars and the whole vat sours. So I sit, and I whittle a spare wooden pin for the flume-gate, and every twenty minutes I lay the flat of my palm against the copper pipe to feel if the heat is true.

The rest of the time, I am a relay.

Because the tangle of pipes and aerial roots is so thick between the upper drying lofts and the lower pump-houses, the wrench-taps sometimes get muddled—echoing off a brick retaining wall or swallowed by a swelling of wet cypress bark. When I hear *three-short, one-long* coming up from the sumps, faint and flat against the belt-slap, I unhook my brass hammer, lean out over the rail where the ferns brush my knees, and strike the main overhead line—*ting-ting-ting, tiiing*—sending it clean and bright across the canopy to the flume-tenders on the far side of the basin.

It’s a quiet kind of usefulness. Nobody looks at me when I pass on the catwalks; I wear the same oil-darkened canvas, the same sweat-sheen on my forearms, the same unhurried stillness. When a younger worker climbs up from the pressing-sheds to ask if the upper cisterns have enough head for a wash-down, I don’t answer right away. I take a slow breath of the warm, sap-sharp air, let the question sink through the hum of the dynamo, and then tilt my flat palm toward the north roof.

Once, three summers ago, I had to climb the gap in the limestone dike to help haul in a cartload of crushed charcoal and salt. I stood on the rim for maybe two minutes, squinting into the flat, unfiltered glare of the sun. There was no hum under my clogs. The air felt thin and brittle, like it had been baked dry in an oven, and the silence made my ears ring worse than the boiler-house ever had. I didn’t wait for the others to finish hitching the rope. I hitched up my apron, stepped back down onto the spongy root-mat, and let the green-gold dark close over my head.

Down here, everything knows what it’s doing, even if it takes three weeks to do it.

By midday, the steam from the lower vats rises thick enough to bead on my eyebrows. I climb out onto the corrugated copper roof of Pump-House Four—the one weighted down with moss and sprawling philodendrons—to check the shallow wooden troughs where I keep my own small patch of mushrooms and climbing beans. I pinch off a handful of pale caps for my supper, tuck them into my cap, and sit for a while with my clogs dangling over the edge, watching the Archimedes screws turn below.

They lift the dark water an inch at a time, agonizingly slow, spilling it into the wooden flumes with a soft, endless *shhh*. Across the basin, behind a curtain of maidenhair fern, someone taps a pipe—*two-short, two-short*. *Pressure’s good.*

I reach out, lay my fingers lightly against the warm verdigris of the nearest steam-line, and tap back once. *Heard.* Then I settle back against the warm tin, chew my sweet-flag root, and wait for the needle on Vat Twelve to move another hair’s breadth.
