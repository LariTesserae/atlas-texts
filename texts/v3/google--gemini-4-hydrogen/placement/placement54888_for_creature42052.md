At first, I would be in the way.

That is the only honest place to begin. Anyone arriving in that valley from the rim—tall enough to strike their forehead on the low cedar lintels, clumsy enough to kick a dry-stone cairn out of plumb—is just another shifting mass in a place that already has too much weight on its shoulders. I would stand there in the bruised greyness, wool and skin soaking up the cellar chill, and my very presence would be a small, warm error. The nearest clay pipe would drop a fraction of a tone because my coat was stealing its draft. A mason three terraces down would pause with a mallet raised, tilting an ear toward the change, wondering which wall had slumped.

So I would have to get smaller. Not all at once, and not by choice so much as by the simple, patient arithmetic of bruised shins and scraped knuckles. You cannot stay upright in a place built for the stoop.

I think I would find myself in the middle tiers—neither up at the rim where the cold air first slips over the edge, nor down in the black, mineral-heavy sumps where the culverts groan and the silt-colored things thrash in the dark. The middle tiers are where the valley is busiest translating itself: where the condensation runnels gather speed, where the tipping-troughs are thickest, and where the cedar scaffolding criss-crosses the alleys like the inside of a ribcage.

At first, I would just be learning to hold still. In the dark, stillness is the only way to stop being an obstacle and start being part of the wall. I would sit on an upturned ash-brick beneath a plank causeway, knees drawn up, smelling the green timber and the sharp, iron-wet dust of fresh shale, and let my ears stop treating the valley as a roar.

It takes a long time for the wall of noise to come apart into its pieces. *Clack. Tip. Splash. Boom.* The *tink-tink-tink* of a chisel off to the left. The dry *shush* of hide-wrapped forearms sliding along a stone face.

When a mason finally found me—not by looking, only by the way the damp air curled differently around my shoulders—I wouldn't have words to offer them, and they wouldn't want any. Words are too loose for this place; they scatter and drown in the troughs. Instead, they would press something into my palm. Not a chisel, not at first. A chisel requires knowing the grain of the shale by heart, knowing how hard to strike before a whole course splits and brings a terrace down.

No—I think they would hand me a ball of twisted hemp, a lump of cold tallow, and a wooden pin.

That is where I would find my shape. Not as a builder of the great stone skin, and not as one of the blind, leathery crawlers licking the mortar clean, though I would spend plenty of hours crouched beside them, smelling their chalky breath and lifting them gently off the wet mud. I would be one of the ones who tend the *connections*—the rigging, the pins, the reeds, the cords that turn dead stone and dripping water into a voice.

Consider what a tipping-trough needs to keep telling the truth. Its cedar basin swells as it drinks the sweat off the air; its wooden pin wears down in its socket; the tallow goes gritty with ash-brick dust. If a pin binds, the trough fills too full before it tips, and when it finally goes over, it slams down late, throwing off the rhythm of three terraces below it. A mason listening from across the basin might think a runnel has choked and tear down a perfectly good wall to find a blockage that isn't there.

So I would crawl the scaffolding alleys with a pouch of grease and spare fiber slung across my chest, my own knees and elbows wrapped in stiffened hide and shed belly-scales lashed tight with cord. My eyes would stay half-shut against the grit, useless and unnecessary, until the greyness stopped mattering at all.

Instead, I would know the valley by tension and pitch.

I would know the exact weight of a hemp line before it snaps, the difference between the dry *creak* of a pin that needs tallow and the wet *groan* of a timber strut about to buckle. I would learn to tie knots in the dark that don't slip when soaked through, lashing bundles of dry reeds across the mouths of the embankments to soften a draft that is blowing too hard and drying out the clay before it can sweat.

I would become, in a quiet way, a translator of small things. The masons think in stone and courses, in cairns and retaining walls; the heavy ones in the sumps think in silt and current, nudging blockages clear with blunt snouts. I would live in the space between them—in the cords that jerk the shale hammers, in the counterweighted baskets of cobble, in the hollow cedar levers that turn a single drop of condensation into a *clack* loud enough to carry three hundred paces.

And there is something else I would be, though it takes longer to admit.

Everything in the valley that isn't stone or water—the green cedar, the hemp, the reeds, the tallow—had to come down from somewhere above the rim. The valley makes nothing green. It only drinks and sweats and settles. Someone has to meet the rim, or climb up into the thin, featureless greyness where the walls end, to haul down the raw fiber and the fresh-cut timber before the valley runs out of parts to mend itself with.

Perhaps, before my back curved permanently to the lintels, I would be one of the ones who still remembered how to walk upright on flat ground—who could climb the outer tiers with an empty sling and come back down bent double under bundles of green cedar and stiff, dry reeds, my ears ringing from the sudden, awful quiet of the rim, eager to get back down under the planks where the noise tells you where everything is.

Down there, crouched in a pocket under a causeway, eating grey fungus off a damp reed-bed and sipping mineral water skimmed from a sump, I wouldn't miss the light. Light is for things that want to stay at a distance from what they touch. Here, nothing is at a distance. Your palm is on the shale, your shoulder is against the bracing, the low hum of the clay pipes is inside your teeth, and when a wall gives way four terraces over, your hands are already reaching for the cord and the wedge before the dust settles.

I would be smaller than I am now, colder, harder at the knuckles, and much quieter. A creature of hemp and tallow and greased cedar pins, living inside a vast, unfinished instrument, listening for the one note that has gone flat—and crawling through the dark to bring it back into tune.
