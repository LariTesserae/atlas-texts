I would not be on the rim. The rim belongs to the wind and the sky’s glare, and nothing up there has anywhere to hide.

I would find myself much lower—at first, not even in the light at all, but curled in the dry, earth-warmed dark of the honeycomb beneath the basin floor, waiting out the white hours. Down there, the sun is only a rumor you feel through the ceiling: a faint tightening in the limestone overhead as the upper tiers take the heat, a slow shift in the air’s dampness as the mist burns off the highest walls. You sit with your knees drawn to your chest, wrapped in felted hair and oiled linen, your bare soles resting against stone that holds just enough warmth from the deep rock to keep the numbness out of your bones. In the dark, your fingers know what they are doing without looking—braiding sinew cord until the twist is even, or running a thumb along the edge of a copper chisel to feel for the tiny, rolled burr that means it needs honing on wet slate.

When the ceiling goes quiet—when the stone above stops expanding and settles into the first cold sigh of dusk—that is when I would climb.

Up through a shoulder-width shaft, bracing with my elbows and knees where the limestone is already worn smooth by generations of the same climb, lifting the slate cover just enough to taste the air before sliding it aside.

I would be one of the keepers, though not one of those who walk the high western terraces where the dawn hits first and the mist condenses fresh off the peaks. My place is at the bottom of the lattice, in the lowest tier of the basin, where the valley walls rise terraced and steep on every side and the sky is only a narrow square of pewter overhead.

Here, the water is old. It has taken days to make its way down from the rim, spilling in glassy sheets from pan to pan, resting in deep cisterns, slipping through covered culverts beneath the causeways. By the time it reaches my tier, it has carried the cold of a hundred stone faces and the dissolved salt of a mile of limestone. It moves so slowly here that you can lay your palm flat on a weir and barely feel the pull against your skin—just a steady, patient thickening, and then a silent fold over the slate lip.

This is where the valley tries hardest to close itself. Because the water sits longest in these low pans, the white calcite crusts build fastest along the sluices, brittle and sharp as broken shell, choking the notches if they are left for even a few nights. And in the corners where the sun does reach for one fierce, vertical hour at midday, the pale grey-green algae gathers in paper-thin films against the submerged blocks.

So my work is small, slow, and close to the water.

I move along the waist-high walls with my knees dark from clay and my palms greenish-gray from the copper I hold. I rarely need the slate visor—by the time I emerge, the rim has already swallowed the sun, and the bottom of the basin is oiled black—so it hangs loose against my collarbone on its cord, clicking softly against my ribs when I kneel. My eyes stay half-shut anyway, heavy-lidded, trusting the broad, pale callouses of my feet to know the slickness of the stone better than sight ever could.

If another keeper comes along the same wall, carrying a sling-bag of wet clay or a bundle of dried stalks from the deep chambers, I step down into a knee-deep pan without a sound and wait, motionless, letting the cold water ring my shins until they pass. Neither of us looks at the other’s face. There is nothing to say. In a bowl of stone and flat water, a word would bounce off three walls and linger where it isn’t wanted, muddying the quiet we need to hear the machine work.

Instead, I tilt my head and listen.

From down here, you can hear the whole valley above you, translated into small, distinct notes. A hollow *plink* from a covered cistern two tiers up tells me a chamber is still half-empty, taking its time. A wet, heavy *click* far to the north means a stone plug has seated itself under the weight of a full pan. And every few nights, beneath the causeway at my feet, there is the sound I am really waiting for: a thin, wrong *hiss*, no louder than breath through teeth.

A weeping seam.

That is where I belong most—not even on the causeways, but down in the narrow, dripping pocket between two brimming cisterns, wedged shoulder-to-shoulder with the dark masonry. You pry up a slate slab, drop into the cold gap where the water presses against the other side of the blocks, and feel along the joinery with your fingertips until you find the tiny, icy thread forcing its way through. You pack the dark, fine-grained clay into the crack with the heel of your thumb, working it deep until the hiss stops and the silence returns, whole and tight.

Then back up onto the wall, to scrape the salt-crusts from the notched weirs with the copper chisel, leaving the limestone edges sharp enough to nick skin, and to gather the slimy algae mats from the oldest pans into a woven pouch so they can dry on the warm stones below.

By the time the first pale light touches the highest retaining walls far above my head, the lowest tier is clear. The water slides over fresh-cut slate without a sound, disappearing into the last covered culverts to feed the blind, rootless things waiting in the dark beneath us. I lower the cover over my shaft, pull the slate flush into its groove, and climb back down into the dry, quiet earth—just another piece of the masonry, resting until the glare is gone.
