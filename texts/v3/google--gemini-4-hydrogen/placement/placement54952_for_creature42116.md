I would not be the one who climbed up from the lowlands with a gourd and a bone awl, took a taste of the sweet sap, and went back down into the clouds. If I found myself on that high shelf, I would not leave it. And I would not want eyes to squint against the midday glare, or blood to rush and hurry through veins in a place where nothing else is in a hurry.

I would be one of the domes.

At midday, you would not see me. I would be wedged into the damp shadow behind the roots of the oldest tree—the one nearest the back wall, where the limestone sweats without stopping and the forty-foot drops fall into the deepest basin. Pressed flat against the wet slate, rim sealed tight, I would be just another asymmetrical knot of travertine, pitted by decades of mineral drip and tufted with emerald moss. 

From the outside, nothing happens. Inside, the whole amphitheater is awake in me.

Without eyes, space is made of pressure and pitch. Beneath the floor, the flooded chimneys sing their continuous, organ-like bass note, and I feel it through the cartilage of my lower rim—a map of where the rock is solid and where it is hollow, where the warm updrafts are strongest. Every few breaths, a heavy drop detaches from the overhang and strikes the deep basin nearby. *Tong.* A bronze-bowl ring that shivers straight through my water-sacs. By the pitch of that ring, I know how full the pool is. A half-tone higher than last week; the basin is nearing its lip.

Above me, high in the moss-wrapped boughs, I can feel the leaf-bladders swelling. It is not a sound, exactly—more a slow, tightening pull in the wood of the tree, a weight gathering over weeks. I wait for it the way other creatures wait for dawn.

When the afternoon mist finally rises over the sheer lip of the cliff, dimming the fierce sun and cooling the slate, I stir.

First, a faint, glassy overtone inside my shell as my chambers equalize. Then I lift an inch from the floor, extend four thick, translucent pillars of water and cartilage, and rise clear of the shallow mirror-puddle. I do not walk so much as glide, in slow, deliberate pulses that leave the water barely troubled behind me.

Because I live beside the oldest tree, I know its anomalies. When I climb its spongy trunk, gripping the wet moss with my sensitive rim, I pass the pale, healed scar on the lower bark—deeper and more ragged than the neat marks my kind leaves when we press our pores to drink the mineral-sweet sap. Something fast made that wound, something warm-blooded that carried its own rhythm up the cliff and took it away again. Down in the dry crevice behind the roots sits the small, sharp wedge of flint it left behind. I have brushed against it before. It does not hum with the rest of the limestone; it is dense, cold, and deaf, a piece of another world that cannot hear the mountain.

I climb past the scar, grazing on silver-green lichen, drinking from the condensation runnels, waiting for the tension in the canopy to break.

When it comes, it is everything at once. The stem above flexes; a gallon of water drops like a fallen stone onto the hollow slate. The *boom* rolls around the curved walls of the amphitheater and slams through my body. It is violent and wonderful—a sudden weather that shakes the whole jungle-island. A heavy, calcified plate along the ridge of my dome cracks cleanly and slides off, clattering onto the rock. Without the booms, we would grow too thick with stone, too rooted to move, until we fused into the floor like the trees. The shock keeps me light enough to wander.

In the quiet aftershock, while the empty leaf-bladder curls slowly back upright to begin another month of filling, a fine, pale dust drifts down from the orchids high in the branches. It settles onto the wet moss, and onto the fresh, pearlescent crust of my back.

That is when I descend.

I cross the open, bare slate in the cool mist, carrying the orchid dust toward the next tree-island across the humming floor. Halfway there, I pause at the rim of the deepest basin to draw in lime-rich water. Another dome is already there—one of the dozen or so of us scattered across the shelf, though I have no name for it and no way to tell it from the boulders except by the way it answers the cliff.

We do not touch. We simply stay still at the water’s edge, letting our internal chambers hum together in a brief, glassy harmony, while far below us in the dark, the pale salamander hangs motionless, untouched, waiting out the slow rhythms of the pools.

Then we part—one toward the sunlit lip, one toward the distant boughs—each of us carrying a dusting of pollen across the stone, keeping the lonely jungles alive, listening to the mountain breathe.
