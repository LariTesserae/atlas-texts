To live here at all, I would have to stop being an animal.

Anything with lungs and a quick pulse would only be an interruption in the basin—a noisy, sweating thing flinching at the *thoom* of the cisterns, squinting through the steam, waiting for a horizon that never comes. There are no flies here to keep me company, no rats to startle at my boots. To belong to this place, I would have to shed bone and hurry alike, and wake up instead as something the water itself could bear to carry.

I think I would find myself on the fourth tier, high up in the permanent veil of mist just beneath the blackened trusses.

Down on the lower walkways, the basin is all surge and appetite—the great cast-iron beam engines rocking through their thirty-second strokes, the flooded sumps drinking the runoff, the dark-emerald ferns choking the brick arches. Down there, the air is thick with the work of lifting water. Up on the fourth tier, the roar arrives softened through three levels of perforated iron grates and wet foliage. You still feel the cisterns in the soles of your feet—that deep, chest-rattling *thoom* every half-minute—but up here it shares the space with quieter sounds: the steady *shhh* of condensation sheeting down corrugated tin, and the *plink-thunk* of heavy drops falling from the iron roof-ribs.

I would be one of the tenders—the ones who stay so high in the mist that anything below barely knows we are there.

I would stand roughly the height of a man, though stooped a little to clear the low-slung copper pipes that sweat warm droplets onto my shoulders. No eyes to trouble with the bruised, twilight-colored glow seeping through the wired glass overhead. No mouth to taste the boiled iron and crushed greenery in the air, because I would be made of the same stuff: a dense, braided column of pale root-fiber, toughened by decades of steam and darkened on the outside by a crust of black engine grease and verdigris. Over my upper half, a broad cowl of overlapping fungal shelves—translucent, copper-tinted, slick with mineral dew and faintly warm.

I would not think in sentences. I would think in pressure and vibration.

The perforated iron grate beneath my broad, pad-like soles would be my whole map of the world. Through it, I would read the basin the way a hand reads a pulse: the slow sink of the piston, the groan of the sluice gate, the thirty-second pause before the counterweight returns. And beside that great hydraulic rhythm, I would feel the smaller, tighter hum of the oil-cooled transformers strapped to the brick piers, feeding the dim amber bulbs in their wire cages.

If I have a particular habit—a leftover trace of whatever I was before—it would be lingering near the junctions where the water and the electricity touch.

The others spend most of their slow hours down near the boiler housings and the main flanges, weaving pale root-gaskets into weeping joints or leaning their grease-slicked weight against ferns that have strayed into the gear-wheels. I would do that too, when the grates told me a pipe was losing pressure. I would take ten or twenty strokes of the engine just to turn and settle my weight forward, one heavy transfer per swing of the counterweights, drawing warm groundwater up into my core until a limb stiffened like a charged hose, venting a quiet hiss of steam from my flank as I reached.

But mostly, I would stay near the roof-ribs and the highest transformer piers. Up there, you can feel what the wired glass is doing. Even without eyes, you know when whatever lies outside the basin turns colder, because the steam rising from the sumps hits the glass and condenses faster. The rain on the corrugated tin thickens. The *plink-thunk* quickens by a fraction, sending a heavier load back down through the tiers, and the beam engines below take up the weight without changing their pace.

And I would keep watch—if you can call it watching—over the amber bulbs and the small, patient things that gather near them. Not for the light, which means nothing to me, but for the dry warmth of the glass and the steady hum of the coils. When a transformer housing grows a little too choked with waxy vines, or when a leaking flange overhead begins to drip cold condensate straight onto a junction box, I would lean in over the course of an hour, weave a tight, swollen collar of my own root-tissue around the copper seam, and leave it behind to stiffen into the permanent crust of the pipe. Beneath me, the flat plates of mineral and mycelium would keep creeping an inch a day toward the amber glass, undisturbed.

Sometimes, on the narrowest cantilevered catwalks where the mist hangs thickest, another tender would come up from the lower tiers—perhaps to dry its fungal crown near the trusses, or to check a groaning valve. There is no room to pass, and no need to hurry. We would simply lean together for a dozen strokes of the engine. Six minutes of stillness, while our damp mantles pressed close. A slow trickle of mineral-rich water would pass between us, along with a few copper-tinted spores in the steam, and a faint, stray charge borrowed from the dynamos. Nothing spoken, nothing named—just a quiet confirmation that the pressure was holding, and the rhythm was clean. Then, with the patience of two shifting walls of brick, we would slide apart, one descending toward the warm sumps to drink, the other turning back toward the glass.

I think I could stay like that for a very long time—longer than any animal life could measure.

And when the decades finally caught up with me, there would be no dread in it. My braided core would simply grow too heavy with mineral scale to draw the warm water up into my limbs. A lift that once took a single stroke of the engine would take two, then four, then none at all. I would choose a brick pier near one of the humming transformers—somewhere warm, out of the way of the gears—and press my flank against the wet mortar. I would sink my root-fibers into the seam between the bricks and let the waxy vines climb my shoulders.

In a year, I would be indistinguishable from the wall, just another crusted stanchion sweating in the jaundiced halo of an amber bulb. And in ten, a new shoot of pale, fibrous root would have already detached itself from my base, swollen with warm groundwater, and taken its first slow, unhurried step onto the vibrating grates.
