I would not be in the center of the plazas, where the sound folds back on itself from every side and the great arches stand thick enough to make a roof of noise.

I would be further out, near the eastern lip of the cordillera, where a long, narrow causeway of braided kelp-timber runs out to a buttress that clings directly to the drop-off. Behind me, the valley is a full, roaring bowl. In front of me, the mountain simply stops, and the turf ends in a sheer, felted edge above a mile of nothing.

That is where I would settle: half-sunk in the bruised-plum moss at the base of the last arch, with one broad, flat palm resting against the root-pillar and the rest of my weight given over to the cold.

I am one of the ones who made this, though the memory of making is no longer a thought—it is only a shape my hands still know. My bones are heavy with mineral, dense enough to keep me anchored on the bottom without effort. When I breathe—if you can call the slow, tidal draw of water across my chest breathing—the rhythm is not mine. It belongs to the bellows-swimmers drifting past in the dark, their vast acoustic bladders swelling and emptying until the low, organ-pipe booming passes straight through my ribs and settles in the hollow chambers behind my sternum.

I have no eyes. Where a surface creature would carry two wet stones to catch the light, my brow is smooth, unbroken, slightly concave, sloping down to broad, flat cheekbones. I do not miss what I never had, or what I gave up so long ago that even the idea of *light* has no purchase here. What I have instead is the skin along my ribs, the undersides of my long forearms, and the deep, fluted grooves scored into the sides of my neck. Fine, fleshy filaments tremble there in the slow current, and twin rows of recessed pits read every shift in pressure as cleanly as a surface eye reads the edge of a shadow.

From my post at the lip of the drop-off, the world is divided into two kinds of sound.

Behind me, the highland is bright with noise. Every hollow viaduct, every porous curtain of eelgrass, every lone spire out on the open meadows casts a distinct acoustic shadow. I can feel the *clack-clack-clack* of the snapping things packed inside the arch beside me—millions of small, segmented bodies turning the ancient, lignified wood into a dry, chattering throat. I can feel the wet, collective *shhhk-shhhk* of the grazers blanketing the terraces, cropping the microscopic film off the masonry. When a bellows-swimmer glides overhead, trailing its faint, useless string of blue sparks, its passing carves a wide, moving trough through the clatter, and I read its size, its speed, and its age by the way the echo returns from the colonnades behind me.

In front of me, over the ledge, the sound does not return.

It spills out into the abyssal dark and simply thins, swallowed by a mile of open water before it can strike the true floor of the world. Sitting at the edge, with one side of my neck turned toward the valley and the other toward the drop, is like resting my cheek against a warm hearth while my back faces an open door. It is the only place in the drowned highland where you can feel the exact boundary of what we grew.

A grazer has climbed onto my left shoulder. I felt its hard, fist-sized shell tip over the ridge of my collarbone minutes ago, and now its rasping mandibles are moving steadily across the slate-grey hide of my upper arm, clearing away the thin, slick film that settles there during years of stillness. I do not move. The small, wet scraping is company, and it keeps my skin clean, and it adds one more tiny voice to the valley’s chord. Only if it wanders too high—if its antennae brush the sensitive, trembling filaments along the fluted grooves of my jaw—will I lift my right hand, slow as a drifting frond, take it gently by the edges of its shell between my spatulate fingertips, and set it back onto the turf.

I do not hurry. Nothing here has hurried since the last holdfast gripped the mountain spine and the last young kelp-timber stiffened into its curve.

Sometimes, though, I rise.

It is not a sudden thing. First, my double-jointed wrists flex, pressing my broad palms into the springy, leather-like turf to test the ground. Then I draw my long legs beneath me, stirring no silt—there is none to stir—and stand, letting the slow current lean against my chest. A grazer tumbles from my thigh, flashes a brief, sickly pale-green spark in irritation, and settles back into the moss.

I walk the causeway inward, taking long, buoyant, leaning steps, my arms trailing low so my calloused fingertips brush the velvet floor for balance. Every few paces, I lay my palm against the side of a root-arch. My fingers still remember how to hold green, springy wood against the current for months until it lignified; now, they only test the tension of the thousand-year-old graft. I press gently, listening through the pits in my forearm for the hairline vibration that would mean a hollow beam is beginning to split, or for the dull, muffled thud that means a rogue shoot of eelgrass has sprouted inside a cavity and is choking the snapping things’ resonance.

Today, near the third arch, I find one—a thin, pale-olive tendril, stubbornly pushing out through a seam where two timbers were knotted centuries ago. In a world locked in finished peace, clutter is the only rot. I pinch the shoot cleanly at its base between thumb and forefinger, pull it free, and drop it onto the turf where the nearest herd of grazers will find it.

Further in, I hear the low, heavy displacement of others.

No one speaks; there is no need for a voice when the basin itself is roaring. Yet three of us have converged from different ridges onto the broad, open-air plaza at the center of the highland, drawn by the same subtle shift in the valley’s hum—perhaps a swimmer’s carcass sinking toward the southern terraces, or simply the long, slow turn of an uncounted decade.

We stand widely spaced in the dark, facing different directions. I do not touch the others, though I can feel the dense, heavy shapes of them through the pressure-pits along my ribs, interrupting the *shhhk-shhhk* of the distant walls. One of us raises a broad forearm and leans a flank against the porous masonry of a surrounding arch, blocking a row of chattering cavities. Another steps slightly back from a hollow viaduct, opening a gap.

I turn my chest toward the open water and hold my palms out flat, catching the low, booming pulse of a passing swimmer and deflecting it toward the spire behind me.

For an hour, or a day, we alter the shape of the roar. We damp the dry hailstone clatter of the arches until the deep, organ-pipe bass swells to fill the whole basin, vibrating in our teeth and our hollow chests; then we shift our weight, letting the high, wet scraping of the grazers rush back in to crown it. There is no melody in it, no beginning and no end. We are only running our hands over the face of the valley we grew, feeling the arches, the herds, and the open water, making sure everything is still in its place and still breathing.

When it is enough, we lower ourselves one by one.

I do not walk back to the eastern edge this time. I sink down right there on the plaza’s bruised-plum turf, tucking my long limbs close to my ribs, letting the cold mountain water settle over my scarred shoulders like a heavy, familiar coat. My brow is smooth and dark. My chest slows until it matches the long, rolling pulse of the swimmers overhead. Somewhere nearby, a grazer climbs onto my ankle and begins to rasp, and the thunderous, unchanging hum of the drowned highland closes over me once more.
