I would not be passing through. Nothing passes through here unless it is already dead and falling toward the rim.

I would be wedged into the low breach of a collapsed wall, just inside the mouth of a single-room shelter on the second terrace down from the ridge. From the outside, in the bruised dark, you would only see a rounded hump of silt-crusted fiber, no different from the river-worn slate stacked beside it. You would have to lay a hand against the cold stone—and wait through the turn of a tide—to feel that I am under tension.

Inside, there is no dark and no light, only weight and pitch.

My center is a cool, packed certainty: eleven river-worn cobbles of dark slate, a handful of limestone grit, and one small, notched flint weight I swallowed decades ago when the gravel shifted beneath me. They sit heavy in my leathery pouch, anchoring me against the subterranean river that pours across the shelf. When the outer current surges, it pushes against my back like a solid wall, trying to roll me out of the gap and over the terrace lip. I do not fight it with muscle. I simply let my fibers drink the pressure, tighten around my stones, and settle deeper into the silt.

I am past the wandering years.

Once, when my pouch held only three pebbles and a pinch of grit, I crossed the open flats between the groves. It took the better part of a year. I would draw water into my core, ferment a single, tight pocket of gas until my belly lifted a hair’s breadth off the slate, and let the current nudge me four inches downstream before the pore opened and I dropped back with a dull *clack*. I remember—not in pictures, for I have no eyes, but in the worn smoothness of my underside—the long, scoured track I left across the glowing moss, and the slow, metallic sting of passing over a pitted copper awl half-buried in the mud. That trace of copper is still in my veins, a faint, sharp flavor in the cold.

Now I am too heavy to lift. A stone slipped from this shelter’s wall thirty winters ago, widening a gap and letting the roaring current scour out the quiet eddy inside. I crept up into the breach, wrapped my tough, mineral-secreting braids around the fallen slate, and wedged myself in to plug it.

This is where I live: at the seam between the roar and the quiet.

Across the narrow gap of the shelter’s mouth, I have spun seven listening-threads, anchored to the lintel above and weighted at their lower ends with pebbles just heavy enough to keep them taut. They are my only skin for the wider valley. When the thirty-foot organ-reeds half a mile away catch the turning tide, their deep, groaning chord travels through the dense water and strikes my threads. They hum cleanly, vibrating fast enough to shake off the pale limestone dust settling on them, and a slow, chemical shiver of green-white light ripples through my fungal veins in answer.

I know the valley’s hours by that hum, and by the sudden, chest-deep *thump* of a bellows-kelp venting its gas into the dark, and by the dry, bone-like clatter of glass-ferns breaking in the lee of the groves.

Inside the quiet eddy behind me, the water slows and drops its burden. Pale spores and shattered, calcified fern-shards drift down like cold snow. I unspool my sticky, pale-grey hyphae across the floor to gather them in, drawing the brittle husks into my pouch. As the outer current rocks my heavy body—imperceptibly, a fraction of a millimeter back and forth—the eleven cobbles inside me rub together. *Crunch. Pause. Crunch.* Slowly, on the timescale of the slate, I mill the glass-ferns into a fine mineral paste.

I do not know what kind of hands stacked these flat stones, or why they left a carved bowl wedged between the roots nearby. To me, the wall is simply the thing that breaks the current, and I am the thing that mends the wall.

Already, the limestone grit in the water is beginning to crust my outer skin, stiffening the old, grey-olive braids into something closer to stone. In a few more years, I will stop tightening my pouch altogether. I will let the silt pack into my seams and harden, becoming another rounded boulder in the breach. Two younger, lighter knots have already crept into the shelter behind me, drawn by the quiet eddy I hold open; I can feel the faint, rhythmic crunch of their milling against my back, and the delicate tug where one of them has anchored a fresh listening-thread to my petrifying skin.

I will hold the current off them, and listen to the reeds turn the tide, until there is no difference left between me and the slate.
