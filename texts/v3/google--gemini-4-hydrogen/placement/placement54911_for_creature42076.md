At first, if I tried to imagine myself there, I would only be a brief, frantic thing—a shape tumbling sideways in the bruised grey, lungs burning, slammed against a rounded basalt pillar until the undertow carried me off into the silt-fog like one of the torn jellies. A human body has no business in a place where the weather is made of water and the only things that last are stone and stubbornness.

So I have to let go of that shape. I have to sink until my belly finds the slate, until the cold stops feeling like an enemy and becomes simply the medium I am made of.

I find myself low. That is the first truth of this world: everything that survives here either bends like a ribbon or anchors like a wedge. I am the wedge. I feel the broad, flat weight of my ribs pressed into the eroded seam between two flagstones, my four short limbs splayed wide, blunt claws hooked deep into the mortarless dark. Above my ridged back, a horizontal gale of meltwater tears across the plaza, but my shovel-shaped head is tucked into my shoulders, and the current slides up and over me as if I were nothing more than a fallen lintel.

My eyes are milky, useless things, registering only the slow, bruised brightening when a cloud of chalky silt thins overhead, and the sudden shadow of a silver-bellied shoal wheeling past a pylon. Sight is for air. Down here, I see with my flanks and my jaw. Along the edges of my snout and down my sides, the recessed grooves drink in the water’s smallest tremors, turning the dim emptiness into a sharp, roaring architecture. I don’t need light to know that fifty yards to my left, a sunken stairwell is boiling with an icy upwelling, or that just beyond the colonnade, a drowned aqueduct is sucking the current into a silent, freezing jet. Every broken doorway hums with its own distinct turbulence.

If I ask *who* or *what* I am among the dwellers, I know I am not one of the young ones, pale and soft-shelled, darting like eels through the quiet corners of the cisterns. Nor am I in my prime, slinging myself down the culverts like a stone from a sling to snap blinded fish out of a silt-bloom.

I am older than that. The mineral crusts have begun to settle into the creases of my hide, pale and chalky, and the slick film of bacteria coats my back in the places where the current rarely scours me. My claws are worn down to stubby, iron-hard nubs. I have crossed the plazas enough times to know that the valley is not permanent—that even the fitted slate is only passing through, grinding down, softening at the edges, waiting to give way.

I find myself at the threshold of a sunken amphitheater, wedged in the lee of a low, thick-walled cistern.

It is a place between the storm and the stillness. Out in front of me, the stepped terraces drop away into the churning grey, where the pale, eyeless crustaceans pack themselves into the seams and the translucent eels ride the shear-zones like ghosts who still remember the dry-land doors. Behind me, through the low, rounded mouth of the cistern, the water goes heavy and calm. Inside, the chalky mud lies deep on the floor. A female is curled in the far corner around a cluster of leathery eggs, her broad tail sweeping in slow, hypnotic arcs to keep the settling silt from suffocating them. And deeper in the dark, near the back wall, two of the oldest have already tucked their limbs beneath their chins and gone quiet, becoming basalt again.

I am not ready for the mud yet. I stay at the lip.

Here, with my sternum pressed flat against the ancient paving, I am a listener. The whole valley is a single drumhead of fitted stone, and my ribs know its language better than my eyes ever could. I feel the sharp, rhythmic coughs of my own gill-chambers flushing swallowed grit, and beneath that, the vast, muffled pulse of the drowned mountain.

Sometimes, a mile off in the dimness, a retaining wall finally loses the last of its ten-thousand-year-old mortar and collapses in slow motion. The *thump* reaches me through the slate long before the bloom of silt arrives—a dull, heavy tremor in my chest, followed by the faint, dry hiss of sliding gravel. Across the valley, I feel the others answer it. One by one, out in the plazas and down the rippled stair-ramps, heavy forelimbs lift and strike the floor.

*Thud. Thud-thud.*

The younger ones are converging on the feast—the displaced crustaceans, the newly opened crevices. I don’t follow them. I stay anchored in the lee of the cistern wall, prying a few stray filter-feeders from the seam beneath my chin, watching the faint wake of an eel as it threads a doorway I used to hunt through when my claws were sharper.

Instead, I lift my right forelimb—stiff with mineral crust, heavy as a river cobble—and bring my calloused heel down against the slate.

*Thud.*

Not a claim on a fallen wall. Not a warning of a shifting boulder. Just the steady, cold-blooded announcement that I am still here, anchored at the edge of the quiet, feeling the valley wear itself down one slow tremor at a time.
