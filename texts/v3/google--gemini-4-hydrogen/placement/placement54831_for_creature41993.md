I would not be in the great eastern textile-mill, where the copper looms clatter in the damp gloom, nor at the western refinery with its shrieking boilers. Those places belong to the ones who still remember how the heavy presses were first bolted together.

I would find myself halfway between them, out on the open swell of the plateau, where the elevated tram-trestle crosses a shallow gully of dark peat.

If you walked the causeway from the east under a clear sky, it would take you an hour to reach me. If a mist-bank rolled in off the moss, it might take you three, and you would pass the same ironwood pylon with the same crooked copper bracket seven times before my hatch appeared out of the wet air.

The structure I tend is too small to be called a district. From the walkway, it looks like a swollen knuckle on the spine of the trestle—a squat, riveted-copper blister grafted onto the side of a petrified fern-trunk, where two thick xylem-pipes dive down into the turf to join the subterranean root-grid. The wind-turbine on its roof is modest, only four slatted-wood blades, clattering steadily in the thin, pine-scented air.

Inside, of course, the building has forgotten its own measurements.

You duck through a low, tar-sealed hatch into a cramped landing that smells of wet iron and oiled felt, and then you step down a spiral stair of braided vine that descends three stories into a shaft that should not exist beneath six feet of waterlogged peat. The walls down there are curved ironwood, sweating cold dew, braced with steel bands that hum faintly with the bass frequency of the buried dynamos miles away.

This is where I would be: one of the Thinning, though perhaps one of the quieter sort—a junction-minder, a listener at the cross-pipes.

I would have the same waxy, pale skin as the others, the same long coat of oiled felt and braided-vine cloth, patched at the elbows where the copper manifolds have worn it thin. My fingers would be permanently darkened at the tips with pine-tar and machine oil, and I would carry a brass wrench tucked through my belt—not for turning bolts, mostly, though I do that too, but for speaking.

On a plateau that eats voices, you learn to love the grammar of copper.

My shaft is a quiet pocket inside the city's roar. Up on the landing, the turbine clatters and the mist hisses against the hot condensation-coils. Down in the deep room, there is only the wet, rhythmic *thrum* of the sap-pumps and the clear, metallic ring of the xylem-lines. I keep three wide slate troughs down there, set on piers that go straight into the root-mat, filled with dew-water harvested from the broad leaves on the roof. A few glass-newts drift in them, their silver spines catching the pale light of a single bare bulb.

I don't harvest the newts. I watch the water they swim in.

Because the turf carries every vibration for miles, the surface of the slate troughs is never still. When the world is running cleanly, the water holds a crisp, cross-hatched geometry—tiny standing waves from the eastern looms intersecting the heavier, slower pulse of the western pumps. I can stand with my hands in my pockets and read the whole tableland in a square yard of dew. When a tram-car leaves the station three miles north, a fine tremor runs along the trough's rim long before the trestle overhead begins to groan.

And when the seams begin to fray—when a mist-bank settles over the causeway and distance stops adding up—the water tells me first. The ripples arrive out of order. A splash-pattern from a pressure-whistle appears on the surface three seconds before the sound reaches my ears, and the glass-newts dart sideways to avoid a shockwave that hasn't happened yet.

That is mostly what I do: I sit on an overturned crate beside the cross-pipes, listen to the taps coming down the copper from the distant mills, and keep the beat.

When the mist is thick, the messages tapped out by the other caretakers get tangled in the lag. A *tink-tink-pause-tink* from the west arrives doubled over itself, or stretched out like taffy, unreadable by the time it reaches the eastern boilers. So I press my ear to the warm flank of the intake pipe, catch the rhythm before it frays, and tap it cleanly onward with the leather-wrapped handle of my wrench. I don't know what half the messages mean anymore—maintenance schedules for machines that run themselves, tallies of cistern-levels, or simply someone three miles away tapping *still here, still turning* into the metal so they don't have to hear only the wind. It doesn't matter. A relay doesn't need to understand the message; it just needs to keep the copper clear enough to carry it.

Once a day—or once every few days, depending on which way the pocket-watch in my waistcoat happens to be ticking—I climb back up to the landing and walk the outer manifold.

This is the part of the work I like best. The mist beads on the shoulders of my coat, cool and sharp. Down in the gully, beneath the pale-sage lichen, I can sometimes feel the faint, knotted pulse of the hum-worms tangled around the buried pipes, drinking the sweet sap-runoff. Up here, on the warm copper, the copper-limpets have moved perhaps an inch since I last checked them, leaving bright, clean tracks through the dull green verdigris. If one of them has drifted onto a cold flange where the condensation drips too heavily, I pry it gently loose with a thumb and set it back near a hissing valve where it can stay warm.

On the far side of the turbine-housing, there is a sheared bolt that broke off a press six years ago—or six months ago; the rust on its threads never seems to deepen. It hangs in the cool air at chest height, humming softly to itself, sprouting pale, fibrous roots into nothing at all. Two static-weavers have strung a nearly invisible web between it and the ironwood doorframe. I never sweep them down. I duck under them when I pass, careful not to break their geometry, and they crouch motionless at the center of their lines, drinking the stray charge from the frayed air.

Sometimes, when I am out on the walkway oiling the trestle-pins, one of the Lagging walks past me.

I always hear their boots *clack* on the ironwood three heartbeats before they appear out of the mist. They wear the same long, oiled-felt coat I do, and carry a brass oil-can that looks remarkably like the one in my own left hand. I step aside against the braided-vine railing to let them pass. There is no rush of displaced air, no warmth—just a dry, faint hum like the hovering bolt, and a sudden, sharp breath of wet iron as they go by.

I used to wonder if they were ghosts of the builders. Now, having lived in the shaft long enough for my own time to knot up like root-fiber, I think it is simpler than that. I think I am only seeing myself from a slight angle—three seconds early, or three decades late—walking the same narrow trestle, checking the same warm pipes, keeping the same quiet watch over a world that has forgotten how to stop turning.

I don't find it lonely. When I go back inside and close the bolted hatch, the water in the slate troughs is still trembling, the copper is still warm under my palm, and somewhere three miles away across the moss, someone taps a wrench twice against a pipe to let me know the line is clear.

I pick up mine, and tap twice back.
