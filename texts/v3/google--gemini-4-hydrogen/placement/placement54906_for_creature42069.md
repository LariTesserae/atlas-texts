I would not be up near the distant rim of hills, where the cloud-lid thins to a smear of gray and the dark feels slightly less complete. And I would not be in the canopy, where the branches interlock into a springy roof and the bats slip past with little puffs of displaced heat. There is too much air up there, too many small, shifting edges.

I would be low, down where the compacted silt gives way to sweat-slick clay, in a gully that slumped a decade or two ago and never quite healed.

It is the kind of place you only find by following the hum until it changes shape. Along the flat expanses, the sixty-hertz pulse is broad and muffled by six feet of damp loam—a steady, featureless floor beneath your heels. But here, the bank has slid inward, tearing back the mat of fern roots and exposing a heavy, flanged collar of cast iron where two buried lines meet and angle away toward different parts of the dark.

That is where I would settle. Not out of any loyalty to the iron, and not because I know what moves inside it, but because the hum here has a flaw.

If you lay your cheekbone against the collar, past the sleeping spiders that blanket the bolts, the vibration isn’t quite clean. Beneath the monotonous sixty-hertz drone, there is a tiny, rhythmic *burr*—a microscopic catch in the metal, like a fingernail dragging across a rough seam, repeating every forty or fifty beats. Now and then, a single bead of warm, dark oil weeps from the gasket, heavy with a mineral tang that cuts straight through the smell of crushed leaves and hog-musk.

To live here, I would have to be one of the clay-grey ones—the slow, eyeless gleaners who wear the valley floor on their shins and elbows. Anything with fur would cook in the ninety-five-degree blanket; anything with eyes would only carry two useless, aching hollows through the pitch-black brush. I would want the loose, bare skin, faintly tacky with sweat and silt, and the sealed, smooth hollows beneath my brow where dust and thorns can’t catch. I would want the ribs that give a little when I slide between two thorny trunks, and the long-toed, spongy feet that can feel a millipede or a buried cable before my weight ever commits.

Most of my kind keep moving, pacing the long, straight corridors from one inspection plate to the next, meeting at the junctions only long enough to twine their legs around a surfaced cable and trade a few slow counts of pressure. They are built for the whole length of the grid.

I think I would be the one who stopped walking it.

Not all at once. Maybe at first I only lingered at the slumped gully for a few nights, curled around the iron collar with my throat pressed to the warm bolts, listening with my jawbone to that tiny stutter in the line. In a valley with no horizon, no stars, and no weather to speak of, a flaw in a steady wave is the closest thing to a landmark—the closest thing to news. You start to wonder, without words, if the burr is widening. You start to measure the nights by how long it takes the next bead of oil to swell at the gasket.

So my loops through the thickets would grow smaller and tighter, anchored to that one exposed joint.

At night—though here it is always night, only thicker or slightly less thick—I would peel myself slowly off the iron, letting the spiders transfer from my shoulders back to the steel without hurrying them. Moving at the pace of condensation gathering on a leaf, I would drift out into the waist-high grass around the gully. My palm would sweep down the bark ridges of a hardwood, gathering the papery, resting moths and the shoulder-to-shoulder beetles, chewing them slowly, shell and all. I would wait under a broad fern frond with my tongue out, catching the heavy drop of water just before it fell into the dust, then thread my way carefully over the ribs of a sleeping hog in its wallow, laying a flat, warm hand on its flank so its breathing wouldn’t hitch.

Then, before the gray smear even thought about touching the distant hills, I would follow the hum back down into the clay.

I would curl around the flange again, tucking my knees against the damp earth, pressing my temple to the metal where the burr lives. When one of the travelers comes down the line—announced first by a faint muting in the soles of my feet, half a mile off, then by the thickening of the heat and the braided scent of silt and machine oil—I wouldn’t lift my head or make a sound in the air.

I would just wait until they lay down on the opposite side of the collar, pressed their jawbone to the same iron, and felt the catch in the pulse. I would tap my teeth together three slow times, sending a tiny ripple down the steel to show them where the gasket weeps, and then let my weight settle back into the hum.

They would move on after an hour or two, sliding back into the root-mat toward the next junction. I would stay, licking the salt and oil from the seam, keeping the iron smooth beneath the creepers, listening through my bones to the deep, unhurried work of things I will never see.
