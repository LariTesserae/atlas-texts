It would not begin with sight. The light in the blister is too bright for that—a pale, stinging blue that bounces off raw quartz and ice until there is no up or down, only glare. 

It would begin in the teeth. A low, steady ache where the jawbone meets the skull, and beneath it, a deeper hum in the breastbone, like a heavy timber being dragged across stone. Not noise, exactly—there are no ears to catch noise here—just the fact of the mountain’s weight pressing in through the water, continuous and solid as a wall.

And then the cold: thin, sharp, rushing across my ribs. When I open the pleats along my flanks, the water pours through them, bright with tiny silver bubbles that prickle like frost. When I clamp them shut, the current slides past me smooth and fast, trying to peel me off the rock.

That is where I would find myself: wedged into the leeward notch behind a blade of pale quartz, one broad, calloused hand hooked into a seam in the basalt, the other resting against the sinew-lashing of a slate cairn. I would not be swimming in the open blue. Nothing that wants to stay whole swims the middle of this room.

I would be one of the listeners.

There are only four of us in this pocket, and each of us knows the whole eighty paces by heart, the way a blind creature knows the inside of a shell. One of the others is bolder at the crossings—the one who watches the gas-filled bladder-weeds spin into the shear-zones and shred, counting the eddies, waiting for the three-heartbeat lull after a ceiling pocket detonates so they can unhook their tether and hurl themselves across the void on the strength of their copper ballast. Another is the one who climbs the ice-chimneys when the winter storms bury the skylights in black snow, hauling their heavy, awkward body out into the dry wind to pry goat-bone and frozen root from the timberline scree.

I can do both when I must. My harness is scarred from the copper chunks banging against my hips, and my palms are leather-hard from gripping wet glass. I have felt the terrible lightness of the open air, the way the world outside the mountain has no weight to hold you up and no roar to steady you. I never stay up there long.

My place is in the dead-zone, where the current splits around the jutting quartz and leaves a narrow pocket of bruised, swirling calm.

Above me, hung on thick leather thongs from a bone hook driven deep into a soft seam, are the stone bells. Seven slabs of slate, hollowed out with patient scraping until each one has its own thickness, its own voice. In the wash at the edge of the dead-zone, they knock against each other endlessly—a frantic, dry clatter that cuts straight through the low cavitation-thunder of the room.

When I crouch there, with my milky lids drawn half-shut against the glare and my chin pressed flat to the bare stone wall, I can read the whole mountain through those bells.

If the snowfields miles above us have begun to melt fast in a sudden warm wind, the pressurized jets in the floor widen, the cross-currents swell, and the outermost bell—the thinnest, highest-pitched slab—catches the edge of the wash and begins to chatter twice as fast. If a ceiling fissure chokes shut with ice, the rhythm goes uneven, skipping a beat before the shockwave hits. And once in a great while, when the mountain itself groans and climbs another fraction of an inch into the sky, the whole flooded bell tilts so slightly that no eye could ever see it—but the dead-zone shifts by the width of a finger. Suddenly the second bell falls silent, and the fourth begins to swing.

That is what I wait for. That small, stubborn change in the tempo.

When I am not pressing my jaw to the wall, I tend the moss and the cords. I slide down behind the cairns—into the only real shadow in this blinding blue lens—and graze the tough, pale-green crusts slowly with my flat teeth, careful never to tear them from the rock. I haul myself along the basalt ribs to take the knotted root-fiber cords between my teeth, letting the taut, whipping strings vibrate straight into my skull until I know which vent is singing sharp and which is going slack. 

Sometimes, as the cords fray in the relentless wash, a pale, armored crawler detaches itself from the trembling stone nearby to gather the drifting threads. I watch it ride the eddy for three heartbeats with its hooked legs tucked tight, then clamp back onto the rock a hand’s breadth from my knee. Usually I leave it alone. It keeps the seams clean of mineral dust, and its quiet, blind persistence feels closer to my own than anything else in the chamber. Only when the winter dark lasts too long and the moss grows thin do I reach out, pluck one from the stone, and crack its plates with a piece of copper from my belt.

Mostly, I just hold my place. Tethered to the rock, weighted down against the updrafts, breathing the freezing, sunlit churn. Listening through bone to a mountain that is still rising, and counting the clicks of seven hollowed stones until the current changes its mind.
