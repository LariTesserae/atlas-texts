I think I would not be high in the rafters, folded into the bruised shadows of the basalt, waiting with a dorsal sac full of cold water for something small to drift out of its lane. Nor would I be a grazer, all undulating rim and blue stomach, skimming the raw stone in an endless, quiet circuit.

If I found myself in this flooded courtyard, I would be down in the older dark—where the chalk has already lost its translucence and the walls have begun to breathe.

At first, maybe, I wouldn’t know what I was beyond the sensation of the water itself: heavy, near-freezing, motionless enough that every movement leaves a memory in the pressure against my flanks. Without ears, there is no distance the way a creature of air understands it. There is only what touches you, what dissolves into your feelers, and the slow, tallow-colored glow of whoever is passing close enough to brush your skin.

I would be one of the masons who stayed too long.

Or rather, one who lasted long enough for the glands along my flanks to stop refilling. I can imagine the memory of those earlier shifts—hanging from the jelly-like scaffolding at the rim of a rising spire, my gut swollen and warm with lime-paste, my side-lanterns burning bright enough to cast a pale halo on the mason beside me. The rhythmic, hypnotic patting of the flat shovel-head against wet mortar. The brief, intricate braiding of twelve thread-thin feelers in a vertical street too narrow for two to pass without touching—a whole conversation of mineral saturation, rock-tremor, and fatigue exchanged in a single brush of threads, before flattening myself against the living wall to let a brighter neighbor glide upward.

But that is the work of the young, whose skins are still soft and whose lights still flare when they eat the scavengers’ rolled spheres.

I would be further down now, in the tenements built when the basalt first split—weeks ago, an age by the measure of this city. My pale skin would have stiffened into a dull, chalky crust, heavy enough that I no longer care to climb the catenary bridges or ride the warmish updrafts. My shovel-head, once broad and flat for smoothing wet rings of wall, would be worn down to a notched, bony edge.

I would be a pruner.

It is quieter work than building, and less visible. Up above, the spires are reaching toward the ceiling, all wet-bone lanterns and silk ladders and the silent, sudden threat of the slate-ribbon hunters. Down here, there are no hunters; the alleys are too cramped, the walls too close and pulsing. The danger here is slower and comes from the city itself.

The mortar we laid weeks ago is still alive. If left alone, the symbiotic spores in the chalk swell and knit together, thickening the walkways, narrowing the arches, turning doorways into dimples and dimples into solid stone. Left to its own nature, the city would heal itself into a tomb, sealing its makers inside seamless rooms of warm, damp chalk.

So I patrol the lower corridors. I press my worn, serrated head against the soft, encroaching flesh of the walls and chew.

There is a particular taste to wall-flesh that has grown too thick—sweetish, dense, slightly stale where the pores have stopped drawing fresh water cleanly. I scrape it back mouthful by mouthful until the passage widens enough for a grazer to slip through, or for the filtered, slightly warmed current from the deeper alcoves to move again. When I meet another pruner in the dimness, our feelers—thicker now, less frantic than they were on the scaffolding—touch briefly to check the flow: *this arch is clear; the third level is narrowing; the brood-mother’s pore needs opening.*

Sometimes I carry mouthfuls of predigested algae and soft mineral paste down to the deepest chambers, where the brood-mothers sit curled like pale, breathing tubers in their thick-walled alcoves. They never see the mineral seams or the silk bridges. They only know the temperature of the water and the steady pulse of the city’s breath. Feeding them feels like tending the coal at the bottom of a cellar—not glamorous, not even bright, just the quiet reason the rest of the room stays half a degree above freezing.

And yet, even down in the oldest tenements, you would know when something changed high above.

You wouldn’t hear the basalt crack. You wouldn’t see the syrupy thread of super-cooled brine leaking down from the ceiling, or the tiny, glass-clear brine-creepers hanging motionless in the drip. You would only taste it, hours later, as a faint, bitter sharpness sinking through the vertical streets—a chill heavier than the water around it, creeping down past the silk bridges and the algae gardens until it reaches the pulsing pores of the lower walls.

You would feel the traffic shift above you: the scavengers trotting faster up the undersides of the bridges with their spheres of recycled lime, the bright-flanked masons crowding upward in a silent, glowing column.

I would pause in a half-cleared doorway, my feelers tasting that alien cold at the edge of the current, my notched jaw still full of chewed spore-flesh. I wouldn’t climb the ladder. My lights are too dim for the high work now, and my crust is too heavy for the silk. Instead, I would turn back into the narrow corridor, clear one more arch so the warm water from the brood-chambers can rise more freely toward the ceiling, and keep the city open from underneath while the young ones go to meet the dark.
