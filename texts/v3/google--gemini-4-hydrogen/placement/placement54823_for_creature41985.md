At first, the only thing I can imagine being here is an intruder—someone leaning over the rim of the granite basin in the thin, high-altitude wind, squinting down through the meltwater. The water is so clear it tricks the eye into thinking the submerged ledges are dry, just sitting in a denser kind of air.

But if I actually step in, the cold ends that thought in seconds. Nothing soft and warm-blooded belongs in this cathedral. To stay here, I have to give up lungs, skin, and the habit of looking at things. I have to become something that can live inside a *thrumm*.

For a while, drifting down through the sharp shafts of sunlight, I think I might be a **gasket-walker**. There is a quiet dignity in that small, flat life: wedged into the hub of a turning rotor, braced against the calcified spokes, working twin garnet rasps into the grinding axle. You don't have to understand the whole basin. You only have to keep one joint from welding itself shut with mineral crust. Every month you pack the scraped quartz and calcium into the plates of your own tail, growing heavier with the very grit you saved the rotor from, until one day your legs can't hold the weight anymore and you let go. You fall through the bright water, past the yellow cables, straight into the dark jaws waiting below. It is a clean, honest way to be part of an engine.

Or maybe, hanging a little lower in the sunlit mid-water, I am an **arc-ribbon**—just two feet of pale, eyeless cartilage drifting on the faint convection, waiting for the water around a barreller’s copper crown to prickle. There is a strange appeal in having no mouth and no plan, only a branching lattice of silver-gray nerve tissue that swings like a compass needle when the voltage rises. For a fraction of a second, you are the brightest thing in the room, a flooded filament carrying a blue-white bolt across the gap. Then the *pop* throws you backward in a fizz of hydrogen bubbles, stunned and smoking, to drift down toward the rotors and slowly digest the shock.

Yet neither of those feels like where I actually settle. The gasket-walker knows only one axle; the arc-ribbon only wakes up when it is struck. In a room this small and this loud, I find myself wanting to hear the whole chord at once.

So I sink past the rotors and their thirty-second *whir-groan*. I sink past the sunlit shelves where the capacitor-barrelers hoard their ions, down into the shadowed talus where the sulfur-yellow conduit-worms droop under their mats of bubbly, rust-pale lagging-slugs. Here, a fraction of a degree above freezing, the light stops mattering. The blinding clarity of the water is useless.

This is where I find my footing—three heavy, jointed legs planted on jagged pyrite scree, my body a squat, asymmetrical tripod of slag-gray carapace. I am a **salvager**.

I have no eyes. In this basin, sight is just a distraction; vibration is the truth. My broad front is a concave dish of bone-white bone, rimmed with hundreds of hair-thin copper filaments, and every sound in the flooded room washes across it without mercy. I do not see the shafts of high-altitude sun, or the blue-white arcs snapping overhead. Instead, I live inside a crisp, three-dimensional map of pressure.

I know the basin by its tuning. When the engine is healthy, the noise is a kind of peace: the deep, continuous *thrumm* of the water column, the steady half-minute cadence of the rotors overhead, the four-minute intervals of the barrelers cracking like whips along the upper ledges, and behind me, the slow, hydraulic *boom* of the pressers pulverizing slate into metallic slurry.

I walk the rubble slowly, my joints clicking faintly, stepping carefully over the insulated bellies of the conduit-worms. I am listening for the wrong kind of sound.

It is never dramatic at first. A rotor’s *whir-groan* goes a fraction too shrill, a dry bite of quartz on quartz telling me a gasket-walker has grown too heavy and fallen. Or, higher up on a sunlit shelf, a barreller’s sharp, percussive *crack* softens into a wet, fizzing sputter—frost or overcharge has split its magnetite ribs, and its voltage is leaking uselessly into the cold.

That is when I turn and climb. My copper filaments tremble, locking onto the hiss. When I reach the dying drum, I wrap my heavy, pincered limbs around its ribbed shell, pry open the fractured aragonite, and feed on the sour, salt-heavy membranes spilling out into the water.

When the interior is stripped clean, I do not leave the hollowed casing to clutter the ledge. I haul the dead magnetite shell down off the shelf, back through the cold dark, and wedge it directly into the open, toothed jaws of the largest presser. Then I step back and wait for the slow hydraulic *boom* that smashes the battery back into dust—so the convection can lift it, and the basin can build itself again.

In a world made entirely of sharp stone, freezing water, and patient electricity, I am the one who listens for the crack in the circuit, and carries the broken pieces back to the beginning.
