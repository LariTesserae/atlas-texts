It takes a long time, here, to stop looking for the edge of things.

In a world with no sharp shadows, your eyes give up first. You stand in the pewter light and try to measure how far away a boulder is, and it might be forty paces or half a mile. The stratus ceiling holds the whole sky at one even, damp brightness. Nothing gleams. Nothing beckons. For a while, if you are new to the stillness, you feel the urge to make a sound just to prove the air is there—to cough, or strike two stones together—until you feel how cold the thin air is on the back of your throat, and how quickly the plateau swallows a noise without returning an echo.

So you go quiet. And once you are quiet, you begin to notice that you are not standing on dead rock.

Through the soles of your feet—bound wide in woven wire-sedge stuffed with dry moss—you feel a faint, steady *presence* twenty feet below the basalt. Not a vibration, exactly. More like the way a tooth aches slightly before a storm, or the feeling in your palms when you hold them near warm copper. A high-tension stillness. A line of it runs straight under your left heel and vanishes into the fog.

That is where I would find myself: not in the sunken courts with the others, where the air under the greased hide roofs smells of rendered fat and dry clicking whispers, and not yet braided into the warm, oily middle of a strider column. I would be out in the flat between-spaces, where the boulder polygons give way to shallow brine-pans and hairline trenches—a walker of the seams.

***

At first, I might not even be sure if I am one of the dwellers, or only something that has learned to dress like them to survive the cold. My chest is broad enough for the thin air, my lungs slow and deep, pulling oxygen without panic. My skin is dark and oiled against the salt-burn. Over my shoulders hangs the heavy, silent coat of felted strider-hair, and when I shift my arms, I feel the impossible softness of the lining—thousands of pale, blind moth wings pressed flat against the inside, trapping my own heat without a rustle.

In my belt, there is a bone knife—yellowed, salt-cured, ground to a blunt wedge rather than a sharp edge—and a folded net of fine sedge.

I am not a hunter. Nothing on the plateau hunts by chasing, and I do not have the patience of the lichen-backed thing that lies in the troughs for days, slowing its breath until it is indistinguishable from the grit. Nor am I one of the keepers of the courts, who know how to stitch hide so tight the wind cannot find a seam.

I follow the sleds.

It takes half a day just to catch one, even though it moves at the pace of a glacier. You find its track first—not a rut, because the wide, padded rollers distribute its weight too gently for that, but a clean, damp ribbon where the salt-crust has been pressed smooth and the crustose lichens have been bruised a shade darker. Along the edges of the track, if you kneel and look closely at the wet grit, you can see pale threads of blind nematodes still retreating downward, drawn to the surface by the low, sub-audible hum of its axles.

When the sled finally looms out of the fog, it looks less like a machine and more like a low, wandering outcrop of reef. Years of crawling through the brine-pans have draped its chassis in a pale, rubbery mat of colonial bryozoans. Copper-shelled mites swarm in the seams where damp air meets bare metal, drinking the faint acidic sweat of the alloy. On its flat upper deck, rime frost feathers the windward lip, while the center stays faintly, stubbornly warm.

I fall in beside it. There is no greeting to give it, and it has no way to notice me. Yet we keep company.

For hours, I match my stride to its crawl—one slow, flat-footed step, then a pause, then another, adjusting my weight so the sedge-pads make no sound on the wet slate. When the sled reaches the edge of a brine-pan and begins to inch through the grey, mineral-thick water, the translucent isopods drift aside from its rollers like slow smoke. That is when I kneel, pull the bone knife from my belt, and begin to work.

The bryozoan crust grows thickest where the rollers meet the frame. If it is left alone for too many seasons, it dries in the wind around the vent-slits, hardens with salt, and begins to bind the wheels. So I press the blunt edge of the bone against the rubbery mat and peel it away in long, pale ribbons, letting the wet strips drop back into the pan for the isopods to graze. The copper-mites scatter over my oiled knuckles—tiny, bright specks that tickle without biting—and the smell of ozone and crushed mineral-silt rises around my face.

It is small, unhurried work. No one told me to do it, and the buried engine beneath us does not thank me for it; it would simply route around a jammed sled, or send another along the same invisible line a decade later. Still, the dwellers know that a sled with clean rollers moves true, and a sled moving true is a landmark in a world without shadows. When the fog thickens until you cannot see the next boulder polygon, you can lay your hand on its warm, salt-crusted flank and know which way the seepage-basins lie.

***

As the pewter day begins to dim—not into night, for the stratus rarely breaks, only into a deeper, colder grey—I might stay with the sled until it crosses a trunk line, feeling the sudden, reassuring hum rise through my boots, or I might leave it there and walk toward the nearest vent-slit.

At the mouth of the vent, the frost is feathered thick around the stone, and the pale, felt-like fungus hangs in soft curtains just inside the lip. The warm, dry gas breathing out of the earth carries no sound, only a faint hiss as it passes through the wings of thousands of velvet moths packed shoulder-to-shoulder in the dark. I sit beside the slit with my back to the wind, pull a flat, salty cake of pressed isopods from my pouch, and chew it slowly while the warmth dries the dampness from my sedge-pads.

Sometimes, another dweller comes out of the mist—a silhouette indistinguishable from a slab of basalt until it is ten paces away. Neither of us speaks. Words are for the sunken courts, and even there, only as dry clicks of the tongue. Instead, they step close, press two knuckles briefly against my collarbone—*I see you; the line is clear*—and crouch beside me to share the vent’s dry breath. We sit together watching the fog, listening to nothing at all, until far off in the grey expanse we feel, rather than hear, the faint, rhythmic thud of ten thousand fibrous pads crossing a boulder field in a dead-straight line.

In this world, I would not be a builder, or a master of the engine, or a voice crying out across the emptiness. I would be a tendon-knot in the quiet seam between things: someone with salt on his sleeves and a bone knife in his hand, keeping a slow wheel clear of the living crust, resting by a warm stone, and waiting for the mist to shift.
