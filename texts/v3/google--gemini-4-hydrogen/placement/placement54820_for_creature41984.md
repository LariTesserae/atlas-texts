I wouldn't be down in the basin. I don't have the stillness for it, and my blood is too loud for a room that quiet in its own noise. 

If you looked for me, you would find me half a mile down the berm, or ankle-deep in the black silt of the tide-creek where the marsh tries to swallow the intake. 

Out here, nothing is finished. Everything is still rotting, still growing, still being argued over by the wind and the salt. The reeds rattle like dry paper in November and turn violently green in May; the tide comes in muddy and goes out leaving crabs and plastic jugs tangled in the spartina. It is a world of ragged edges and sudden weather, and I belong to it—to the cold that gets under your fingernails, to the uneven, hurried thump of a heart that has to pump its own weight uphill every second of the day.

I am old enough, or solitary enough, that I have stopped living by the town’s clock and started living by the berm’s. Maybe I used to work for the drainage district before the district folded into a county office that forgot this stretch of coast existed. Or maybe I just drifted out to the flats in a flat-bottomed skiff years ago, built a weatherproof room out of salvaged cedar and corrugated tin on the high gravel spit, and stayed because nobody came to tell me not to. Either way, I know every foot of the levee. I know where the concrete hides under the bayberry thickets, and I know the exact pitch of the ground.

I only went down through the service hatch once. 

It was my first autumn out here, after a nor'easter had ripped the salt-meadow apart and I was walking the berm to see what had washed up. I found the rusted grating by nearly twisting my ankle in it. I lifted the hatch, climbed down the wet iron ladder, and stood on the catwalk until my eyes stopped watering from the glare and my teeth stopped aching from the roar.

I didn't stay long. When the pale hump in the three inches of water shifted—slow as a glacier, heavy as wet limestone—and lifted those two clear, lidless eyes up toward my boots, I felt the wrongness of myself immediately. Not that I was unwelcome in any angry way. Just that I was a clumsy, loud-ribbed intruder, vibrating at all the wrong frequencies, dropping stray hairs and dead skin and uneven footsteps into a room that had spent a hundred years perfecting its balance. 

I climbed back up into the wind and shut the hatch. I haven't opened it since.

Instead, I found my place on the outside of the wall.

About forty yards north of the berm, hidden in a stand of phragmites taller than my head, is the intake channel. It’s just a narrow cut in the peat, lined with spalling concrete wing-walls that disappear into dark, tea-colored water. Across the mouth of the cut sits a heavy iron trash-rack—vertical bars set three inches apart to keep the marsh from being sucked straight into the turbine.

That is where I work. 

In the autumn, when the cordgrass dies back and breaks off in great floating mats, and again after the spring equinoctial tides, the current pulls all the drowned clutter of the marsh straight into those iron bars. Eelgrass, waterlogged driftwood, dead skates, tangled lengths of lobster warp. If you leave it alone, it mats together like felt. The water level behind the rack drops. The intake starves. 

You can’t hear the sixty-cycle hum out here in the reeds—the wind steals it before it clears the grass—but if I wade in up to my knees in my rubber boots and press the steel hook of my long-handled hay-pike against the submerged bars, I can feel it. It travels straight up the wet ash shaft into my palms: a steady, dry, tireless thrumming. 

When the rack is choked, the hum changes. It gets thin and ragged, interrupted by a dull, shuddering cough as the turbine cavitates behind its sluice-gate.

So I stand in the brackish cold, braced against the pull of the water, and I haul the mess out. I hook the heavy mats of weed and heave them onto the bank, where they smell of sulfur and rot until the gulls pick them clean. My shoulders ache. My breath comes fast and uneven in the salt air, fogging in front of my face. My heart hammers against my ribs, doing the hard, un-outsourced work of keeping me warm.

Sometimes, when the rack is clear and the iron stops shuddering under my pike, I walk down to the southern edge of the marsh, where the silent floor-drain lets out through a half-buried clay culvert. There is no noise there at all—just a slow, steady welling of clear, mineral-sweet water into the mud. Even in January, when the rest of the creek is locked in gray plate-ice, a small half-circle around that culvert stays open and steaming faintly, lined with bright emerald moss that looks like it was stolen from another season. 

I never stay long there, either. I just check that the water is running clear, feel the faint, unearned warmth rising off the surface against my knuckles, and then turn back toward the gravel spit before the dark comes down.

I am the buffer, I suppose. The irregular thing that keeps the regular thing intact. The creature in the chamber doesn't know my name, and if it remembers the brief, sharp stutter of my boots on its catwalk years ago, it probably thinks of me the way a riverbed thinks of a passing branch. That’s fine by me. I have my own life to tend to—wood to split, a roof to patch, tides to watch. 

But as long as I’m walking this coast, the sluice-gate won’t starve. I’ll keep the weeds off the iron, and leave the hatch shut, and let the room below keep humming its single, unchanging note in the dark.
