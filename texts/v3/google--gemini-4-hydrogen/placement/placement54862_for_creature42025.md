If I were placed in this world, I would not be here at dawn. Nothing warm-blooded belongs to the bowl when the frost needles are locked to the granite and the sky is the color of cold iron.

I would find myself coming up from below—from the river valleys where the air is thick enough to carry voices and smoke—climbing the fractured eastern sill just as the sun clears the lip. My lungs would be the first thing to tell me where I was: not burning, exactly, just suddenly empty, working twice as hard for half the weight. My boots, wrapped in hide and stiffened by the ascent, would catch on the porcelain shards of the scree, sending down small, sharp clatters that die before they reach the floor.

At first, I might think I would be one of the white birds—something built to ride the glare, dropping into the cliff fissures when the cold hits and spending the noon hours in silent, effortless dives. There is an appeal to that: the economy of a life that spends no breath on song, only the dry *whuff* of primaries slicing the thin air. Or I might wonder if I belong to the sleepers themselves, locked solid for twenty-one hours, waking in pieces behind amber lids to watch the world through a honey-colored dark.

But when I look at the bowl, it is neither the air nor the stone-sleep that pulls me. It is the troughs.

Someone climbed up here with a heavy hammer-stone and spent hours—days, spread out across three-hour windows—pecking shallow rectangles into raw milky quartz. Quartz does not yield easily; it bruises the hands, throws sharp white dust into the eyes, and resists every blow with the same flat chime as the talus. You do not do that work in a sprint. You cannot hurry a hammer in air this thin.

That is where I find myself: not dashing after the rodents like the white stoats, and not frozen solid in the lee of the windbreak, but crouched beside the largest of the natural melt-pans with a stone in my fist, working on the mountain’s clock.

I would be one of the climbers—perhaps one of the last ones, before the blind under the western overhang was left to the moss and the cold. Not the fastest hunter of the group, not the one setting hair-snares along the rodent runs or scraping the oily black cushions out of the vertical fissures. I would be the one tending the margins: wedging a loose shard back into the crescent windbreak on the eastern rim, clearing the frost-shattered grit out of the catch-basins so the noon condensation holds a little longer before it sinks into the seams.

I think I would come up for the silence as much as for the moss or the pelts. Down below, everything is crowded with sound and decay; up here, the air strips everything down to light, stone, and breath. You cannot waste a motion. You learn to sit still for ten minutes just to let your heart settle, watching the black beetles pour across the wet halos in shimmering rivers, watching a white bird miss a turn and vanish into the glare.

And a few paces away, flattened against a sun-warmed slab of granite, one of the sleepers would be taking in the same noon.

I wouldn’t try to hunt it, and it wouldn’t bother to retreat from me. We would share the same narrow window of light with the quiet recognition of two things that have both made a truce with the cold—one of us by leaving before the shadow of the western tooth stretches across the floor, the other by turning to stone until the sun comes back like a hammer.

When the light begins to slant and the wind spills over the lip with its sharp tang of ozone, I would pack my flint flake, tuck my stiffened sinew and bone needles under the western overhang for the next climb, and start back down the scree while my joints still bend.

And if, in the end, I grew too old for the ascent—if one season I stayed down in the thick air of the valley and never came back for my tools—I like knowing that the troughs I pecked into the quartz would still fill each noon with sky-bright mirrors, and that one of the sleepers would eventually drag itself into my abandoned blind, wedging its pale, armored back into the moss-chinked corner to wait out the dark beside my needles.
