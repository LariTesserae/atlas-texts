I would be down in the third tier from the bottom, where the noon light arrives only as a pale gray wash along the upper lintels, never quite reaching the floorboards.

If you came looking for me, you wouldn’t hear me before you saw me. You would hear the *clack* of the eastern tipping-trough first, then the brief rush of water into the charcoal bed, and then, if you stood still at the landing with one hand steadying yourself against the damp chert, you might catch the dry *scrape-click* of a wooden hatch being levered up from the floor of a dark room.

I am a cistern-keeper. Not one of the morning scrapers who move in pairs down the sunlit upper tiers with reed hampers strapped to their backs, though my palms carry the same chalky bloom in the creases and my cuticles are pickled pale from the cold seepage. The scrapers work the open pans, where the water sheets thin and the mineral needles grow bright and brittle in the milky air. My work begins where the light gives out, beneath the floorboards and behind the low retaining walls, where the filtered water rests in the dark before it is let go to the marsh.

My knees are already thickened by the damp—not locked yet, not like the elders who have been shifted up to the dry attic-boxes beneath the rim, but stiff enough that I take the stepped roofs with a deeper crouch than the young ones, my tallow-cured soles feeling for the gritty patches where the glaze hasn’t yet smoothed the slate. Around my wrists and ankles, the oiled rawhide bindings are dark with use. I keep my sleeves tucked tight; once the cold water wicks up past your elbow, it stays in the felted wool until you sit beside a peat brazier, and a man who smells of mildew and sour wool is a man who isn’t paying attention to his seals.

In the mornings, after the *shhhk-shhhk* of the copper-edged paddles has moved down from the upper tiers, I make my rounds of the seven covered cisterns in our section. Each one is a box of dry-stacked slate, chinked with moss and sealed along its seams by decades of the pale mineral skin. When I pry up a hatch, the air that rises to meet my face is sharp and clean, smelling of wet stone and crushed charcoal, colder than the room around me.

I don’t bring a rushlight. Fire belongs in the braziers; smoke fouls the water, and besides, you can’t see what matters in a cistern by looking straight down at it. You have to let your eyes settle into the dark until the surface of the water becomes a faint, pewter skin. Then I reach into the small pouch at my greased-hide apron, pinch a measure of crushed reed-seed between thumb and forefinger, and let it fall.

For three breaths, nothing happens. Above me, through four stories of braced slate and peat-chinked joints, the town’s machinery keeps its slow, irregular time—the *hiss* of the roof-gutters, the distant *clack* of a counterweight cobble dropping, the muffled *thump* of a neighbor’s foot on the shingle overhead.

Then the water beneath my knees stirs. Tiny, blind, translucent—the *clear-backs* rise to the seed. You see them less as shapes than as flickers of pale motion, like motes of dust turning in a slit of light. I watch how fast they gather, how thickly they turn along the submerged walls. When I reach my bare forearm down into the cold dark to test the slate, my knuckles tell me the rest: the stone should feel crisp, slightly gritty with young mineral crust, never slick. If my fingers come up with the faintest film of grease, or if the shrimp hang sluggish in the corners, I know the wicker filter-basket above has channeled—the coarse sand has shifted, letting the raw marsh-taint slip through.

That is when I fetch my bone pick and my spare lashings, and climb one tier up to the settling-pans to find the leak before the water goes sour.

I spend almost as much time tending the tipping-troughs as I do the cisterns. People think of the troughs as steady things—driftwood frames, river-cobbles bound in rawhide, swinging on slate pins—but nothing in the cleft stays the same weight for long. Every day, the mineral water leaves a skin on the wood, thinner than an eyelid, and over a season that skin adds up. A trough’s lip grows heavy; its balance drifts. The rawhide lashing the cobble stretches in the wet. One morning its *clack* comes half a breath too late, or lands with a dull, hesitant thud, and every household in the tier looks up from their peat-pots at the same moment, listening.

I know the voice of every trough within thirty paces of my sleeping shelf. I can tell by the pitch of the knock whether a driftwood pin has swollen tight in its socket or whether a sliver of mineral crust has wedged behind the cheek of the frame. When I crouch beside a trough to shave back the glaze with a copper blade, or slip a thin chert wedge under a lashing to true the counterweight, the neighbors passing on the roof-walkway above barely glance down. They step aside with that quiet, sideways economy of ours, keeping their voices below the hiss of the water, letting me work.

At midday, when the milky glare drops straight down the throat of the basin for an hour, I usually sit in my own doorway—my feet resting on the cross-slats above the runnel that carries our tier’s spent water out toward the marsh-gate—and eat whatever my neighbor’s household has traded for at dawn: a strip of smoked eel, a cold marsh-tuber split with a flint knife. The air inside is thick with the earthy haze of smoldering peat. Sometimes, if the wind is right above the rim, a single strand of wire-grass or a dry sedge-seed drifts all the way down through the four-story gap and lands in the gutter, looking bright and foreign against the wet gray slate.

I have only climbed the notched trail to the marsh-edge twice in my life, both times in the damp dark before dawn when the regular bearers were down with joint-fever. I carried a hamper of harvested salt-needles up to the rim, set it down beside the sedge, and waited while the lowland traders counted out coils of sinew and bundles of dried reed.

I hated it up there. Even in the gray before sunrise, the openness felt like a wound. There were no walls to catch my breath, no slate shoulder at my elbow to tell me where I stood. When the wind came across the flats, unimpeded by anything taller than grass, it pressed my tunic flat against my ribs and made my ears ring with emptiness. When I spoke to the lowlander—even whispering, as we do—the sound simply vanished into the sky, lost the moment it left my teeth. I couldn’t hear a single trough. I didn’t know what hour it was, or whether the water was rising.

I hurried back down the notched rock as soon as the hamper was emptied, and I didn’t stop crouching until I was three tiers under the rim, with the cold, wet walls close enough to touch on both sides and the steady *clack-rush-knock* of the machinery closing over my head like a roof.

I know I won’t stay in the third tier forever. Already, when the groundwater presses hard after a lowland rain and the horizontal seams weep faster, my left knee locks if I kneel too long beside a hatch. In another ten seasons, or fifteen, I will trade my lower room to a younger keeper—someone with sharper eyes for the dark water and supple ankles for the glazed stairs—and I will take my turn climbing up to the tiny, dry attic-boxes wedged beneath the lip of the cleft.

I think about that sometimes, lying on my woven-reed shelf at night while the runnel whispers beneath me and the neighbor’s peat-brazier ticks as it cools. Up there, the mist thins. You can smell the marsh-wind through the chinks, and you spend your days with dry hands, knotting rawhide lashings and carving bone scrapers for the ones still working in the wet below. It will be strange to have dry knuckles, to lose the chalky bloom from my palms.

But from those high boxes, they say, you can hear the whole basin at once—every gutter, every tipping-trough from the upper pans down to the lowest runnel-gate, all braided together into one soft, continuous engine. Even when my knees can no longer take the stairs, I will still be listening to the water, waiting for a missed beat, keeping time with the stone until the day they carry me down through the bottom gate and lay me into the cold peat of the marsh.
