I would not be on the upper tiers, where the sky-slot is widest and the transit pods chime most often. I would find myself lower down, on the shadow-heavy eastern wall—three tiers above the geothermal floor, where a thick vein of pale quartz cuts diagonally through the black basalt like a fault-line of frozen milk.

In a city built inside a crack, you learn to know your address by the light and the drip. My gallery sits just under an angular overhang that catches the midday blade for only four minutes instead of ten, and only when the sky above the ridge is clear. For the rest of the day, the world outside my threshold is a study in wet charcoal and silver mist.

If you stepped off a pod onto my landing, you might think the room behind the doorway was empty. It is small, amber-lit, and dry, warmed by a branch line from the deep phase-change exchangers. The floor is matte-dark alloy, seamless where it meets the raw slate of the back wall. Along the long bench sit trays of ceramic-fiber shims, spools of hairline magnetic wire, and—more often than not—one or two of the six-limbed maintenance crawlers, resting upside down with their legs folded neatly over their flat carapaces like sleeping crabs.

I am a *seam-tuner*—not one of the geologists who read the slow rise of the mountain itself, though I work alongside them, but someone who keeps the peace between the city and the stone.

Because the ridge is young, it is not finished settling. Frost pries at the fracture planes every night; the midday thaw lets them slip back by the width of a hair. Most of the time, the molecular joins where our alloy becomes slate absorb the strain without complaint. Most of the time, the field-stabilizers inside the bridges cancel every tremor before a sole can feel it.

But sometimes, when the freeze-thaw cycle has been particularly sharp, a span begins to *hum*. Not loudly—never loud enough to wake someone in a residential cell—just a faint, persistent vibration in the ceramic rail, a tiny sympathetic buzz that turns a quiet bridge into a stringed instrument.

That is when someone taps twice on my landing rail, or sends a clipped, three-word glyph to my bench slate.

I put on my matte-charcoal outer weave, cinch the pale ceramic cuffs so nothing can snag or trail mist, and step out to the *seam*.

Even after years, I never cross it without pausing for a breath. It is my favorite place in the cleft—that invisible, three-inch plane of warm shear-air where the gallery ends and the mountain begins. I stand with my toes on the bone-dry alloy, holding a mug of hot, mineral-sharp broth, and lean just my left shoulder out into the freezing mist until the weave prickles with rime. Then I pull back, and watch the frost vanish in a heartbeat. It is how I wake my lungs up before a crossing: a reminder that the warmth is borrowed, and the cold is what is real.

Out on the bridge, there is no wind to push me, only the thin, saturated air and the steady *tink* of meltwater hitting the collector vanes below. My boots grip the damp alloy without a sound. I kneel halfway across the span, lay two bare fingers—already wind-burned and rough at the knuckles—flat against the ceramic rail, and close my eyes.

In a world of acoustic courtesy, you learn to listen with your bones. Through the rail, I can feel the whole geometry of the pocket: the distant, papery rush of the gale high above the rim; the faint, rhythmic click of a transit pod docking two tiers up; and beneath it all, the tiny, tight thrum of the stabilizer coil that has drifted a fraction of a degree out of phase with the basalt anchor.

It takes twenty minutes with a tuning key to bring it back to stillness. No hammers, no shouts—just small, deliberate adjustments until the rail beneath my palm goes completely dead, as quiet as the rock it is grown into.

On the walk back, I usually stop to check the undersides of the walkways. That is where I find the crawlers that have taken a bad knock from a falling icicle, or jammed a knee-joint with refrozen silt. The residents treat them like barn cats—stepping over them, clearing a clogged hinge with a gloved finger when they pass—and I suppose I am the one who mends them when they come home limping. I unhook the damaged unit from its magnetic track, tuck it under one arm like a heavy, cold dinner plate, and carry it back across my threshold.

Inside, with the door chimed shut and the amber light steady, I eat simply: a steamed cake of cultured protein, a bowl of basalt-salted broth, a crisp sheet of dried lichen-starch from the lower vats. I do not speak for hours at a time, and I do not miss it. When you live in a stone amphitheater, silence is not an absence; it is the medium everything else floats in.

Once a day, just after noon, I set my tools down and step back out to the rail.

For four minutes, the sun clears the jagged lip above and drops a sharp, blinding blade straight down the quartz vein behind my landing. The rime-ice all along the overhang crackles and lets go at once, turning the steady *tink* of the cleft into a sudden, soft rain. I lean my forearms on the warm alloy, tilt my face straight up into the fierce high-altitude light, and close my eyes. My skin drinks the heat until my cheekbones ache.

Sometimes, while the light is still on the stone, a shadow flickers across my eyelids—too small and too fast for a transit pod. I open my eyes in time to see an alpine swift banking cleanly between the bridges, snatching a bead of meltwater from a dripping collector vane, and shooting back up through the bright slot of sky.

Nobody on the tiers speaks. Nobody waves. We just stand there—lean, quiet, anchored to the sheer walls—and watch the only thing in the mountain that doesn't need a bridge. Then the shadow claims the quartz again, the water slows back to a single, clear *tink*, and I turn back through the invisible seam to my warm bench, my quiet crawlers, and the work of keeping the stillness true.
