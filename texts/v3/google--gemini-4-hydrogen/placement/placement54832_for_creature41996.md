I would be on the third ledge down from the rim, just before the steps lose their patience and turn into talus.

Up on the plateau, the wind has room to lie flat and cool. The stone there is old and settled; you can put your palm against it at noon and feel nothing worse than the sun’s own warmth, and if you leave a stone on top of another stone, they will still be neighbors next year. When we climb down in the morning—five of us, roped together only by habit and the unspoken rule that nobody drops out of earshot—the first thing that hits us isn’t the glare. It’s the *thickness* of the air rising to meet us, smelling of wet chalk, iron, and crushed shell, like the inside of a kiln that hasn’t been swept.

That’s where I stop to tie my eyes.

I’m not the fastest knapper in our party, and my shoulders aren’t what they used to be for hauling slabs of precipitated salt up the sheer steps in the late afternoon. What I am is the one who walks in front with the long pole. On the plateau, people call it *sounding*, though down here you don’t listen for echoes so much as you feel for the give—the tiny, hollow tremor through ash-wood when a shelf of travertine has been hollowed out from underneath by sappers. You learn it in your wrists first, before your ears ever catch it.

So I would be the crust-reader: old enough to have tallow-back scute plates strapped over my shins that are scarred from three different descents, and careful enough to check the sinew lashings twice before I step off the basalt onto the pale flats.

My smoked mica slits narrow the world to a horizontal band. Everything above the rim disappears; everything below turns into glare and shadow, sharp as broken glass. When I wrap the damp cloth over my mouth and nose, my own breath turns warm and wet against my cheeks, tasting faintly of the plateau’s cistern water before the sulfur gets into it.

Down on the floor, you don’t walk the way you walk anywhere else. You set your heel, wait a half-breath, feel the salt-crust crackle without sinking, and only then bring your weight across. Ahead of me, the basin is already loud—a dry, papery roar of glint-wings wheeling on the steam thermals, and underneath it the endless, granular hiss of chalk-runners pouring over the flats like spilled grain. Every time my pole taps a travertine ridge, a drift of them parts around the hook, blind and pale, their waxy backs catching the sun so hard it hurts even through smoked mica.

The others follow in my track: two young ones with empty hide frames on their backs for the carapace-bundles, a knapper whose thumbs are permanently notched with old obsidian scars, and one heavy-shouldered hauler carrying the spare cordage and the water skins. Nobody talks much once we’re below the overhangs. Talking dries your mouth, and the basin has enough noise of its own.

What I like—if *like* is the right word for a place that would digest you before dark if you sat still—is the honesty of the turnover.

Up on the plateau, people spend a great deal of energy pretending things last. They build low stone walls to mark whose goat-run is whose, they argue over boundaries their grandfathers drew, they carve little notches into doorposts to count winters. Down here, we don’t pretend anything. Last week, we lashed two tallow-back ribs across a hissing rift near the western pans so we could reach a fresh obsidian blister without climbing three hundred paces around. Today, when I reach that rift and tap the near bank with my pole, one of the ribs is already half-buried in a fresh lip of travertine, white and brittle as dried froth, and the sinew binding it has been chewed to threads by something small and patient.

I don’t curse it. I just signal the hauler to bring up a fresh length of cordage, we re-lash the wood-and-bone span, and we cross one at a time while the steam sighs wetly against our ankles.

While the knapper squats on a flat basalt shelf above the rust-slime pools—striking long, ringing *tinks* out of a black volcanic core so we only have to carry the sharp blades back up—I stand a few paces off, leaning on my pole, watching the basin work.

A pan of mineral water below us is shrinking in the heat, its edges crusting white. I can see the plate-backs moving in the warm trickle, their ridged shells looking so much like broken travertine that you only spot them by the pale, scoured tracks they leave through the rust-colored slime. Between them, the water twitches where glass-needles dart—nothing visible of them except tiny dark eyes and a thread of amber gut. In another hour, if that seep shifts, they’ll curl up in the silt and turn themselves into quartz pebbles until the next wet afternoon.

Nearer the fissure, a chimney-colony has raised a fluted tower almost to my waist since our last visit, whistling softly as steam threads through its porous sides. As I watch, a sapper must shear through its buried root, because the whole spire leans, pauses, and shatters into chalky rubble. Before the dust has even settled, the floor boils with chalk-runners surging in to strip the exposed, pale-pink worms from the wet stone. Three glint-wings drop out of the glare like thrown knives, snatching runners off the rubble and banking back up into the thermal with a dry *snap* of chitin wings.

It takes less than ten breaths. By the time the knapper beside me finishes a blade and lays it on his hide cloth, the chimney is gone, the worms are gone, and only a scatter of damp chalk marks where a tower stood.

There’s a strange kind of relief in watching it. Down here, you never have to wonder what your work is worth to the world: it’s worth whatever you can carry out before the sun drops behind the rim. Nothing more.

Once, late in the afternoon—when we’re already heavy with salt slabs and tied bundles of hollow chalk-runner husks that rattle at every step like dry reeds—I catch sight of a tallow-back across the flats. It’s moving slow as a drifting boulder along the edge of a warm channel, its pillar legs sinking slightly into the wet gravel, its shovel-lip sweeping mud and arthropods alike into its maw. The fused bone plates along its spine are stained yellow with sulfur and white with old salt. A pair of heavers watch it from the black slot of a talus crack, their splayed legs motionless, their serrated jaws waiting for a misstep that hasn’t come yet.

Maybe it’s forty years old. Maybe it will live another ten, or maybe tomorrow a gypsum crust will give way under its front cushion and bring every scavenger in the basin running. We don’t go after it; we don’t have the spears or the light left to butcher something that size before dark, and a healthy tallow-back isn’t something four people with hooked poles argue with. We just stand on the firm basalt shelf, dusted white from boot-wrap to brow, and watch it pass.

Then I turn my pole toward the steps.

When we climb back out onto the plateau in the cooling dusk, the air feels thin and almost tasteless. My legs ache from the careful, testing stride; my eyes still see the ghost of travertine glare when I blink. The others head straight for the cisterns to wash the chalk out of their hair and unbind their scute-wraps.

I usually sit on the rim for a few minutes first, my back against stone that doesn’t sweat or shift, listening to the basin far below. Even from up here, through the evening quiet, you can hear a faint, dry roar rising out of the crack in the earth—millions of wings folding under the overhangs, millions of legs still scraping the salt-crust in the dark, and the stone already laying down a thin white skin over the obsidian flakes we left behind.

Tomorrow, none of our marks will look like ours. And in three days, when the salt-bags are low again, I’ll tie the smoked mica over my eyes, take up my pole, and go back down to see what the ground has decided to be next.
