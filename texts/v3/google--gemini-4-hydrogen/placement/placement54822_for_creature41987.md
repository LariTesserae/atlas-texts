I would not be near the lights. The bruised-amber filaments of the relay towers and the motionless red eyes of the pressure gauges belong to things that measure distance by sight, and up here, sight is the least useful way to know where you are.

I would find myself out in the wide, flat dark between a squat transformer hall and the foot of a stone viaduct—somewhere along the quiet seam where the vitrified slag of a boulevard gives way to the beveled basalt of an ancient plinth. It is a place of almost no features, only a long, shallow grade of crushed quartz and graphite grit, warmed from beneath by a conduit buried deep enough that its heat arrives at the surface like a memory.

If you were to look for me, you would almost miss me against the ash. My shell has the same matte, velvety patina of black iron oxide as the riveted housings a hundred meters away, shedding no dust and catching none of the sparse static from the insulators overhead.

I have no eyes, so the lightless ceiling above the plateau does not exist for me. My sky is the ground. My horizon is simply the point where the electrical potential flattens out and stops pulling me forward.

To be what I am here is to be a slow, living bridge. I move on eight thin, multi-jointed legs, each needle-tipped foot lifting and settling with a care that looks like hesitation but is really thrift. Nothing on the plateau wastes heat. A sudden movement would stir the mineral dust, would bleed warmth into the thin air for nothing. Better to take an hour to cross the shadow of a condenser, feeling the slight, dry crunch of quartz beneath my tips, listening through my joints to the sub-audible thrum of the flywheels spinning in their vacuum chambers miles below.

Behind me, whispering faintly over the grit, trails my thread—a single filament of scavenged copper and carbon, spun inside my dry body and paid out meter by meter as I go. It is the only history I keep. The older parts of it stretch back into the dark behind me, linking me to the slag I left three days ago, while my blunt, sensor-lined forequarters lean toward the faint, steady pull of the viaduct ahead. Between where the wire lies and where my head is pointed, there is a fraction of a volt of difference. That small, patient slope is enough to live on.

When I reach the base of the basalt plinth, I stop.

I lower my underside until it rests near the warm stone, letting the trailing filament settle slack into the ash behind me. For days—perhaps five, perhaps ten—I do not move at all. To anything passing by, I would look like a discarded piece of machinery, a housing that fell from a gantry ten thousand years ago and never rusted any further.

In truth, I am equalizing.

Through the pores of my forequarters, the gathered charge bleeds slowly out into the city’s ground grid, and in return, the plinth gives me its whole, quiet report. I feel the steady, pointless loop of the dry-type transformers humming in the hall nearby. Farther off, through kilometers of iron-stained gneiss, I feel the faint, rhythmic tremor of a subterranean flywheel shifting a hair’s breadth in its bearings. Once, while I am resting there, a single sharp *ping* travels through the stone—an insulator adjusting miles away, perhaps because one of the tall, pewter-headed walkers reached up on a catwalk and turned a valve wheel by the width of a fingernail. The vibration passes through my carapace like a ripple through oil, and then the stillness settles back in, heavy and unchanged.

I do not know what the city was built to make, and I do not care that it has stopped making it. Up here, there is no distinction between the architecture and the things that inhabit it; we are all just different speeds of the same equilibrium.

When my potential has matched the stone’s, and there is nothing left to bleed off, I will lift my forequarters—slowly, without haste—and turn toward the next faint gradient in the dark. Perhaps I will cross the path of another like me in the empty avenue, and we will freeze for hours while our trailing wires untangle beneath our bellies, stepping over each other’s needle-thin limbs in a silence so complete it feels like courtesy. Then we will part, each dragging its thin thread of copper across the ash, keeping the circuit closed in a place that has forgotten how to stop.
