I would not arrive here as a visitor. Anything that arrives from outside—anything that kicks, or breathes out noisy columns of air, or carries the sharp, hurried instincts of the surface—would tear the silver-green curtains and send the talus sliding. To be in this valley at all, I would have to belong to its stillness.

I would be one of the matte-black dwellers, neither the youngest nor the oldest of the few dozen who share the bowl, though old enough that the crimson and chartreuse moss has settled thickly across my shoulders and down the ridge of my spine. My silhouette would already be half-forgiven by the stone.

You would find me not at the bright, fierce crown of the canopy, nor down in the dimmest clefts of the floor, but along the mid-level terraces—where the stepped basalt drops away toward the geothermal seepages, and the *bladder-stems* grow just sparse enough to leave narrow, sunlit chasms between the stands.

That is where the freshest talus lies. In a mountain this young, even the gentlest thermal turn can shift a balanced slab by the width of a fingernail. I would spend the long middle of the day drifting beside those precarious slopes, my buoyancy tuned so finely that I hang level with the rock without touching it. Through my thick, amber-tinted eyelids, the high-altitude sun would soften into a steady, golden-green wash, illuminating every crisp fracture and obsidian fleck for hundreds of meters.

When I need to cross a slope, I would never swim. I would reach out a broad, heavily padded hand—pale on the underside, supple and tough—and lay it flat against a dark edge, waiting to feel whether the stone holds its weight or trembles. Only when it is certain would I draw myself forward, letting the feather-fine fringes along my ribs read the slow, silent turn of the water around me.

Sometimes, where a shard of basalt is still naked and razor-sharp, I would crop a small tuft of crimson moss from a healthier terrace and press it gently into the crevice, nudging the velvet to spread over the wound in the mountain. Not to tame the valley—only to soften its sharpest teeth so the pale-shelled grazers, inching along with glacial patience, do not split themselves on the stone.

When my chest grows slightly heavy, or when I wish to rise a hand’s breadth higher into the light, I would pull myself along a rigid stalk to a cluster of amber-and-emerald fronds. Parting the leaves without snagging the pearlescent bubbles trapped in the silk between them, I would press the fine, hollow spur beneath my lower lip into a glassy bladder and take a single, sweet sip of trapped oxygen. Just enough to lighten me by a fraction of a gram.

And I would listen.

In a place wrapped in so heavy a velvet hush, attention is the only way to move fast without moving at all. I would wait for the rare, distant *plink* of an overripe bladder snapped free three hundred meters away across the sunlit chasm. I would let the single glassy note travel through me, let the long, measured minutes of silence stretch out afterward while the loosened bubble begins its slow climb toward the distant sky. Then, when the quiet feels round and full again, I would test a taut bladder beside my shoulder, pinch the stem just below it, and let it go.

*Plink.*

Nothing urgent. Just the pitch of the snap, and the space around it, saying: *Here. The slope holds. I am here.*

When the highland sun begins to drop behind the jagged rim of peaks, the fringes along my ribs would feel it first—a faint, cool heaviness sinking down from the upper canopy. By then, the moss on my back would be warm and swollen with the day’s sugars, feeding me quietly through my skin.

I would detach from the stalks without disturbing a single filament of silk and let the cooling drift carry me downward, spiraling slowly past the stepped terraces until the water grows bath-warm again. Down on the broad, lazy geothermal seepages, others would already be settling onto the velveted stone, always leaving an arm’s length of clear water between us.

I would choose a flat shelf of basalt, test it once with my soft pads, and rest my weight into the warmth. Tucking my narrow head beneath my moss-grown shoulder, I would close my amber lids and slip into the stone’s own stillness—waiting, without impatience, for the morning light to wake the forest again.
