I would not be on the stone. Nothing built the way I am meant to be built ever touches the stone, unless it is dying and its spars have already begun to crack.

If you looked for me in the bruised-indigo gloom of the basin, you would find me low down—not up among the high, shrieking transit ribbons where the swift ones ride the currents like seeds in a riptide, and not inside the booming cathedral-shells where the crowds gather to scrub the damp from their joints. You would find me a dozen yards above the raw basalt terraces, hanging upside down from a secondary tension-cable where the sub-bass is thick enough to swim in.

To you, the space around me would look empty. To me, it is crowded with geometry.

My three-pronged hooks are closed around nothing—an inch of invisible field-sheath wrapping a matte-black filament no thicker than a hair. Above me, the city rises in tiers of tension and negative space, a mile-high harp played by the warm updrafts. Below me, the freshly fractured floor of the valley rings back every vibration like a struck plate. I don’t have eyes to see the slate twilight, and I wouldn’t want them; light here is a lazy, flat thing that tells you nothing about the weight of the air. Instead, I have the bone-white basket of my crown, its nested tuning-forks humming in sympathy with the valley, and the taut, pearlescent membranes folded neatly along my hollow spars.

I am one of the tuners, though not one of the bright, far-ranging ones who carry news across the districts. My frame is wider-set, my spars a little thicker-walled, suited to the heavy, sluggish air of the lower basin. A long time ago—or perhaps only a few shatterings ago; time here is measured in how long the stone stays sharp—one of my lower ligaments tore in a sudden thermal shear. It healed with a slight knot in it. Whenever the lukewarm mist begins to breathe up from the creases of the rock, that knot swells a fraction and drops my personal pitch by a quarter-tone. It makes me useless for the precision choirs of the upper rings, where a fraction of a hertz can throw a transit current off-line.

Down here, though, a quarter-tone flat is a gift. It means I feel the mist before the rest of the district hears it.

That is where I spend my hours: at the seam where the young stone meets the lowest moss-nets. My work is small and unglamorous, the kind of tending that keeps the great chords from rotting at the root.

When the damp rises, it clings first to the gossamer curtains of aero-flora draping the lowest pylons. You can hear the plants drinking—a slow, sagging *shhh* as their hollow bladder-vines fill and their condensation-sails grow heavy. Left alone, a waterlogged net will drag a tension-cable out of true, bending a clean fifth into a sour, wobbling smear that ripples up through three tiers of the city. So I unhook, flare a single wing-fold to catch the rising warmth, and step across the air from one standing-wave node to the next, finding purchase in the dense, vibrating pockets where the noise locks into stillness.

I prune the heavy tangles with my serrated spurs, letting the wet lace fall down onto the jagged glass below. I never watch it land—I *hear* it land: a soft, wet *slap* that tells me, without a single glance, that the stone beneath that ridge is getting old.

Fresh basalt rings like a bell when a droplet hits it. Older stone—stone that has seen a hundred mists and gathered a skin of mineral dust in its pores—absorbs the blow. It answers with a dull, muffled thud. Part of what I do, hanging here in the warm gloom, is keep count of the dead spots. When enough of the lower terraces go dull and soft, I send a low, pulsing harmonic up the pylon-line to the upper tiers, and when the air finally dries, the parabolic array will gather above us and shatter the floor clean again.

It is a solitary post, mostly, though in a world made of sound you are never truly alone. Even down here, I am constantly inside everyone else’s thoughts.

Half a kilometer above me, two couriers pause on opposite rims of a hollow ring. I don’t know their names, if tuners can be said to have names beyond the signature of their spars, and I don’t need to climb up to listen. Their conversation washes down over me as a braided, shifting architecture of damped chords—one swallowing a slice of the high wind-shriek, the other angling a membrane to sharpen the sub-bass off a pylon. A dispute about a transit-line, maybe, or a warning about a heavy drift of jellies clogging the eastern spires. Their words make the fluid in my crown-cilia sway in complex, mathematical ripples.

And every now and then, off to my left in the shelter of a smaller, moss-hung ring, I feel a sudden, neat absence. A three-foot sphere where the city’s roar simply vanishes, eaten by exact, opposing geometry.

Phase-cancellation. Two tuners have tucked themselves inside the ring, pulled their spars tight, and flared their sails in perfect mirror-opposition, erasing the valley so they can press their cartilage crowns together and speak in the rapid, clicking purr of bone-conduction. From where I hang, that bubble of dead silence feels like a small, smooth pebble resting in a rushing stream. The ambient hum bends around it. I adjust the tension of my own cable with one hooked toe, nudging the harmonic a hair wider so the rising draft doesn’t snag on their little wall of nothing and tear their privacy apart.

I don’t envy them the quiet. Quiet, to me, feels too much like holding your breath.

Still, I know the difference between a chosen silence and the other kind.

You feel it first in the jellies. Down in the sharp creases of the stone, swarms of them drift upward through the mist—faint, sickly-amber sparks to eyes like yours, though to me they are only a soft, rising hiss of gas-bladders brushing against basalt. Usually they float up into the lower cables, where the pale, hollow-boned striders drop on silk tethers to pluck them out of the air.

Then, on some afternoons when the mist is thick and the sub-bass is heavy enough to make my hollow ribs ache, the hiss of the jellies simply stops.

No strider footsteps on the wire. No manta-clicks echoing down from the high spires. Just a sudden, sharp *slap* of damped cables racing down from the upper districts—the city’s panic-ripple.

I don’t think; my ligaments fold before my crown has finished translating the wave. I collapse my pearlescent sails flat against my matte-black spars, becoming nothing more than a dry reed pressed tight against the underside of a pylon, and I stop damping. I stop adjusting. I let my broken joint stiffen and go mute.

Above me, gliding down through the miles of empty twilight, comes the blind spot with teeth.

To you, it would be a sleek, eyeless thing with dish-shaped ears occupying half its skull. To me, it is a moving hole in reality—a creature that flies without whistling vents, without disturbing a single standing wave, stealing through the storm in a sheath of near-silence. As it passes twenty yards overhead, I don’t hear its wings. I hear the *lack* of the city behind it, a swift, cold erasure sweeping across the harp-strings of the valley, listening for the one faint, panicked tremor of a spar against a wire.

In that moment, hanging motionless above a floor of knives, I understand why we keep the stone young, why we braid the air with noise, why even our unborn seed-pods are rocked in the cradle of a continuous, low lullaby. We do not make the roar because we are loud. We make it because if we stopped, there would be nothing left to see by—and the things that hunt in the quiet would own the dark.

The erasure passes. It drifts out over the bruised-indigo haze of the deeper basin, taking its pocket of nothing with it.

For a long beat, the whole district holds its breath, the warm mist beading silently along our folded membranes.

Then, because my post is at the bottom where the sub-bass begins, it is often me who starts it again. I uncurl one long, multi-jointed limb, touch a hook to the invisible sheath of the lowest wire, and let a single, low, slightly flat hum bleed out into the filament.

Above me, a higher wire answers. Then another. Within seconds, the chords weave back together, the mist thickens along the sharp glass below, and I am once more what I was made to be: a small, steady note at the bottom of the world, holding the floor in tune so the sky can sing.
