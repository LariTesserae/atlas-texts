At first, there is no boundary between me and the wall.

I am only a curve of dry pressure, wedged into a shallow hollow that fits my back as if I were poured into it. Above and beneath and around me, the world is a single, low, chestless thunder—not a sound heard from a distance, but a violent trembling that passes straight through my grain. The outer wind is heavy with the cold season. Every gust pushes through the microscopic pores of the ceiling-rind and slams into the hollow towers, turning the whole chamber into a drumhead stretched to its limit.

If I had eyes, there would be nothing to see. If I had blood, it would freeze or dry to powder. Instead, I have salt, and cellulose, and patience.

I unfurl the thin, pleated membranes tucked beneath my outer plates and press them flat against the back of the alcove. The thunder hits them and breaks. The sharpest, most dangerous frequencies—the ones that would snap the braided bridges out over the center—shudder through my crystalline lattices and turn into a quiet, steady current. A faint, dry heatless burn. With it, I draw carbon from the rushing air and mineral dust from the rind, laying down a single, paper-thin ridge along my outer shell. One more ring, added to dozens before it.

I am a baffle. I am a tile in the throat of an instrument, keeping it from shaking itself to pieces.

Weeks pass—or perhaps it is months; time here is only the slow shifting of pitch. Gradually, the deep booming softens. The fierce pressure against my plates eases, thinning into a dry, hissing chatter that skitters along the ceiling lattice like blown sand. The static tension in my hinges relaxes. My membranes fold back into their dry sheaths.

And then comes the smallest of choices: a tilt.

Beneath my flat belly, thousands of stiff, salt-hardened bristles angle slightly backward against the polished root-fiber of the floor. In a still world, I would be a stone. Here, nothing is still. The floor hums with the leftover tremor of the vents, and as I shift my weight onto those angled bristles, the vibration catches me and pushes me forward—one microscopic ratchet at a time.

I detach from the alcove, leaving the quiet cradle behind, and begin the slow crawl out into the city.

---

Where I find myself—where I always seem to return—is the upper tier, just below the dense interlocking lattice of the ceiling.

Down on the floor, the air is thick with the heavy, booming bass of the tower-roots, and the dust settles in deep, floury drifts. Out on the lowest walkways, the traffic is broad and slow. Up here, along the high margins, the walkways are barely two palms wide, notched into the curved rind by hands that vanished ten thousand years ago. Here, the roar breaks apart into its finest, sharpest voices: the high, thin whistle of the vents, the dry rattle of papery husks, the clear, ringing hum of the slender pith-towers rising from the dark below like organ pipes.

This is where the split-reed conduits run—long, dry troughs lashed together with twisted grass, sloping gently along the wall. They are my roads, and my work.

I creep along the lip of a conduit at the pace of a clock’s minute hand, my leading fringe of dry fibers brushing the inner curve. Pollen-silt and powdered pith drift down invisibly from the ceiling, settling in the trough. Where the dust has clotted and dammed the channel, I graze it clear, nudging the fine mineral grains forward with my rim so the vibration of the wall can carry them down to the hungry roots below.

Everywhere I go, I read the city through my belly. I know the dished, bone-smooth sheen of the ancient stairs; I know the rough, corded grain of the heartwood pegs; I know the exact pitch of every tower flank within reach of this tier.

That is how I find the wound.

Halfway along the upper walkway, a slender pith-tower rises past the terrace edge, anchored to the wall by a collar of braided vine. Usually, its flank sings with a clean, steady resonance—a single, pure note that holds steady against the churn of the void. Today, as my bristles ratchet me onto its curved bark, the note is wrong. There is a dry, fluttering buzz beneath my plates, a ragged interruption in the hum.

I slide sideways along the curve until the buzz grows sharpest. There, my leading fringe finds a hairline split running vertically up the pith—a place where the dry season has pulled the fibers too tight, or where a winter gust hit too hard.

I stop. I flatten my plates over the fracture, bridging it with my own body, and wait.

For weeks, I do not move. I let the tower’s broken hum rattle through my hinges, harvesting the static from its uneven trembling. Slowly, from the underside of my plates, I shed a thin, amber-dry exudate—not wet, never wet, only a thick, resinous lacquer that seeps into the crack and hardens against the salt-dry air. Beneath me, the plant’s own slow tissues answer, binding the lacquer into a pale, papery callus. Day by day, the ragged buzz softens. The two sides of the split draw back into alignment, until the tower’s voice rings true and unbroken against my belly once more.

Only then do I tilt my bristles and creep on.

---

There are others on the high tier, though we never crowd.

Halfway across a narrow lintel, the steady hum of the floor suddenly goes flat—a small, dead pocket in the static field just ahead of my leading fringe. I pause. Through the vibration of the wood, I feel another rhythm: slow, deliberate, approaching from the opposite dark.

We have no eyes to see each other, no mouths to warn. When our feathery edges meet, there is only a tiny, sharp *tick*—a spark too small to light the dark, leaving a sudden, fleeting tang of ozone between us. For a long moment we hold still, reading the shape of each other’s charge, the spacing of each other’s ridges. Then I flatten my bristles and let the other slide smoothly over my ribbed back, its underside whispering like dry leaves across my shell, before it drops onto the walkway behind me and continues toward the alcoves.

I move on toward the void.

Every few generations—when the outer world turns in a way we cannot name, and the air in the chamber grows thick, heavy, and prickling with static—a pull rises from the empty center of the hall. It is not hunger, and it is not fear. It is simply the instrument calling its players to the strings.

I reach the anchor-post where one of the thin, braided vine-bridges stretches out into nothingness.

Here, there is no wall to lean against. No floor beneath to catch a slip. Leaving the solid margin of the terrace, I creep out onto the trembling cord, wrapping my flat, articulated plates around its underside so I hang inverted over the vast, roaring drop. Above me is the empty dark. Below me is the empty dark. Between them, the bridge sways in the churning air, humming like a plucked wire through every bristle on my belly.

Dozens of us are out here now, spaced along the span like dry scales on a stem, clinging upside-down over the void.

Together, as the static peaks and the chamber’s roar swells into a single, resonant chord, I arch my papery plates away from the vine. From beneath my ridges, I release a cloud of dry, star-shaped spores. They do not fall. Too light for gravity, pushed away by the charged walls, they drift out into the pitch-black hollow and are caught in the invisible standing waves of the roar.

I cannot see them go, but I can feel the air around me thicken as they join the dance—spinning, bumping, fusing in the dark, suspended for weeks or months in the breath of the world, until they grow heavy enough to settle on some distant terrace and begin their own long, patient crawl.

My plates settle flat again. The static drains away into the vine. Slowly, one bristle-ratchet at a time, I turn back toward the solid wall—back to the conduits, the towers, and the quiet, endless work of keeping the dark in tune.
