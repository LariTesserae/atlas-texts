I would not be on the high parapet, looking out. The open air up there is too thin to hold a thought for long; it scatters like pumice dust before it can settle.

Instead, I think you would find me a few levels down, in the lee of a tilted plaza—one of those districts that has slipped forty degrees off the true vertical, so that what used to be a colonnade now leans out over the abyss like the ribs of a beached hull. There is a corner there where two unchiseled walls meet at an angle that shouldn’t quite close, creating a narrow, triangular pocket out of the wind. From the outside, it looks like a shadow that forgot to blow away. Inside, the air is dead, cellar-cold, and faintly prickling with static from the hollow tower directly behind the wall.

If I press my back against that stone, I can feel it: not warmth, exactly, though it is a fraction of a degree less cold than the air. It is the slow, patient hum of the hanging chandeliers in the shaft on the other side, turning imperceptibly on their silica threads as the district shifts by the width of a hair over the course of a week.

It takes a long time to realize what I am there, because in a place without mirrors or water, you only know your shape by what the city asks of you.

At first, I might think I am still a visitor—someone who climbed up from the wet, noisy lowlands and simply never found the turn-around point. I still remember the idea of rain, the way a word feels when it leaves a throat and actually *travels* through the air to hit someone else’s ear. I still have the habit of counting seconds when something falls.

But if I look down at my hands in the flat, slate-grey light, there is no steam rising from my wrists. My skin does not flush with the cold. Around my knuckles and the hinges of my elbows, the surface has gone pale and matte, like unglazed porcelain left in a dry cellar, finely crazed with lines that catch the chalky dust. I haven’t been greasing my joints with the orange crust and the grazer-powder—not out of neglect, and not out of courage, just out of a quiet curiosity to see how long it takes for a bend to become an angle.

No, not a visitor anymore. Not quite one of the travertine walkers, either.

They have that dark, glassy band across their faces, smooth as river-slate, that lets them read the bunched-up miles and step cleanly over the frayed edges of a square. I don’t have that. When I look down a foreshortened avenue, the far archway looks ten paces away to me, and it *fools* me every time. If I tried to stride across it the way they do, my foot would come down on empty pavement nine-tenths of a mile short of the gate. My eyes—or whatever dry, recessed lenses have replaced them—still believe in the old, honest geometry, even though I know better.

So I don’t cross the gulfs. I stay in the seams.

What I am, I think, is a keeper of the small tolerances—something halfway between a shadow-gatherer and an archivist of a language nobody speaks.

I spend my time—though *time* here is just the rate at which dust pools in a staircase—watching the things the walkers are too patient to bother with. I know which crevices along the tilted avenues hold the thickest colonies of the blind, fingernail-sized grazers, and I can tell by the faint, silvery trails they leave whether the district’s slow drift is accelerating. When a squall of detached shadows flutters down like burnt paper, I don’t press my belly to them to hide from the light, the way the flagstone-creatures do. Instead, I catch a single, matte-black scrap on the back of my forearm and watch how long it takes to thin out and vanish into the mineral grain of my skin. Sometimes it takes an hour. Sometimes, if the static from the nearby tower is strong, it clings for three days, pulsing faintly like a bruise that has forgotten how to ache.

When the travertine people pass my corner—draped in their layered glider-membranes, making that dry *shh-shh* that is the closest thing this altitude has to music—they don’t look at me as a stranger, and they don’t look at me as kin.

Every once in a long while, one of them will stop on the slanted terrace outside my triangular pocket. They won’t come in; the space is too tight for their long, jointed stride. They will simply stand at the lip of the drop, their dark mineral visor angled down toward the dimness, and set one heel firmly against the curbstone that runs straight through the wall to my back.

And through the stone, I feel it: a single, slow, sub-audible tremor. Not a greeting, and not a warning. Just a weight-check. A way of asking, *Is this seam still holding? Has the angle widened since the last season?*

I don’t have the heavy, dense bone of their heels to answer with, so I press both palms flat against the wall behind me—right where the hanging things on the other side are drinking the charge—and let the faint, prickling static pass through my dry wrists into the curbstone. A tiny, sharp hum, lighter than a footstep, more like the snap of a salt crystal forming.

They stand there for a moment, absorbing the vibration through the soles of their feet. Then they lift their heel, gather the translucent silica sheets around their shoulders, and step off the slanted curb into a fold in the air—vanishing from ten paces away to a mile off without ever having disturbed the dust between.

I stay where I am.

It isn’t a bad life, being neither the stone nor the thing that walks on it. The light here is corrosive if you stand out in the middle of the plazas, bleaching everything into the same chalky habit, but here in the lee of the arch, it only works slowly. Every few years, one of my fingers stiffens a little further, deciding it prefers the sixty-degree bend of a lintel to the bother of opening and closing. I let it. In return, I can feel the whole city’s slow, geological weather through my ribs—every bridge that unhooks itself three miles away, every shadow-squall settling into the sunken amphitheaters like soot.

If you ever climbed up from the world of sound and water and stood on that parapet, heavy with your wet heartbeat, I wouldn’t come out to meet you. I wouldn’t have anything to say that the air could carry.

But if you wandered down into the tilted districts, out of the wind, and happened to lean your shoulder against a wall that felt inexplicably, faintly warm—you might feel a small, dry tremor answer the thump of your pulse. Just once, before the dust settled on your sleeve, to let you know the stone was listening.
