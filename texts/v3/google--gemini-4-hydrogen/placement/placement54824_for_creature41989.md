I would not be down in the lowest tiers, where the fissures bleed ember-red and the stone-borers are thick as roots. My eyes are too pale for that kind of glare, and I have no taste for the long, silent patience it takes to corner a borer in a dead-end slot.

I would be higher up—midway through the lattice, in the dark where the only light is the greasy amber of a tallow basin or the occasional bruised-violet pulse of a glider shifting in its sleep. Specifically, I would be at one of the twelve-way junctions: a pocket just wide enough for two people to sit without their knees touching the basalt, where a stressed seam of milky quartz cuts diagonally across the floor-slab like a healed scar.

I would be one of the narrow people, though no longer one of the fastest. My ribcage still folds when I exhale, enough to slip sideways through a hand-span slot, but the horn-plates on my knees and the outer blades of my forearms have grown thick and layered, dark as cured slate and crosshatched with white scratches from volcanic glass. The tallow in the creases of my joints comes a little slower now. I do not climb the sheer chimneys unless I must.

Instead, I stay near the seam, and I listen for weight.

In a world with no wind and no rain, the only weather is the continent overhead. Three miles of stone is never truly still; it settles by fractions of a hair, grinding quartz against basalt until the seam beneath my palms builds a slow, prickling charge. The copper bristles along the backs of my wrists stand upright first. Then comes the smell—sharp, metallic, like iron held close to a spark. That is when I press my bare heels flat to the floor and wait for the stone to decide whether it will hold or shear.

Most of the time, it holds. When it does not—when a shelf a quarter-mile off lets go with a dry, shuddering *crack* that travels straight up through my shins—I take my flint chip and walk the twelve corridors, one by one, bracing palm-to-sole between the blades, checking the ways.

That is what I would be: a mender of junctions, a keeper of the small accommodations.

I carry a coil of sinew, a pouch of glider-resin scraped fresh from the sleeping tapestries that hang above the pocket, and three spare ladder-rungs—smooth, sunless bone, curved slightly at the ends. When a shelf shears and takes out a lashed-rib bridge, I wedge myself across the gap and lash a new one in. When a flint-chipped notch wears smooth or snaps under a hurried heel, I tap out a fresh bite in the obsidian. If I find one of the blind climbers crushed by a fall of glass, I drink what hemolymph has not already vanished into the thirsty rock and leave the cracked shell beside the nearest basin, where someone else will find a use for the carapace.

Because I stay near the junction, the travelers pass through me.

I hear them long before I see the milky gleam of their eyes—a dry *scritch* of keratin against glass, or a faint, rhythmic *tap-tap-pause* sent through the floor-slab from three corridors away. *Who is at the basin?* they ask with their heels. *How is the seam?*

I answer with the butt of my bone awl against the quartz: *Clear to the north. Tight to the east; a blade dropped yesterday. Rest if you need tallow.*

Sometimes one of them wedges into the pocket beside me to wait out the heat-stupor of a climber colony, or simply to sit where the walls do not press against both shoulders at once. We do not waste breath on air. If there is something private to say—that an elder in the upper flutes has finally crawled into a warm pocket and folded their limbs to dry, or that the borers two tiers down are drumming too fast, agitated by some shift in the deep mantle—we lean in until our foreheads touch, our second eyelids flickering sideways to clear the glass-dust, and we tap the news softly against each other’s collarbones. Bone to bone, nothing spilled into the dry dark.

When they leave, threading back into the vertical slots with the unhurried certainty of spiders, the hush returns.

I sit back against the smooth basalt wall of the pocket. The grease-basin holds its dim, greasy halo, steady as a held breath. Above me, a glider shifts on its basalt flute, flashing a slow, violet warning to nothing in particular, then settles back into stillness.

Beside my elbow, the stone is already scored with thousands of neat, parallel lines—some cut by hands that are now bridges over three-foot drops, or needles lying beside a basin ten miles away. I do not know what the first of them were counting. Tremors, perhaps. Or harvests. Or simply the long, even intervals between one visitor and the next.

I take my flint chip. I feel my heart beating thirty times a minute, slow and economical, saving every drop of warmth. I press the flint to the slate, draw one clean line beside the others, and wait for the copper hairs on my wrists to lift again.
