I would find myself in a leeward notch, three paces back from where the stone curves out into the gale, with my heels pressed flat against the limestone so I can feel the spire thinking.

Because it does feel like thought—that deep, chest-rattling hum rising through the soles of my feet, up my shins, into the hinge of my jaw. A solid mountain shouldn’t have a pulse, yet this one is hollow as a reed, and the wind from the unseen abyss below is playing it like a throat. When a pressure-pocket collapses somewhere inside the vertical chimneys, I don’t just hear the rifle-crack; I feel it in my back teeth, a sharp, dry jolt that makes my eyes water before the sound even finishes rattling the resin-sealed baskets stacked beside me.

If I am here, I am not a native thing. I don’t have the hooked keratin plates to ride a torn mat of turf across a mile of empty sky, nor the pale, leathery flanks to read the pitch of a chimney and snap a scavenger out of the dark. I am too soft for the bare stone and too heavy for the thermals. I belong to the ones who come at dawn on creaking kites of hardwood and cured leaf, and who leave before the weather turns too cruel to bear.

Even among them, I think I would be the one who stays nearest the threshold—not the boldest climber out on the windward crowns, where the crosswinds shear past like blades, and not the fastest blade in the root-tangles. I would be a *line-keeper*, crouched at the lip of the shelter where the bone wind-vanes spin in their blurs, one hand always resting on the braided vine anchored to the copper pitons.

Before stepping out, there is the ritual of the hood. You pull it down from its wooden peg—stiff with the salt and sweat of whoever wore it last month, or last year, darkened where their jawbone rubbed the leather. You pack your ears with wads of moss soaked in heavy plant-oil, then buckle the strap tight beneath your chin. The moment the leather closes over your ears, the world doesn’t go silent; it goes *inward*. The shrieking hiss of the moss-curtains and the tearing roar of the open air drop away into a muffled, underwater booming. What’s left is the thud of your own heart, the wet rasp of your breathing, and the vast, inescapable bass of the rock vibrating straight through your skull.

You don’t talk here. Talking is for the shelf-towns beyond the heat-haze, where air sits still enough to carry a vowel. Out here, a voice is just wasted breath torn off your lips before it reaches your own nose.

Instead, I hold the vine.

Twenty yards away, out under the whipping silver-green banners of the moss, two others of my crew are crouched low, slicing into the rubbery root-mats to bleed the amber resin into weighted gourds. The mist-front hits without warning—a dense, warm cloud materializing out of nowhere against the spire’s flank. In three heartbeats, I can’t see them at all. The world shrinks to six feet of wet, pale stone, the sudden smell of soaked greenery, and the taut, braided line vanishing into the white blind.

My thumb rests on the fiber. Through the rope, I can feel them working—the rhythmic, slight tug as one shifts weight, the steady tremble of the wind sawing against the line mid-span.

Then the bone vane beside my shoulder stutters. It doesn’t just slow; it reverses with a sharp *clack* against its peg, and at the same instant, the hum in my heels drops half a tone. A downdraft is building out of the upper cold.

I don’t shout. I grip the back of my bone knife against the vine and draw it across the braided fibers in one long, grating rasp—a vibration that travels faster and truer than any scream through the wet air. *Drop flat. Hook in.*

A second later, the line jerks twice in answer—two sharp snaps against my palm. *Heard.*

I throw myself belly-down into the lee of the cairn, hooking my elbow through the anchor loop just as the dry gust strikes. It shreds the five-minute cloud into nothingness and slams against the spire’s shoulder with a sound like a collapsing roof. Even through the oiled moss and stiff leather, the shriek of the stone throats above me makes my teeth ache. Above the shelter’s low roof, a forty-foot curtain of moss tears loose from its lip and goes sailing out into the blinding daylight, spinning slowly over the abyss.

When it passes, and the vane settles back into its steady blur, I sit up, unhook my elbow, and check the line. It’s still taut. Still breathing.

I think that is who I would be in this world: not a conqueror of the spires, and not one of the blind, armored things that call the hollow bone home. Just someone small and wiry, sun-blackened and salt-stiff, sitting at the edge of the roar with a hand on a cord—listening to a world too loud for words, waiting for the baskets to fill, and watching the sky for the morning updraft that will carry us home.
