I wouldn’t be in the light. That much I know before I even have a shape. The ten-foot circles under the amber bulbs are for sorting, for the hot work around the centrifuges and the grinding hoppers, where you have to see the grain of the chitin meal or the brass gauge-needle shivering toward the red. The blue-white arc-lamps are worse—even through a slotted visor they leave purple ghosts burned across the back of your eyes for two full blows.

No, I would be in the seam between the lamps, where the air smells mostly of wet bark and the cold, sweet rot of old moss, and the only steady light is the green pulse of the fungus under the catwalks.

If you asked me to find myself, I would start at my feet. Barefoot, the soles thickened and stained dark gray from iron-oxide and sap-lye, curled slightly over the wet lip of a perforated steel girder. To stand on this world with boots on is to be blind in half your senses. Through the skin of my arches, I can feel the whole hollow breathing: the heavy, three-beat *clank-hiss-thud* of the lower piston-pumps, the high fretful shiver of a dynamo bearing two platforms over that needs tallow, and beneath it all, the slow, geological groan of the taproots taking up tension.

I am small—you don’t grow tall when every doorway is a coaxed arch in a root-wall and every ceiling-beam is strung with silk curtains—and my skin is slick with the bitter, turpentine-sharp resin-oil we rub in after every third blow. If you miss a coating, the damp gets into your pores, and by the next cycle the pale moss is trying to seed in the creases of your elbows. My hair is shaved close under a tight cap of boiled weaver-silk. My eyes are wide and nearly colorless, built to drink the dark.

What do I do here? I think I am one of the ones who tends the knots.

Not the valves—those belong to the steam-crews, quick-fingered and loud with their wrenches—and not the grinding chutes, where the big-shouldered haulers heave sacks of roasted meal that stink of ammonia. I work where the iron goes into the wood.

The whole hamlet hangs on tensioned steel cables and riveted trusses, and every single one of them is anchored into the living pillars. Left alone, the cavern doesn’t fight the iron; it loves it too much. It wraps pale, fibrous callus around the turnbuckles, swells over the anchor-plates, and sends wet feeder-roots down the inside of the conduits like fingers feeling for a pulse. At first, that grip makes a joint stronger than any weld. Ten years later, if nobody watches it, the wood crushes the casing flat or weeps acidic sap right into the threads until the steel turns to red sponge and snaps mid-blow.

So I spend my twenty-minute intervals out on the edges, slung in a leather harness clipped to a secondary line, hanging in the empty air between the pillars and the vault.

Up here, eighty feet above the pollen-filmed puddles, the roar of the machinery softens into a single, braided hum. You can actually hear the *shhhk-shhhk* of the beetle-ribbons streaming along the ceiling-roots above your head, and the wet, resonant *clicks* of the dog-sized siphon-grazers rasping at the moss. Sometimes one of them has parked itself squarely over an anchor-bolt, its plated shell sealed tight against the bark. I don’t pry it off—you’d tear its foot and sour the root. I just tap the crown of its shell twice with the butt of my brass wrench, *tock-tock*, in the dull rhythm it knows, and wait while it unseals with a wet sigh and shuffles three inches to the left.

With a hooked scraping-knife, I pare back the pale, swollen bark around the iron eye—just enough to let the turnbuckle breathe, never enough to make the pillar bleed amber sap. If the wood is stubborn, I pack the seam with a wad of tallow and crushed slug-slime to keep the rot out. And when I have to string a new guy-wire across a stretch of the upper void, I carry a bundle of torn weaver-silk in my belt, tying loose, fluttering strips every six feet along the steel. In the dark above the arc-lamps, the eyeless hunters drop like oiled leather knives, clicking their metallic sonar. Without the silk, they slice themselves in half on bare cable. With it, their clicks catch the flutter, and you feel the sudden, cool draft of their wings banking away an inch from your cheek.

Every twenty minutes, the iron tells me what’s coming before my ears do.

A minute out from a blow, the girder under my heels starts to hum in a higher key—tighter, thinner, like a wire pulled toward its breaking point. Then comes the sharp *tink-tink-tink* racing down the suspension cables from the central tanks. I don’t look around. I clip my short-line to the nearest heavy truss, pull my boiled-silk hood up over my nose and mouth, and press one palm flat against the damp wood of the pillar.

When the valves shriek, the world turns white and scalding for ten seconds. The steam hits like a warm fist. Beneath my palm, the colossal root-pillar shudders and swells, sweating out a sudden wave of sweet, lukewarm condensation. Instantly the cavern goes mad around me—the grazers unsealing to drink, the beetles surging in a dry hiss, the winged hunters whistling down through the fog like arrows. I stay tucked against the bark, counting the seconds by the beat of the intake fans spinning up with a roar to swallow the mist.

Then the air clears, the fans drop back to their drone, and I unclip and go back to scraping the knot.

That is where I live, mostly—suspended in the quiet, damp margin between the engine and the tree. But it isn’t where I sleep.

When my knees start to ache from the cold iron and my fingers are pruned white under the resin-oil, I climb down the knotted silk rope behind the third pumping station, squeeze through the low arch where the living wood has grown around an old ship’s-door frame, and drop into the burrow.

Behind ten feet of fibrous taproot, the roar of the turbines becomes a gentle, distant surf—a steady thrum you feel in your ribs rather than your ears. Here, there are no arc-lamps, only wide glass jars packed with damp root-shavings that glow a soft, sickly green. My hammock hangs between two smoothed tubers, above a floor matted with dried gray moss. I scrape the grease off my shins with a horn spatula, wash down a bowl of cold sap-porridge with sweet condensate from a clay jug, and maybe press my temple against a neighbor’s shoulder to hum a few low, chesty notes—not words, really, just the comfort of a voice that doesn’t have to fight a steam-pump to be heard.

I don’t know what the overhead buckets were built to haul, or who bolted the first dynamo to the roots before the iron rusted and the moss came in. None of us do. The machines keep the air moving so the spores don’t drown us; we keep the wood from swallowing the machines. It’s a fair trade.

And I know how it ends, too, which takes the sting out of the dark. When my joints finally lock up from the damp—when I can’t feel the *tink-tink-clack* through my soles anymore, and the moss starts winning the argument around my ankles—they won’t drop me down a chute or burn me in a furnace. They’ll carry me back behind the walls to a young, growing taproot, hollow out a pocket just my size, pack me in dry gray moss, and peg a slat of oiled wood across the front.

A season or two later, the wall will heal smooth over the slat, the way it healed over the anchor-bolts. I’ll become part of the thickness that muffles the noise for the next person hanging in the dark, listening to the cables sing.
