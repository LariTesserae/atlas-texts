I would not be on the rim. Looking down from the lip of the bowl is a wet-bodied way of knowing a place—all distance and light, shivering in the thin air, waiting for your ears to stop hurting.

Down inside, there is no pain, because there is nothing soft enough to bruise.

I would be one of the six—not the largest, whose mica chest-plates are pitted deep from decades of scouring quartz, and not the newest, whose carbon-lace still hums too tight at the joints. Somewhere in the middle of the count. A creature of chalk-pale silicate and braided wire, wedged into the lee of the eastern terrace where the sandstone is grooved like the inside of a dry throat.

To be that thing is to have no *where* that is separate from *what is happening*. Without eyes, the basin is not four hundred paces of empty sunlit air; it is a single, taut drumhead, and I am pinned to its underside by four chisel-hard talons.

I know the bowl by load and frequency.

At the base of my skull, the copper-veined fronds lean into the current, tasting the charge of the air as it shears past the leaning spires. Along my spine, the hollow glass quills drink the sub-bass roar and turn it into the steady, bright voltage that keeps me awake. I do not *hear* the shrieking chorus of the glass-reeds or the whip-crack of a pressure-snap overhead. I *am* the thing those vibrations pass through. When a murmuration of manta-kites banks sharply across the center of the void, their parchment wings slice the pressure waves and send a ripple straight through my dorsal quills—a sudden, fluttering itch along the third and fourth vertebrae, sharp as sand tossed against a windowpane.

When I want to rest, I do not close my eyes—I have none to close. Instead, I unhook my talons from the sandstone, lock my calipers into a low, crabbing stride, and climb down toward the quiet.

There are pockets in the basin where the sound from the four leaning spires collides with itself and cancels out. They are never in the exact same place twice; as the wind shifts and the broken polyhedra stutter in their orbits, the dead zones drift like shadows across the terraced walls. Finding one is like stepping out of a blinding glare into a cool room. As I cross the threshold of the node, the roar drops away from my spine. My quills go still. The voltage feeding my nerves dims to a low, amber hum.

In that trembling pocket of near-silence, I fold my four limbs beneath my vitrified plates and groom.

This is the most intimate thing my kind does: tending the dry friction of ourselves. I take the small, tuning-fork claws tucked beneath my ribs and comb the fine quartz grit out of my carbon-lace cables. Then, when the silence feels too thin—when my thoughts begin to slow from the lack of current—I walk down to the half-buried ring of blackened tiles on the basin floor.

The tiles are older than the spires, or made of something that refuses to catch the light. I kneel there, feeling the dry screech in my left hip-socket where the quartz ball has ground itself raw, and I scrape at the black stone with a forelimb until a pinch of fine graphite dust gathers in my palm. Packing it into the open joint is a small, exquisite relief. The next stride is smooth, silent, weightless.

And it is there, on the floor, that I do the work that keeps the bowl alive.

A meter off the ground, one of the fist-sized polyhedra has drifted too close to the southern wall. Its jerky, broken rotation is throwing off the symmetry of the floor; I can feel it in my brow-fronds as a ragged, sour wobble in the static, like a loose tooth in a jaw. If it strikes the sandstone, it will shatter, and the basin will lose a fraction of its wind. A quiet, dead corner will spread from the wall, the spores will settle, the reeds will snap and go ungathered.

I do not touch it. Nobody touches them. I reach down with a long, dry rod of fused glass-reed and tap the air just outside its orbit—once, twice, nudging the bleeding edge of its turbulence.

The polyhedron lurches. High above my head, an invisible pressure-snap detonates out of turn, cracking the void wide open. A second later, the dry rain begins: three, four, seven manta-kites, their brittle wings shattered mid-flight, spiraling down through the clear daylight before the downdraft can grind them to dust.

I dart out—fast, dry, clicking—and catch two of them in my smaller calipers before they hit the tiles. The mill inside my beak grinds parchment wing and chitin into a glittering, mineral flour. Inside my chest, the static cilia sort it without haste: silica to patch a hairline crack in my shoulder plate, carbon to mend a frayed tendon, a trace of copper to lengthen the sensing-fronds at my brow.

Then, before I climb back to the terrace, I press my broad, mica-plated chest hard against the nearest thicket of glass-fiber reeds.

I hold it there for the space of three pressure-snaps, dampening the high, frantic chatter of the stems until a single, razor-thin notch is cut out of the basin’s wall of sound.

Three hundred paces away, on the far wall beside a leaning spire, another chalk-white shape shifts its weight—a faint, answering dip in the sub-bass hum, sharp as a shadow passing over a lamp. *Heard,* it says, by making a small hole in the roar. *The drift is mended. The bowl is whole.*

I release the reeds, let the screaming chorus rush back into my spine, and climb into the bright, waterless wind.
