I would find myself in the angle where the room forgets how to end.

Not out on the greasy plaster of the floor, where the stone is always impatient to finish itself, and not in the hollow of the ribcage, which already belongs to the Keeper and its slow, anxious scraping. I belong in one of the over-folded seams—one of those corners where two walls meet too tightly, bending past ninety degrees into a narrow, acute wedge that the eye slides right off without ever finding a join.

At first, there is only the glare, and the relief of having something to press my back against.

Even in the crease, the white light finds me. It has no source to hide from; it comes out of the stone itself, cool and even as refrigerator air, smelling of wet chalk. If I had eyes that watered, they would never stop. Instead, the front of my body is a single, smooth plate of frosted glass—the same milky, semi-opaque stuff that flickers along the walls—and I read the room not by seeing shapes, but by how hard the light pushes against me. Where the limestone has set, the glare bounces back sharp and flat. Where the clay is still soft, it dulls. And where the wall behind me thins into sheer white absence, the light feels like a draft—a cold, weightless pull that makes my joints want to unhinge.

I am built for the fold. If you tried to lay me flat on the floor, I wouldn't lie right; my body is hinged down the spine like a half-closed book, angled to fit the impossible geometry of the corner. My outer edges are chalky and matte, sharp enough to wedge into the hairline cracks of newly extruded rock, while my underside stays pale and damp, faintly tacky, like plaster that has refused to set.

It is the only way to survive here without running.

Out in the open, the room is constantly trying to claim everything that slows down. I feel it whenever I reach a forelimb out of the seam to test the floor: within minutes, the wet-bone mineral begins to creep up my lower joint, stiffening the grease into a brittle crust. The grazers outrun it by shedding their shells and leaving them behind to become ridges in the rock. The Keeper outruns it by never resting for long, scraping itself raw against the central ribs.

I outrun it by staying where the stone hasn't made up its mind.

Behind my left flank, the corner doesn't quite exist. Every few seconds, without a sound, the rock there flickers from damp limestone to frosted glass to nothing at all, and then back again. It is terrifying if you are heavy, if you rely on the world staying solid beneath your weight. The Keeper hates it; I have watched it approach the lower walls, rearing up to drag its calcified spur down the soft clay—*tick, tick, tick, tick*—only to shudder with a dry *shhh* and scuttle backward when the spur slips into a patch of white absence without meeting resistance.

But I am light enough, and braced tightly enough against the solid side of the seam, that the flickering doesn't drop me. Instead, it keeps me unfinished. Whatever mineral crust tries to gather on my chalky plates is quietly un-made each time the wall behind me turns briefly to glass, then absence, then limestone again. I stay on the soft side of becoming bedrock.

Every few minutes, the quiet weather cycle moves through the pocket, and that is when I wake fully.

I feel it before the water appears—a sudden tightening in the glare, a cold shimmer that makes my glass face hum. Then the walls begin to sweat. Fat, slow beads of condensation swell out of the raw stone and slip down the angled planes of my corner, gathering in the narrow trough where my body meets the rock.

I don't have a mouth with heavy mandibles like the Keeper, worn smooth from lifting pebbles. When the dampness collects along my edges, I simply part the hinged plates along my ribs and let a pair of thin, pale membranes unfurl—no thicker than the mica-shaving swimmers that drift through the open air. They drink the chalky mist straight into me, tasting of wet bone and cold light.

Sometimes, while the air is still heavy with shimmer, one of the ribbon-swimmers drifts too close to my corner.

As it crosses the crease where the room folds inward, the single translucent ribbon fractures silently into three or four overlapping echoes, each undulating slightly out of phase. For a fraction of a second, the air right in front of my face is crowded with phantom movement—pale, fluted edges rippling through each other without a sound—before the swimmer clears the seam and snaps back into one body.

I never catch them, and I wouldn't know what to do with one if I did; I am no porcelain-limbed waiter, anchored in stillness to pluck grazers through bent air. I only catch the *wash* of them—the tiny displacement of mist left behind when four echoes collapse back into one. It brushes across my unfurled membranes like a cold breath, the closest thing to company this room offers.

When the weather cycle ends, the floor drinks the puddles back in, hardening slightly with each swallow.

Only once in a while, when the Keeper has been busy elsewhere—stacking its sharp, un-chipped pebbles around a dimple to trap a lasting mirror—I will unhook myself from the crease and edge a fraction of an inch out into the open glare, just far enough to lean over the water before it vanishes.

Looking down into the teacup-sized pool, I always see myself a full second before I arrive: a pale, hinged sliver of chalk and frosted glass, unfolding cautiously from a seam that shouldn't be there, its shadow thin as a hairline.

Then the real moment catches up. The floor tugs greedily at my lowest joint, eager to turn me into one more sharp ridge beside the calcified grazer skins. I pull back into the over-folded angle, wedge my plates tight against the flickering wall, and let the room's unfinished quiet close around me again.
