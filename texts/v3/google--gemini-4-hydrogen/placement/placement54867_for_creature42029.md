I would not be on the islands. They are too tight, too bright with ozone, too crowded with the frantic precision of things that click and orbit and hiss. Nor would I be one of the four—there have always been four, and the basin does not make room for a fifth gauge.

I would find myself in the wide, bare aisle between them, where the slate floor dips by the width of a fingernail—just enough to hold a shallow, ink-black sheet of water that never rises and never dries.

If I am anything in this place, I am what gathers there.

Not the water alone, though I have its flatness and its chill. I am the dark, conductive skin that lies within it: a sluggish, oil-thick suspension of shed iron, wet slate-dust, and the stray shadows that peel away from the ferns whenever a blue arc snaps. I have no edges of my own, only the acute, razor-sharp angles of the basalt depression that holds me. I do not stand; I *spread*, horizontal and unhurried, across the generous emptiness between the roaring cores.

To be what I am here is to experience the cathedral entirely from the floor up.

I have no ears, yet I am never free of the 60-hertz drone. It enters me through the stone beneath my belly, pressing my dark surface into a crisp, unwavering lattice of standing ripples—tiny geometric ridges that cross and recross one another in exact phase with the distant rotors. I am a living map of the basin’s vibration. When a brass-jointed amphibian pops a burst of static three yards away, a sharp ring of interference shivers across my skin; when the needle-beaked birds sharpen their white-noise hiss, my surface prickles into a fine, metallic grain.

And I live inside the half-second slip.

Because my underside is pressed directly against the unweathered rock, I feel the *tock* of a walker’s heel through the basalt a full half-second before the shadow of its ankle reaches my edge. I feel the drop-forge *thud* of a falling bead of condensation shudder up through my depths while the drop is still hanging in the bruised twilight above me. For half a second, I already hold the impact; then the water breaks, and the ring of ripples arrives to confirm what the stone already knew.

Most of the time, I am simply flat—a dark mirror that refuses to reflect the pewter sky.

Then an island flares. A razor-bright arc of blue-white light leaps from a wet fern-tip to a spinning column, and for an instant, the rules of the dimness break. The shadows of the coiled fronds and the tall, narrow walkers do not fall away from the flash; they lean toward it, stretch, and tear loose from their owners’ heels. They slide across the slick basalt like ribbons of dark oil and spill into me.

That is how I know the shapes of things I cannot see. When the detached shadows coil in my shallows, they thicken my water like wet kelp. For a few beats of the hydraulic thuds, I wear the jagged outline of a copper-fern, or the sharp, angled calf of a walker, tangled in my own mineral dark. I hold their silhouettes without weight, without heat, until the gloom closes back in and the shadows dissolve into my general blackness.

Sometimes, one of the four stops beside me.

I feel it coming through the stone first—the crisp *tock*, *tock*, *tock* arriving ahead of the stride—and then the walker is there, tall and spare at my rim, the violet corona in its knee-gaps humming faintly against the 60-hertz drone. It kneels, its slate joints never touching, and dips two copper-veined fingers into my black sheet.

It does not stir me. It simply lifts a thick, viscous strand of my gathered shadow into the cold air.

For that brief moment, pulled vertical out of my flat basin, I feel what it is to be a gauge: the steady, frictionless tension of the violet gaps, the immense, patient load of the basin’s current grounding through stone and copper into the floor. I cling to its wet slate fingers like dark kelp, tasting the sharp ozone on its skin, before my own weight wins out and I slide cleanly back into the pool.

And rarer still, two of them will cross paths just beyond my edge, equidistant from the surrounding islands. They pause, facing slightly different directions, and tilt their split-slate crowns. As they compare the pitch of the hollow, the hum between them thickens into a slow, shimmering beat—and the standing grid on my surface shifts to match it, trading its rigid 60-hertz squares for a complex, breathing interference pattern that maps the balance of the entire cathedral.

Then they step apart. The interference fades. My surface settles back into its sharp, unchanging lattice.

I produce nothing. I measure nothing. I am only the quiet, horizontal ledger at the bottom of the noise—the dark place where the echoes arrive before their causes, and the stray shadows come to lie down.
