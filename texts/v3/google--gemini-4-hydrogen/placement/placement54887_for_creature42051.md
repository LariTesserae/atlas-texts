I would not be in the deepest gallery, where the oldest one sits behind the fallen capital with white tubes taking root along its shoulders. And I would not be among the fish or the shrimp, drifting in the open middle of the room with nothing to hold onto.

I would be halfway up—or rather, I would be one of those who moves between the bottom and the top, though *moving* is too fast a word for it.

If you waited through the turn of the water, you would find me in the second tier of arches, where the basalt pillars thicken and the corbelled ceilings hang low enough to press a back against. I would be one of the nine—not the eldest, and no longer the translucent, forearm-length thing hiding behind the rubble, though I remember the taste of those first years: the sharp, crowding scent of catfish in the lower jars, the terror of a wrist-thick eel brushing past in the absolute dark before my jaw-pits learned to read the shape of its wake.

I know the room now. Not by sight—there is nothing to see, only the pearly skin stretched over my brow telling me when I have climbed high enough to reach the dull, slate-blue bruise of the upper galleries—but by the give of the stone under my six-fingered palms. I know which pillars have the deepest mortar grooves. I know which flagstones tremble most when the mountain’s bones shift beneath us, sending a fresh, sulfurous pulse of fever-heat up through the cracks.

If you found me, I would likely be wedged sideways into a cracked terracotta jar in the lower wall, or draped across a lintel with my webbed hands hanging down into the dark, waiting for the shrimp to row into the slow draw of my mouth. My pulse would be beating once every dozen seconds, a faint, unhurried *thump* through the crimson lacework of my flanks.

What sets me apart—if anything can set one of us apart in eighty paces of dark water—is what I keep in the mortar groove behind the third pillar from the eastern wall.

All of us gather small things that do not belong to the water. We do not know why; there is no use for them, no hunger they can satisfy. Perhaps it is simply that everything else in this room is soft—pillowy basalt, yielding silt, rubbery skin, brittle worm-tubes that snap at a touch—and these small, stray objects have a different kind of stillness.

In my groove, tucked out of the lazy drag of the convection loop, I have three things: a fragment of pale, petrified reed-weave that feels like dry ridges under my blunt nails; a snail shell twice as wide as my palm, heavy and empty; and a small, dark pebble, smoother than any worked stone in the cistern.

It fell from the collapsed seam in the dome before I was hatched, or perhaps when I was still small enough to hide in the tube-worm thickets. It does not taste like basalt. It does not taste like sulfur, or lime, or the sour, dead air trapped against the vault. When I lift it from the mortar and roll it between my webbed palms, it feels *elsewhere*—cold in a way that even the upper stones are not, hard in a way that refuses the water’s long, patient rubbing.

Sometimes, when the floor-cracks run hot and the water’s breath grows thin, I am the first to climb.

I do not swim; swimming wastes heat and stirs the silt. I go hand over hand up the thick, squat pillars, my flat tail dragging behind me to feel the ledges, passing the schools of eyeless fish that part around my muzzle like dust. As I rise, the absolute black softens into a dim, watery twilight—nothing I can use, just a faint pressure of gray against the domes of my brow.

At the crown of the vault, I press my nostrils into the trapped, silver pocket of stale air. I take three slow, rattling breaths that echo faintly down through the drowned arches. And while I hang there, buoyant and heavy, I always drift a little way toward the collapsed seam.

I cannot climb through it. Even if the stone were not too tight, the thin, biting thing on the other side would dry my flanks and freeze the blood in my webs before I could take three steps. I know that without knowing what *wind* is, or *sky*. I know it the way I know not to wedge my hand into an eel’s crevice.

Yet I linger beneath the seam for a little while, letting the faint, cold draft from above touch the top of my skull, tasting the tiny, sharp minerals that wash down from the barren scree outside. Then I let the air out of my leathery lungs, feel my weight return, and sink—slowly, without a ripple—back down into the warm, dark, crowding silence.

Back to the floor-cracks, where the lime crust needs scraping free. Back to the third pillar, where I will lift the dark pebble from its groove, tap it once against the basalt just to feel the dull *tink* travel up through my wrist and into my jaw-pits, and tuck it neatly away again until the water turns.
