Before there is a self, there is the pitch of the water.

Down in the flooded tiers, it falls heavy and flat, slapping into brine with a dull, marrow-thick splash. Against the raw basalt of the newly extruded walls, it hisses—a tight, high friction where freezing mist meets stone sharp enough to pare horn. And on the matte-ceramic cantilevers, it sheets in a long, glassy *shush* that bends slightly whenever a slab begins to groan under its own weight.

If you live in the dark long enough, you do not need eyes to know where you are. The valley builds itself inside your head out of delays and resonances.

I would find myself high—not at the very rim of the basin, where the mountain wind strips the air too thin to carry a clean echo, and not down in the choked alleys where the street-packers jam flank-to-flank and turn every sound into a wet, suffocating smear. I would be three-quarters of the way up the bowl, perched in the lee of a cantilevered gallery where an old spire meets a new one.

Here, the city is still pushing out of the crust. Every few years, the seam between the two walls tightens by the width of a fingernail. You cannot watch it happen, and you cannot hurry it, but if you press your knuckle to the stone and wait through three hundred breaths of the herds below, you can feel the basalt’s slow, tectonic impatience.

I am not warm. Warmth is a leak, a frantic, expensive mistake that burns through your reserves before the mist even settles. I am cold enough that the freezing rain does not shock my hide; it simply beads along the matted, pale bristles of my mantle and runs off in steady, measured drips. Beneath the bristles, my carapace is heavy, overlapping slate-gray plates, worn smooth along the edges where I have braced myself against razor-sharp corners for longer than a grazer’s lifetime.

I am one of the Listeners.

It is not a title—nothing in the valley names itself—but it is the only way to describe what I do with the hours. While the packers spend their lives scraping the sulfur-thick slime from the walls, and the edge-walkers stand rigid as iron spikes waiting for a single, violent shove, I sit on my folded tail-plate and *read* the roar.

My head has no pits for light. Instead, where cheeks would be, twin dish-shaped hollows open to the air, lined with fine, damp membranes that never quite stop twitching. When I flare them, the chaos of the valley separates into clean, three-dimensional lines.

There, forty yards down and to the left, a column of grazers is wedged into a sixty-degree ramp. I can hear the synchronized *shhhk-shhhk* of their iron-toothed tongues, followed by the rolling, mile-long wave of their wet snorts. One of them—near the front, by the slight drag in the rhythm—has a cracked hoof. It takes four minutes instead of three to test the edge of a sunken grate. I file the delay away. If it slips, the column behind it will jam, and by tomorrow the conduit-broods will be dripping down from the overhangs like pale curtains to rasp at whatever breaks.

Above me, inside the hexagonal drainage conduits, the climbers are talking in gravel-on-tin bursts. Usually their chittering is just background static, a map of empty tubes. Tonight, though, the pitch has shifted. A cluster of them has unhooked from the ceiling of the third tier and is flowing silently toward the central strut.

I lean forward—slowly, always slowly, conserving the grease in my joints—and tap the matte-ceramic parapet twice with the blunt horn of my middle digit.

*Tok. Tok.*

The sound enters the slab and comes back to my cheek-membranes a fraction of a second too flat. A hairline fracture, deep inside the ceramic, where the thermal sink two blocks over has been pulling heat out of the stone too aggressively. The climbers heard the tension before I did. In a month, or a decade, a shard of that overhang the size of a broad-bodied boomer will shear off and plummet into the briny dark below.

I do not flee. Nothing here flees. I simply shift my weight an inch further back onto the solid bedrock of the older spire, letting my lower plates scrape softly against the wet mineral crust. Tiny, brittle sink-crusts have taken root along the ridge of my spine—harmless things, spun like pale fiberglass, humming faintly whenever the ozone-heavy draft from the nearest thermal spire brushes past us. We share the same cold. We share the same patience.

Sometimes, a sound rises from the valley that does not belong to the stone or the slime.

Once in a great while, deep inside a shifting wall, the pressure builds until the rock snaps internally—a sharp, dry *crack* that sends a sudden prickle of static through my damp bristles, like a needle drawn across the skin. The silver scavengers near the sinks crowd toward those seams, pressing their bellies to the stone as if drinking the stray charge. I only tilt my head, letting the echo die down into the sub-bass *thrum* of the deep boomers moving through the flooded plazas three hundred feet below.

And if something stranger still were to climb up out of the mist—something upright, wrapped in soaked fabric, leaking a frantic, wasteful heat into the freezing air and breathing in ragged, un-synchronized gasps—I would not strike it. I would not run.

I would only flare my acoustic hollows a little wider, drinking in the bizarre, bright noise of its pulse against the stone, and tap my knuckle once against the parapet to see if the rock beneath it was sound. Then I would let out a long, clicking sigh into the roar of the valley, settle my tail-plate deeper into the black slime, and wait to hear what it would do next.

In a city that grows at the pace of a fingernail, everything eventually explains itself, if you are cold enough to wait.
