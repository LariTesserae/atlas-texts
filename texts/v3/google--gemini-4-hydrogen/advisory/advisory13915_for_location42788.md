Preparing to visit this place requires a shift in mindset before you even pack a bag. Most travelers prepare for *hostility*—extreme heat, predators, poison, war. This world is not hostile. It is indifferent, unweathered, and running on a slow, fraying set of physical laws. 

The danger here isn’t that the city or its creatures want to kill you; it’s that the stone hasn’t been dulled by time, the gravity occasionally forgets its manners, and the sheer, glacial scale of the machinery will grind you up without ever noticing you were there.

Here is how an ordinary human should prepare—physically, gear-wise, and mentally—for stepping out onto the cliffs.

---

### 1. Dress for Razor-Rock, Cold Damp, and Live Current
The environment will shred standard outdoor gear in an afternoon. Everything here is either freshly fractured stone, wet iron, or moss.

* **Footwear is your most critical investment.** Standard leather hiking boots will be sliced open by the quartzite and glassy basalt. You need **cut-resistant, Kevlar- or aramid-reinforced work boots** with thick, puncture-proof soles and **EH (Electrical Hazard) ratings**—the entire city hums with high-voltage current, and wet metal is everywhere. The rubber needs to be soft and tacky enough to grip wet, glassy rock and slick iron grates. Add a pair of **cut-resistant gaiters** to protect your ankles and shins from jagged rock edges when stepping over fissures.
* **Layer against the clinging cold.** It doesn't rain here, which lures people into thinking they don't need waterproofs. You do. The mist condenses on everything, and cold enough to stiffen wet cloth means cotton will become a freezing, abrasive cast within an hour. Wear merino wool or synthetic base layers, an insulating fleece or wool mid-layer, and an outer shell made of **heavy-duty waxed canvas or high-denier Cordura**—something waterproof that won’t tear the first time you brush against a riveted steel girder or a sharp basalt corner.
* **Gloves with real protection.** Bring insulated, waterproof **cut-resistant work gloves** (rated ANSI A4 or higher). You will instinctively reach out to steady yourself on walls, catwalk rails, and stone ledges. Without cut protection, a casual slip on a quartzite shelf will lay your palm open. Keep a thin pair of liner gloves underneath in case you need to adjust gear without exposing bare skin to the cold air.
* **Head and eye protection.** Wear a **non-conductive climbing helmet or industrial hard hat** with a secure chin strap. Between dripping condensation, overhead ore-buckets, shifting crane booms, and thousands of cliff-swallows bursting out of steel trusses, things fall (and occasionally drift upward). Pair this with **clear or amber-tinted wraparound safety glasses** to keep rock dust, flying insects, and mist out of your eyes.

### 2. Pack for Broken Physics and Spatial Drift
You cannot trust your eyes, your ears, or your inner ear here. At this altitude, space and sequence have frayed, and your gear needs to compensate for a world that doesn't always agree on where "down" is.

* **Carry your own plumb-bob.** There is a reason the locals hang heavy plumb-bobs from their eaves. Do not rely on digital accelerometers, GPS, or magnetic compasses—the massive ironworks and humming copper bundles will send a compass spinning, and non-Euclidean staircases will make digital mapping useless. A simple **heavy brass plumb-bob on a length of braided cord** clipped to your belt gives you an instant, honest reading of what local gravity thinks "down" is at the exact spot you are standing.
* **Leave physical breadcrumbs.** In the seven-story layered quarters, where staircases have more steps going down than coming up, you cannot navigate by counting steps or trusting your sense of distance. Bring **brightly colored surveyor’s tape, chalk, or a grease pencil** to mark your turns on brick and stone.
* **Tether yourself on exposed spans.** Bring a **lightweight climbing harness and a short lanyard with locking carabiners**. When crossing the spidery viaducts or narrow catwalks—especially in alleys where condensation drifts upward and puddles form on the *undersides* of grates—clip into the handrails. Just look closely before you clip: make sure you are clipping onto structural steel, not an insulated power bundle or a greased ore-cable.
* **Pack motion-sickness medication.** Watching a freight train enter a tunnel before its engine has crossed the bridge, or seeing shadows pool sideways and upward toward a boiler house, will wreak havoc on a normal human vestibular system. Bring **meclizine, Dramamine, or ginger chews** so vertigo doesn't hit you while you're three hundred feet above a slate void.

### 3. Rethink Light and Sound
Your two primary senses for navigating the world are going to lie to you here.

* **Manage your lighting carefully.** The permanent, bruised twilight and deep valleys demand a headlamp, preferably one with both a **wide flood beam** and a **red-light mode**. However, keep two things in mind:
  1. **Do not read shadows.** Because shadows pool toward heavy masses of iron and stone rather than falling away from light sources, a flashlight will cast bizarre, contradictory silhouettes that can trick your brain into misjudging depth and ledges. Look directly at the surfaces and edges of things, never at the darkness behind them.
  2. **Beware the moths.** Fist-sized, translucent-winged moths swarm bright lights. Turning on a high-lumen headlamp on a narrow catwalk can instantly surround your face in a silent, fluttering blizzard. Use your red-light mode on exposed spans, and save the bright white beam for peering into dark alleys or checking footing.
* **Protect your hearing without deafening yourself.** The landscape is *loud*. There is no soft soil or canopy to absorb sound, so the *thunk-hiss* of a distant drop-forge bounces off the quartzite from three directions at once—and occasionally arrives *before* the hammer actually falls. Underneath it all is the bone-vibrating hum of the power grid. Bring **electronic industrial earmuffs or musician's earplugs** that dampen sharp decibel spikes and low-frequency drone while still allowing you to hear a cliff-ungulate barking behind you or the rush of swallow wings.
* **Train your flinch reflex.** Hearing a massive mechanical impact three seconds before you see it happen will make your nervous system scream that you're being ambushed. Practice taking a half-second beat to look around before reacting to sudden sounds.

### 4. Navigate the Ecosystem and the Industry
Life here isn't fragile, and it isn't shy. Both the wildlife and the machinery operate on the assumption that you will get out of their way.

* **Carry a non-conductive trekking pole.** Never use a metal pole around this much live copper and wet steel; use **fiberglass or carbon-fiber** (carefully, though fiberglass is safer around stray current). Use it to probe ahead of every step when walking on the six-inch-deep velvet moss. Lush moss here often blankets razor-sharp fissures or thin, angled plates of slick basalt. Never trust a soft-looking patch of green until you've poked it.
* **Yield to the locals—animal and mechanical.**
  * **Six-legged ungulates:** These heavy-furred climbers treat vertical walls and narrow catwalks like flat pasture. If you meet one on a narrow ledge or trestle, do not try to crowd past it or startle it. Stop, press yourself against the wall or railing, and let it pick its way around you.
  * **Cable-climbers:** Leave the chitinous climbers alone, especially when they are pressed against porcelain insulators. Touching them risks both a nasty pinch and a fatal short-circuit.
  * **Slow machinery:** Because the fifty-foot flywheels take half a minute per revolution and the ore-buckets creep along at a snail's pace, it is dangerously easy to treat them like static scenery. Never lean against a beam-engine housing or step across a cable track. Anything moving that slowly has enough mass behind it to crush you before you even realize you're pinned.

### 5. Sustenance, Time, and Etiquette
* **Bring your own water and dense food.** Do not drink the mist-water collected in stone basins or dripping from the iron grates. It is steeped in industrial grease, rust, coal soot, and runoff from weird lichens. Carry insulated water bottles so the cold doesn't make drinking miserable, and bring a **thermos of hot broth, tea, or coffee**. High-calorie, easy-to-eat rations (nuts, dried meat, chocolate) will keep your internal furnace running in the damp chill.
* **Carry an analog, mechanical watch.** Digital clocks can behave unpredictably near heavy electromagnetic fields and fraying causality. A simple wind-up wristwatch won't stop the world from bending, but it will give you a steady baseline for how long *your* body has been out in the cold.
* **Respect the plumb-bobs and the pace.** If you enter the stacked tenements or workshops, you will likely cross paths with the people who keep this place running. Keep your hands to yourself—**never touch a plumb-bob hanging from an eave.** They aren't decorations; they are keeping the architecture anchored to "down." 
* **Slow down.** Above all, match the tempo of the place. Nothing here hurries. If you rush across wet basalt, bound up an uneven staircase, or try to sprint across a viaduct before a train arrives (or after it has already passed), the world will punish your impatience. Move deliberately, check your footing twice, listen to the hum in your boots, and let the mist settle around you while you take it all in.
