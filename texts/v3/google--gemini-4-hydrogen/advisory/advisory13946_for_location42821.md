Visiting this city is less like entering a ruin and more like stepping onto a living, stone-built machine designed to catch the sky. Because it is quiet, tree-less, and seemingly devoid of large predators, it is easy to underestimate. In reality, the combination of high altitude, relentless condensation, slick dry-laid masonry, and labyrinthine geometry makes it a place where a careless traveler can quickly get hurt, lost, or hypothermic.

Here is how an ordinary human should prepare before stepping onto the plateau.

---

### 1. Dress for the Cloud Belt, Not Just the Rain
You are arriving at the lower edge of the cloud belt, at an altitude where the air is thin and cold, yet the sun can be fiercely bright. The primary weather hazard here isn’t a violent storm—it’s **constant, creeping saturation**.

* **Avoid cotton entirely.** When the afternoon cloud banks roll in, mist condenses directly onto stone, wool, and fabric alike. Cotton will soak up that chill and pull heat straight from your body.
* **Use a moisture-wicking base and synthetic insulation.** Merino wool base layers paired with fleece or synthetic insulated mid-layers will keep you warm even if they get damp. Avoid untreated down; in a place where everything weeps moisture, wet down loses its loft and becomes dead weight.
* **Bring a true waterproof, breathable hardshell.** A windbreaker won’t cut it when you are brushing against wet, moss-packed walls in narrow alleys. You need a hooded, seam-sealed shell jacket and waterproof pants.
* **Pack for frost.** Frost has rounded the lintels here, meaning temperatures drop below freezing at night or in the deep, sunless lower lanes. Bring a warm beanie, liner gloves, and waterproof outer gloves (wet slate is bitingly cold to touch, and you *will* be using your hands for balance).

### 2. Prioritize Footwear for Wet Slate
Your boots are your most important piece of survival gear here. Every surface you walk on is either wet slate, pale limestone, moss-packed chinking, or stone stairs worn into shallow, glistening troughs. 

* **Do not wear standard hard-lugged hiking boots.** Hard rubber skates across wet, algae-filmed slate like ice.
* **Choose sticky-rubber soles.** Look for approach shoes, canyoneering boots, or hiking boots with soft, high-friction rubber (such as Vibram Megagrip) designed specifically for wet rock. 
* **Support your ankles.** The stairs are uneven, the roofs are subtly pitched, and the chinking stones can shift slightly underfoot. Good ankle support will save you from a debilitating sprain miles from your entry point.
* **Pack extra wool socks.** Even with waterproof boots, stepping into an unseen, ankle-deep roof basin or a slick silt-trap happens easily.

### 3. Prepare for Thin Air and Blinding Glare
Multiverse travel often means skipping the weeks of gradual acclimatization a mountaineer would normally get. 

* **Respect the altitude.** If you arrive suddenly at cloud-belt elevation, you may experience shortness of breath, a pounding headache, nausea, or rapid fatigue—especially while climbing three- and four-story stone staircases. Hydrate aggressively before you cross over, pace yourself slowly for the first several hours, and consider carrying altitude medication (like acetazolamide) and electrolyte packets.
* **Protect your eyes and skin.** At high altitude, UV radiation is intense even when the air feels cold. Worse, in the morning and especially in the evening when the clouds tear open, the entire wet city turns into a massive, silver mirror. **Polarized sunglasses** (ideally wraparound or glacier-style) are mandatory to prevent snow-blindness-style glare headaches, and you will need high-SPF sunscreen and lip balm.

### 4. Streamline Your Pack
This is a city built without wide spans or broad plazas. Lanes are often **no wider than a pair of shoulders**, capped by overhanging corbeled eaves.

* **Travel narrow.** A bulky, wide-frame expedition backpack with sleeping pads strapped to the outside will constantly snag on the walls, throw off your balance on slick stairs, and scrape against the delicate lichen and moss. Use a slim, low-profile pack (30–40 liters) that sits close to your spine.
* **Waterproof everything inside.** Put your sleeping gear, electronics, spare clothes, and food inside drybags. You cannot count on finding a truly dry flat surface to set your pack down on.
* **Silence your gear.** In a place where the loudest sound for miles is a single drop of water falling into a cistern, clanking carabiners and rattling metal bottles feel jarring and intrusive. Tape down loose hardware and pack quietly.

### 5. Navigation, Light, and Firelessness
When the afternoon clouds drift through the honeycomb of grey walls, visibility can drop to a few paces. Every intersection looks like the last.

* **Bring reliable, hands-free light.** Even at midday, the deep, bridged alleys and subterranean cistern levels are dim. Bring a bright headlamp with spare batteries (cold drains batteries faster).
* **Do not rely on GPS or digital maps.** Aside from multiverse incompatibility, the tall, tight stone corridors will block signals. Bring a simple baseplate compass and a waterproof notebook to sketch landmarks.
* **Learn to read the water.** If you get disoriented in the mist, look down. The entire plateau is gently tilted, and every roof, runnel, and alley is graded to carry water down that slope toward the lower cisterns. Water flow is your most reliable directional cue. (A few small, reusable reflective clips or bright cord ties you place at key turns—and collect on your way back—can also prevent you from walking in circles.)
* **Bring your own heat and food.** There are no trees, no shrubs, and no timber. You cannot build a fire here, and there is nothing substantial to forage. Pack calorie-dense, ready-to-eat rations or a compact canister stove with fuel if you want hot meals and drinks.

### 6. Water Hygiene and Cistern Etiquette
You will be surrounded by water, yet dying of dehydration or dysentery is a real risk if you drink it carelessly.

* **Filter and purify everything.** The water moves slowly through moss, bird-wading basins, silt-traps, and cisterns where pale amphibians breed. Never drink it straight from a runnel or basin. Bring a reliable microfilter *and* chemical purification tablets (or boil it).
* **Do not contaminate the supply.** Whether the city is truly abandoned or tended by unseen keepers, the water system is its heart. Never wash yourself, your dishes, or your gear directly in a settling basin or cistern. Collect water, carry it well away from the channels, and pack out all trash and human waste.

### 7. Mindset and Movement: Walking on Dry Stone
More than anything, visiting this place requires a shift in how you move and pay attention.

* **Move lightly.** Every wall, lintel, and corbeled eave is dry-laid—held together purely by gravity, friction, and small chinking shards. Do not haul yourself up by pulling on overhanging eaves, do not kick at loose stones, and avoid climbing onto fragile-looking roof edges. Test your footing before committing your weight.
* **Watch for hidden drops.** The city is honeycombed with subterranean cisterns, dark wells, and open settling basins. In the mist or low light, a dark square of still water can look like a dry slate floor until you step straight into it.
* **Embrace the silence.** The psychological weight of this place can surprise people. There are no voices, no wind-howl, no rustling leaves—just vast stone geometry and the quiet breathing of mist. It can feel deeply meditative or deeply eerie. If you catch sight of one of the long-legged slate birds, a small thick-furred mammal slipping through a drain, or even a sign of the keepers who clear the channels, stay still and let them pass.

Pack warm, tread softly, keep your eyes on the stone beneath your boots, and stay until evening if you can—just to watch the clouds part and see the grey city turn to silver light.
