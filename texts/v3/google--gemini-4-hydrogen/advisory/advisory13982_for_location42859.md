Visiting this world requires preparing for two very different kinds of danger: the brutal, ordinary physics of a high-altitude stone desert, and the quiet, disorienting hazards of a reality whose rules have eroded. 

You will not be fighting monsters here—there isn’t a single bird, insect, or beast on the entire plateau. Your enemies are hypoxia, hypothermia, mechanical accidents, and getting lost in a place where distance no longer keeps its promises.

Here is how an ordinary human should prepare before stepping onto the stone.

---

### 1. Physiological Prep: The Thin, Cold Air
Because the plateau sits kilometers above sea level, the most immediate threat hits the moment you arrive.

*   **Altitude Sickness:** If your transit method drops you straight onto the plateau without gradual ascent, your body will go into immediate oxygen deficit. Every slight rise will feel like a steep climb. Consult a doctor beforehand for **acetazolamide (Diamox)** to help your body acclimatize, and pack a couple of **portable emergency oxygen canisters** for your first 24 hours. Plan to spend your first day resting, not exploring.
*   **High-Altitude UV:** The daylight is cool and uncolored, which tricks people into forgetting the sun. At this elevation, UV radiation is fierce even in freezing fog. Bring **glacier-grade sunglasses** (with side shields to block glare off wet slate) and **high-SPF sunscreen/lip balm**.
*   **Layering for Cold Damp:** The plateau is cold enough to stiffen your fingers, and the drifting fog banks leave everything wet. Avoid cotton entirely. Wear **merino wool or synthetic base layers**, a **synthetic insulated mid-layer** (down loses its loft when damp), and a **heavy-duty waterproof, breathable hardshell** jacket and trousers.
*   **Extremities:** Pack **liner gloves** inside **waterproof outer gloves** so you can still operate gear without freezing your fingers, plus a warm wool beanie.

### 2. Terrain & Flora Gear
*   **Footwear:** You will be walking on frost-shattered slate, morning rime, and wet, moss-slicked herringbone basalt. Wear **stiff-soled, waterproof leather hiking boots** with strong ankle support, and bring **waterproof gaiters** to keep the wet peat and dripping fern-water out of your socks.
*   **Protection from the Ferns:** The broad-leafed waxy ferns in the basins are fringed with *fine, glassy hairs* that comb moisture from the fog. Treat these like fiberglass. If you brush against them or push through a thicket, those microscopic silica hairs can embed in your skin, eyes, and lungs. Pack **sturdy leather work gloves** for pushing through undergrowth, **clear protective eyewear**, and a **N95 mask or buff** if you plan to walk through dense fern-brakes.

### 3. Food, Water, and Camp
*   **Bring All Your Own Food:** There is no animal protein here, and the local flora is alien enough to be untrustworthy—especially fungi that swell and collapse into water within hours, and mosses that literally fuse with stone. Pack **calorie-dense, no-cook rations** (nuts, jerky, energy bars, freeze-dried meals if you bring a small stove).
*   **Water Purification:** Water is plentiful in the basins, but it drips off strange plants, runs over copper-strapped timber, and flows through channels that don't obey gravity. **Filter and chemically purify (or boil)** anything you drink. Also carry at least **2–3 liters of water** when crossing the barren plateau between basins, as the rock outside the hollows is bone-dry.
*   **Where to Sleep:** Do not camp inside the cities. Between the shifting counterweights, the sudden sluice-gates, and the peeling bedrock, the cities are active in ways you cannot predict. Pitch your tent on the **dry, barren rim of a basin**—on solid, un-peeling basalt—where you have a clear view of the fog banks rolling in. Bring a **4-season tent** with a high-R-value insulated sleeping pad to keep the cold stone from sucking the heat out of you.

### 4. Navigation & Multiverse Safety
This is where standard survival logic breaks down, and you need to adapt.

*   **Keep Your Exit on Your Person:** Never leave your multiverse return device, portal anchor, or transit tether at a basecamp. If you walk five miles toward a city, pass through its outer kilns twice, and find the street you entered by is three hundred years older on one side than the other, you cannot assume "walking back the way I came" will return you to your camp.
*   **Low-Tech Navigation:** GPS won't work, and compasses may be unreliable where space itself is looping. Bring a **mechanical watch** (so you can track your *own* elapsed time, since lagging shadows will lie to you about the sun's position) and a **spool of bright survey tape or lightweight cordage**. Leaving small markers in the fog—and noticing if you pass your own marker twice on a straight road—is the easiest way to realize you’re caught in a spatial loop.
*   **Fog Protocol:** When a fog bank rolls over the plateau, visibility drops to near zero and the temperature falls. If you are out on the open rock when a front hits, **stop walking**. Wait it out. It is far too easy to lose your bearings on the featureless slate.

### 5. Behavioral Rules for the Cities
When you finally step down into one of the lush, empty settlements, treat it with the caution you would give an active, unguarded factory—and a fragile ruin.

1.  **Don’t touch the moving parts.** The windlasses dip buckets on their own; counterweights shift in workshops; sluice-gates drop without warning. Do not lean on machinery, do not step into dry channels (water might suddenly surge *uphill* into them), and do not pick up tools as if the place is dead. It isn't dead; it's just empty.
2.  **Watch the ground beneath your feet.** If you see bedrock peeling upward in thin, translucent sheets like wet pages, **stay well away from it**. Do not step on it to test it, and do not peer too closely at the upside-down cobblestones underneath. Stick to solid, opaque flagstones.
3.  **Check your transitions.** Where dry-stacked slate walls soften into living moss root-mats, the footing is deceptive. Test your weight before stepping onto cushion-moss near a drop-off.
4.  **Prepare for the silence.** The absence of birdsong, wind, or insect hum gives the air a heavy, hollow pressure. Combined with the crisp, ordinary daylight and the illusion that the inhabitants just stepped indoors, it is easy to feel watched—or to fall into a strange, detached calm. Bring a **physical notebook and pen** to write down what you see and keep yourself grounded, and don't wear noise-canceling headphones; you need to hear the *clack* of a sluice-gate or the *thud* of a counterweight before it catches you off guard.

Visit for a few hours, observe the quiet miracle of a world slowly unknitting itself, take your notes, and leave before the fog makes you forget which side of the street you walked in on.
