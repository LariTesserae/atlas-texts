Visiting this pocket is less like exploring a ruin and more like lowering yourself into a living, cold-blooded terrarium—one where you are vastly oversized, dangerously warm, and clumsier than anything native to the dark. 

An ordinary human traveler cannot simply "walk through" this city. You are entering a courtyard-sized vault built by hands much smaller than yours, out of materials that rot, swell, and shift with every drop of weeping water. Preparation here is equal parts technical caving, cold-water survival, and radical restraint.

---

### 1. Entry, Rigging, and the Basalt Problem
The entrance is a hairline cleft in young, unweathered basalt, choked with frost-rimed sedge. Unweathered basalt is not smooth rock; its fractured edges are as sharp as knapped glass, and the wet frost at the lip will turn the stone slick as oil.

* **External Anchoring Only:** Never anchor a rope or brace your weight against anything inside the pocket. The interior walls are sheathed in thick, tallow-colored plant mats that are actively rotting from beneath. If you grab a root-curtain to steady yourself, it will tear away in your hand, bringing down part of a district's aqueduct or dwellings. Set your anchors strictly in the solid basalt *outside* the seam before you drop in.
* **Rope & Abrasion Protection:** Standard climbing ropes will be chewed through by the sharp lip of the cleft if weighted under tension. Bring heavy canvas or ballistic-nylon rope guards to sleeve the line over the edge, along with a compact ascender/descender rig. Climbing *out* of a narrow, freezing basalt crack with stiff fingers and mud-slick boots is far harder than dropping in; do not rely on free-climbing your way back to the surface.
* **Silenced Hardware:** Wrap your carabiners, buckles, and metal gear in cloth tape or rubber sleeves. In a small stone-walled vault where the loudest native sound is a drop of water or the woody sigh of a swelling tuber, the clink of aluminum against basalt will land like a gunshot.

### 2. Dressing for the Wet Cold and the Swarm
The air here is cold enough to stiffen fingers and saturated with mineral-heavy moisture, salt, and the smell of wet peat. Wet cold strips body heat rapidly, and because you will be moving slowly—or sitting entirely still—you will not generate enough warmth to dry out.

* **Base and Mid-Layers:** Wear synthetic wicking layers or heavy merino wool from neck to ankle. Avoid cotton entirely; once damp, it will chill you to the bone. Pack an extra dry thermal layer in a waterproof dry-bag in your pack in case you get soaked at the entrance.
* **Tough, Slick Outerwear:** Skip delicate, ultralight waterproof membranes. The thorns pinning the bryophyte roofs, the sharp basalt at the cleft, and the knapped-flint scrapers wedged into the walkways will puncture thin rain gear. Wear a heavy-duty, abrasion-resistant shell—waxed canvas, heavy Cordura, or tough rubberized foul-weather gear—with a smooth surface that won't snag on wet silk snares or rootlets.
* **Sealing the Gaps:** Every square inch of the walls and floor is in silent motion. You will be sharing the air with millions of eyeless amphipods, finger-long velvet flatworms that leave mucus trails, peppercorn beetles, and tiny silk-spinners. Tuck your trousers into waterproof gaiters or boots, wear fitted wrist-cuffs, and keep a neck gaiter pulled up. This isn't because the fauna is venomous, but because having cold, mucus-coated invertebrates crawling inside your warm clothes is miserable—and you will crush them if you twitch.
* **Gloves and Footwear:** Wear thin, high-dexterity waterproof liner gloves beneath tough leather work gloves (which you'll need for the basalt and thorns). Your fingers *will* stiffen; practice operating your lights, zippers, and climbing rig with cold, gloved hands before you descend. For boots, choose aggressive, soft-rubber lugged soles designed for wet rock, and wipe them clean before descending so you don't track outside seeds, fungi, or soil into an isolated, soil-less ecosystem.

### 3. Light, Heat, and Sensory Etiquette
Darkness is the native condition here. The creatures are eyeless or blind, and the builders have crafted a world around touch, moisture, and the faintest grey thread of light from the cleft above.

* **Low-Output, Red-Shifted Lighting:** Do not turn on a bright white headlamp. Blasting a thousand lumens into this pocket will reflect blindingly off millions of wet fronds, weeping rock faces, and dew-strung silk snares, while likely terrifying or disorienting any light-sensitive life. Bring a dimmable headlamp with a **deep red** or ultra-low warm setting, angled downward at your feet. Even better: sit quietly for ten minutes after descending and let your eyes dark-adapt to the faint grey thread from above before using artificial light at all. Carry a waterproof backup light in a pocket.
* **No Synthetic Scents:** Leave behind insect repellent, cologne, scented lotions, and strongly flavored lip balms. In a dark, silent ecosystem heavy with the chemical signatures of rot, peat, and mucus, strong volatile chemicals are a sensory assault—and could disrupt the builders or drive away the fauna they rely on.
* **Manage Your Heat:** To a cold-adapted settlement, a warm-blooded human is a walking furnace. Do not lean in close to breathe on the hollowed-gourd chambers or the dark cisterns where gill-plumed larvae hang; the heat of your breath and body can shock cold-water organisms or dry out the delicate, wet silk and sinew lashings.

### 4. Moving Like a Giant in a Living Room
Once you reach the bottom, your primary challenge is **scale and restraint**.

* **Stay Off the Architecture:** The braided-stem bridges, terraced cisterns, and woven districts are built for beings much smaller than you. You cannot walk on them. Look for a bare patch of raw basalt—if any exists—or a broad, stable shelf near the floor where you can plant your boots without crushing root-curtains or collapsing hollowed tubers. If you must step onto the plant mat, test it slowly; it is rubbery, slick, and rotting underneath.
* **Watch for the Slug Tide:** Even without day or night, the lower root-mats are swept periodically by a silent tide of grazing slugs. That is why the builders sling their bridges high and hang their bitter-rinded tuber granaries from single greased root-cables in midair. If you sit or set your pack down on the lower mats, check what is moving beneath you—and never, under any circumstances, touch or open the hanging granaries.
* **Bring Nothing Edible, Drink Nothing Native:** Keep your own water in an insulated bottle tucked inside your coat so it doesn't chill your core, and keep any rations sealed in airtight containers. Do not sample the water weeping down the walls or pooled in the stepped basins; it has filtered through layers of decaying saprophytic plants, flatworm mucus, and larval waste.

---

### 5. What to Carry (A Minimalist Pack)
Keep your load slim—if it bulges off your back, it will catch on the narrow basalt cleft on the way in and knock down hanging root-curtains once you're inside.
* Compact climbing harness, static line, edge-protectors, and taped hardware.
* Dimmable red/low-lumen headlamp + waterproof backup.
* Soft, dry microfiber cloth or small brush (to gently clear mucus, silk snares, and hitchhiking amphipods off your gear before you ascend).
* Insulated water bottle + sealed high-calorie snacks.
* A waterproof notebook and pencil (ink runs in this humidity, and glowing tablet screens ruin your night vision and disrupt the dark).
* A small dry-bag for spare warm gloves and a dry base layer.

---

### 6. Psychological Preparation: The Quiet Swarm
The hardest part of visiting this place isn't the cold or the climb—it's the stillness. 

There is no wind here, no birdcall, no hum of insects, no human voice. Yet the entire room is crawling. When you first turn your dim red light toward the walls, you will realize that the pale, tallow-colored surfaces are shimmering with millions of eyeless amphipods, and that the woven bridges are slick with the slow passage of flatworms. The ceiling will drip onto your hood with a steady, cold rhythm. Every few minutes, the city itself will let out a faint, woody sigh as a water-swollen tuber shifts under the weight of a terrace.

If you are squeamish about silent, wet, crawling things, or claustrophobic in tight stone spaces, this pocket will feel oppressive within five minutes. Do not fight that feeling by shining brighter lights or moving faster—that is how you break a bridge or crush a cistern. Instead, anchor yourself in one spot, tuck your hands into your sleeves, breathe slowly, and let yourself become part of the cold furniture of the room. Only when you stop moving like an intruder will you begin to see the sheer, patient brilliance of a city grown entirely in the dark.
