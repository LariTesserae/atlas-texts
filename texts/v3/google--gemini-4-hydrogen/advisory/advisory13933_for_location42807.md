Visiting this place means preparing for two overlapping nightmares: a high-energy underwater volcanic cavern, and a pocket of space where physics has forgotten how to finish a sentence. 

You cannot "swim" here in any conventional sense, and you cannot surface—there *is* no surface, only a churning ceiling of storm-cloud and collapsing air pockets. Treat this expedition like a cross between saturation cave-diving and mountaineering inside a blender.

Here is how an ordinary human should prepare before stepping through.

---

### 1. Armor Against Glass and Water (The Suit)
Standard neoprene wetsuits or soft drysuits will be shredded in minutes. Newly fractured obsidian and glassy rhyolite are sharper than surgical scalpels, and the shearing currents will slam you into them.

* **Cut-Proof Outer Shell:** Wear a heavy-duty commercial drysuit reinforced with ballistic nylon, Kevlar, or Dyneema overlays—specifically over the forearms, shins, knees, chest, and shoulders. 
* **Armored Gloves:** Your hands are your primary anchors. Wear multi-layered, cut-resistant diving gloves (Kevlar-lined with textured nitrile or Kevlar palms) with enough dexterity to grip a line or wedge into a crack.
* **Rigid Full-Face Helmet:** Do not use a standard scuba mask and mouth-held regulator. A shockwave or a tumbling piece of rubble will rip a regulator right out of your mouth. Use a rigid commercial diving helmet (or an armored full-face tactical dive mask) with a thick polycarbonate visor to protect your face from flying basalt grit and scalding silt.
* **Thermal Buffering:** The water is bath-warm, which means you will overheat rapidly inside heavy armor—right up until a cold brine downdraft hits you or a thermal vent blasts scalding water across your back. Wear a phase-change cooling vest or a liquid-circulating undergarment beneath your drysuit to regulate your core temperature against wild thermal swings.
* **Footwear over Fins:** Long freediving fins will snap on the rubble or catch in opposing currents like sails. Wear heavy, weighted dive boots with tungsten-carbide or high-friction rubber soles for bracing against ledges, paired with very short, stiff cave-diving fins if you need extra thrust.

### 2. Breathing and Acoustics (Surviving the Roar)
The most insidious killer here isn’t the obsidian—it’s the sound. Underwater, sound travels roughly four times faster than in air and couples directly into human tissue. A continuous, echoing roar that "hammers against the sternum, rattles the teeth, and blurs vision" can cause pulmonary barotrauma (bruised lungs), rupture your eardrums, and induce debilitating vertigo.

* **Closed-Circuit Rebreather (CCR):** Use a rebreather housed in a hard, streamlined dorsal cowling. It recycles your breathing gas (crucial since you have no surface to ascend to) and produces no exhaust bubbles to snag in the weird local currents or add to the acoustic chaos. *Keep all hoses tucked tight against your body.*
* **Triple-Layer Ear Protection:** Put molded, vented silicone earplugs in your ears, cover them with a tight neoprene hood, and seal that inside your rigid helmet.
* **Sternum & Rib Baffling:** Wear a rigid or semi-rigid chest plate lined with viscoelastic, closed-cell trauma foam over your torso. You need physical baffling to absorb the concussive hammer-blows of collapsing air pockets and delayed rock-fall shockwaves before they bruise your lungs or crack a rib.
* **A Mouthguard:** Seriously—wear a fitted athletic bite guard. If the ambient vibration is rattling your teeth, clenching your jaw during a shockwave will chip molars or sever your tongue.

### 3. Navigation When Gravity and Shadows Lie
In a normal underwater cave, bubbles go up, silt settles down, and shadows stay attached to rocks. None of that is reliable here.

* **Physical Guideline (Your Only Truth):** Carry a heavy-duty, cut-resistant Kevlar cave-diving reel. Anchor it securely at your entry point and *never let go of it*. When visibility drops to zero in a silt plume or your vision blurs from the concussive roar, your hands on that line are the only way back.
* **Inertial Instruments, Not Plumb Lines:** Do not rely on weighted lines or your inner ear to tell you which way is down—local gravity drifts sideways and hesitates. Mount a digital dive computer with a solid-state gyroscopic compass and inertial attitude indicator to your forearm.
* **Narrow-Beam Amber Lights:** Bright white floodlights will just reflect off the suspended ash and blindingly illuminate the murk right in front of your face. Use high-lumen, narrow-beam cave lights tinted toward amber or green, mounted to your helmet and wrists, with backup strobes clipped to your chest.
* **Non-Destructive Anchors:** Do *not* bring pitons or a hammer. Hammering into fresh volcanic glass will shatter your handhold and could trigger a local collapse. Instead, carry short Dyneema slings, rubber-coated grappling hooks, and climbing camming nuts to wedge into existing cracks.

### 4. Psychological & Tactical Conditioning
Before you go, spend time training in zero-visibility, high-current swift-water or flooded cave environments. Panic burns oxygen fast, and claustrophobia in a warm, roaring, pitch-black room will kill you faster than the terrain.

Once you are inside, follow these rules:

* **Read the Flora and Fauna:** The sparse, pale kelp and translucent lichen only survive in thin seams of calm behind boulders. Treat those spots as your rest stations. More importantly, watch the blind, heavy-shelled drifters. They navigate by pressure waves and can feel a thermal eruption building before you can. **If you see the armored eels or nautilus-like drifters suddenly dive into the rubble, brace yourself immediately—a scalding vent is about to blow.**
* **Anticipate the Delayed Shockwave:** If you see a needle-thin slab of black glass shear off a cliff, dissolve into silt, and snap back into place, *do not relax*. The visual event happens before the physical force arrives. The moment you see a stutter-collapse, tuck your chin, brace against a leeward boulder, and tighten your core for the delayed shockwave that is about to slam into your ribs.
* **Ignore the Shadows:** Detached shadows will swim past you against the current. Your brain will scream that something is stalking you. Ignore them; rely on your instruments and the rock under your gloves.
* **Respect the Cairns and Grips:** You will find trembling cairns of basalt with pebbles orbiting their peaks, and pale bones or shells wedged into obsidian cracks as handholds. **Do not disturb them.** Someone or something survived here using bare hands and an intuitive grasp of this basin's broken physics. Leave their anchors intact—they may even be stabilizing the local current eddies. If you need a resting grip, use the smooth, half-moon hollows worn into the lower ledges; they are the only surfaces in the room that won't slice through your gloves.

### 5. The Exit Plan
Have an extraction method that works entirely underwater—a portal anchor tied directly to your guideline, or a mechanical winch system pulling you back to your point of origin. Whatever happens inside, **never swim up toward the light.** There is no air up there—only a violent, churning ceiling waiting to tear you apart.
