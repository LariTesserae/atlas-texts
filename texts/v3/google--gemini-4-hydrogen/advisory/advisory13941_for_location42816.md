To visit this pocket as an ordinary human, your first and most important preparation is a shift in mindset: **you are not going for a walk, and you are not going exploring.** You are going to wedge yourself into a space the size of a low cupboard—a jagged, kiln-hot stone crack packed to the brim with biting insects, razor-sharp quartz, and stray electricity. 

If you arrive unprepared, you won’t just be uncomfortable; you will be sliced open, electrocuted, deafened, and suffocated within minutes. Here is how to prepare your gear, your body, and your mind.

---

### 1. Insertion and Geometry: Know How You Fit
Before you even pack a bag, you have to solve the geometry problem. The pocket has barely enough clearance for a hand between some of its shelves, and there is no way in or out through the hairline fault. 
* **Scout or map first if possible:** If your method of travel allows you to send a small probe or a pinhole camera ahead, do it. You need to materialize into the *largest* available void in the pocket—likely curled into a tight fetal position or a low crouch. Materializing even a few inches off-target means intersecting with freshly cleaved basalt or splintered quartz.
* **Zero-bulk loadout:** Forget backpacks, utility belts, or anything that protrudes from your torso. Every millimeter counts. Everything you wear must be conformal, hugging your body tightly so you don't get wedged between a ceiling overhang and a floor-plate.
* **Hands-free extraction:** Do not rely on an exit device that requires two hands, fine motor skills, or reaching into a pocket. You may not have room to bend your elbow once you are inside. Set up a voice-activated, bite-switch, or chest-mounted dead-man’s switch for your return jump.

### 2. The Suit: Armor Against Stone, Current, and Chitin
A normal jumpsuit or hiking gear will be shredded by the unweathered crystal facets on your first shift of weight, leaving you bleeding in a pile of silica dust and blind scorpions. You need a specialized, multi-layered shell:
* **Outer Layer (Cut & Puncture Resistance):** Wear a tight-fitting outer skin of woven aramid (like Kevlar) reinforced with flexible ceramic or high-density polyethylene plates over your knees, elbows, shoulders, shins, and spine. This stops the razor-sharp quartz veins from slicing you and keeps scorpion stingers and beetle mandibles from reaching your skin.
* **Middle Layer (Electrical Protection):** Because stray industrial currents run through the iron-rich basalt, touching two points of rock with bare skin—or conductive fabric—can send voltage through your body. You need a thick, continuous **dielectric (insulating) rubber or neoprene layer** underneath your armor. 
* **Static Management:** The ultra-fine rock flour is magnetized and prone to static arcs. Keep a thin, conductive copper-mesh weave *on the very outside* of your insulating layer (a mini Faraday cage) so any stray current or static discharge routes harmlessly around your body rather than through it. Ensure there are no exposed metal zippers or D-rings touching your skin.
* **Hermetic Sealing:** Armored mites and silverfish-like runners will find any gap. Every seam—wrists, ankles, neck—must be sealed with locking gasket rings or heavy-duty electrical tape. No exposed skin anywhere.

### 3. Breathing and Cooling: Surviving the Kiln
You cannot breathe the air here. It is thin from altitude, baked to brick-kiln heat, stagnant, and choked with pulverized silica and ozone. One deep breath of that flour-soft mineral dust will lacerate your lungs.
* **Low-Profile Rebreather:** A standard scuba tank on your back will trap you against the ceiling. Use a **compact, closed-circuit rebreather** mounted flat across your chest or abdomen. It recycles your air, keeps the silica and ozone completely out, and provides steady oxygen regardless of the thin ambient pressure.
* **Passive Cooling (No Heat Pumps):** Do not bring a battery-powered air conditioner; in a sealed, stagnant stone cupboard, an active heat pump will just dump its exhaust heat into the rock pocket, baking you (and the local arthropods) even faster. Instead, wear a **phase-change cooling vest** (lined with pre-frozen gel or specialized paraffin packs) directly under your insulating layer. This gives you a hard time limit—probably 20 to 30 minutes before the packs melt and your core temperature spikes—but it keeps you cool without adding heat or noise to the space.
* **Hydration:** Put a bite-valve water tube inside your helmet. Even in a sealed suit, the heat will make you sweat, and you won't be able to take your helmet off to drink.

### 4. Sensory Protection: Noise and Darkness
The pocket acts as an acoustic amplifier for the heavy machinery roaring above it. The noise isn't just loud; it is physically oppressive.
* **Double Hearing Protection:** Wear custom-molded silicone earplugs *underneath* a padded, sound-attenuating helmet. This will cut the high, metallic whine that would otherwise rupture your eardrums. Note that you will still *feel* the bone-deep bass roar in your ribs, your jaw, and your sinuses—no earplug stops bone conduction—so prepare for mild nausea and disorientation.
* **Helmet and Light:** Wear a low-profile, impact-rated helmet with a scratch-resistant polycarbonate visor (quartz dust will scratch glass and soft plastics instantly). Mount a **dim, wide-angle red LED** on your brow. Bright white light will reflect off the floating dust and blind you, and it’s needlessly harsh on a pitch-black ecosystem. Keep the light low so you can watch the static sparks snap in the dark without washing them out.

### 5. Etiquette of the Pocket: Moving Slowly
Once you arrive, you will immediately realize you are sharing a tiny box with thousands of tightly packed, desperate creatures. 
* **Move like a glacier:** The local beetles move a fraction of an inch per hour. Follow their lead. If you thrash or shift quickly, two things happen: you grind your suit against razor-sharp quartz, and you kick up clouds of conductive grey rock flour, triggering a storm of blue-white static sparks around your helmet.
* **Accept the contact:** You will feel the dry, rasping *hiss* of chitin against your armor. Scorpions and beetles will crawl over your visor and pack into the warm creases of your joints. Do not swat at them. Your suit is sealed; they cannot hurt you, and crushing them only creates moisture and chaos in an ecosystem that survives on stillness. Let them treat you like a slightly warm, oddly shaped boulder.
* **Watch your weight:** Try to brace yourself on the flatter basalt plates rather than the splintered crystal shelves, and keep your fingers curled into fists unless you are actively bracing—an unguarded fingertip in a narrow crack is easily pinched or sliced.

### 6. Psychological Preparation
The combination of extreme claustrophobia, total darkness, bone-rattling industrial noise, kiln heat, and being covered in insects is a textbook recipe for a panic attack. A panic attack in a space this small—where you cannot stand up, stretch your arms, or take off your helmet—is fatal.
* **Practice beforehand:** Before your trip, spend time in a pitch-black, cramped space (like under a low bed or in a tight closet) wearing your full, sealed suit, rebreather, and earplugs. Practice slowing your breathing while listening to loud, low-frequency industrial noise through headphones.
* **Set a strict timer:** Put a vibrating timer on your wrist or inside your helmet set for **15 minutes**—well before your cooling packs fail or your nerves fray. When it buzzes, observe for one last minute, trigger your extraction, and leave the pocket to its patient, vibrating dark.
