Preparing for this place is less like packing for a hike and more like rigging for a cave dive inside a watchmaker’s case—if the watch were submerged in ice water and its gears didn’t agree on which way was down.

Because you are an ordinary human, the chamber will not kill you through malice; it will kill you through *friction*, *cold*, and *panic*. Everything about your preparation should be aimed at eliminating reasons to rush, thrash, or lose your bearings.

Here is how you should get ready before you step—or slip—across.

---

### 1. Thermal & Abrasion Armor (The Suit)
The water sits just above freezing, and there is no air pocket to climb into if you start shivering. Worse, you cannot swim hard here to generate body heat; the space is too tight and the vegetation too dense. You will be nearly motionless, which means the cold will sink into your core fast.

* **A heavy-duty, cold-water drysuit:** Do not use a wetsuit. Even a thick wetsuit flushes cold water against your skin, and in a space where you can barely move, you will hit debilitating hypothermia within twenty minutes. You need a trilaminate or vulcanized rubber drysuit layered over thick, moisture-wicking thermal undergarments (merino wool or high-loft fleece).
* **Cut-resistant outer protection:** This is the detail most travelers overlook. The basalt and slate here are newly cleaved—razor-sharp and actively shedding microscopic grit. A standard neoprene or lightweight drysuit will slice open the first time your knee or shoulder brushes a rock edge, flooding your suit with near-freezing water. Wear Kevlar-reinforced knee, elbow, and seat pads, or a tough, abrasion-resistant over-suit.
* **Heavy drygloves with cut-resistant liners:** You will be moving by gently bracing yourself against stone and parting curtains of plants. Bare hands will go numb in minutes and bleed immediately on the slate. Fit your drysuit with locking wrist rings and heavy rubber drygloves, wearing thin Kevlar or Dyneema cut-resistant liners underneath for dexterity and slice protection.
* **Seal care:** That microscopic slate grit will get into everything. Bring a small squeeze-bottle of clean fresh water and a microfiber cloth to rinse your suit’s neck/wrist seals and zipper *after* you exit, before you try to peel the suit off.

---

### 2. Breathing & Buoyancy (Surviving Without Air or "Up")
There is no surface here. Your breathing rig is your only lifeline, and standard recreational scuba gear is dangerously wrong for this chamber.

* **Cold-water-rated, environmentally sealed regulators:** At 1–2°C, moisture from your breath can freeze inside a standard regulator’s first or second stage, locking the valve open in a violent "free-flow" that will drain your air tank in a couple of minutes. Ensure your regulators are specifically rated for ice diving.
* **A full-face dive mask:** In near-freezing water, exposed skin around your lips and cheeks triggers an immediate, blinding "ice-cream headache" (the mammalian dive reflex combined with trigeminal nerve shock) that makes it hard to think straight. A full-face mask keeps your face dry and warm, and—crucially—it cannot be knocked out of your mouth if you bump a rock or get disoriented.
* **Streamlined, snag-free air supply:** Do *not* wear bulky twin tanks on your back. The chamber is no larger than a cistern and packed wall-to-wall with bladder-fronds and hair-thin filaments; a back-mounted rig will wedge you into the fracture or tangle you hopelessly. Instead, use a compact **sidemount cylinder** tucked tight along your ribs, or a small **chest-mounted pony bottle** (with a redundant, equally compact bailout bottle).
  * *A note on bubbles:* Standard open-circuit gear vents loud exhaust bubbles. In a tiny, silent stone pocket with no surface and shifting gravity, your bubbles won’t just rise and vanish—they may pool against invisible gravity panes, stir up razor-sharp slate grit, and violently jostle the fragile bladder-fronds and spawn. If you are trained and certified on a **cold-water closed-circuit rebreather (CCR)**, its silence and lack of bubbles make it ideal here. If you aren't trained on one, stick to open-circuit—never learn a rebreather in a hostile environment—just breathe slowly and shallowly.
* **Ditch the fins:** Swim fins are useless in a room stuffed with rubbery weeds. One kick will either wedge you between rocks, stir up blinding silt, or shred a century’s worth of slow-growing filaments. Wear flexible, thick-soled rock boots instead.
* **Minimal, distributed weight:** Normally, divers wear weight belts to sink. Here, "down" changes every few feet. A heavy belt might suddenly pull you sideways into a sharp wall or pin you against an invisible boundary. Weight yourself just enough to offset your drysuit’s buoyancy, distributing small soft-lead pouches evenly around your torso so no single part of you feels dragged in an unpredictable direction.

### 3. Lighting & Navigation in Folded Space
The chamber’s bruised, slate-blue dimness only lets you see a few inches, and the water smears edges like thick glass. Yet blasting the place with a 2,000-lumen cave-diving spotlight is a mistake.

* **Low-lumen, diffuse lighting:** Bring a small, dim, wide-beam light—ideally with a **red or warm amber filter**—mounted to your wrist or mask, plus two tiny backup lights clipped flat to your chest. Harsh white light will scatter off the "thick" water and blind you with backscatter, and it may shock or damage the blind crustacean and gelatinous spawn that evolved in near-total dark.
* **A short, taut tether (handle with extreme care):** If your multiverse transit method leaves a fixed exit point, you need to be able to find it again when "up" and "back" stop making sense. Normally, cave divers lay a thin guideline. Here, space is pleat-folded—a loose line can drift through a fold and wrap around you from behind. If you use a tether, keep it short, bright, and **taut** (on a wrist-mounted finger spool with a gentle lock), and never let loops of slack float free into the weeds.
* **A sheathed ceramic line-cutter:** Do not carry a big metal dive knife; it will snag. Carry a tiny, flat ceramic line-cutter (like an Eezycut) strapped to your forearm. If the hair-thin filaments wrap around your valves or wrists and you can’t untangle them by feel, you need a clean, one-handed way to free yourself.

### 4. Preparing Your Mind (The Hardest Part)
Physical gear keeps you alive; mental discipline keeps you from tearing your gear apart.

* **Expect your own body to surprise you:** Because space is tightly pleat-folded, you may reach forward through a curtain of ribbon-weed and feel fingers brush the back of your own wrist—your *own* fingers, coming from behind. Your brain will scream that something is grabbing you in the dark. Train yourself beforehand: **if something touches you, freeze.** Do not jerk away. Feel it calmly, trace it, and verify whether it is a rubbery bladder-frond, a sharp rock, or yourself.
* **Prepare for severe vertigo:** With gravity changing every few feet and no horizon to anchor your eyes, your inner ear will rebel. Nausea is common. Before you transit, eat a light, bland meal (avoid dairy, grease, or coffee), hydrate well, and consider testing an over-the-counter motion-sickness preventative (like meclizine or ginger) *on dry land* days beforehand to make sure it doesn’t make you drowsy.
* **Adopt the pace of the chamber:** Nothing rushes here. The crustacean takes minutes to row an inch. The water turns over once an hour. Match that tempo. Every movement you make should be deliberate, slow, and small—parting bladder-fronds gently with the backs of your gloved hands rather than pushing through them.
* **Know your turn-around limit:** Set a hard, conservative timer on a vibrating wrist dive-computer—say, **15 minutes** total, or the moment your air drops to 75% (whichever comes first). In a place this cold, cramped, and disorienting, fatigue sneaks up quietly. Leave while you still feel completely fine.

---

### 5. Etiquette & What Not to Touch
When you enter, you are stepping into a place where even a tied knot leaves a faint afterimage hanging in the water beside it. Actions here seem to linger.

* **Leave the pre-tool marks alone:** You may spot the four flat river-pebbles balanced in a cleft, the finger-grooves in hardened grey clay, or the braided fiber knot with its ghostly twin. Do not touch them, do not shine bright lights on them for long, and do not add your own cairn or marker. In a chamber where balance itself barely holds, a single careless kick or exhaled bubble could topple something that has waited quietly for ages.
* **Give the fauna wide berth:** If you see the pale crustacean or the pulsing ribbon of spawn under the pebbles, hold your breath for a few seconds (never hold your breath while ascending, though here there is no ascent—still, keep your throat relaxed) or angle your exhaust away, dim your light, and drift back slowly.

### Final Pre-Transit Check
Before you open the way in:
1. Check your drysuit seals and zipper—fully closed, no hair or fabric caught.
2. Breathe from your primary regulator for a full minute; confirm it delivers air effortlessly and doesn’t leak.
3. Tuck every hose, gauge, and strap flat against your body. Nothing dangling.
4. Take three slow, deep breaths. Remind yourself that the darkness is small, the cold is patient, and as long as you move slowly, you will have plenty of time to see what lives in the ribs of the world and slip back out unscathed.
