On the surface, a sun-drenched, bath-warm chamber tucked beneath a quiet limestone quay sounds like a Mediterranean paradise. In reality, you are stepping inside an active, freshly built hydraulic engine. 

Because the room is small, rigid, and newly cut, it has none of the softening buffers you might expect from an older marine ruin. There is no cushion of kelp, no rounded edges worn down by centuries of tide, and no quiet deep water to absorb the shock of the sea. Preparing for this place means gearing up less like a casual swimmer and more like someone stepping into a bright, deafening, stone-lined washing machine.

Here is how an ordinary human traveler should prepare for the visit.

### 1. Dress for Abrasion, Not Cold
The water here is sun-heated to bathwater warmth. Your biggest thermal risk isn’t hypothermia—it’s **overheating** while fighting the pulsing current. Do not wear a standard wetsuit. 
*   **Full-body skin protection:** Wear a thin, tough, full-length rash guard and dive leggings (Lycra, spandex, or 0.5mm neoprene at most). The limestone here is brand new, with crisp chisel ridges and corners sharp enough to catch a fingernail. When a swell surges through the intake and pushes you sideways, bare skin against that unweathered stone will shred instantly.
*   **Gloves and Booties:** Pack durable, grippy water shoes or thin-soled dive booties, plus lightweight abrasion-resistant gloves (like Kevlar-lined or tough synthetic mechanics' gloves). You will want to brace yourself against the stepped ledges and the recessed floor channel without slicing your palms on the masonry or slipping on the fine grit skating across the flagstones.

### 2. Protect Your Eyes from the Glare
At midday, the room has no shadows. Sunlight drops straight through less than a meter of clear water and ricochets off pale cream stone, chalk-white blocks, and bright mortar. 
*   **Tinted Dive Mask:** Bring a low-volume, **tinted or polarized dive mask**. A standard clear mask will leave you squinting against the blinding caustic nets and may trigger a fierce headache within minutes. A lightly smoked or amber lens will cut the glare enough for you to actually appreciate the mason's tool marks and the dusty rose hue of the mortar.

### 3. Prepare for the Acoustic Assault
Sound travels four times faster underwater, and this chamber is a rigid stone resonator. The swells don't just push you; they arrive as low, chest-rattling booms that vibrate straight through your ribs and sinuses. Add the constant roaring hiss of the bubble plume and the hollow, jar-like drone of the terracotta pipes, and it is easy to become disoriented.
*   **Vented Earplugs:** Never wear solid earplugs underwater (the pressure change can rupture your eardrums), even in shallow water. Instead, use **vented diving earplugs** (such as Doc’s Proplugs). They allow pressure to equalize while taking the brutal edge off the booming swells and high-frequency hiss.
*   **Thin Hood or Bump Cap:** A very thin (1mm) neoprene hood—or even a lightweight, perforated kayaking helmet if you don't mind looking silly—will muffle the noise further and save your scalp if a surge lifts you unexpectedly into the heavy cedar ceiling beams.

### 4. Choose Your Breathing Gear Carefully
You cannot stand up and take a breath inside this room; the ceiling grate is submerged, and the upper half of the chamber is choked with turbulent, aerated water.
*   **Avoid bulky scuba tanks:** The room is only a few paces across, with sharp corners and narrow entryways. A heavy back-mounted tank will constantly clank against the masonry, snag on the hatch, and turn you into a human pinball in the current. 
*   **Freediving vs. Compact Air:** If you are a confident swimmer, **freediving** with short, stiff fins (long fins will just bash the walls) is the cleanest way to experience the room. Drop in, ride a surge or two, and exit. If you want to linger and study the stonework, use a **streamlined chest-mounted pony bottle** or a **surface-supplied "hookah" rig** anchored on the quay above (just keep the air hose taut so the current doesn't loop it around the cedar beams).
*   **Mind the Bubbles:** Remember a basic rule of physics: aerated water is much less dense than solid water. When the bronze-grated intake blasts a white cloud of bubbles into the upper half of the room, **you will lose buoyancy** and sink toward the flagstones. Do not rely on floating; stay braced or kick steadily.

### 5. Plan Your Entry, Exit, and Positioning
*   **Use the Hatch, Not the Lintel:** Unless the lagoon outside is dead calm, avoid swimming under the seaward lintel. Low overheads in a surge zone are prime places to get pinned or slammed into stone by an incoming wave. If you can open the square timber-framed hatch in the deck above, drop in from there—and tie off a knotted hemp rope to the timber frame so you have a reliable handhold to haul yourself back out against the current.
*   **Respect the Valve:** Stay clear of the far bulkhead near the floor. The cedarwood flap-valve is weighted with a heavy river cobble and snaps shut with a dull *clack* every time a swell recedes. Getting a hand or foot caught under that weighted flap—or getting pulled toward the suction of the three terracotta pipes during a strong surge—is a serious crush and entrapment hazard.
*   **Secure Everything:** The fine lagoon grit sweeping across the floor will work its way into everything. Ensure your mask strap is tucked, pockets are zipped, and any breathing valves are rinsed thoroughly with fresh water the moment you climb back onto the quay.

### 6. Timing and Traveler’s Etiquette
*   **Check the Weather and Tide:** This chamber is exhilarating in a gentle midday swell, when it behaves like a warm, rhythmic pulse. In rough weather or a storm surge, it becomes a violent stone blender. Verify the local sea state before dropping in.
*   **Hydrate Beforehand:** Because the water is warm and you will be working against a steady rhythm of currents, you will sweat underwater without realizing it. Drink plenty of water on the quay before and after your dive.
*   **Leave It Unmarked:** You are visiting a structure in its very first season. The mortar is still curing against the salt; the first delicate ribbons of sea lettuce and hair-algae are just beginning to anchor in the quiet eddies. Keep your movements deliberate, don't pry at the fresh brick-lime seams, and leave the weighted valve to do its rhythmic work undisturbed.
