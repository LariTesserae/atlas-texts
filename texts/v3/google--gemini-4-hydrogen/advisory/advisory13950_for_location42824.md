Visiting a place like this is less like exploring a cave and more like stepping inside an unfinished thought. You aren’t going into a ruin or a wilderness; you’re stepping into a root-cellar-sized pocket of reality where the world poured the foundation, turned on the work-lights, and walked away before the plaster finished setting.

Because the room is small, it’s tempting to under-prepare. Don’t. Everything in this chamber is sharp, cold, unstable, or quietly turning into stone. As an ordinary human, here is how you prepare to walk in, stay intact, and walk back out.

---

### 1. Protect Your Eyes—and Your Sense of Depth
The most immediate assault in this room isn’t a monster; it’s the light. A shadowless, refrigerator-bright white glare with no source means your eyes have nowhere to rest and your brain loses its primary tool for judging distance. Without shadows, sharp mineral ridges look flat, and shallow dimples look like solid ground.

* **Glacier Glasses (Category 4):** Standard sunglasses won't cut it. Bring wrap-around mountaineering or glacier glasses with side shields. You want to block the glare coming off the white walls and wet puddles from every angle.
* **A Wide-Brimmed Hat or Visor:** Even though the light comes from everywhere, a dark, matte brim gives your eyes a physical frame of reference and cuts the top-down intensity.
* **Motion-Sickness Medication:** Take something like meclizine or dimenhydrinate an hour before you cross over. The corners of this room fold slightly too far inward, causing your gaze to slide off them without finding a seam. Combined with the shadowless glare, that impossible geometry will trigger vertigo and a splitting headache within minutes.
* **A Matte-Black Anchor:** Bring one small, completely non-reflective object—a strip of black gaffer tape on your sleeve, or a matte-black trekking pole. When the room’s angles start making you nauseous, stare at the black object for five seconds to reset your visual baseline.

### 2. Dress for Cold, Grease, and Rapid Calcification
The air sits at refrigerator temperature (just above freezing) and smells of wet chalk. Every few minutes, the walls sweat fat beads of condensation, which the floor then drinks back in, hardening slightly wherever the water touched. Anything organic that stays still here calcifies.

* **Layer for Stationary Cold:** You won’t be hiking miles in a room the size of a root cellar; you’ll be standing mostly still, which makes cold seep in fast. Wear a moisture-wicking thermal base layer and a warm wool or fleece mid-layer.
* **A Slick, Non-Porous Outer Shell:** Wear waterproof, smooth-surfaced rain gear (like heavy Gore-Tex or coated nylon) over your insulation. Avoid canvas, cotton, or fuzzy outer fabrics. The floor is faintly greasy and half-set, and the air-swimmers shed or brush past. You want a surface that wet chalk, condensation, and mineralizing dust can’t soak into—something you can wipe clean before it hardens onto you.
* **Rigid-Soled, Puncture-Resistant Boots:** The stone here has never been touched by erosion. Every edge is fresh-extruded and razor-sharp. Wear heavy hiking or caving boots with thick, rigid soles and good ankle support.
* **Cut-Resistant, Waterproof Gloves:** Do not touch the walls, the floor, or the air with bare hands. Between the sharp, un-set stone, the pebble rings, and the airborne swimmers (which are as flat and potentially sharp-edged as mica shavings), thin leather or nitrile-coated cut-resistant gloves are essential.
* **Cover Your Neck and Face:** Keep your collar up, or wear a buff/balaclava. You don't want pale, bare skin exposed to the cold mist—or looking like a target to anything hunting from the walls.

### 3. Breathe Carefully
* **Wear an N95 or P100 Respirator:** You’re breathing air saturated with wet chalk and surrounded by a tiny, frantic ecosystem of drifting, translucent swimmers and calcifying shed skins. A snug respirator keeps mineral dust out of your lungs, warms the refrigerated air slightly before you inhale it, and prevents you from accidentally breathing in a mica-thin organism. (Bring spare filters; the dampness will eventually clog one.)

### 4. Navigate Without Trusting Your Eyes
Because the stone hasn't finished deciding to be solid in every spot, your eyes will lie to you. Parts of the room flicker silently between damp limestone, frosted glass, and sheer white absence.

* **Bring a Probing Pole:** Carry a sturdy, rigid trekking pole or fiberglass rod. Before you put weight on *any* patch of floor—even in a room this small—tap it firmly. If the stone flickers, feels spongy, or gives no feedback, **do not step on it**. Stepping into "sheer white absence" might drop you into nowhere, or worse, leave your foot trapped if the stone decides to solidify around your ankle a second later.
* **Never Sit or Kneel:** Stay on your feet the entire time. If you sit on the half-set floor through a condensation cycle, the greasy mineral and moisture will start setting into your clothes. Keep shifting your weight slightly from foot to foot so your boots don't begin to fuse to the damp plaster.
* **Watch the Puddles, Not Just the Walls:** When the weather cycle hits and condensation forms shallow mirrors in the floor’s dimples, **look down into them**. The reflections run a full second ahead of the room. If you see a pale shape skittering across the ceiling in a puddle, or a long porcelain limb twitching in the reflection, you have a one-second head start to step back before it actually happens above you.

### 5. Respect the Fauna and the Spatial Creases
The chamber feels dead silent, which makes it easy to forget how crowded it is. Everything here is moving—grazing, drifting, hunting—and distance doesn't work properly.

* **Stay Away from the Fractured Corners:** The porcelain-white predators on the far wall don't need to cross the room to reach you; they wait until prey drifts near a spatial crease, then reach *through* the bent air to pluck it off the stone. In a room this small, the "far wall" is only a few paces away. Avoid standing near corners that look fractured, warped, or where drifting swimmers briefly split into overlapping echoes. Keep your movements slow and unhurried so you don't trigger a predatory twitch.
* **Don’t Touch the Salt-Crystals:** If a predator strikes nearby, you may see pale ichor freeze into a tiny salt-crystal before it hits the ground. Leave it alone. Anything that crystallizes that fast in mid-air is not something you want reacting with the moisture on your skin.
* **Watch Your Step Around the Structures:** There are deliberate, mandible-built pebble rings on the floor catching water, tally-marks scratched into soft clay, and an ancient calcified ribcage cradling liverwort near the center. Don't kick the pebbles, smear the tally-marks, or disturb the grazers. Whatever is testing whether the walls are real is still in the room with you.

### 6. Set a Hard Time Limit
The greatest danger in this room isn't getting eaten; it's lingering. Everything in this pocket is slowly turning into architecture—shed skins become mineral ridges in hours, bones fuse into the floor, and water hardens the stone bit by bit.

* **Bring a Mechanical Watch or Timer:** Set an alarm for **15 to 20 minutes**, maximum. The blinding, silent, shadowless white makes it almost impossible to sense how much time has passed. A simple ticking watch on your wrist also gives your ears a steady, human rhythm inside the refrigerated quiet.
* **Keep Noise to a Minimum:** Don't shout, drop gear, or play music. The room's acoustic baseline is just the *shhh* of carapaces and the *tock* of water drops. Sudden noise in a sealed, hyper-quiet space could panic the grazers or draw the attention of the predators on the wall.

### 7. The Exit Protocol
When your timer goes off, leave immediately—don't wait for "just one more weather cycle."

As soon as you step back out into normal space:
1. **Scrape your boots and pole** right away, while the greasy plaster is still damp and before it finishes setting into permanent limestone on your treads.
2. **Wipe down your outer shell and gloves** with a damp cloth, then strip them off into a sealed bag if they’re coated in chalky residue.
3. **Breathe normal air and blink in real shadows.** Give yourself a few minutes to let your depth perception and inner ear settle before you try to walk anywhere tricky. You’ve just spent twenty minutes inside an unfinished rock; it takes a moment for the real world to feel solid again.
