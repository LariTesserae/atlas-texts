Before you pack a single piece of gear, understand what this chamber actually is: **it is a lethal wet-bulb environment.**

At 45°C (113°F) and near-total humidity in stagnant air, human sweat cannot evaporate. Your body’s primary cooling system shuts down the moment you arrive. Left unprotected, an ordinary human resting quietly in that air will begin drifting toward heatstroke in under half an hour; working, climbing steel stairs, or wearing non-breathable protective gear will cut that window in half.

This creates the central dilemma of visiting this vault. Every surface is coated in hazards that demand a full-body barrier—bat guano, rat urine, iron-bacteria slime, softening sacks of caustic lime, sharp raw basalt, and tens of thousands of insects—yet sealing yourself inside a protective suit turns your own body heat into a slow cooker.

Preparing for this place means solving that paradox, and then preparing your mind for what it feels like to be inside it.

---

### 1. Thermal Survival & Time Budgeting
You do not "visit" this chamber indefinitely. You run a **strictly timed sortie**—ideally 20 to 30 minutes maximum—unless you bring active cooling.

* **Active Cooling:** Do not rely on ice packs tucked into pockets; they will melt in minutes and leave you carrying dead weight. Wear a **phase-change cooling vest** (calibrated to ~15–20°C so it doesn't shock your skin) or, for longer stays, a **closed-loop liquid-cooled undersuit** fed by an insulated ice-water bladder in a backpack. This goes *under* your barrier suit.
* **Pre-Hydration & Pre-Cooling:** Spend the hour before transit drinking cold electrolyte solution—not plain water—and lowering your core temperature slightly. Once you are inside, you cannot safely take off a respirator to drink.
* **Zero-Rush Pacing:** Move at half your normal speed. Every hurried step spikes your metabolic heat and increases your odds of slipping on guano-slicked concrete. Treat the vault like an underwater dive: watch your timer, and leave *before* you feel dizzy, nauseous, or unusually irritable.

### 2. Respiratory & Atmospheric Protection
The air down here is a stagnant soup of steam, aerosolized storm-runoff, ammonia from thousands of bats, fungal spores (*Histoplasma* from the guano), caustic dust near the softening lime sacks, and whatever trace gases seep down from the city’s mains.

* **Multi-Gas Monitor:** Clip a calibrated four-gas detector (O₂, CO, H₂S, LEL) to your chest. Fifty meters underground with decaying organic runoff and stagnant air, oxygen displacement or pockets of hydrogen sulfide are real possibilities. If it alarms, you leave immediately.
* **Respirator:** A simple cloth mask or loose N95 will clog with moisture and fail within minutes. Use a **full-face elastomeric respirator** or a **half-mask paired with sealed goggles**, fitted with **P100 particulate filters** (for spores, bacteria, and lime dust) combined with **organic vapor/acid gas cartridges**.
  * *If you can afford the bulk:* A **PAPR (Powered Air-Purifying Respirator)** with a hooded shroud is worth its weight in gold here. It keeps positive pressure inside your hood—keeping midges, dust, and spores out—and the constant airflow across your face significantly reduces claustrophobia and panic, even though the air it pulls in is still 45°C.
  * *If your gas monitor shows low oxygen:* Do not enter without a **closed-circuit rebreather or SCBA**.

### 3. The Barrier Suit (Pest, Chemical & Fall Protection)
Everything solid in this room is alive, wet, sharp, or caustic. Your outer layer needs to be **slick, sealed, and tough**.

* **The Suit:** Wear a **smooth, hooded chemical-barrier coverall** (such as Tychem or coated ripstop) over your cooling vest and thin moisture-wicking base layers. Avoid textured fabrics, fleece, or canvas—cockroaches, cave crickets, and spider silk will cling to them instantly.
* **Tape Every Seam:** Use heavy-duty chemical or duct tape to seal your cuffs to your gloves, your pant legs to your boots, and the hood around your respirator/goggles. Leave no gaps, no open pockets, and no exposed skin. A cockroach or cricket finding its way inside your collar while you are climbing a wet steel stair is a fast way to lose your footing.
* **Head & Eye Protection:**
  * Wear a **hard hat or bump cap** *over* your hood. The ceiling is raw, blast-scarred shale and basalt, and thousands of free-tailed bats hang directly overhead. You will be rained on by guano, condensation drops, and the occasional dislodged bat. A wide-brimmed hard hat keeps the worst of it off your visor.
  * If not using a full-face mask or PAPR hood, wear **indirect-vent chemical splash goggles** treated with heavy-duty **anti-fog** (or scuba-style spit/gel). In 45°C steam, untreated lenses will blind you in thirty seconds.
* **Hands:** Double-glove. Wear thin nitrile gloves underneath **cut-resistant, waterproof, insulated work gloves** (nitrile-coated Kevlar or neoprene). You need grip on wet iron handrails, protection against sharp drill-scarred rock and rusting rebar, and thermal insulation in case you accidentally brush a copper condensate line or manifold.
* **Feet:** Wear **EH-rated (Electrical Hazard), steel-toed, puncture-resistant rubber or neoprene boots** with deep, soft-rubber, slip-resistant lugs. The floor is a treacherous mix of wet concrete, mineral slime, algae, bat guano, and shallow warm puddles hiding uneven basalt. You also have open relay boxes sparking blue, so electrical insulation in your soles is non-negotiable.

### 4. Lighting, Sound & Navigation Gear
The sodium-vapor lamps cast bruised amber pools dimmed by crusts of dead drain flies, leaving deep shadows in between.

* **Beat the Steam Backscatter:** A bright headlamp right between your eyes will hit the thick steam and blind you with white glare, like high beams in fog. Bring a **high-CRI, neutral-white floodlight** mounted low—on your **chest or waist**—angled downward at the floor. Carry a second waterproof (IP68) flashlight as a backup. Neutral-white light lets you distinguish orange rust, red boiler indicators, pale lime slurry, and shallow puddles that look identical under amber sodium light.
* **Hearing Protection:** The chest-rattling thrum of the cast-iron sump pumps, the sharp hiss of the pressure-relief valve, and the metallic *tock-tock-tock* of condensation will exhaust your nervous system and mask subtle dangers. Wear **foam earplugs** or **electronic level-dependent earmuffs** that attenuate the low-frequency roar while still letting you hear a sudden change in pump pitch or a valve blowing.
* **A Non-Conductive Probe Pole:** Carry a **fiberglass walking stick or trekking pole** (never aluminum, given the sparking relays and bundled conduit). Use it to:
  * Test the depth and footing of warm puddles and catch-basins before stepping.
  * Sweep sagging cellar-spider hammocks off unused scaffolding and walkways.
  * Tap overhead pipes or handrails lightly before grabbing them—not just for heat, but to clear shifting sheets of cockroaches before your hand lands.
* **Waterproof Everything:** Seal any electronics, journals, or instruments in IP68 cases or heavy Ziploc bags with silica gel. The humidity will condense *inside* unsealed battery compartments within minutes.

### 5. Tactical Awareness Inside the Vault
Once you step in, certain specific features of this chamber require constant respect:

* **Watch the Lime Pallet:** Near the east stair, those softening paper sacks of lime are turning into wet calcium hydroxide. Wet lime looks like harmless pale mud, but it causes **severe alkaline chemical burns** on skin and eyes, often without hurting until the damage is already deep. Do not kneel in it, and do not track it onto your gear.
* **Respect the Manifolds & Relays:** Everything metal near the junction house and pressure-mains may be scalding hot. Open relay boxes sparking blue should be given a wide berth, especially while standing in conductive runoff.
* **Mind the Tidal Shift:** The sump basins rise and fall slowly over the afternoon and night. If you set a bag down on a "dry" patch of rock, it may be under three inches of iron-bacteria water an hour later. Hang packs on dry hooks or keep them on your back.
* **The "Don't Flinch" Rule:** When your light hits the brick junction house, tens of thousands of roaches and cave crickets will ripple away in a single dark sheet. Rats will dart along the overhead cable trays at eye level. A bat may swoop past your visor. **Train yourself beforehand not to flinch.** A sudden jerk on guano-slicked grating can send you into a catch-basin or onto a hot steam pipe.

### 6. Psychological Preparation
The hardest part of this location isn't a single monster or trap; it is the **cumulative sensory weight**.

Your chest will vibrate with the pumps. The air will feel thick enough to chew, hot as a fever, and heavy with the smell of wet concrete, hot iron, stagnant storm-water, and sharp ammonia. You are fifty meters underground in a half-built room that feels abandoned by its builders yet relentlessly alive.

Before entering, practice **slow, deliberate nasal breathing** inside your respirator while warm. Panic accelerates your heart rate, spikes your body temperature, and fogs your mask. Keep your attention anchored to small, mechanical tasks: *check footing, sweep pole, read timer, check partner.*

### 7. Extraction & Decontamination
Leaving the chamber is only half the operation.

* **Staged Doffing:** Do not bring your outer suit into a clean space or your home world. Strip out of your taped barrier suit, gloves, and boots **outside the chamber** (or inside an airlock/decon bag), peeling the suit inside-out so guano, lime residue, and insect frass stay trapped inside. Keep your respirator on until the dusty outer layers are bagged and sealed.
* **Immediate Cool-Down:** Get into cool, dry air immediately. Pour cool water over your head, neck, armpits, and wrists. Drink cold electrolytes slowly over the next two hours.
* **Medical Watch:** Monitor yourself for the next 48 hours for signs of delayed heat exhaustion, hidden alkaline burns from lime splatter, or—if your seal failed—respiratory symptoms from fungal spores or *Legionella* in the warm steam.

Visit with respect for the heat, keep your hands to yourself, watch your timer, and leave the cellar to the things that were built to thrive in it.
