Stepping down through that buckled lip of salt-crust is less like entering a cave and more like climbing inside a living brass instrument that has been left out in a blast furnace. Everything in this blister of selenite and gypsum is hostile to human softness, yet an ordinary traveler can survive—and even appreciate—it if you prepare for its three defining realities: **acoustic violence, absolute desiccation, and shadowless glare.**

Here is how to pack, dress, and comport yourself before you drop below the rim.

---

### 1. Defend Against the Sound (And the Vibration)
The roar here isn’t just loud; it is structural. It shakes your teeth and turns your ribcage into a resonating box. Standard foam earplugs alone will not be enough, because much of the dawn bass and noon shriek will reach your inner ear straight through your skull and spine.

* **Double Hearing Protection:** Wear deep-fitting, high-NRR foam or silicone earplugs *underneath* a pair of heavy-duty passive earmuffs. Skip active noise-canceling electronics—the pressure waves and constant vibration can confuse the microphones, drain batteries, or produce disorienting feedback.
* **A Soft Mouthguard:** The prompt’s warning about the low groan vibrating in your molars is literal. A simple boil-and-bite athletic mouthguard will keep your teeth from chattering or grinding against each other during the low-frequency morning hours.
* **Vibration Dampening:** Wear thick, shock-absorbing insoles in your boots to decouple your feet from the humming stone. A snug, padded compression vest or tightly wrapped torso band can also help dampen the nauseating sensation of your chest cavity vibrating in sympathy with the wind-throats.
* **Acoustic Fatigue Plan:** Even with protection, relentless infrasound causes vertigo, migraines, and a creeping sense of panic. Know your limits. If you start feeling seasick or disoriented, either seek out one of the inhabitants’ double-walled pith alcoves (if invited) or climb back out onto the desert shelf for an hour of open air.

### 2. Seal Out the Light and the Dust
The selenite dome acts like a giant diffuser, turning the desert sun into a blinding, shadowless milk-white glare that bounces off every pale wall. Combine that with perpetual gypsum dust, salt grit, and the noon chaff-storm, and your eyes and lungs will be the first things to fail.

* **Glacier Glasses (Category 4):** Bring wrap-around, polarized sunglasses with leather or rubber side-shields—the kind mountaineers use on snowfields. Because there are no shadows, light attacks from below and the sides just as fiercely as from above.
* **A Sealed Dust Mask or Respirator:** Do not rely on a simple cloth bandana. You need a well-fitted P100 or N95 half-mask respirator. The air is thick with powdered mineral, shed plant-wax, and—at midday—millions of weightless, glittering chaff-scales from popping bladder-plants. Breathing that in unfiltered will leave you coughing up chalk within an hour.
* **Full-Coverage, Breathable Clothing:** Wear light-colored, tightly woven linen or cotton that covers every inch of skin, including a hood or wide-brimmed hat with a neck cape. Avoid greasy sunscreens or heavy lotions on exposed skin; in this environment, anything oily will instantly cake into a gritty paste of gypsum and salt.

### 3. Carry Your Own Oasis
There is not a single drop of water inside the dome, and the dry wind-roar will strip moisture from your body faster than you realize.

* **Over-Pack Water and Electrolytes:** Bring more water than you think you need for the duration of your stay, stored in rigid, dust-proof canteens with covered spouts (you don’t want to drink salt-crust off your bottle lip). Mix in electrolyte tablets, as you will be sweating constantly without ever feeling damp—the air evaporates sweat the instant it hits your skin.
* **Protect Your Mucous Membranes:** Pack sterile saline nasal spray and preservative-free lubricating eye drops. Even behind goggles and a respirator, the bone-dry air will crack your nostrils and dry your eyes. A thick, waxy lip balm (applied sparingly under your mask) is essential.
* **Keep Your Water Sealed:** Out of respect and basic caution, never spill water on the city’s structures. The terraces, retaining walls, and plaster are made of gypsum, salt-brick, and plant-wax—materials that have survived for centuries precisely because they have never met a puddle.

### 4. Dress for Narrow Ledges and Brittle Walls
You will not be walking across the empty center of the chamber; the standing waves there are violent enough to shred dust devils. All movement happens along the steep, seven-tiered perimeter on shoulder-width stairs and braided-fiber tightropes.

* **Slim, Body-Hugging Gear:** Leave the wide hiking backpack outside or carry only a tight, low-profile sling pack. A bulky pack will catch on cliff ribs, knock fragile bone-white plant stalks into the void, or throw off your balance on a zig-zag stairway.
* **Flexible, High-Grip Footwear:** Wear lightweight approach shoes or flexible climbing boots with sticky rubber soles. You need to be able to feel a braided catwalk underfoot, grip stone that has been polished waxy-smooth by grit, and wedge your feet into shallow hand-carved steps.
* **Tactile Gloves:** Wear thin, durable leather or synthetic climbing gloves. They will protect your palms from jagged salt-crust and the sharp, porcelain-brittle stalks—and, crucially, they are thin enough to let you feel vibrations in the handrails. (Avoid sitting or leaning heavily into the thick amber-lichen mats unless you want thumbnail-sized salt-ticks investigating your seams.)

### 5. Learn the Etiquette of Silence and Touch
In the alleys, nobody speaks, and shouting is useless. You are entering a dense, ancient city built entirely with bone, flint, and soft copper, where survival depends on silent coordination.

* **Watch Your Hands on the Railings:** The taut sinew-lines strung along the handrails are not grab-ropes for hauling yourself up—they are bell-pulls used to send sharp physical pulses to warn climbers of oncoming traffic. Keep a light touch on the wood or stone, feel the sinew-lines for incoming vibrations, and yield in a doorway or wider terrace if you feel a pulse from above.
* **Visual Communication:** Since you won’t know the locals’ practiced hand-signs, carry a small, durable notepad with a thick charcoal pencil or wax grease-pencil (ordinary ink pens often dry out or clog with dust). Simple drawings, pointing, and open-handed gestures will go a long way.
* **Respect the Quiet Pockets:** If a local gestures you into a double-walled pith alcove where the roar drops to a bearable thrum, keep your voice low and your movements calm. Those spaces are sanctuaries for sleep and delicate copper-work; bringing loud, clumsy energy into them is the fastest way to wear out your welcome.
* **Tread Lightly Around the Flora:** The hollow stalks, rattling seed-pods, and bladder-plants aren’t just scenery—they are the city’s dry garden, raw material, and acoustic fabric. Don’t snap stalks for walking sticks or crush the lichen terraces.

### 6. Time Your Visit to the Dome’s Pulse
The chamber’s personality shifts dramatically with the sun.

* **Arrive at Dawn or Late Afternoon:** In the early morning, the selenite roof is cool, the glare is softer, and the sound sits in a deep, belly-rattling bass. In the evening, the wind-throats slacken and the city settles into a long, amber-lit hum. Both are far more manageable for an outsider’s nervous system.
* **Avoid Noon in the Open:** Midday is the hardest hour. The ceiling heats and expands with sharp, pistol-crack snaps that cut right through the roar, the pitch climbs into a white-hot shriek, and the bladder-plants pop open all at once, filling the central void with a blinding blizzard of chaff. If you are inside at noon, try to be tucked under a deep terrace overhang or resting in a quiet pocket until the heat breaks.

Prepare well, keep your weight close to the wall, and let the sound wash around you rather than fighting it. Few places in any world look—or ring—quite like this one.
