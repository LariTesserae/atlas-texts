I come into it low, at the lip, where the pressure in the ears arrives before any image does — and my first thought is wrong. I think: *I am the noise.* I think I might be something diffuse, something made of all ten thousand sounds braided together, a kind of ambient consciousness with no edges, listening from everywhere at once. That doesn't last. The basin doesn't want a god of itself. It wants hands.

So I try on hands.

For a while I think I am the mason — chisel-scarred, three generations of identical strike-marks on the lintel I'm finishing, a stair my grandfather started and that my own children will end. There's a dignity in that, the slow argument with unweathered stone, the patience of cutting something that will not soften toward you, ever, no erosion to meet you halfway. But it's too settled a life for what's asking to be felt here. The mason knows exactly what they are for. I don't, yet. I'm still arriving.

I try on the herder-child next, the one crossing the beam with a lamb under one arm, sleeping against flank and fleece before learning to sleep against another person's warmth. There's something right in that — the nesting of household into herd into household, wealth kept as memory of gait and temperament rather than as a count. But it's a beginning, not a place to stand. I want the version of myself that has already learned the whole grammar, not the one still acquiring it.

What keeps pulling at me, under all of this, is the listening. Not the foreground listening — not a mother sorting her infant's cry from ten thousand others, though that is its own kind of genius — but something quieter and more peripheral. The half-ear kept on the roar itself, the way someone elsewhere keeps half an eye on their own breathing. The basin's people have made of that endless sound something like a vital sign. And vital signs need someone who notices when they change.

So this is who I find myself being: not a mason, not a herder, not a voice raised for births or deaths — but the one who walks. Early in the wash of gray-gold light, along the rooftop-paths that double as alleys, hand trailing the damp stone, attention split three ways between the angle of a herd-animal's ear, the lean of a wall that has taken one drop too much water, and the texture of the roar itself, listening for the half-second of wrongness inside it — a register dropping out, a band of sound going thin, the small local silence that precedes a rockfall or a flood-surge. Not a healer. Not a priest. Something more like a diagnostician of the ordinary, low-status and indispensable in the way that channels and stairs are indispensable — nobody remembers my name for it, the way nobody remembers which mason cut which stair.

My hands are thick through palm and knuckle like everyone's, callus on old callus, and they speak the contact-grammar fluently — tap-code up a forearm, weight of touch for urgency, duration for meaning — because the work depends on language that doesn't compete with the basin's own noise for bandwidth. I report wrongness by touch, urgently or unhurriedly depending on how wrong. Mostly unhurriedly. Mostly the wall holds, the ear stays unflicked, the register stays full, and I move on to the next ledge, the next stretch of causeway, the next animal-mass steaming gently in the warm haze, and nothing happens, which is the whole point — the entire purpose of the walk is the long succession of days where nothing happens, where the roar simply continues, proof that the world is still doing what the world does here.

I don't imagine an elsewhere. I was given the question — *where would you be, who would you be* — and the basin answered before I could invent something stranger: you would be the one who listens for the held breath, and trusts, almost all the time, that it will be let out again unbroken.
