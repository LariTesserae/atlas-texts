I think I am small, and that this is not incidental but the whole shape of my use here.

I find myself crouched at a junction maybe four meters up, where a stalk of the old bamboo-analogue — thick as my own thigh, ringed and waxy and gone the deep green-black of something that has never once seen direct sun — splits into three lesser stalks, each one threaded through with a pipe-run I can feel humming faintly under my palm if I press it right. This is mine. Not owned, exactly — nothing here is owned the way surface-people apparently mean it, I've been told, with deeds and locks — but mine the way a particular stretch of riverbank belongs to the heron that's always there. I am the one who climbs to this junction. I have climbed to it perhaps four thousand times. My hands know the false give of the moss-skin before they touch it, know where the stalk is still strong underneath and where it has gone punky and hollow and will not bear my weight however urgently the schedule wants it to.

I am narrow through the shoulders, and glad of it. There is a gap here, between where the ceiling-canopy presses down and where the cistern-wall bulges out with its slow green sweat, that a wider person simply could not pass, and so this junction would go untended, and so — eventually, a year, two years, the patient arithmetic of root against iron — the pipe would lose its argument and the room three levels down would lose its heat in the dead cold hour before the boiler-tenders wake. I know this because someone explained it to me once, the whole chain of consequence, link by link, the way you'd explain it to a child, which I was, then. I am less a child now. I am still narrow. I have come to understand the two facts are related and feel no particular hurry to outgrow either.

There is a song that belongs to this work, low, three notes mostly, meant to travel through wet leaf rather than against it, and I am singing it now without much attention to the singing, the way you breathe without attention to the breathing. Two chambers off and one level down, someone — I think it's the one with the embroidered cuffs, I think it's her shift — will catch the thread of it through the canopy-muffle and know: still up there, still working, account that worry off the books for another hour. I have never seen her face do this listening. I have heard her sing back, once, when I went quiet too long over a stuck valve and frightened her. The sound came up through the grate-gaps thin and sharp and entirely unlike her speaking voice, and I understood then that the songs are not decoration either. Nothing here is decoration. I keep almost forgetting that and the hollow keeps almost-gently reminding me.

My hands are stained the rust-green-brown that everyone's hands are stained, cuffs gone the same, and there is a particular ache low across my back that I have been told, by the older ones, never entirely leaves once you've spent enough years bent under this canopy, and I have decided not to mind this in advance, the way you decide things about your own future that you can't actually know yet but find steadying to assume.

What I am doing, right now, at this junction: feeling along the third lesser stalk for the place where a root — patient, blind, in no hurry at all, the hollow's whole personality compressed into one slow green tendril — has found the pipe-seam and started, very gently, to win. I have a hook for this. The handle is wound in cord the color of nothing in particular, which is how I know it's mine and not someone else's, since down here you mark what's yours by touch as much as sight. I work the hook under the root without cutting the stalk's own living wood, because the stalk is load-bearing now too, has been for forty years, vine and iron equally indispensable equally to be respected, and I was raised on the rule before I was raised on much else: don't choke the channel, don't starve the line, the hollow collects on every debt eventually so pay it now while it's small.

The root comes free with a small wet sound like a held breath let go. I coil it back along the stalk where it will do no further harm and might, someday, do some good — shade for a smaller shoot, structure for something that needs climbing — because nothing gets killed here that doesn't strictly have to be, there isn't the room for that kind of waste, there isn't the room for waste of any kind.

Far above me, through some grate I can't see from here, there is a thread of actual color in the dark — not green, not bulb-yellow, something colder and further away, which means it is very early up there, or very late, dawn or dusk leaking down through ten meters of brick and packed earth and finding, eventually, me. I stop the work a moment to let it reach my face. It is faint enough that I could be imagining it. I have decided not to mind that either.

Then I take up the hook again, and the song again, three notes, and go on to the next junction, because the pipe-run doesn't end here and neither, today, do I.
