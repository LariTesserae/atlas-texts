The cold comes first — that's how I always know I've surfaced from sleep before I open my eyes. Cellar-cool air settling into the gap between blanket and skin, and underneath it, far off, the chuff of the lower turbine, steady as something breathing in another room of a house too big to hear all at once.

I am Tessamy Drook, sap-tapper, third tier, and my hammock is slung between two root-cables in the lower residential hollow, close enough to the amber-zone that when I open my eyes the world arrives the color of weak tea. I lie there a moment doing the thing we all do without naming it — listening to the cavern instead of looking at it. The frond-whisper overhead. A platform somewhere groaning through its slow settle. No alarm-bell, no shouted name. Good. Nothing's gone soft in the night.

My hands give me away before anything else does — when I bring them up to rub my face I find them already faintly amber-stained at the knuckles, resin worked so deep into the skin over the years that no amount of dry-scrubbing lifts it anymore. Small hands, practical, the nails kept short because long ones catch on bark-fiber and tear. I am narrow through the shoulders the way all of us are, built for ducking under canopy-platforms, and when I sit up in the hammock my head doesn't come close to the lattice-floor of the level above — a clearance I've never once had to think about and would notice instantly if it were gone.

The apron's where I left it, looped over the trunk-spur beside the hammock, amber-stiff with old sap, smelling of sweetish heavy pine-that-isn't-pine. I put it on by feel. Tool-belt next — the long-hooked testing pole clipped at my hip, because today's a tap-line day out past the third gantry, and the platforms out there haven't finished hardening at the edges. You test before you trust. Everyone learns this the same way: once, badly, and then forever after, carefully.

Outside the hollow the light does its argument — warm gold where the bioluminescence wins, a hard white seam where it crosses an arc-lamp's territory, and for three steps in between, neither, just grey, just memory of where the rope-bridge is supposed to be underfoot. I cross it without looking down. You learn the floor's give the way you learn a stair in a dark house you've lived in thirty years.

Past the foundry-hollow — heat rolling off it in a wall I walk through and out of in four strides, soot-leaves dark and glossy above the flue-vent, still alive, still breathing it in — past the loom-shed where the night-weavers are just trading off shift, a tired nod exchanged across a gap of forty feet that the acoustic geography happens to favor, their voices arriving clear as if they stood beside me though I can't see them at all through the trunk-mass between us.

At the base of trunk-column Seven I stop, the way I always stop, and lay my palm flat against the bark before I take the gear-lift up. It's chalky and cool and faintly gives, the way it always does, like leaning your hand against something that is still, ever so slowly, deciding what it's going to be. I don't think of it as prayer. Nobody here would call it that. It's closer to checking in with a very large, very patient neighbor before you climb up onto its shoulders for the day's work.

The lift chuffs upward, chain-and-counterweight, patient as everything else down here. Above me the canopy-platforms stack away into gold-grey distance, evenly spaced, branching at intervals too regular for comfort if I let myself think about it directly — so I don't, the way none of us do, not on a working morning. There's a tap-line waiting at the fourth platform, sap running slow and steady out of a wound in the trunk that the tree — the column, whatever it is — doesn't seem to mind, or has decided, in its vast unhurried way, to tolerate.

Three hundred meters of quiet dark between here and the next lit cluster. A moth-shape passes somewhere above, papery, unbothered, drawn equally to my lantern and the arc-lamps further on. The cavern doesn't end anywhere I've ever walked to. It just keeps implying more of itself, the way it always has, the way it presumably always will, until — someday, not today — it decides otherwise.

I check the pole, test the platform edge, and get to work.
