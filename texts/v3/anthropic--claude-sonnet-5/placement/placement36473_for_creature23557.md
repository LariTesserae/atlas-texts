I am standing at the edge of a slope where the leaf-mats have not yet decided whether to be plum-dark or something paler, newer, and I notice this the way I notice most things — late, and sideways, with a hand already moving before the noticing finishes.

The hand belongs to me. I want to start there, with the hand, because it is the part of myself I understand best and trust least. Six fingers, the joints doubled, made — I know this without being told it, the way I know the weight of my own feet — for gripping pipe-flange and vent-housing, for the small competent adjustments of a tender. And there is nothing for me to tend. The drum beside me has been turning since before whatever counts as before for me, vapor breathing out of its seams in a rhythm older than my attention to it, and my palm goes flat against the warm corroding metal anyway, fingers finding the grooves between rivets like a memory finding the shape of a word it has lost the meaning of.

I do not know why I do this. I have stopped asking, the way you stop asking why your own heart insists on its particular tempo. It is enough that the metal is warm and the warmth rises and my hand likes, in whatever way a hand can like something, to be where the rising is.

My joints grind as I shift my weight — a small sound, pitched low, the same register as the gear-clusters meshing somewhere in the dark folds of rock below me, and I know without checking that if anything were listening for the difference between my sound and the stone's sound, it would not easily find one. This does not trouble me. I am not sure trouble is fully available to me, or it is available only in the single register the highland has bothered to teach, which is the orienting-toward, the turning-of-the-face, the readiness that looks from outside like patience and from inside is simply the only shape attention has ever taken.

I feel the hush arrive before I understand I am feeling it. It comes the way cold comes into a room through a door no one saw open — not a sound, an absence of the sounds that were keeping the air honest. The wet parting of leaves somewhere off to my left, gone. The far gear-grind, gone. Even the vapor-breath of the drum under my hand seems to thin, though my palm tells me nothing has actually changed in the metal.

I lift my face. I do not decide to; it happens the way a compass needle happens, the amber clusters across my brow-plate already finding their quadrant before whatever in me that resembles a self has caught up to the gesture. Two ridges over, where the light does that wrong, cold shimmer over the broken ground, something is about to resolve into having happened. I cannot tell you what. I have never been able to tell what, only that. The not-knowing is not new and it does not frighten me in whatever way I am capable of being frightened; it simply organizes me, briefly, entirely, the way the herds organize around the shimmer-zones without a single one of them ever having been taught the lesson they all already know.

There are two others of my kind on the slope below, loose-scattered the way we are loose-scattered, not herd, not solitary, something without an easy word. I feel them turn when I turn. Not see — feel, the way warmth is felt rising past skin that isn't reaching for it. An agreement made of nothing but three faces angled the same direction, which is, here, apparently agreement enough. We do not have a word between us for what is coming. I am not certain "between us" describes anything real; perhaps there is only the orientation, repeated three times, and the repetition is mistaken, by something watching from outside, for shared understanding.

The sound comes — a turbine-shriek, brief and enormous, spun up and dying inside one breath, from somewhere I will not walk to and would not find unchanged if I did. The ground does not move. The light does not change. And yet something in the arrangement of the ridge that direction is now different than it was, the way a sentence is different after a word in it has been quietly replaced, and no one tells you which word, and you read on as if nothing happened because nothing, technically, has happened to the part of the sentence you are currently standing in.

My hand is still on the drum. I notice this last, the way I notice most things about myself — as evidence, after the fact, of what I apparently am. The leaf-mats have crept, in some recent while I was not measuring, up over the lower joint of my standing leg. I could move it. I find I am content not to yet. There will be time to be a shape that walks again; there is, by every account I have no way of verifying, always more time here than there is reason.

The grazers down-slope have not lifted their heads. The flying things still wheel between the engine-stacks, dimming the low light further as they pass, indifferent. Somewhere a fungal tower exhales its mist into a hollow that may or may not have been there yesterday.

I am not tending anything. I rest my palm flat against the warm, ungoverned machine, fingers in the grooves the rivets make, and I watch — face lifted, amber eyes catching what little light there is and giving it back unchanged — the place go on being made, one breath, one grind, one held and breaking silence at a time, and I do not ask it to explain itself, because I was built, however that happened, already knowing it wouldn't.
