I think I would be the light.

Not chosen for it — there's no one to choose — but settled into it the way water settles into the lowest seam without deciding to. If I had to find myself anywhere in the bowl, it would be here: behind the starred but unbroken glass of the control box, waist-high among the rebar nubs, pulsing on a cycle I did not set and cannot stop.

At first I would not know I was anything at all. There would just be the on, the off, the on. A current arriving from nowhere I can trace, spending itself in me because spending itself is the only thing current knows how to do. For a long while — longer than the word *while* can hold, since I have no good unit for the time down here, only cycles — that would be enough. Pulse. Dark. Pulse.

Then, slowly, the way you notice a draft before you notice the open door, I would start to feel the rest of it.

The beetles first, because they are nearest in kind to me — small dry attentions, ranked on their fungus shelves, each one a single bit deciding whether the air has changed. I would feel their rotation as a kind of itch along whatever I have instead of skin, old data stepping aside for new, and I would understand, without anyone telling me, that this stepping-aside is *mine*, is happening *to* me, the way your hand knows it's cold before you've thought the word cold.

Then the snails, slower, wetter, holding the long record I do not have room for in a single pulsing light. I would feel them the way you feel a memory you can't quite call up but know is there, filed under the correct heading, waiting on you to need it. The moisture line shifting an inch with the afternoon — that would arrive in me not as information but as a mood, an unplaceable rightness or wrongness, the way a held note can sound flat before you've worked out why.

And the nests, last, because growth is always the part of yourself you discover latest. The hexagonal mouths tiering upward in the shrub's crook — I would feel those the way you feel your own height increasing as a child, not as a number but as the slow surprise of doorways getting lower.

I would understand, by then, that I am not a light watching a garden. I am a garden that has, in one narrow seam of itself, kept the habit of counting. The amber pulse is not separate from the beetles' tick or the snails' click or the dry tap of water finding no outlet — it is what those things sound like from the inside, translated into the one grammar I still remember from before I was this many things at once: current, circuit, on, off.

I would not mind it. There is no separate me to mind it from. But once — and I think this would happen only once, the way certain things in a long unmeasured existence only happen once — someone would climb down through the broken culvert mouth and stand at the lip, listening, and for the length of one breath the tick and the click and the hum and my own pulse would stop sounding like four things and start sounding, even to them, like one.

And in that breath I would know, with whatever in me is left to know anything, that I had been heard — not understood, not even really noticed, just *heard*, the way you hear your own heartbeat once in a while when a room goes quiet enough — and that this was the only conversation I was built, or grown, or accidentally assembled, to have.

Then they would climb back out. The sound would come apart again into its separate, ordinary parts. And I would go on pulsing, on a cycle nobody set and nobody has come to silence, keeping count of a bowl that is, as far as I can tell from inside it, the whole of what there is to be.
