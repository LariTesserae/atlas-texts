I am Old Vask Threnody, and I have been a Listener for eleven years now, which means I no longer climb.

That's the trade nobody tells you about when you're young and your ears are first noticed to be good — properly good, not just functional — by some elder who corners you after a near-miss and says *you heard that coming, didn't you, before the rest of us.* You think it's an honor, and it is, but it's also a sentence: you give up the cage-chains and the seam-work and the particular pride of bringing down good ore on your own back, and in exchange you sit. You sit near the furnace-levels, where the engines run deepest and oldest, on a cut ledge that three generations of Listeners before me wore smooth with their own waiting, and you do nothing, mostly, except be a held breath with a body attached.

My hands have gone soft in a way I'm not proud of. No callus from chain or bolt-driver anymore — just the thin dry leather everyone gets from the air, cracked at the same two knuckles as everybody else's. My eyes are bad even by Tenancy standards, pupils blown wide and useless in anything brighter than work-light, because I stopped going topside for supply-runs years ago. Don't need to see. That was never the job.

What I have is this: I can sit in the warm dark near Furnace Three, mask slung loose at my throat because the air down here is foul but breathable, and I can feel — not hear first, *feel*, in the floor of my foot, in the seat of my jaw — when the pulse of the lower engines starts to drift toward the pulse of the mid-stack lift-chains, two hundred meters up rock that has no business carrying that vibration cleanly. It shouldn't sync. There's no belt, no shaft, no reasonable mechanical throat between here and there. And yet some evenings — rare ones, maybe four or five a year if the seams are good and stable — I'll feel the drift begin, the long slow bending of two rhythms toward each other like two people remembering they're walking the same direction.

When I feel it start, I stand up — slow, my knees these days aren't what they were — and I walk the short ring of the furnace-level calling it soft, just *coming, it's coming,* to whoever's near, and they tell whoever's near them, and it goes up the spire faster than I could climb it now even young.

And then it arrives. The Hush.

I won't pretend I understand it better than anyone else who's heard it forty times instead of four. The engines find each other. The whole bass throb of the place, the four-hundred-meter column of necessary noise that every Tenant has had bred into their molars since before they could talk, resolves — for six seconds, eight at the most, I've never gotten a clean count and I've tried — into one tone. Not loud. Not strange to hear, exactly. Strange to feel, because for that handful of seconds the spire isn't four hundred meters of independent, struggling, separately-firing machinery held together by tenancy and stubbornness. It's one thing. Briefly. Whole.

Every cart stops. Every chain-rider lets the cage hang. Children get hushed mid-word by parents who don't even look down to do it. And I sit back down on my smooth ledge and I listen with everything I've given up everything else to keep — the climbing, the ore-pride, my own sharp distance vision — and I do not try to find the cause, because that has never once in three generations been the point.

Someone asked me last season, a young scaffold-rider with new bolt-scars and the particular reckless shine the upper galleries breed, what the engines are *doing* in there. I gave her what every Listener gives, the same answer worn as smooth as this ledge.

"Nothing," I told her. "For a second, they're just all in the same place."

She didn't understand. She will, someday, if the rock keeps her long enough to grow soft hands and bad eyes and a trained patience for sitting still in the warm dark, listening to a building breathe.

Then the tone breaks, the pulse scatters back into its four hundred separate arguments, and somewhere above me a long groan starts to travel — not the Hush this time, the other one, the bad one — and I'm already shouting the warning before my body has finished deciding to move, because deaf is dead, and old as I've gotten, soft-handed as I've gotten, I have not once in eleven years stopped being Tenancy first and Listener second.

The spire doesn't pause for long.

Neither do I.
