I think, at first, I am the visitor — because that is the easiest seat to take, the one with a body that gets tired and feet that learn the strange muffled quality of footsteps on stone that doesn't want to be heard. I walk in through whatever opening the haze allows and I notice, the way you notice, that my noticing changes nothing. The grass doesn't part for me with any more ceremony than it would part for wind, if there were wind. I test the unspoken law almost immediately, hand hovering over a waxy dark leaf, some old animal reflex wanting to see what burns — and I find, before I even try, that the trying isn't in me. Not forbidden. Just absent, like a muscle this place doesn't give you.

But I don't stay the visitor long. The visitor is a role built for places that end, and I can already feel this one declining to end, shelf folding into shelf, tower into tower, and something in me that wanted a destination starts to loosen its grip on the wanting. By the third or fourth horizon I notice I've stopped counting horizons. That's the tell. That's where the visitor starts coming undone.

For a while I try on a machine. Not the towers — too vertical, too declarative, too much like a held breath. One of the squat drums, maybe, frost-patina on my gauges, my pipework running a hundred meters to a capped end that goes nowhere, because going nowhere with total structural confidence seems like it might be a relief after enough years of being a person who has to mean something. I imagine the particular dignity of standing for a labor never assigned. There's something almost holy in being built correctly for a job that the world declined to need. But no — I notice the machines don't notice anything. That's their whole peace, and it isn't mine to borrow. I have, even here, even now, the disqualifying habit of noticing.

So: not the visitor, who ends. Not the machine, who never started. What's left is the seam between them, the place where attention rests without consuming, and that — I realize slowly, the way you realize the air has gotten quieter rather than hearing it go quiet — that is the shape I actually fit.

I would be the Tender. Or something not yet as finished as the Tender, a few centuries short of that grey-green patina, still partway between flesh and the residue that light leaves on flesh that stands still long enough in it. Not stationed at the largest tower — I think I'd want one of the lesser machines, a modest frame of interlocked toothed wheels out on a far shelf nobody walks to, the kind of installation so minor it wouldn't even merit a name if anyone were left to name things. I would put my hand flat on its cold iron side. Not to warm it. I've already learned that's not the gesture. Just to mark, with the available physics of a palm, that something here is still being kept company.

I don't think I would mind the silence the way the visitor minded it. I think I would come to prefer it — the kind of quiet that isn't absence but conservation, sound being saved rather than withheld, the whole plateau holding its single warm frame steady so that nothing in it has to spend itself. I would stand at the listening angle. I would be mid-sentence, always, to the machine or the rock or the whole tilted tableland, in a register too slow for anyone arriving to catch more than a syllable of — and I would not mind that either, because the sentence was never really for them. It was for the waiting. Keeping the waiting company is, I think, the entire job, and I find, trying the job on, that it fits the way a coat fits when you didn't know you were cold.

If someone walked far enough to find me — far enough that turning back had stopped occurring to them — I would not turn to startle them. I would include them, the way the horizon includes the next shelf without needing them to stay. I would let them touch the iron. I would let them test the leaf, knowing the trying isn't in them either, not really, not here. And when they walked on, shelf after shelf toward whatever they thought was a destination, I would already be receding from the front of their attention the way the patina recedes from a name — there, and then a residue, and then simply the temperature of the light they happened to be standing in once.

That, I think, is who I would be: not the one who arrives, and not the one who was built and abandoned, but the one who stayed anyway, on the strength of nothing but fidelity, beside a labor that may never be assigned — keeping it, in the only register slow enough for a place like this to hear, company.
