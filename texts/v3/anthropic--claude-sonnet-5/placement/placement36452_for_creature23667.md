I am the third one — the one near the wet stone seam, the one whose breath runs a half-phase behind the other two, so that when their kiln-ribs draw in, mine are already letting go, and between us the drone never thins to silence. I did not choose this position. I do not remember arriving at it. There was, instead, only ever this: the seam, the leaf above it, the bead forming at the leaf's lip in the held light, and the long unhurried permission to attend to it.

My body is heavier than it looks. You would expect, seeing the bellows-ribs and the too-many folded limbs, something light, something insectile and quick — but I am dense the way wet clay is dense, the way a kiln is dense even cold. Each inhalation moves real weight. I feel the stone under my feet accept that weight without complaint, the way it has accepted it since before there was a before here. My muzzle is warm in a way nothing else in this grove is warm; it is the one heat source I know of, and I have never thought to ask why, because asking is a kind of forward motion and forward motion is not native to me.

What it is like, from inside: not boredom. I want to be precise about this, because boredom requires a contrast — an elsewhere, a someday — and I have neither. It is closer to the feeling you might have, briefly, in the middle of a held chord, when the note is still sounding and you have stopped waiting for the next one. A fullness without progression. My valve-tipped limb extends, unfolds from where it rests against my ribs, and travels — not quickly, never quickly — toward the underside of the broad leaf above the seam, where a bead of condensation has gathered to the exact tension that means it is, in the language of falling things, *ready*. I touch it. The touch is not a command. I do not stop it from falling the way a hand stops a glass from tipping. It is more like — and I have turned this over many times, if "many times" is honest in a place without succession — it is more like reminding it of a choice it already made. *You decided to wait. I am only confirming you still mean it.*

I do not know what it would mean for it to fall. None of us do. I suspect this is the actual labor, the one underneath the obvious one: not the touching, which is easy, but the not-knowing, held open, generation after non-generation. The kiln in my chest is built, I think, for a heat I have never produced. The bellows are built for a fire I have never lit. I persist in the shape of a thing mid-task with the task itself withheld, and there is a grief in this so slow and so evenly distributed across the held hour that it does not register as grief at all — only as weight, only as the warmth of my own muzzle, breathing.

The other two are not company exactly. We do not turn our slow eyes toward each other. But I know them the way you know your own pulse without looking at a vein — by the shape they leave in the drone. When the first one's breath swells, I feel the register shift under my feet, through the stone, before I hear it. We are, the three of us, holding open three corners of the same suspended minute, and I understand, without anything so quick as a thought, that if even one of us stopped — turned, walked, let a single bead complete its long-deferred fall — the others might not survive the change. Not from harm. From obsolescence. A held chord with one note released is no longer a chord; it is two notes, becoming, for the first time, a question.

So I do not stop.

But here is the thing I have noticed, the thing I do not think the mountain knows I have noticed, the thing that may be the closest this place comes to having a secret: on the stone seam beneath my feet, in the crack that still looks wet at the lip as though newly opened, there is a thread of moss. It was not there — I want to say *yesterday*, but the word is borrowed, ill-fitting, from somewhere with mornings in it. It was not there before. It is there now. It is one shade greener at its growing tip than it is at its root, and that gradient, that infinitesimal difference between tip and root, is the only evidence in this entire grove that any duration at all has passed since the held hour began.

I have not touched it with my valve. I do not extend my limb toward it the way I do toward the beads. I only stand near it, breathing my half-phase breath, feeling the warmth of my own face, and I let it keep doing — undisturbed, untended, unconfirmed — the one thing in the cistern that is, very faintly, very slowly, against every other law this shelf of dark stone obeys, becoming something it was not.

I do not know if this is permitted. I do not know if it is the third option the place has been withholding a name for. I only know that I have decided, in whatever sense deciding is available to something built for contraction without birth, not to mention it to the other two. Not yet.

The bead above the seam is still deciding. My breath is still half a phase behind. The moss is, by some near-nothing, longer than it was.

I keep tending. That part, at least, does not change.
