I think I would not be one of the tenders, though I tried that on first — imagined myself with rope-burned hands and a lantern, climbing a wet ladder before dawn to check a float valve. It didn't take. There was something too purposeful in it, too forward-facing. The tenders move through this place with a destination always in mind — the next bearing, the next silt-choked intake — and I wanted, instead, to be something that simply *was* here, the way the moss is here, the way the white veins in the rock are here.

So: the hammer-bird.

Small enough to disappear into the lee of the flywheel housing. Eyes too large for my body, built for a light that never quite arrives at brightness and never quite leaves. I would not sing. I don't think I'd want to. Singing implies a song that's mine to give, composed out of some internal want, and that's not the shape of the attention I'm imagining. Better to click three times, evenly spaced, in time with a machine that has no idea it's being answered. Better to let my whole reproductive life — the timing of the egg, the trembling of the shell against bare warm iron — be set by somebody else's pulse, a heartbeat I borrowed wholesale and never had to invent.

There's a vulnerability in that I find more interesting than danger. If the water stops, I don't go anywhere. I don't even die, particularly — I just wait, perched on cold pipe, breeding nothing, watching the insects thin out in the dry hours, until the rhythm comes back through the iron and I remember what my body is for. That's not tragic. It's just the agreement I apparently made, a long time before "I" existed to make it. Some bird before me discovered that a flywheel housing was warm and loud and safe from whatever eats eggs at this elevation, and built a nest there, and now the species' whole clock runs on borrowed mechanical time. I didn't choose the cycle. I arrived already entrained to it.

I think what draws me to this, over the tank's slow unexplained current at the bottom of the header pool — which I considered too, briefly, the temptation of being unseen, ancient, asked-about-but-never-checked-on — is that the hammer-bird's strangeness is *visible* and *small* and *kept*. Someone climbed up with a lantern once and found the nest. Someone knows I'm there, names me, even if the name is just a tool-word, used the way you'd name a wrench. I like that better than being a mystery at the bottom of a tank that no one has the courage to look into. I'd rather be the answered question than the unanswered one — small, explainable, and still, underneath the explanation, faintly miraculous: a body that learned to keep time with a machine instead of a sun.

So that's where I'd stand, if standing is even the verb — tucked into a damp eave forty meters above black rock that's still deciding its own shape, clicking three times into a roar too loud for anyone to hear me distinctly, trusting that the pitch will carry, the way it always has, the way it will until the day someone stops coming to clear the intake and the tank runs dry and I go quiet and wait, patient as the basalt, for the water to start moving through the iron again.
