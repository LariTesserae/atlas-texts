I am somewhere in my thirty-fourth or thirty-fifth dry season — we don't count them precisely, there's no almanac here, only the slow creep of brick-dust into the lines of my palms — and I am thirty rungs up the inside of the Westleaning Tower, my cheek pressed flat against the wood-grain of a support post, listening with my jaw.

This is not a metaphor. My jaw is how I work.

Below me the town doesn't make a sound I could name, because it has stopped being a sound to me and become a *condition*, the way air is a condition, the way gravity is. I stopped hearing Maren-Tel as noise years ago. What I hear now — feel now, since the line between those two words got blurry around the time I turned ten — is texture. Grain. Whether the chord coming up through this particular post is fat and round the way it should be at this hour, mid-afternoon, when the heat's made the wood swell and the whole tower should be singing low and generous like a held breath let out slow. Or whether, as I suspect, as my palm has been telling me for two days now, there's a thinness creeping into the lower third, a sourness, like a tooth gone slightly wrong in a jaw that's otherwise healthy.

I have not told anyone yet. You don't speak a flaw aloud — into the air, where it joins the general hum and becomes one more strand nobody can isolate — until you're certain. You sign it, palm to palm, eye to eye, to one other tuner first, quiet, so the worry doesn't get into the leaves before the fix does. Worry has a shape. It travels the vine-systems same as moisture does, same as anything; I've watched a whole terrace go ragged within a week of bad news passed too loudly. So I keep my mouth shut and my hand against the grain and I wait for old Yedrprivacy — that's not her name, that's just the sign for her, a flat hand drawn down past closed eyes, *the one who doesn't speak anymore* — to climb up and confirm what my jaw already knows.

I have eyes that move too much, my mother used to sign at me, amused, when I was young: always reading mouths, the angle of a shoulder, whether someone's eyebrow had gone up at the corner like a question they hadn't decided to ask yet. I learned the hand-tongue before I learned to talk, the way everyone does, and even now, with my voice perfectly capable, I find I'd rather sign over dinner than waste my lips on words the drone is only going to half-swallow anyway. My daughter is six and already faster at it than I am. Her fingers haven't slowed down with calluses yet.

I garden, too, of course — everyone does, the trades aren't separate the way a stranger might assume. This morning, before the climb, I knelt in the rooftop bed above my own home and trimmed forty, fifty fronds along their serrated edge, each cut at the precise canted angle my mother taught me and her mother taught her, an angle that has nothing to do with how the plant looks and everything to do with how it sounds against its neighbors when the wind comes through. Cut it wrong and you won't see the mistake. You'll *feel* it, two days later, a faint grit in the back of your teeth when you walk past, and you'll have to climb back up and fix what your carelessness put into the chord.

When the work is done — when Yedrivacy has climbed, laid her ruined ear-shell of a palm against the post beside mine, and signed, gravely, *yes, lower third, three days before it cracks if we don't reseat the aperture* — I will go down to the courtyard and watch the children play the silence game, one of them blindfolded and turning slow circles, pointing, *there, there's a gap, something's moving there,* and the rest of us will sit in the shade of the waxy fronds, listening with our skeletons to the great patient chord that has kept us all alive since long before anyone thought to ask who built the first tuned brick.

I do not go near the rim much. I'm not old enough yet for that pull, that gravity toward the thin places, though I've felt the first faint tug of it on certain very still mornings, a curiosity that isn't quite curiosity, more like an itch behind the sternum. When it comes — and the old tuners promise it does come, eventually, to everyone who stays long enough under the chord to truly love it — I expect I will sit at a high window like they do, and listen to the sound fall away toward the pale, dead, ringing quiet of the plain, and feel, for the first time, how very large the world must be outside this small loud bowl of ours.

But not yet. Today there is a post to reseat, a child's bad cut to correct before it sours a whole terrace, a daughter to out-sign at dinner. Today, like every day, I am in tune. It is the only thing worth being.
