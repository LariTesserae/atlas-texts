I am perhaps eleven years in this body, which here means old enough to climb alone, young enough that my grip still aches by evening.

Right now I am sixty meters up a trunk my mother's mother called *the patient one* — not because it is more patient than other trunks but because it has been patient *so far*, which is the only kind of trust anyone affords wood here. I am wedged in a fork wide enough to seat three, my toes curled into a seam of bark, waiting for my cousin to finish coiling the line that will let us cross to the next causeway without touching the bare root-floor forty meters under us, where the fog is still burning off in slow gold streaks.

The light is doing what it always does, which is to say nothing, evenly, from everywhere. I stopped finding that strange before I stopped finding most things strange. What I have not stopped doing is listening — under the hiss of leaf-static, under my cousin's breathing, under the far-off struck-glass call that might be a bird and might be someone's cousin signaling something three kilometers off — for the particular low note that means *this trunk is done arguing with its own weight.* My mother's mother heard it once, three days before *the patient one* lost a shelf the size of a hamlet. She moved us in a single afternoon, no debate, no grief yet, only grip and rope and the long climb sideways to a trunk that hadn't been named anything yet because we hadn't lived on it long enough to need to.

I carry a gourd-knot of dew at my hip, a sliver of knapped stone my father traded for at a meeting-causeway two years back, and a half-formed private theory — not yet spoken aloud to anyone, because you don't say a new theory about the light until you're old enough that being wrong won't follow you — that the glow brightens fastest over the places where something large has recently fallen. That the forest is, in some clumsy enormous way, *paying out* faster wherever it has just taken the most. I haven't tested this. I may not live long enough in one place to test much of anything, which my mother says is not a tragedy, just the shape of the debt we're all standing under.

My cousin signals: *ready.* I flash back: *wait* — one beat, no more, just long enough to feel whether the fork under me hums right. It does. I uncurl my toes, take the line in both long-fingered hands, and go.
