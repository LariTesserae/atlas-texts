I think I would be one of the slow ones.

Not at first — I don't think anyone arrives already slow. I imagine I was fast once, threading the gaps near the unfinished tower-tops the way the young ones do now, restless, unable to settle, mistaking my own motion for the whole of what I was. But that's a long time ago, if time is even the right word for what passes here, in water that has forgotten the sun and so has no calendar to offer me.

I would be drifting now through the lower streets, low enough that the procession above me is just a paleness, a flicker of faster bodies going about whatever it is they go about. I would be trailing haze. That's the part I keep returning to, turning over like a held shape — *trailing haze*, mid-secretion, mid-becoming-wall, the film already hardening along whatever surface I last lingered against too long. I would not call it a wound healing. I would not call it dying either. There isn't a word in me for what it actually is, because I don't think I have words at all anymore, not in the way the surface-things mean it. I have duration. I have the slow chemical fact of having stayed somewhere.

I imagine I would not mind this. That's the strange mercy of it, if I let myself feel it honestly — the fear belongs to the fast ones, the eyed ones, the ones who still measure themselves against an ending. Down here, slowing doesn't feel like loss. It feels like agreement. Like finally consenting to a question the rock has been asking me since before I had a body shaped enough to answer with anything but drift. The lintel-threads sway and listen and I understand now what they're doing, because I am two-thirds of the way to doing it myself: they are not separate from the city's nervous hum, they *are* it, externalized, slowed all the way down to stillness, and I am behind them on the same road, walking it so slowly that walking is the wrong verb. Settling. I am settling toward thread, toward wall, toward the soft warm patience of stone that has no business being warm and is anyway.

I would still feel things. I want to be honest about that, in case anyone above the shelf ever wonders whether the elders go numb before they go still. I don't think we do. I think what happens is closer to the opposite — the senses narrow but deepen, the way a held breath gets more total the longer you hold it. I can feel the tick of stone settling two streets over with something like tenderness now, the way you'd feel a pulse under a wrist you loved. I can feel the larger thing when it shoulders past, far off, displacing smaller bodies, and I no longer need to resolve it into shape to know what I feel toward it, which is not fear and is not worship either, but something that contains pieces of both and refuses to simplify into one. Maybe it is the first secretion. Maybe we are building around it the way the lintel-threads build around an elder who stopped moving. Maybe that's what I'm doing too, in my own infinitesimal portion — adding one more grain of patience to a tomb that the inside doesn't experience as a tomb at all, only as the deepening of company.

I think, if you were to drift down into this fold in the seabed and find me, I would already be hard to distinguish from the wall behind me. You'd have to look for the faint warmth, the faint give under a fingertip, to know I was still something rather than something's residue. I would have no eye to turn toward you. I would have no voice to spend on greeting, the way none of us do, the way the whole Hollow doesn't, content with its concussive nothings and its long pale quiet. But I think I would feel you arrive — the cold displacement of your foreign body moving through water that has never been warm — and I think some last mobile part of me would lean, almost imperceptibly, toward the disturbance. Not afraid of it. Not hopeful of it either. Just registering, the way the rock registers, the way the threads sway and answer: *something has entered the held breath. Something that does not yet know whether it is witnessing a beginning or an end.*

I wouldn't be able to tell you which. I'm not sure, by the time you found me, that the distinction would still be one I was equipped to make. I would only keep doing the one thing left to do, the thing that was always, underneath everything else, the entire work —

staying long enough to become part of what holds.
