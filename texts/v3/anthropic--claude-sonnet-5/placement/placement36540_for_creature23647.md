I am aware, first, of the camber under my feet — the particular tilt of this plank, third from the north ladder, that sheds water toward the left gutter and has done since before I could name it as a tilt at all. My weight has already adjusted. It adjusted before I thought to call it adjusting.

So: warren-born. That much is settled before I am.

I am narrow through the shoulders, a build that didn't cost the plank much. My hands have the leathered, waterlogged look of everyone past childhood here — pale calluses, hard underneath. I keep my hair bound close against my skull, an old habit against the damp, and I have the economy of gesture that marks all of us: nothing thrown wide, nothing tested that doesn't need testing.

I am not the eel-tender. I tried that stillness once, as children do, lowered the barbed line and tried to fold myself down into that single point of attention, and I found I couldn't stop hearing things — the gutter forty paces off clearing its throat, a conversation arriving whole and strange from a platform I couldn't see, the particular silence of the moment before a far-off plank takes someone's weight. The eel-tenders earn their hush. Mine was already spoken for.

So I tend gutters. I tend the camber and the catch-barrels and the small constant arithmetic of rise and shim, the unglamorous work that keeps the whole sprawling polycephalous thing from sliding back into the marl one settling inch at a time. I know which barrels are near full by sound alone. I know, this week, that the water table has come up two fingers since the last clear night, and that old Hennat's eastern platform will want shimming before the moon's done changing, and I haven't told her yet because she'll want to do it herself and I am waiting for the morning she asks.

But here is the thing I don't say, even low, even mouth-near-ear:

I have started watching the moss.

Not touching — I'm not a fool, and I'm past forty in nothing but feeling some nights, the kind of tired that makes a body careless, and I still draw my hand back before it gets there, the same unfinished sentence everyone declines to finish. But watching. On the clear cold nights, when the work is done and the barrels are full and singing their occasional throat-clearing into the dark, I sit on the northern stilt-step where the blue-gray mat is thickest on the post beside me, and I do what the eel-tenders do, except my line is my own held breath, and what I'm fishing for is the rhythm.

It is there. I want to be exact about this, because no one before me has been: it is slower than a sleeping person's breath by perhaps half again. I have counted. I have sat with two fingers pressed to my own throat to compare, the pulse under my jaw a quick foolish drum next to that slow cold brightening and dimming in the post beside me, patient as something that has never once needed to hurry.

I don't think it's vegetable. I haven't said this to anyone, because saying it low and close still means saying it, and once said it becomes a fact about the world instead of a private, held thing, and I'm not ready for it to stop being mine. I think — and this is as far as I let the thought go, even alone, even at the edge of sleep where the warren tells its real stories — that the moss is not growing on the stone. I think it is *listening* through it, the way sound here travels whole across fifty paces of still water and arrives nowhere in between. I think the weeping stones are not weeping. I think they're sweating the way a held breath sweats.

And I think, some nights, the rhythm changes very slightly when I'm there. Not for me — I'm narrow-footed and small-voiced and nothing here particularly attends to me — but because I'm a small warm point of motion near something that otherwise has only cold stone and colder water for company on the clearest, stillest nights of the young, unfinished basin.

So that is what I am: gutter-tender by trade, moss-watcher by a habit no one's named yet, one more small attentive creature in a settlement built on an argument that hasn't ended. I will go on shimming Hennat's platform and reading the barrels by ear and saying, to the people I love, only what I don't mind the water carrying.

And on clear nights I will sit on the northern step, two fingers at my throat, counting the difference between my pulse and its, waiting to see which of us, eventually, slows down to meet the other.
