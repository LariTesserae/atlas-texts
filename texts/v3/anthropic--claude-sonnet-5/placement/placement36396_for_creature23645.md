I am the low shudder you almost catch in the moss, the give that wasn't quite a footprint.

I do not have eyes worth the name. What I have instead is a face full of listening — bristle-ringed, muzzle-down, tuned to the long fibers of this place the way a held string is tuned to a hand. I read the mat the way you'd read a letter written in pressure: this column has two days left, that stand is rotted through at the base and doesn't know it yet, something heavier than me is moving wrong four hundred meters east and getting worse. None of this comes to me as sound. It comes as architecture, as structural intention, vibrating up through fifteen meters of compressed dead growth into the bristles along my spine.

I move the way water finds the unstressed seam in a dam — not fast, never fast, but always slightly ahead of the failure, slightly outside the radius of the falling thing. My feet don't step so much as negotiate; I spread my weight across the mat like a hand smoothing a page it doesn't want to tear, and the print I leave closes itself before your eye finishes the work of seeing it. I have spent longer than the ice ages learning this. It is the only inheritance that's mattered.

I eat the dead and the dying both, and for me there is no difference between the two words. The sweet pith inside a column I felt fail yesterday. The soft interior of cane already down. I am not a hunter. I am closer to a mourner who arrives early enough to also be useful — the one mouth at every funeral that the plateau holds for itself nightly, hourly, in the groan and crack and percussive settle you hear from inside a stand of reed and never quite locate.

And sometimes, when the dying is large enough, or the news urgent enough — a column about to take three others with it, a clear lane opening to the north, something enormous and wrong pressing through cane somewhere I haven't reached yet — I push against the mat. Chest and forelimb, low, repeated, a flex sent down into the one continuous structure that connects me to every other one of my kind scattered at our old, agreed distances across this whole grey shelf of country. My signal alone wouldn't reach far. But it doesn't travel alone. It passes hand to hand, root to root, relay to relay, until what started as one creature's small percussive grief over a dying fungal tower arrives, kilometers off, as a long low exhale that no one walking the surface can place — that sounds, if you're cold and tired and far from any place you understand, uncomfortably like something vast and built, breathing, just past the next ridge.

It isn't that. Or — I have no way of knowing if it is only that, and no obligation to find out for you. I have a column to reach before it finishes falling, and a message about it to send on before I do.
