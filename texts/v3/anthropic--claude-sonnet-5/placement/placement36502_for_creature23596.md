I am not at the center of the bowl, where the gapways widen enough for two people to pass without turning. I am near the rim, where the stone walls begin their lean inward, in a house that shares its eastern beam with my aunt's house and its western wall with old Hennit's, who keeps the fat dozing birds that nest along our shared eave and his.

I think I am young—young enough that the turn isn't fully settled into me yet. I notice this because this morning, going out, I caught my shoulder on the doorframe. Not hard. Just enough to feel the wood's reminder, the small honest correction of it, and to hear my grandmother's breath catch behind me—not in alarm, just in the reflex of someone who has flinched at that particular sound for sixty years, the sound of a body not yet angled right.

My hands smell of vine-sap. That's the first true thing about me. I prune the mortar-spirals on the south-facing wall of the granary-that-is-also-three-families'-storeroom, have done since I was tall enough to reach the third course of stone without a stool. I don't know why the spiral curls left at the second knuckle and right at the fourth. My fingers know. That's enough. My father's fingers knew before mine, and his mother's before his, back along a thread I couldn't number if you asked me to, and I have never once been asked to, because no one here asks why with any real expectation of an answer. The why settled into stone long before settling into speech, and we are a people who trust stone over speech most days.

I am crouched now—this is where I find myself, this is the moment I am made of—at one of the damp seams that runs along the base of the granary wall, the dark capillary line in the pale hard earth that never widens and never dries. My hand is flat against it. I am not thinking of anything in particular. This is not a vigil, not a ritual with a name. It is closer to the way you might rest a hand against a sleeping animal's flank without deciding to, just to feel that the breathing continues, unbothered, even.

Light comes down through a gap in the canopy in one hard clean shaft, cold despite its brightness, and it has moved half its own width across the packed earth since I crouched here, which tells me, without needing a bell or a voice, that it is nearing the half-day. Somewhere above and behind me a fat bird shifts in the eave-nest and resettles without falling, and the small hip-high browsers that wander the wider gapways make their soft cropping sounds against the moss, and none of it disturbs the quiet so much as it furnishes it, the way breath furnishes a held room.

I am narrow-shouldered. I am low-voiced—I have not spoken aloud yet today, only the half-words exchanged with my aunt over the shared wall this morning, pitched to die before they reached the next house. I carry, without ever having been told to carry it, the conviction that the stone walls curving gently around this whole bowl are not a wall at all but a rib, or a shoulder, or the long curl of a spine, and that I live, all of us live, in the warm dark crook of something that has not finished resting.

I do not find this frightening. I have never found it frightening, any more than a child finds a parent's sleeping breath frightening. It is simply the shape the world takes here, the fact beneath the facts, the reason the doors are narrow and the voices low and the vines trained into patterns no one alive devised. I rise now—carefully, the old habit already mostly in my knees and hips even if my shoulders haven't fully learned the doorway-turn—and I go back toward the granary wall to finish the pruning before the light-shaft slides past the third roof-beam, which is how I will know it's time to go in for the midday quiet, where my family will eat without much talking, and listen, as we always do, for nothing in particular, and hear, as we always do, exactly that — nothing, vast and unbroken and held, which is the only sound that has ever meant, here, that everything is as it should be.
