I would be small, at first. That's the part I keep returning to, trying to get right—not the end of it, which is vast and red and slow, but the beginning, when I was still quick enough to be afraid.

I think I'd be one of the alarm-callers. Not a Sole, not yet a Quiet—something in between, something that hasn't settled into its final shape because it hasn't had the time. A clutch-sibling among many, hatched in the lee of a basin-swell where the tallow ran thin enough that the colonies hadn't claimed it, where the smell was sharp instead of sweet and the rock underfoot still had a little grit in it that hadn't been worn away. My body would be small and segmented, low-slung, built for speed over a few body-lengths and nothing more—because a few body-lengths is the whole of an alarm-caller's life, the distance between *hearing the click* and *being wrong about where it came from*.

I would know the world entirely through the rock. Not through eyes—I'd have eyes, vestigial, a smear of light-sensitivity good for distinguishing the basin's general gloom from the basin's general gloom and not much else—but through the long bare strip down my spine, the place where the nerve comes up close to the surface and reads the stone the way the text says, like a second skin worn loose. I would feel the many-legged ones before I heard them in any way you'd call hearing. I would feel the lie in their clicking, sometimes—the half-beat that doesn't track with the footfall, the false position—and I'd learn, the way my kind learns, which is to say by being eaten when I learned wrong.

The roar wouldn't be ambient to me. It would be the only thing there is. I wouldn't experience it as noise pressing in from outside; I'd experience it as the medium I'm made of, the way you don't experience the air you're breathing as a substance until someone asks you to notice it. My whole life would be call-and-answer—not metaphorically, literally, a string of decisions made in fractions of a second, contributing my own clicks and shrieks to a structure too large for me to perceive the shape of, the way no single thread perceives the shape of the cloth.

And I think—because this is the part that makes the shape of the story rather than just the fact of it—I would be old for one of my kind, somehow. Lucky, or careful, or simply small enough to not be worth the chase often enough. I would have outlasted clutch-siblings, outlasted the basin I hatched in, moved on through three or four others as the tallow thinned and thickened behind me, and I would notice, the way you notice your knees before you notice your hair, that I had begun to slow down.

That nothing was actually catching me anymore, but also that I no longer cared as much whether it could.

That the deep bass note under everything—the one I'd felt in my teeth my whole life without separating it from the rest of the roar—had started, gradually, to feel less like background and more like an *address*. Like something at the bottom of the lowest basin had been, this whole time, patiently saying my name in a register I hadn't yet grown the patience to hear as language.

So I would go down. Basin after basin, each one a little quieter to me, not because the sound was lessening but because my need to react to it was. The clicking would still happen around me—younger, faster things, doing exactly what I did for exactly as long as I did it—and I would simply no longer be implicated in it. I would feel myself thickening along that point of contact with the rock, my underside spreading, finding more of the wet stone to rest against, needing less and less reason to lift away from it.

And at the lowest point—the deepest cup of damp, where the tallow pools instead of merely filming—I think I would simply stop going anywhere else. Not in defeat. In the way a question stops, once it's been sufficiently answered.

I wouldn't call. I wouldn't need to. Everything above me would go on calling around me instead, the way it always had to the ones who got here first, and I would lie there feeling the small loud lives flow past on either side like water finding the path around a stone too old to be called an obstacle anymore, and I would breathe—slowly, so slowly that the mist above would learn to time itself by me rather than the reverse—and somewhere in that long red-glowing patience I would understand, finally, without anything so fast as a thought, that the barrenness of the ground and the fullness of the dark were never two different facts.

I would simply be what the floor became, once it was done refusing to grow anything, and started, instead, to wait.
