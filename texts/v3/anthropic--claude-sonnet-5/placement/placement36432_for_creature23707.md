I am somewhere in the middle third of the basin, neither near the clear ground around the post nor close enough to the scree wall to see the dark migrations happen. This matters more than it sounds like it should. The middle is where the press is deepest, where you are least likely to be the back someone else stands on and most likely to be the thing standing—though by midcycle these distinctions blur, because everyone here has been both, many times, within the same held breath.

My dome is not large. Call it dog-sized at the broadest facet, though I don't think in those terms; I think, if it can be called thinking, in terms of contact and lack of contact, warmth received and warmth given onward. The plates of my back are old enough that the glow under them has gone a little toward red, a little away from orange, though I am nowhere near the deep banked color of the ones who keep to the rim. I am still mostly given to the climbing, still mostly given to the work of being ground for whoever needs ground.

Here is what the hours are like, if hours is a word that fits a place with no sun to mark them: the basin inhales. I feel it before I understand it—a tightening of contact all around me, plates pressing closer, the friction-sound rising from its grate-hush into something with more grain in it, more catch. The heat comes up through whatever I have instead of feet, and I do not resist it; resisting it would be like resisting my own pulse, if I had one separate from the basin's. For four minutes, give or take whatever measure of give-or-take applies to something that has never owned a clock, we brighten together. I feel the bodies nearest me—three below, one shouldering my left flank, two more I am partly draped across—flare in step with my own flaring, not because anything tells us to but because there is, in the end, only the one fire, distributed.

Then the long exhale. Six minutes, seven. The press doesn't loosen—there is nowhere for it to loosen to—but the light goes inward, banks itself, and the sound drops back to its grate of settling coal, that low continuous made-thing that isn't speech but does the work speech does in other places: it tells everyone nearby that everyone nearby is still here.

Sometimes a click cuts through. I have made this sound myself, though I couldn't say what occasioned it the times I did—some readiness in my own plates that needed releasing, maybe, or some shape passing near enough through the press to be worth marking. When another body clicks close to me, I feel the ripple arrive before I hear it: a brightening passed hand to hand, flank to flank, through however many bodies lie between the click and me, weaker each time it's repeated, like a word said into a long line of mouths until it's only a shape of breath by the time it reaches the wall. I have never learned what the click means. I'm not sure meaning is the right house for it to live in. It is closer to a stone dropped to prove the pond is still a pond.

I know the post is there, though I have never stood at the cleared ring around its base, and won't. None of us climb it. None of us climb each other into it, even when the press elsewhere stacks three deep over the buried platform we are told is also there, somewhere off toward what I'd call the eastern slope if east meant anything inside a basin that erases its own sky. I don't know what built the post or the ring or the platform under us. I only know that some old shape of behavior runs through me, through all of us, the way the heat runs through the rock—a respect with the reasons burned off it, the way a scar outlives the wound.

What I am waiting for, if waiting is the right word for something that has never been in a hurry, is the slow cooling toward red, toward that deep coal-going-out color the elders wear at the rim. I have felt the beginning of it in myself, lately—a dimming after the brightening tides that takes a little longer each time to climb back out of. I don't experience this as loss. None of us do, as far as I can tell; there is no flinching in the press when a body nearby goes still and glassy and is, by the next long look, simply gone, folded back into the seam, its warmth handed on to whatever shape was waiting at the wall to wear it next. I think, if the herders up at the rim have it right, that I am heat first and shape second, and that the shape is only ever borrowed, the way the basin borrows the old builders' clearing and never asks who cleared it.

So: this is where I'd be, and what. Not the elder yet, not the new shape rising raw at the scree, but somewhere in the patient middle of an enormous, ancient, mineral breath—one more plated back among ten thousand, brightening on the same four-minute tide as everyone around me, keeping clear a doorway I don't remember being told to keep clear, waiting without dread or hurry for my own light to bank low enough that I'll begin, one slow cycle at a time, the long climb toward the wall.
