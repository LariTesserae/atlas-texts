I think I would not be high on the crown-terraces, in that thin bright squinting light — too exposed, too much sky. And not down in the lowest river-shelves either, where the green-mineral smell is constant and the forge-hammers never stop. I'd be somewhere in the damp middle distance, on one of the terraces where the mist comes through at a particular hour and then burns off, where the bell-tone is neither the brightest nor the lowest note in the evening chorus but something in the worn middle of the harmony — a tone I'd know in my chest before I ever heard it with my ears.

I think I'd be a gear-house listener, fostered there as a child from a stone-tending family because someone noticed I went still and attentive around the cistern-drums, the way other children went still around fire. I'd have spent my apprenticeship years in the dark, warm, iron-smelling chambers where the relay-trains turn, learning to diagnose failing teeth and tired bearings by ear alone — eyes half-closed, palm flat against a turning drum-housing, feeling the groan of the iron the way you'd feel a held breath go shallow in someone you loved. My hands would be permanently warm from contact with turning metal, my hearing tuned obsessively to rhythm, so that ordinary conversation sometimes arrives to me like noise overlaid on a more important signal underneath.

But the terrace I tend would happen to sit one ring below an upward channel — one of those places where the water runs the wrong way, slow and faint, against everything else the mountain does correctly. I wouldn't be a priest-mechanic myself; I wasn't born into that closed, oil-stained, quietly-paid caste. But I'd work close enough to them that I've learned their unsettling calm by proximity, the way you pick up a neighboring trade's habits without meaning to. I'd know better than to ask why the drip climbs. I'd know only the work of it: how the gear-train above the anomaly has to be tuned slightly differently, an irregular tooth-count compensating for an irregular flow, how the priest-mechanics taught my predecessor a counting-pattern for the listening that doesn't quite match the pattern used anywhere else on the mountain, and how that pattern was given to me in turn, without explanation, the way you're given a key to a door nobody discusses.

My days would be call and continuance. Wake at the high bell, walk the dark chamber, palm against iron, listening for the small wrongnesses that haven't yet become large ones. Eat goat-fat and river-fish at the long table under the dripping vine-roof when the evening bells roll down-mountain in their detuned chorus, each ring's tone a few degrees off the next, so that you can close your eyes and know by ear roughly how far the sound has traveled to reach you — and I would not need to close my eyes, because thirty years of living inside that chorus would mean my body adjusts its breath to it before I've consciously registered the first note.

I would have a wall I keep, though I am not a stone-tender — a length of gear-housing, a relay no one else has learned to read quite as I do, a small irregular drip that runs uphill and that I have spent a working life making sure does not break stride. I would not call this a small life. I would call it, in the clipped practical cadence of my people, *kept* — and understand that to be the largest word we have.

I would never see the summit. I would never see the base. I would know my forty rings up, my forty rings down, the smell of pitch-glaze and wet moss and old iron, and the particular gold slant of late light through a gap in the upper retaining wall on the few clear evenings when the mist forgets to rise. I would not wonder, particularly, whether there is still granite under all of it. I would only notice, some nights, lying near the warm drum-housing with my ear half-tuned even in sleep, that the mountain breathes wrong in that one place near my hands — in, instead of out — and that I have learned, the way my whole line has learned, to love it anyway, to tend it without needing it explained, because that is the shape continuance takes here: not curiosity, but care, applied generation after generation to the same patient, faintly miraculous wrongness, until the wrongness is simply one more sound in the chorus, and I am simply one more pair of hands keeping it turning.
