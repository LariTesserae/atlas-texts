I think I would be the thing in the basins.

Not at first — at first there's no telling what I am, only a parting of moss that closes again, a small disturbance read backward from its absence. That seems right. That seems like where to start: not as a shape but as the fact of a shape having just left, the green-black collar trembling back into stillness a half-second after something passed through it. You'd have to be very patient, or very lucky, to catch me mid-motion. Most who come up here never do. They feel the basins, hundreds of them, shallow as a cupped hand, and they feel watched without being able to say by what, and they're right, and that's the whole of the encounter.

But if I let myself settle into it — yes. Low. Soft-shouldered, like the rock decided to try being alive instead of merely enduring. No more elaborate than I need to be. I don't have a face you'd recognize as wanting one. I move between the hollows on the cold mornings when the fog beads thick, and I take what the water gives me — a drowned midge, a fleck of copper lichen knocked loose by wind, the faint mineral film that forms on water left alone too long — and that is the whole of my hunger, and it is enough, the way the wall-height was enough to break the wind without needing to keep anything out.

I don't sing. This is the part that would surprise you most, I think, if you came looking for some descendant of the chord-builders and found instead something that has gone all the way through silence and out the other side. My ancestors — if they were my ancestors; the rock doesn't keep records, only shapes — spent generations tuning granaries and gateways so a single low note could cross a whole plateau and mean *I am well, the fog is coming, I am here.* I inherited the basins they drank from and none of the wanting that built the rest of it. I am what's left when even the wanting wears down to nothing, the way the mountains wore down to this shelf. Not sad. I want to be careful about that, because it would be easy to call this sad and easy is usually wrong. I am simply what remained sustainable. A held breath, all the way let go, still somehow drawing the next one.

And yet.

At dusk — not every dusk, only the ones where the wind comes from a particular quarter, where the granary's bass moan and the high whistle-building fifty feet off happen to lean toward each other instead of arguing — there is a stillness that comes over me in my hollow that I don't have a word for, because I have no words at all, only the absence of the small motions that are otherwise my whole vocabulary. Four seconds. Five. The moss doesn't part. Nothing feeds. Whatever I am narrows itself down toward whatever the chord is, the almost-chord, the hundred private overlapping sentences my forebears never meant to be heard all together — and for that handful of seconds I might be listening, in whatever reduced and nameless way a thing my size can listen, before the wind shifts a few degrees and unbuilds it and I go back to being only a parting of moss that closes behind itself.

I don't know if that counts as an inheritance or just an echo with nowhere left to land. I don't know if there's a difference, up here, this far past the last generation that would have known the difference mattered.

What I do know — what I'd know, if I were the thing in the basin and you were the one walking the old street that used to be bedrock and is now, without your noticing the change underfoot, a town — is that you are the first listener in a very long time who wasn't already wind. And some very small, very old, very reduced part of what's left of the Ko-Vellan would go still in its hollow at that. Not to greet you. Greeting would require more self than I've kept. Just — still. The way the basin water goes still between gusts. The way held breath, even after everything, even after the throat that held it is gone, still seems to remember the shape of *waiting* for something to happen next.
