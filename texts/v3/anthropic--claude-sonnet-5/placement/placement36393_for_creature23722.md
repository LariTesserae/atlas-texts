I think I am standing on the third catwalk east of the number-six pump-housing, though "east" means nothing down here except "the direction the chain runs," and I have stopped checking. My feet know the slats by their give. There's one, four steps past the ladder, that sits a half-inch low and slaps wet against your heel if you don't lift slightly through it, and I lift slightly through it without thinking about lifting, the way you don't think about blinking.

My hand is on the pipe.

This is most of what I do, if I'm honest about it. I am a Tender, which means I have spent enough years as something louder — a hauler in my twenties, my shoulders still remember the rope-burn geography of it — that the work wore me down into someone quieter. Now I walk the gantries with my palm trailing along the warm black iron, and I stop, every so often, and I press my whole hand flat to a junction or a seam or a rivet-line, and I close my eyes, and I listen to what the pipe is telling me about the pressure four levels down that nobody can see.

It sounds like nothing, to anyone who hasn't done this for twenty years. To me it sounds like a held note about to crack.

I am not young. My fingertips have gone past pruned into something else, something permanently soft and pale that no longer remembers what dry feels like, and there is a horn-hard ridge across both palms from chain-rope, decades old, that the warm water has never managed to soften back down. I stoop. Everyone who has done this long enough stoops — from ducking the low gantry over the second hopper, from leaning in close to shout into ears that have given up the higher registers, from forty years of bending toward iron to ask it questions it answers only in vibration.

Right now the vibration under my palm is wrong in a way I don't have a word for and have stopped trying to find a word for. Not dangerous-wrong. Not yet. Just — attentive. As if the pipe itself were listening back.

Below me, past the lantern-light, the chain runs taut into black water, hauling up something that will clatter wet into the hopper and get fed to a furnace whose contents I have never asked about and never will, because that isn't mine to know, any more than the Feeders need to know what my ear presses for. We each hold our piece. This has always been the arrangement. I learned it from old Tender Maro, who learned it from someone before her, palm to iron, generation over generation, the way you'd pass down a prayer you'd stopped admitting was a prayer.

The water hits the hull below me in its ordinary chaotic slap, arrhythmic, mechanical — and then, underneath that, for three full seconds, something slower. Something with a shape to it. A push and a long pull and a push again, vast, patient, coming up from further down than any platform anyone has built.

I don't startle. None of us startle anymore. My hand, already flat against the pipe, simply stays where it is a moment longer than the work requires — not gripping, just resting, the way you'd touch a sleeping flank to be sure the sleep is only sleep. Around me, on the catwalks I can't fully see through the steam-haze, I know without looking that other hands are doing the same small private thing against other iron surfaces, all of us pausing in the same unspoken half-second, none of us mentioning it, none of us ever having mentioned it.

Then it passes. The arrhythmic slap resumes its arrhythm. My hand comes away from the pipe.

There's chain yet to haul. There's a hopper three platforms down that's been clattering oddly since the morning surge, and a young Feeder named Dessa who's been sent to fetch me about it, and I can see her now picking her way along the lower walkway with the particular gait we all learn early — weight shifting, always shifting, anticipating the surge before it arrives.

I go to meet her. The lanterns hold their small orange ground against everything above them, which is dark, and steam, and more dark, and the suggestion of a ceiling none of us have ever seen and none of us, if we are being honest with the part of ourselves we don't speak aloud, particularly want to.

The work has always run.

I am only one of the hands that keep it running, and that is enough — that has always, somehow, been enough — to be down here, warm and waterlogged and half-deaf and alive, link over link, in the small light, listening to what the dark underneath the dark chooses, every so often, to let us hear.
