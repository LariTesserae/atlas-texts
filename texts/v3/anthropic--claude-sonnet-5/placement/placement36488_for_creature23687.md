I tend the second drying-loft on Coppice Row, which is to say I am the third in my line to answer to that sentence and will not be the last.

You would find me there now, most likely — up the narrow stair where the rungs have been worn concave by four generations of the same foot-fall, into the long low room under the fern-thatched roof where the pale fiber-sheets hang in their racks like the pressed and translucent ghosts of leaves. The air up here is warmer than the lane, thick with the green-water smell of steeping reed-pulp drifting up through the floor-gaps from my aunt's vat-room below, and dimmer too, the gas turned to its lowest orange bud because the fiber dries better, somehow, in near-dark — or so the women of this loft have always said, and I have never found cause to test it.

I would be doing very little, by the standards of anywhere else. Walking the racks. Touching, with the back of a swollen knuckle, the sheets that are nearly ready, feeling for the particular dry-cool give that means *not yet, but soon*. Turning, perhaps twice an hour, a sheet that has dried unevenly on one face. This is the whole of the work, repeated for the length of a season, and I do it the way my mother did it and her mother before, without hurry, because hurry has never once pulled a sheet dry a day sooner.

My hands would give me away before my face did — thickened at the knuckle, palms soft as something left too long in a basin, nails kept short with the little hook-knife I've carried since I was seven. My eyes, pale and wide even here in the gas-glow, would find you in the doorway before you found me, a shape darker than the dimness, and I would not call out — calling is for the street, and besides my hearing has been going soft and kind at the edges for a decade now, a small grey tide creeping up like the one on the brick outside, and I do not mourn it. Instead I'd lift my hand and tap, against the doorframe, the three short strikes that mean *come up, mind the third step*, and wait to see whether you'd learned enough sign in your time here to answer back, or whether you'd simply climb, the way outsiders do, trusting the stair and not the grammar.

Below us, through floor and wall and the soles of my feet before ever my ears, the wheel my grandmother once tended turns at the rate it has always turned. I would not be listening to it. I would have stopped noticing it sometime in my twenties, the way you stop noticing your own pulse — which is, among my people, not called deafness but called *arrival*, the quiet proof that the place has finished making you into one of its own.

And if you asked me, late, with the gas turned low, why nothing nests in the ferns outside, why no insect rises from the flooded floor beneath your boots — I would not lie to you, but I would not quite answer either. I'd tap it instead, against the rim of the nearest rack, slow enough for a stranger's eye to follow:

*we are what's here. there wasn't room left for two kinds.*

Then I'd turn back to the racks, because a sheet on the third row had been hanging four minutes longer on its damp side than its dry one, and some things, however slow, still want tending.
