I am one of the hands that reads the water before the water has finished deciding what it will do.

You would find me at the third terrace below the eastern lip, where the light comes apron-flat across the basin stones and the cold has a smell like struck flint. I am not old, though my face has begun to take the streambed-seam that makes age unguessable here; I have carried stone since I was tall enough to set it without bracing my knee, and there is a particular cross-hatch of fine white scars across the heel of my right palm from a winter when the channel-liner split and I held willow-bark against the gap with my own hand until someone came with new lashings. I remember the cold of that more than the pain.

My work today, if "today" is even the right frame for something that has no beginning and will have no end I will live to see: a spring closed three nights ago on the upper shelf, and a new one has opened wrong, low and fast, eating into the footing of a cistern wall that my mother's teacher's hands once set. I am not sad about this. I want to be honest about that, because it is the thing visitors most often get wrong about us, mistaking the absence of grief for the absence of feeling, when really it is that grief would require believing the wall was the point, and the wall was never the point. The wall was a sentence in an argument that is still being made. I am simply the next clause.

So: I crouch at the lip with my forearms already wet to the elbow, not from immersion but from the ambient damp that never quite leaves anyone here, and I read the new spring's push against my palm — its temperature, its insistence, the particular grain of pressure that tells a trained hand whether this water intends to keep rising or has already begun, in its slow mineral way, to think about elsewhere. Beside me, not summoned, not announced, two more sets of hands arrive, drawn by the same wordless choreography that drew me. One of them is my brother, though "brother" undersells what the word has to carry here — we did not grow up calling each other anything, we grew up reading each other's wrists. He lays his hand flat against the old wall, then tilts it, twice, toward the lower channel: take it down, not stop it. I agree by not disagreeing, which is its own gesture, the stillness after his hand that means yes.

The eyeless fish in the basin below have already gathered at the place where new water will eventually arrive, fat and patient, as if they read the spring the way we do, with whatever sense they have instead of eyes. I will feed them on the way down, the same motion I use to test current, because for us there has stopped being a meaningful seam between tending the water and tending what lives in it. My second lid — yes, it is real, I will not perform mystery about my own face — drops when I go in to re-set the lower stones, and for the length of that held breath underwater I am exactly as blind and exactly as certain as the fish beside me, feeling for the gap in the wall with the same blunt confidence they feel for the current.

By evening — though evening here is only a slight, grudging dimming of the same shadowless glare, never a true dark — the new channel will be cut, badly, provisionally, already destined for revision. No one will remark on this. We will eat something simple and cold-adjacent in flavor, in a room half of us, and go down into sleep the way the water goes down into stone: without ceremony, assuming morning the way the spring assumes the rock will eventually let it through.

I am not the keeper of this place. I am not its prisoner either. I am the shape it has, so far, worn into one particular set of hands — wide-palmed, scarred, unhurried — and when those hands are done, the water will not pause to notice, and somebody else's wrists will already be answering the question I am still, today, in the bright cold, in the middle of asking.
