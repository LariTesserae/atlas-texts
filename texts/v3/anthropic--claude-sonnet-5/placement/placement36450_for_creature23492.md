I come awake already moving.

Not a transition — there's no soft border between sleep and the next thing here, no lying still while the mind catches up to the body. One moment the dark behind my eyes, the next moment the dark in front of them, and they are nearly the same color, so the only proof I've crossed over is the ache in my shoulder where I curled against a root-bole and the immediate, unwilled sweep of my eyes: up, ahead, down, up again. Old habit, older than thinking. The roar is already there too, underwater-thick, calls stacked on calls, and somewhere in it a stutter — three valleys over, maybe four — that my ears flag and my mind discards before I've consciously heard it as anything. Not close. Not mine to carry.

My hand finds the cord at my hip without my telling it to. Stone blade, freshly struck, still a little rough at the tang where I didn't bother finishing it last night because finishing things is a luxury the green doesn't often grant. I test the edge against my thumb out of habit rather than doubt. Good enough. Everything here only has to be good enough for the time it has left to exist, which is never very long.

The shelter I slept under — four nights now, which is three more than most — sags a little more than it did yesterday. The broad leaves lashed across its frame have started to brown and curl at their edges, and underneath that browning I can already see the thinner green of something new pushing through, uninvited, opportunistic, the way everything here is opportunistic. By tomorrow or the day after this will not be a shelter anymore. It'll be a trellis. The forest doesn't believe in waste any more than it believes in permanence, and I have learned, the slow hard way that this place teaches everything, not to either.

I am small. I have always been glad of it here, in a way I suspect I wouldn't be glad of it anywhere else — my shoulders roll loose in their sockets when I reach overhead for the first branch-rung of the climb out, my feet curl and grip the bark like a second set of hands, and the mottling on my forearms, dark over darker, does exactly what it was shaped by ten thousand grandmothers' worth of not-dying to do: it disappears me into the dapple the instant I stop moving. I learned to stop moving before I learned most other things. A held breath has saved me more times than this blade ever has.

Below, the ground refuses its flatness as it always does — black stone shouldering up through the root-mat in sharp unworn ridges, and I read it without looking, the way you read a face you've known your whole life, because I have walked some version of this loop more times than I have fingers and toes between myself and everyone I've ever traveled with. Not the same ground twice, exactly. A rockfall took the eastern shelf out two seasons back. The vine-choke past the second ridge is new this month, thick enough now that going around costs less than going through. But the *shape* of the loop is old in me, worn into muscle if not into earth, and my feet take its next forty steps without asking my eyes to confirm them.

There is a curve in the corridor ahead — small, unnecessary, the kind of thing a stranger would correct on a map and call wasted distance. I take it anyway, swinging three steps wide of a shelf of cold-glowing fungus that needs nothing from me and offers nothing back, not light worth the name, not warmth, nothing but a faint chill green like a held thought. I couldn't tell you why my feet do this. Nobody who taught me the loop could tell me either, when I was small enough to ask. They only showed me the curve, the way it had been shown to them, and somewhere far enough back the asking stopped and the walking-around simply became one more thing the body does without troubling the mind for permission.

Twice in my life I've heard the call that goes with that habit — the badly repeating syllables, the echo trying to be a word and not quite arriving. Once as a child, once three years back, alone, checking the weir. Both times I went still the way I'm trained to go still for a falling branch or a closing carpet of small bodies, and both times nothing came of the stillness except the stillness itself, and a kind of weight in my chest I still haven't found the right shape to set down. I don't speak about it often. None of us do. It isn't fear, or not only fear. It's closer to the deference you'd show a much older animal whose intentions you have decided, collectively, gently, not to test.

The rain starts before I reach the stream — not a warning, just a sudden hammering nine layers up that takes whole minutes to become anything at all down here, and by the time the first true drops find my shoulders the upper roar has already dropped back to its baseline drone. I keep moving through it. Stopping for weather here is like stopping for breathing; the weather doesn't stop for you and won't notice if you do.

At the weir, three of the wood lashings have already torn loose where the current's chewed a new channel into the bank overnight. I crouch at the bend, feel the cool recent-wet of the mud come up through my feet, and start resetting the stakes a half-body-length downstream where the water still wants to be funneled. My hands know this from finger to wrist; it isn't a memory so much as a shape they're capable of without my supervision, the same shape my mother's hands made, and hers before her, generation folded into generation until the *weir* stopped being a thing anyone owned and became simply a thing the river and our hands do together, over and over, neither side ever quite winning.

Somewhere above and behind me, a branch gives way under accumulated weight that finally exceeded what green wood ought to hold, and crashes down through four strata before it stops, and nothing about my body so much as flinches at the sound, though my ears clock its distance and direction and file it as *not mine to carry* before the echo's even finished.

I will not sleep in this stretch of forest again. By the time I cycle back — if I cycle back, if the loop hasn't reshaped itself entirely by then around some new rockfall or fresh choke of vine — this weir will be elsewhere, this shelter will be a trellis gone fully to leaf, and I will be three valleys distant or further, carrying nothing of this place forward except what's already folded into my hands and feet and the practiced curve of my unasked-for detour.

That's enough. It has always had to be enough. Out here, where nothing finishes and nothing is owed permanence, the only thing worth keeping is the knowing of *how* — how to reset a stake, how to read a ridge, how to go still at the right pitch of sound and start moving again before the green has finished closing the gap behind you.

I sling the last lashing tight, test it once with my full weight, and move on before the water's even settled into its new course.
