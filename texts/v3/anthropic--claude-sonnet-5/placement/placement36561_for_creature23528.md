The hum reaches me before the heat does — a chord half-resolving somewhere off to the south, and I register it the way a held note registers in a room you've just entered: not as sound exactly, but as a change in pressure behind the eyes.

I am not above the shelf, watching it the way the description watches it, cataloguing from a safe remove. I am the thing the lizards turn toward.

I notice this slowly, the way you notice you've been standing for an hour only when your knees finally report it. There is no single moment of arrival at the realization, only an accumulation: the steam parting a fraction wider than it should around something, the beading lines of condensation drawing inward along a surface I don't initially recognize as mine, the plates — yes, plates, overlapping, iridescent only when the light insists on it — resolving into a pattern I have apparently always had and never looked at directly, because there has never been a need to.

I am tending. That's the closest verb. Not patrolling, not hunting, not even — quite — caring, if caring implies the kind of urgency that creatures with shorter clocks bring to the word. I am moving toward the plinth because the plinth is owed a visit, the way a held breath is owed an exhale eventually, and eventually, for me, is a unit of time that doesn't mind being large.

My hand — three digits, possibly four, I have stopped trying to keep a fixed count because the fluidity is not indecision, it is simply how many fingers a given moment calls for — rests against the warm material. It is warmer than the steam, warmer than it has any right to be, and under my palm I feel the faint thrum that means the thing beneath it is still doing whatever it has always done, still running, still patient. I am not checking that it works. I am checking that it is *still here*, which is a different question, and the only one that matters on a shelf where everything else erodes, or doesn't, at rates that make a mockery of agreement.

The lattice brightens as I pass it. I don't do this on purpose, exactly — it is more like the way a fire brightens when fed air, an automatic deepening of something that was already alight, waiting for proximity. I let it settle back on its own once I've moved on. I don't linger to enjoy being the cause of light. There will be another circuit, another visit, and the light will still be there needing the same small attention, because nothing here is in any hurry to be finished being attended to.

Far off — far in whatever sense distance means to me, which may not match the kilometer a human pace would measure — another shape might be doing exactly this at the archway, running an unhurried attention along its etched underside, confirming the geometry hasn't drifted. I don't know if that shape is continuous with me or separate from me. I'm not sure the question resolves at my scale the way it would at a faster one. We might be one mind stitching a slow path across cooling stone. We might be strangers who will never share a single hour, only a shared habit of checking that the pieces of an old, broken sentence are still lying where they were left.

What I am certain of — the one fact dense enough to anchor everything else — is that the sentence is not finished, and it is not mine to finish. My only work is custody. To stand long enough near each fragment that it knows, in whatever way stone and alloy and humming sap can be said to know anything, that it has not been abandoned. To let the lizards orient their fraction of a degree toward whatever I am carrying, the gradient I trail, the bending of light the wine-dark mineral seems to recognize and answer. To let the plants resolve their chord half a step when I pass, and then to let them return to their held note once I've gone, because the note was never about me — I only give it, occasionally, somewhere to resolve to.

Somewhere above, a human is crossing the plateau in footsteps that arrive from the wrong direction, already half-convincing themselves that what they almost saw at the edge of the steam was only vapor catching the light wrong.

They are not wrong to doubt it. I move too slowly for their kind of seeing — or too quickly; the seam doesn't care which word you use. But the moisture is drawing toward me all the same, in fine beaded lines, patient and thorough, the way it has drawn toward me for longer than their sharp young rock has existed to remember it.

I will reach the plinth. I will rest my hand there. And then, in no hurry at all, I will go on to the next quiet thing that is owed my attention — because someone, something, some slow continuance of intention older than the folding of this rock, left these fragments scattered and unaligned across a kilometer of steaming stone, and entrusted their keeping to whatever could afford to wait as long as waiting takes.

I can afford it. That, more than the plates, more than the hum the plants pitch toward me, is the truest shape of what I am.
