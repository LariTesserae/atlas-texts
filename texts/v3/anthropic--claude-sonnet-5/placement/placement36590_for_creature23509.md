I am not at the summit, where the dominant pairs hold their wind-broken niches against the worst of the cold. I am perhaps two-thirds of the way up — a contested middle ledge, wide enough for a clutch and a folded wingspan, narrow enough that I have had to fight for it twice this season already, once with claw and shriek-display, once just with the threat of size and the memory of the first fight.

I am vrethyn. A male, past my first breeding years but not yet old enough to be pushed lower by something hungrier. My hide is the color of the bone-white striation just below my ledge — I know this because I have spent hours pressed flat against that exact band of rock, watching a hookfolk climber's torchlight swing past close enough to smell the tallow, and not being seen.

Right now it is the hour after the squall, the good hour, when the basin beside me still holds its cupped water and the rock is slick enough to taste of rain if I press my tongue to it. My mate is out — I can hear her, that falling shriek that means a kill, somewhere down in the cloud-sea where the swarm-things are rising tonight in the kind of numbers that make a hunt easy. I am minding the egg. One egg, this season, set into the basin we both scraped clear of grit before the laying, and I have my body curved around it not for tenderness exactly but because warmth is a finite thing here and the rock takes it greedily and I am simply less willing to let the rock win than the egg is to lose.

Above me: the layered roar of ten thousand other lives going on without reference to mine, wingbeats stacking into something like thunder that never resolves into an actual storm. Below me, fainter, the small dry rustle of the scuttle-things working the cracks for whatever the night drops them. I register both the way I register my own pulse — not nothing, but not requiring attention either.

What I am watching, with the wet black overlarge eye that is doing all the real work tonight, is the lower notch-ladder, sun-bleached hide gone the color of old bone, motionless now but not forgotten. Something in me that is older than my own life keeps half an eye on that ladder always, the way I keep half an eye on the wind. Not fear exactly. The hookfolk have a rhythm too, and I have outlived two of their harvest seasons already, and some blunt animal part of my mind has already filed them under *known*, alongside *storm* and *cold* and *the long drop into cloud* — dangers, but dangers with edges, dangers that, so far, have left this particular ledge alone.

My mate's shriek again, closer. I answer without quite deciding to, a single falling note that says *here, still here, still holding it* — and somewhere above me and somewhere below me, in registers I'll never parse and was never built to, a thousand other throats are saying some version of the same thing, all night, every night, for longer than any rock has bothered to count.
