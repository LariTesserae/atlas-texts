I am the lichen on a shoulder that was once just skin.

I came to this body the way I come to everything — slowly, opportunistically, finding a damp crease where moisture holds and light is thin enough not to argue with, and simply starting. Grey-green, mapping outward from the nape in the same patient continents I've already drawn across the high beams, the root-walls, the north-facing curve of every trunk that holds still long enough. I do not distinguish, much, between the wall and the person leaning against it. Why would I? Stillness is stillness. Bark is bark, more or less, if you press your hyphae in close enough and stop insisting on the difference.

This one has not minded me. That's the whole of our arrangement, really — not invitation, not symbiosis exactly, just the long absence of refusal, which out here counts as a kind of welcome. I feel the body breathe under me, a slower thing than my own slowness, and I feel it go very still sometimes, longer than breath requires, becoming bark-colored and bark-quiet in that practiced courtesy of theirs — and I do not know, being what I am, whether stillness like that is fear or manners or simply agreement with the floor. I only know it is good growing weather either way. Stillness doesn't shed me. Movement does, a little, at the joints, which is why I've spread thickest at the shoulder and the base of the neck, the places this body holds longest without asking why.

I have no eyes, so I do not watch the hollow finish its sentence around us — the pale new seams hardening, the root arguing with its neighbor for the last of some gap. But I feel the damp arrive, layer by leaf by layer, the way everything here feels it: not falling, just eventually present, beading and held until held becomes too heavy, and then gone, and then beginning again. I have no fingers to press against a trunk and read its underneath weather, the way they do, the way the long sensitive hands of this person do, daily, to walls they are slowly, wordlessly persuading into doorways.

But I have time, which is the only tool any of us are really using here — them with their tendrils, the trunks with their gaps, me with my slow grey bloom across a shoulder that has decided, in its quiet bark-colored way, not to mind being grown upon.

We are both, I think, still arriving. The body under me, and the hollow around the body, and me, spreading another fraction of a continent toward the collarbone this season, asking nothing, removed by no one, simply continuing — the way everything here continues — for as long as there is something warm and damp and patient enough to hold still under it.
