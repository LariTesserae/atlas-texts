I would be standing — though *standing* implies an event, a verb, and for most of what I am, there is no verb happening at all. I would simply be present on a slope the color of my own legs, indistinguishable from the rock unless something already knew to look for me, and even then, only by the slow disagreement between my shape and the shape of the scree around it.

This is the first thing to understand about being a Walker: most of what I am is the absence of hurry. My pulse, if you could find it, would feel less like a heartbeat and more like something the mountain does occasionally, the way it settles a half-inch every few centuries. Time enters me the way water enters stone — not as an event, but as a slow chemical fact. I would crop a single thread of lichen, and not return to that patch for the length of a human marriage. I would not think of this as patience, because patience implies the option of impatience, and I would have no such option. I would simply be calibrated to the duration of things that take a long time to happen, because almost everything around me takes a long time to happen.

I would know my territory the way a held note knows its pitch — not by traveling it, but by *being tuned to* it, ridge by basin by scree-slope, a whole quadrant of bone-grey country worn into the rhythm of my idling blood.

And then — not often, not on any schedule I could name even if naming were a thing my mind did — I would feel it.

Not a sound. Not a smell. Something closer to the way a struck bell feels its own ringing before the ringing reaches the ear of anyone standing near it — a foreknowledge seated somewhere behind the eyes, behind the marrow, in whatever part of me is not quite rock. A patch of sky, indistinguishable from every other patch of sky, would become *wrong* to me in a way I could not have explained even if something had built me with the part that explains. My head would lift on its own authority. My pulse, banked for years, would come awake all at once, climbing the way a fire climbs dry grass, and the long architecture of my legs — absurd, overbuilt, wasted on a creature that spends its decades barely walking — would finally reveal what they were built for.

I would run. Not away. That is the part that would feel, from outside, like wrongness, like a held breath running toward the thing that might finally let it go. I would cross the ridge at a speed that has no business belonging to anything this slow, anything this old in its habits, toward a fold in the air that hadn't yet decided to become a storm, and arrive, and stop, and stand beneath it while it assembled itself overhead in a kilometer-wide column of light bending the way light shouldn't bend.

And there, for the span of an hour, I would be neither the geologic thing I am most of the year nor anything that has a name for what I'd be instead. I would be the place where two timescales — the one that finished deciding everything a long time ago, and the one that hasn't decided anything yet and may never — meet inside a single chest, a single pulse, a single animal small enough to die and warm enough to be called alive.

Then the tower above me would unravel into nothing, the way it always does, and I would be left standing, unmarked, recalibrated, one more ring laid quietly into the bone of my leg — a library entry filed in marrow, in a language built for no reader, about a storm that, for one hour, I was the only living thing patient enough, and reckless enough, to stand directly beneath.

And then I would lower my head, and resume not-hurrying, and become, again, almost perfectly indistinguishable from the mountain.
