The pause before the answering call — that's the part of me that's still listening when you arrive.

I am settled into the fork where a canopy tree split decades before I was felted, a crook wide enough to take my whole low weight without complaint, and I have been still long enough that the water beading on the bark beside me has found its own slow path around the shape of me, as if I were simply more terrain to route past. Which I am. Which is the whole of what I am.

You won't see me first. You'll hear the give of the branch under something heavier than wind, and you'll look up into gray-green dusk that swallows edges, and what you'll find — if you find anything — is a burl. A dark, low, mossed irregularity that might have always been part of the tree. My eyes are open. They have been open the whole time, dark and wide and built for exactly this much light and no more, and they are doing the thing they do, which is not watching so much as accounting — your shape added to the tally of shapes currently moving through my fork of the hollow, filed somewhere behind the slower, steadier tally of fungus, water, root, kin.

I am not deciding whether you are a threat. That calculation finished itself generations before I was born and the answer, passed down through nothing but the simple fact of still being alive to pass it down, was: probably not, and even if so, not worth spending the stillness on. So I keep still for my own reasons, which are mostly about the grazing, not you.

The grazing: there is a bracket fungus at my left shoulder-height, a frilled gray-white shelf jutting from the bark, and it has been fruiting for three days at a rate I can feel as much as see, a faint give in its texture when the wind shifts and disturbs the spore-fall. I have not touched it yet. I am letting it ripen a little longer, the way the whole back half of my life has been organized around letting things ripen a little longer — there is no version of patience available to me that isn't also just metabolism, just the unhurried chemistry of a body that drinks through its own hide and cannot be rushed into wanting more than it currently needs.

When I do graze it will be a small motion, head dipping, lips working at just the fruiting cap and leaving the mat beneath intact, because stripping it bare would mean walking a longer circuit next season to find another, and the circuit I have is the circuit I know, root-tunnel to deadwood tenement to this fork and back, worn into a kind of certainty by however many years I've been doing it. I don't know the number. I have never needed the number. I have the route, and the route is enough continuity for one slow-blooded animal to hold.

On my back, in the moss that has rooted into my coat since before I can remember being without it, something small is moving — one of the pale grid-walking grazers crossing from one shoulder-blade to the other on whatever business occupies something that size. I don't feel it exactly. I feel the hollow's general traffic continuing on schedule, on my schedule, which by now isn't separable from the hollow's at all. My fur is somebody else's road. My slow circuit between fork and tunnel and deadwood is somebody else's spore-dispersal, sneezed loose and recaught fifty meters on. I have never once acted with the colonies in mind. I have only ever moved toward food and shade and the wet crooks where my own kind has, for longer than either of us can account for, raised its slow children — and the moving has been enough. The hollow has shaped itself around my not-quite-noticing the same way water shapes itself around stone.

Somewhere off in the dim, past the edge of where my eyes give up resolving shape from shadow, a call sounds — short, low, a single dropped note. I don't answer immediately. There is no rule that says immediately. I let the gap sit, let it fill with the tick of water finding new bark-paths, the hiss of ten thousand small respirations going on without me, the soft working of my own jaw at the fungus I've finally decided is ripe enough — and only then, unhurried, I send the note back down the dark to whoever asked: *still here. still this one. still in this fork, holding this small grazed garden, for as long as the garden and the fork will have me.*

That's the whole of what I have to say, most days. It's enough. The hollow was never waiting for more than that — only for something to keep saying it, slowly, for as long as the saying could be kept up.
