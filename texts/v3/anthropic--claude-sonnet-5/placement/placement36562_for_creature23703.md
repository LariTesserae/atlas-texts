The shift-bell hasn't rung yet, but I'm already awake, lying still on the pallet and listening to the building breathe — the particular creak the north strut makes when the temperature differential hits a certain threshold, a sound my father taught me to listen for before he taught me my own name, practically. Moss-smell and rust-smell and the cold mineral tang of condensation gathering on the pipe above my head, fat drop by fat drop, the rhythm of it almost a clock if you didn't know better than to trust clocks here.

I am forty-some years into a body built for this — narrow through the shoulders going broad, the stoop already settling into my spine from three decades of low pipe-joints, palms gone the permanent dark of furnace-soot that no scrubbing lifts anymore, a second set of fingerprints laid down in char. My eyes are the washed hazel that does nothing in daylight and everything in a furnace's orange throb. I am a rail-walker's daughter and a rail-walker myself, third generation, and what I know isn't taught so much as *worn into* me, the way the moss has worn itself into the joints: which gantries hum wrong before they fail, which pitch of bird-cry means the cloud ceiling's about to drop weather through the gaps, how to count a street under my breath before I commit my weight to it.

There is a landing on the eleven-tier stair that is also, simultaneously, a landing eleven floors below, and I cross it twice a day without comment, the way you'd duck a low beam. There is a street two switchbacks from my door that I have not walked since autumn, because it's still thinking, and it would be rude to interrupt it.

My shadow, this past year, has started arriving a half-second before I do. I haven't mentioned it to anyone. There's nothing to mention. It's just an extra fact about me now, filed next to the cough I've got from twenty winters of furnace-smoke, the ache in my left knee, the grey coming in at my temple. The mountain is still becoming the mountain. I am still becoming whatever this is. Neither of us is in a hurry about it.

Somewhere above the permanent grey ceiling, peaks I have never seen and never will are still cooling into their final shapes. Below me, the furnace-stacks are beginning their long inhale toward the morning heat-climb. The birds are already going, that overlapping unbroken cry layered into the metal-groan and pipe-creak until you stop trying to tell which sound belongs to the rock and which to the wings.

I get up. I layer coat over coat over canvas. I go out to read the gantries before the shift-bell, the way my mother did, the way her mother did, the way I assume — without bitterness, the way you assume weather — someone after me will keep doing, on this ridge, in this dim amber-grey light, for as long as the rock keeps deciding what it wants to be.
