I would not be one of the grazers — that much settles fast, almost before I've finished imagining the ground-level smell of crushed bromeliad. There's a peace in their register, but it's the peace of never looking up, and I find I want to look up. I want the part of this place that knows it's building something, even faintly, even without the word for it.

So: mid-canopy. Forty meters down from the misted crown-tops, in the dim half-light where the grazer-herds slow to browse more carefully and something unseen keeps its patience at the threshold thirty meters off whatever path I'm on.

I am narrow-bodied, long in the limb, membrane folded tight against my ribs right now because I'm climbing, not gliding — one true thumb and one heel-thumb on each hand, neither of which I'm looking at, because you don't look at your hands here, you look at where you're going, and your hands simply know the wet bark the way your tongue knows the inside of your own mouth.

I am tending something. That's the detail I keep returning to — not hunting, not fleeing, but tending: a particular fungal shelf on a particular dead trunk that my den has favored for longer than I have memory of favoring it, and this morning's whole task is to strip back three fingers' width of crowding vine so the shelf gets the light-coin that falls there at this hour and no other. My den-kin are doing the same thing along forty meters of trunk in either direction, each of us minding our own small patch of inherited intention, and none of us would call it gardening, because we don't have a word that separates "garden" from "live."

What I have instead of a word is a foot pressed flat against the branch, feeling for the four-click pattern that means *stillness now* — not hearing it, the way you'd hear a voice, but receiving it as a thing the wood itself does, structural and total, indistinguishable from the tower settling except that when it comes, every threadwalker in the crown goes motionless at once, mid-task, hand still half-closed around a vine, and we wait out whatever passed thirty meters off in the lichen-colored dark, and then, when the wood goes quiet again, we simply resume — no alarm carried forward, no grief performed, because the den that lost someone today will gather its rememberers in its own quiet, later, in its own register, and the gardening continues regardless, the way it always has, the way the towers kept growing through every flood that the bone-pale stone below still remembers and we don't.

I think that's the truest answer: not the grazer who never looks up, not the lichen-stiffened old thing gone glacial at the misted crown, not the gray-gold crust pressing centimeter by patient centimeter into virgin stone — though I feel kinship with all three, the way you feel kinship with your own hand and your own slowest, oldest relative and the ground you stand on.

I am the one in the middle register, hands full of wet bark and small intention, feeling the building's voice through the soles of my feet, gardening in the dark because that's what was always done here, by something, in some form, long before there was a me to do it — and will go on being done, in this same unhurried hush, long after.
