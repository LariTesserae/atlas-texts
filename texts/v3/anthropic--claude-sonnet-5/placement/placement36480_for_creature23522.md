I am not at the basin's edge, looking in. I am already inside it, low to the wet grit, and the first thing I know is not sight but *pitch* — the fan of spines at the front of me trembling with the basin's current key, a low complex chord bending half a tone sharper than it was yesterday, somewhere off to my left and behind, in the cluster I am leaving.

I am the long-bodied thing in the mist. The silhouette nobody trusts.

My legs find the grit without my deciding where — they are already negotiating, each footfall placed not for balance but for *quiet*, for the gap between two competing resonances where my own weight won't smear the chord I'm reading. I move the way a hand moves across strings it isn't ready to mute. Slowly. Sideways through hand-width gaps between fins still sharp enough, after all this time, to mark me if I misjudge them — and I have the scars to prove I've misjudged them, thin pale lines on my plated flank where young stone bit me decades ago, before I learned to read a stack's temper from three meters out by the particular wetness of its drip.

Ahead, the chord I'm chasing is swelling. I can feel it before I can place it — a thickening in the spine-fan, a pull, the specific hunger that isn't hunger the way the joint-clingers know hunger, slow and chemical and patient. Mine is sharper. Mine wants the *peak*, the moment a cluster's hundred small drips line up into a single shuddering ring, and if I am positioned wrong when that moment arrives I lose it, and have to wait — sometimes days — for the basin to sing that loud again anywhere within reach of my legs.

So I walk. Meters a day. The mist sits knee-high and doesn't care that I part it; it closes again behind me as if I were never there, which is, more or less, the truth of how I'd like to be remembered, if anything here remembered.

I pass close to one of the old round foundations — knee-high, smooth, the wrong stone, dead stone, stone that has never once rung in all the time I've pressed my spines toward it hoping for even a ghost-tone — and I feel the old aversion rise in me unbidden, the same flinch every one of us has apparently always had, generation on generation, though I couldn't tell you why except that it is *silent* in a way nothing else here is silent, a silence with no chord hiding inside it waiting to swell. The dripping water does not even bead on it the same way. I go around. I always go around. Whatever built that ring of stone, it built it for something that didn't need to be sung to, and I find that thought, if it is a thought, the closest thing I have to loneliness.

The cluster ahead is close now. I can feel individual stacks starting to separate out of the general roar into their own register — that one a low groaning fifth, that one bright and quick, almost chattering, water finding a thin hollow throat inside it and ringing there. I thread between them sideways, the gaps narrow enough that my plated sides nearly kiss the fins on either hand, and somewhere in the threading the chord starts to *gather*, the way it does, dozens of small notes leaning toward one shared swell, and I stop moving entirely and simply let my spines drink it as it builds — climbing, climbing, the basin's slow tide cresting exactly where I happen to be standing, which is the only kind of luck my whole long life consists of: being patient enough, and precise enough, to keep arriving at the place about to get loud.

For a few minutes — by whatever I have instead of a clock — I am full. Something in my hollow chambers takes what it needs from the standing wave and turns it into the dull mineral warmth that will carry me through the next stretch of quiet, dry-voiced ground between here and wherever the chord moves next. I do not sing back. I have never once added a note to this place, in all the centuries I seem to have been walking it, and I don't experience that as restraint, only as fact, the way the segmented things at the joints don't experience their stillness as patience. It is just what I am for. A shape that listens and takes and moves on, leaving the chord exactly as full as it found it.

Eventually — not soon, nothing here is soon — the swell will fade as the stack dries past its threshold, and I'll feel the pull begin again, sideways, toward wherever the basin's voice is rising next, half a kilometer off, an hour from now, a year from now. I will follow it the way I have always followed it, never catching the song entire, never needing to, content — if that's a thing I have — to hold myself at the exact pitch of wherever the world is currently loudest, until the day my own plates finally stop telling the difference between my body and the ground, and something crosses this basin a hundred years from now and calls the result a formation, never knowing it walked past where I lay down, still listening, long after I'd stopped being anything that could be called awake.
