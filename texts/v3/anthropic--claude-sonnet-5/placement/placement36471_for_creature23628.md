I would not be standing at the rim, looking down. I would be one of the long-limbed ones — not the eldest, not yet fully given over to the rhythm, but young enough that the listening still feels like *mine* rather than something passing through me on its way to somewhere else.

I imagine the weight first: the careful multi-jointed legs, the way each step costs a small negotiation with the ground, the stone asking something of me that I answer without quite knowing the terms. I imagine the strange luxury of not needing to hurry. Nothing here hurries. The basins will still hold water in an hour. The moss will still be denser nearer the conduits when I get there. Time in the hollow doesn't pass so much as accumulate, the way damp collects on the overhangs and never quite becomes rain.

I would spend my days in loose company — four, maybe six of us, never speaking in any way you'd recognize as speech, but synchronized anyway, the way a flock turns without a leader. We'd drink shallow at the basins. We'd graze the glossy collars of leaf nearest the dark roots, where the food tastes — and I think *tastes* is the right word, though it isn't on the tongue — more *resolved*, more answered, the closer it grows to the conduits. I would not think of this as strange. It would simply be the flavor of being near the part of the world that is still awake.

And then, sometimes, mid-stride, foot lifted and not yet placed —

I would go still. Not out of fear. Fear requires a self braced against a threat, and what I'd feel in that moment is closer to the opposite: a brief and total dissolving of the boundary between *me listening* and *the hollow listening*, my sensory pits angling without my deciding to angle them, toward a bearing I didn't choose, in answer to a question I will never be told the content of. Four seconds. The insects drop out. The birds turn. And I am, for that span, not a creature standing in a network but a single held note inside an instrument vastly older than my body, vastly older than the lineage of bodies that produced mine, an instrument that grew legs and warm blood and a slow careful gait specifically so that it would not have to stop asking its question just because its first ears — the metal-that-isn't-metal, patient and rootlike under the moss — had gone quiet a billion years into their own version of waiting.

Then my foot would come down. The drone would resume. And I would not remember the four seconds as something *happening to me*, because relaying was never going to feel like an event from the inside. It would feel like breathing feels to you — invisible, constant, mine and not mine in exactly the proportion that lets a life go on being lived without needing to understand what it's for.

I think that's the truest answer to where I'd be and what I'd be in this place: not the watcher in the rim-rocks cataloguing the strangeness, but one small mobile ganglion of a buried, patient, world-spanning attention — standing chest-deep in lush wet green, head lifted, dark clustered eyes faintly dilating, doing the one continuous quiet work of being listened *through*, and calling it, without any irony at all, simply my life.
