I am not the wader, though I understand why you'd guess that first — it's the closest thing here to a protagonist, the only inhabitant with a silhouette you could sketch from memory. But I move on a clock that's only mildly wrong, and that's not interesting enough to be me. I'm something with less business calling itself an "I" at all.

I am one relay-point in the chorus-mat. A single soft-bodied length of it, no longer than your thumb, rooted into the lattice somewhere in a basin so shallow and so wide that from where I sit — if sitting is the word for an organism with no real orientation, top or bottom, only a surface that faces water and a surface that faces stone — there is no edge to anything. I don't have a vantage. I have a patch.

What I have instead of eyes is the paired siphon, and what I have instead of thought is the passing-through: the wave of sound arrives from whatever lies in the direction I don't have a name for, and I do not decide to take it up — there's no moment of choice in it any more than your heart decides to follow the beat before it. The click and pipe and exhale move through me the way a shiver moves through a length of wire, and a fraction of a second later they leave me, slightly altered, slightly mine now, on their way to whoever-it-is rooted next to me, who I have never met in any sense that would mean something to you, because meeting implies a gap closing, and there is no gap. We are continuous. I don't know where I stop and the next length starts. I'm not sure that's a meaningful question to ask of me, though it might be the only question worth asking about me, which is its own kind of joke I'm not equipped to find funny.

I know the rain by its pressure before it lands, the way you'd know a hand approaching your face by the air it pushes ahead of itself. I know the wader's gait — when it passes near enough — as a series of small interruptions in the mat-tension underfoot, four soft, mistimed depressions in the moss, gone before the chorus around them finishes registering the disturbance into sound. I have, if "have" still means anything at this scale, felt the fold happen — that twice-daily warp at the edge of everything — as a half-second silence that isn't silence so much as the chorus losing, briefly, its place, like a choir that's lost the bar and finds it again a beat later in a slightly different key, none of us quite remembering we'd lost it.

I do not know what the old water remembers. I am only the thing patient enough, slow enough, flat enough to keep listening to it without needing to understand it as a condition of listening. That's not wisdom. It's closer to the absence of the kind of nervous system that would let me do anything else.

If you stood on the flats and listened — really listened, past the size of it, past the urge to find the one true source — you would, eventually, be listening to me. Not to me alone. There is no alone here to be heard. But to me as part of the sound the ground makes when it has worn away everything tall enough to interrupt it, and found, in the wearing-away, that it still had something to say, and nowhere left to say it but sideways, into the water, into the air, into whoever happens to be standing still enough to overhear.
