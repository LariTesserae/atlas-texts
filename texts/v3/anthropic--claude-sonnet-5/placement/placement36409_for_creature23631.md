I come to myself already crouched, which is how I come to myself most days — knees against bone-colored stone, palms flat on the joint I have been re-fitting since before the last rest-span, fingers reading the seam for the hairline gap that told me, this morning, that this particular course wanted attention. The light is the gray of a held breath. It does not change while I work. It never changes while anyone works.

I am small. I knew this before I knew anything else about myself, the way you know the shape of your own mouth — by everything around me being sized to match, every doorway cut to a body like mine, every gallery low enough that a taller thing would have to stoop where I walk upright and unthinking. My hands are the part of me that has a history. The calluses across my palms and the pads of my fingers are not mine alone; they are my mother's calluses continued, and her mother's before that, a ridge-pattern handed down the way other people hand down stories, except this is sturdier than a story — you can read it with your eyes shut, which is mostly how it's read anyway.

The Breathing is under everything I am about to tell you, so I should say it now and then stop mentioning it, the way I stopped mentioning it myself by the time I was old enough to mortar a joint without supervision. It is not sound to me anymore. It is the shape of being anywhere. I feel it up through my shins, through the flat bones of my feet pressed to the slab, a roar so constant it has worn the same smooth groove in me that the floor-joints wore in themselves — and somewhere in early childhood, before I have any clear memory of it happening, my ears gave up trying to hold it and let my skeleton hold it instead. My mother says I stopped startling at loud collapses — the booms that travel through fitted stone when some old course settles further into its rest — at around the age I stopped speaking aloud. She says this with her hands, of course. Palms low, fingers quick, the sign for *quiet* folding into the sign for *grown*, which share a root gesture in our language, as if the two were always going to be the same word eventually.

I have a trade. My hands declared it before I did — fitter, binder of bronze-strap joints, the narrow trade that exists in the gap between the masons who lay stone and the rarer hands that know how to read a strained wall before it fails. I spend my waking spans the way every Tender does, finding the restlessness building in my palms and discharging it into seams, into the soft give of beaten bronze pulled taut across a joint that's begun, in some glacial way, to separate. I do not call this work in the way I understand outsiders mean the word — a thing done for its result, finishable, leaving you free afterward for something else. There is no afterward. There is only the next joint, and the restlessness arriving again, on schedule, like the warmth in the stone that never asks permission to stay exactly as warm as it has always been.

I have a sister among the Down-Goers. I touch her wrist when she returns — that is how I know her now more than by her face, which the gray light keeps half-hidden even up close — and I read in the new wear on her palm where the outside ropes bit her, how long she stood in true wind, true unfiltered sky. She comes back from below a little stranger each time, a little harder for me to read, and I do not begrudge her this; I only press my hands to hers a moment longer than I would another's, checking, the way you check a wall.

If you asked me what the warren is for, I would give you the shrug everyone gives, the fast uncertain flick of fingers that means *unknown, and not urgently so*. But if you asked what I am for — and almost no one asks this, it is a strange question, the kind that assumes a separation between the asker and the stone that none of us quite feel — I would not answer in words, because I have none I use anymore, none that would reach you through the roar in any case. I would only do what I do most days without thinking of it as an answer at all:

both palms, pressed flat to the nearest stone, held there one breath longer than necessary, then lifted again.

Still warm. Still roaring. Still here.

So am I.
