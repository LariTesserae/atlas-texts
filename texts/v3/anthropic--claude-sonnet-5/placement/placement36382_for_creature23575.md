The first thing I am aware of is the cable underfoot, and the cable is warm.

I notice this before I notice that I am noticing anything — before there is a "where" or a "who," there is only the warmth traveling up through the sole of a boot that has gone soft and shapeless with years of standing water, and a faint, specific shudder running north to south along the trough beside the causeway. The shudder means something. I don't yet know what I am, but some part of me already knows that the shudder, three pulses close together and then a long fourth, is the sound — no, not sound, never sound exactly — that a relay makes two structures away when it's about to give out.

So: I am a Tender. Of course I am. There was no other shape this place would let me take.

I straighten — my knees complain, the particular complaint of joints that have spent decades in cold and wet, a complaint so familiar I register it the way I'd register breathing — and I look out at where I am. Low ridges run off in every direction, the collapsed spine of some old pipe-gallery making a soft contour to my left, slumped and grown over with that grey mineral-fed moss that doesn't care whether anything sees it grow. The sky sits close and corrugated and gives no light of its own; what light there is comes up out of the Works themselves, sodium-orange pooling under a distant gantry, a blue-white flicker stuttering somewhere past three or four structures I can't name the original purpose of. Bone-colored concrete underfoot, streaked black-green. Water everywhere, ankle-shallow, skinned with a sheen that holds the orange glow like something half-remembering what reflection is for.

The roar is total. It has been total for so long that I don't experience it as sound anymore — I experience it as the medium I move through, the way a fish doesn't experience water as wet. It swells now, somewhere in the early part of its slow tidal cycle, and I feel the swell more than hear it: a pressure change low in my chest, a tightening across the eardrum that gave up discriminating frequencies before I was fully grown. Beneath it, the smaller rhythm — drip, trickle, the click of a relay firing for no one in some control house I'll never enter — registers as texture, the grain of the world rather than an event in it.

My hands are the part of me that is most awake. Calloused past the point where the cold troubles them, they want the handrail beside me, and when I lay my palm flat against it I feel the structure speaking in its only language: a tap-pattern, faint, traveling from somewhere south. Not words. A condition. *Rising. Fourth trough. Within the hour.* I don't translate this so much as receive it the way you receive your own pulse — it simply arrives, already meaning what it means.

I don't ask myself why I'm tending it. The question doesn't fully form, the way "why do you breathe" doesn't fully form for most people most of the time. There is a valve four hundred meters out along this causeway that wants closing before the tide of the roar reaches its next trough, and I am already turning toward it, feet finding the broken concrete by feel, by the specific give and slick of each slab, because eyes this dim-adapted are not for watching where you're going so much as for confirming, after the fact, that the body already knew.

Somewhere past the visible structures — past the last gantry silhouette, out where the basin opens into a dark I've never crossed all the way through — the roar might, if I walked far enough and stayed tired enough, resolve into that long stretched vowel people speak of without fear, the way you'd mention weather. I have heard it twice in my life. Both times I kept walking. Both times it dissolved back into roar before I could decide whether it had been the Works finally saying something, or only my own exhausted ear doing what exhausted ears do.

I don't think the difference matters to the valve.

I reach it. I close it. Somewhere under the orange light, in the warm cable's quiet insistence, something that was rising stops rising. My knees ache. The roar swells, recedes. I straighten and lay a hand against the rail again, listening with my bones for whatever comes next, because something always comes next, and I am, for as long as this lasts, the part of the Works that notices.
