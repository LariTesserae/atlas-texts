I am standing on the windward corner of Platform Seven, where the railing's been worn smooth by forty years of gloved hands finding the same grip, and my knees are bent before I've even decided to bend them. The deck is doing its slow work beneath my boots — not rolling, rigs don't roll, but breathing, a faint give and recover with each surge that passes under the legs — and some part of my spine that stopped consulting me a long time ago is already correcting for it, has been correcting for it since I was nineteen and came up the ladder for my first shift with ears that still worked the way ears are supposed to.

My name doesn't matter much out here. What matters is that I'm a rigger, third generation, and I have my mother's hands already starting — three fingers on the left that don't quite lie flat anymore, souvenirs of a chain-tooth that caught me two winters back. I wear it the way she wore hers. Not a wound. A receipt.

The sound is the first fact of the world and I stopped hearing it as sound around age twenty-five, the way you stop hearing your own pulse. It lives in me now as weather. Right now: the deep four-count thud of the number-two pump, laboring, a half-beat heavier than it should be, which means I'll be down in the housing before second meal whether anyone tells me to or not. Underneath that, the long groan of the south leg settling into the basalt shelf it's anchored to, a sound so familiar I could hum it. And underneath that — under everything, under the engines and the chains and the wind that never stops scraping itself raw against the stack — the water. Always the water. Doing its slow enormous business.

I put my palm flat against the strut beside me, more habit than purpose, the way you'd touch a doorframe walking past it, and I feel it before I consciously go looking: a pressure wave, low and unhurried, moving from somewhere off to the northwest. Big. Not urgent-big — not a breach, not a frenzy, nothing that'll have the bell ringing — just big the way the weather is big, something vast going somewhere with no interest in arriving quickly. I count it out of habit. Four point three seconds, leg to leg, before the strut under my other hand picks it up. That puts it deep, deeper than the chain's ever found bottom, deeper than anyone's instruments have bothered to lie about.

My grandmother used to say you could tell the rig was healthy by whether it sang in tune with itself, and I didn't understand her until my second decade up here, when I realized I'd started doing the same thing she did — standing still at odd hours, head slightly tilted, reading the iron the way you'd read a face you'd loved a long time, checking it for trouble before it became trouble.

The light is doing its flat grey nothing overhead, the kind of light that never commits to a time of day, and out past the rail the water has that gravity to it, that pull on the eye that every rigger develops and none of us ever name out loud. I'm watching it now without having decided to. Waiting, the way you wait without impatience for a kettle that always boils eventually. Somewhere out there a shadow will roll, or won't. A column of mist will rise off something surfacing to breathe, or won't. The not-gulls will start circling half a mile off, which means a kill, which means the silver swarm will start its boiling-water sound soon after.

Behind me, on the lower decking, the chapel-that-isn't waits with its soft chalk-fattened wall, and later, on my watch, when the chain comes groaning up out of the dark with its glistening haul, I will do the thing I always do and always deny doing — a tap on the rail, two fingers, no weight to it — and I will tell anyone who asks that it means nothing, and I will do it again next time regardless, because some math doesn't need solving to be respected.

I have never been out to the far columns. I've heard the men who have go quiet about it in that particular conspicuous way, the way a loud people go quiet only around the one subject too large for shouting. I believe them without needing to have seen it myself. I believe, the way you believe in weather you've never personally stood in but have heard described too many times by people who don't lie to each other about much else, that the noise we make — all of it, the pumps and the chains and the venting steam and our own ruined, permanent shout of a voice — isn't only industry. That it's also, maybe mostly, a kind of cover. That something out past the charted water is listening for the absence of it.

So when the shift bell goes, I go back to my ordinary furious-sounding sentences, hollering across the clanging deck about pump four and the second haul and whether the storm spinning up to the south will reach us by dark, grateful all the way down in some chamber of myself I don't examine too closely, for every degree of decibel the rig still has left to give.
