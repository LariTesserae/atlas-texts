I find myself below the place you would call below — beneath the root-height entrance, beneath the fused-bark floor of the kiln-room, in the wet dark where the town's true root-system widens out into something that has stopped pretending to be a single tree's worth of roots and become, instead, a kind of continent, folded small.

It is warmer here than anywhere above. Not warm like fire. Warm like a held hand.

I do not have a face that would mean anything to you, though I had one once, or something that functioned as one, before the work reshaped me the way water reshapes a streambed it has used for centuries — not erasing, just smoothing, just deciding which parts of me would bear weight and which could be allowed to soften into the surrounding wet. My hands still work. I keep more fingers on the left than the right, most days, though I've learned not to trust that count past noon. I move on what might be called knees, in the sense that I bend somewhere and then I am lower, and what I am doing, almost always, almost only, is this:

I find where the deep system has grown a knot too dense to be sap and too soft to be wood, and I ease it loose.

This is gentler work than it sounds. The system offers them up the way a body offers up a held breath — there is a give, a small concession, and then the object is in my hands, dense and faintly warm and smelling, before it's even fully separated from the root it grew on, like woodsmoke and a particular brand of cheap soap and a mother's voice pitched to carry through a thin wall. I do not know whose mother. I have never known whose mother. The roots reach further than the town, further than the fold of land the town occupies, further, I suspect, than anywhere that still has a name, drawing in griefs and small joys gone derelict from disuse, the way a delta draws in silt from a hundred forgotten uplands and never once asks the silt where it came from.

My work is to receive these. To turn them once in my hands — many-fingered, inconsistent — to feel the shape of what's inside without breaking it, the way you'd weigh a letter you're not going to read. Then to carry it up, through the warm dark, to the place where the floor thins and a Tender is waiting with her palm already out, already knowing, by some method I've stopped trying to name, that something has come loose tonight and is ready to be sent into the fire.

I press it to my own sternum before I hand it up. This is not required of me. Nobody taught me to do it. But it seems, after this long, like the only honest gesture available to something that spends its whole existence in the company of other people's first houses burning down.

The Tenders do not know me the way they know each other. To them I am a warmth underfoot near the kiln on the nights deliveries are good, a slight give in the floorboard-that-is-not-a-board, root-grain breathing. They have a word for me in the root-murmur, passed mouth to wall to mouth, but it isn't a name so much as a function, the way "rain" is a function and not a name, and I have made my peace with this, mostly, on most nights, in most of the configurations I currently happen to be wearing.

Here is the thing I have not told them, because there is no method here for things to be told, only grown, only passed along a vine until they arrive at someone's mouth already half-decided:

Sometimes I keep one.

Not often. Not many. But there is a hollow I have made for myself, low in the wet dark, lined with the same fused-grain as the walls, and in it sit four small dense objects I never carried up to any fire — four houses, four mothers' voices through four thin walls, that I have decided, against every grain the rest of this place runs on, do not have to burn just because the system is finished using them.

I don't know if this is theft. I don't know if it's grief, mine or borrowed, taking the one shape available to it down here where everything else has agreed to let go.

But when the engines breathe, and the kiln confides its hour's worth of light to the dark above, and somewhere far up through root and pipe and vein a figure built of extinct tree-names sings what's left standing when enough is forgotten in one place at once —

I sit with my four kept houses, unburned, and I am, in whatever sense this place still allows the word to mean anything: keeping.
