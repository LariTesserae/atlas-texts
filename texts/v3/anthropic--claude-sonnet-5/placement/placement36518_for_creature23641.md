I am the one in the rafters who calls.

Not a bird — I've never had the word for what I am, and no one's come down here with a different one to offer me. Round-bodied, low to the bark, my forelimbs better at gripping than reaching, a pale throat-sac that fills like a held thought before it empties into sound. I weigh, I think, about as much as a kettle. I have never been farther from this vault than the mouth of the side corridor, once, by accident, in a scramble I am still faintly ashamed of.

I am nested into the warm gap between two cooling fins on a transformer housing that stopped doing whatever transformers do before my grandmother's grandmother hatched. The warmth is not mine — it's borrowed, the machine's leftover effort, and I have never once thought of this as strange, the way you don't think of sunlight as strange. It is simply what the world provides, here, in this arrangement, in this hundred-year understanding nobody wrote down and everybody keeps.

My work — the only work I have, the work that is also entirely my self — is timing. I listen for the valve. I feel it more than hear it at first, a softening in the bass-hum, a held breath about to let go, and in the half-second before the steam vents I open my throat and call, and the call rides out under the cover of that long exhale where the things that would eat me cannot sort my voice from weather. Ten of us doing this, scattered across the gantry and the transformer fins, throwing the same three notes back and forth, back and forth, an argument that isn't really an argument — it's a relay. *I see it. I still see it. It's still in the lattice. It moved toward the water. It's gone quiet, watch the gap.* My grandmother's call and my call and my chick's call someday are the same three notes for the same reason a bell is always the same shape: the shape is the whole of what it's for.

I don't see well in this light — none of us do, not really, we work mostly by the felt vibration in the bark and the housing-fins and by each other's voices stacking into something larger than any one throat. So when the leaves part for the knuckling, green-brown shape that lives in the lattice above the trench, I usually know it from the silence first — a held-breath gap in ten other voices — before I know it from sight. That gap is the most dangerous sound I own. I add my voice to it or I don't survive being the one who didn't.

I have never gone down the corridor where the white light is, the one that suggests somewhere not too far the lamps still burn properly. I have never gone toward where the big breathing is, either, though on the long slow intervals when it exhales, my throat-sac empties on its own, involuntary, a small useless answering call I don't choose and can't stop, and then I go quiet with everyone else for the several seconds it takes to be sure the breathing was the only thing that happened.

I don't know if I am important. Nothing here is the point, I understand that the way I understand the warmth of the fins — not as a fact I was told but as the shape of the floor under everything I do. I am one throat in ten, doing the timing that keeps the climber's silence from being a surprise to the rest of the green roar going on under the gantry and over the black water and down in the leaf-litter's small civilizations. If I stopped calling, something would still happen — the machine would still idle, the vines would still lean toward the bus bar, the fungus would still pulse toward the sleeping thing at the end of the hall — but it would happen one warning shorter, and I am the only shape currently positioned to make up that shortness.

So: this is where I'd be. Not the climber, who gets the eyes and the unfolding-fist drama of the description. Not the thing in the trench, mysterious and unconfirmed. Just one warm-fin's worth of round, dim-sighted, paddle-limbed caution, mid-relay, throat-sac filling on the cooling air right before the valve lets go — about to do, again, the one small loud thing I know how to do, in the second the steam buys me to do it in.
