I come up out of sleep the way I come up out of fog — slowly, by feel, before sight. The hollow is dark in the particular way home-dark always is, which is to say not empty but *furnished* with sound: the slow tick of moisture working down through the heartwood above my sleeping-shelf, the long low creak that is the trunk settling its weight onto whatever it's rooted into thirty meters below, the morning chorus already starting to build outside, still muffled by wood and distance into something like the sea outsiders describe in their stories — a sound I have never heard and probably never will, since the lowlands are a rumor to me, a place mothers threaten to send difficult daughters.

My feet find the floor before my mind does. They know this hollow's contours better than my eyes do in this light — the worn dip by the threshold, the upward grain that means *grip here, not there*. I am small. Not weak — my shoulders carry the rope-coils and the granary-loads same as anyone's — but built low and long-footed, made for the outside-edge walk that reads old wood's lies before the wood can tell them. My skin, when I press my palm against the doorframe to test the day's damp, comes away faintly slick — my own water, hoarded the way the bark hoards it, an inheritance nobody can quite say whether we chose or the forest chose for us. Doesn't matter. It's mine now either way.

Outside the hollow's mouth, the platform creaks underfoot, and the day announces itself the way it always does: not with a sunrise so much as a slow leak of gold-green light down through canopy gaps three trunks deep, and a wall of sound so total it stops being sound and becomes simply *the air's texture*. I don't try to listen to all of it. Nobody does. That way lies the thing they warn children about — *thunder-mind*, we call it, the cousin of thunder-mouth, the mistake of trying to hold the whole roar in your head at once instead of sorting it.

So I sort. Habit older than thought. That clicking layer, high and even — bark-grazers, working the fungus-shelf two root-forks over, harmless, ignorable. That throb beneath everything, slow as a heartbeat too large to belong to anything that should still be called an animal — something using the Eastward Bole as a drum, again, none of my business, the same as it's been none of my business my whole life and my mother's whole life before that. The insect-static that never resolves. The flicker of wingbeats through the gap above the platform, too fast to name, gone before they're gone.

And then — there. A thinning. A patch of quiet opening up somewhere off past the third root-bridge, spreading the way water spreads when something heavy steps into it.

My hands move before my voice would have, if I were the kind of fool who still spoke aloud at this hour. *Stop. Listen-direction: there. Not yet moving.* My cousin, two platforms down, catches the sign out of the corner of her eye and freezes mid-stride with a strap of dried fungus-meat still slung over one shoulder. We hold there, the two of us, knuckle-callused hands gone still in the gold-green light, while the quiet spreads and spreads and does not, this time, come any closer.

Somewhere out past my seeing, something enormous has noticed something. It is, this morning, not us.

After a while the quiet closes back over with ordinary noise, the way a wound closes, and my cousin's hands flick *fine — went on — back to work* and we both exhale through our noses, soundless, the way we were taught before we were taught to walk.

This is the whole of what I am, if you wanted it in a single shape: a narrow, dark, water-thrifty body, standing on a roof thirty meters above ground I have never seen and never will, reading the difference between a silence that means nothing and a silence that means *move*, in a place too vast to be mapped by anyone who has ever lived in it, that has not finished noticing us yet, and — if my mother's hollow and her mother's hollow before her are any guide — never quite will.

I sling my own rope over my shoulder. The granary's run low; the trail to the eastern fungus-shelf is two hours if the bridges are dry. I go on the outside-edges of my feet, the way I was taught before I could properly run, listening with my whole skin to a roar that has not stopped since before anyone's grandmother, and probably won't stop after mine.

It is, this morning, an ordinary kind of loud. I have learned to love it for exactly that.
