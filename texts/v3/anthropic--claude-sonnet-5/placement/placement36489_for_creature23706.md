I find myself on the eleventh terrace, though I don't think of it as eleventh — I think of it as *Mother's-Gutter*, because the channel that feeds our cistern was cleared by my grandmother's grandmother and still bears, worn into the lip of it, the shallow groove where her adze slipped once and she never bothered to smooth it out. I am small enough that the groove is at the height of my eye when I crouch to check the night's catch, which is the first chore I am old enough to be trusted with and not yet old enough to be bored by.

My feet know things my mind hasn't caught up to yet. I can feel — not see, feel, in the soft webbing between my toes — the fraction-of-a-degree lean of the whole world, and I correct for it the way I breathe, without noticing I'm doing it, the way the swallows correct for wind. When the flatland traders come up gasping and stumbling at the eighth terrace, gray-faced, gripping the vine-railings like something that might let go of them rather than the other way around, I have to look at my own hands and remember not to laugh, because someone older and wiser than me — probably whoever is currently warden of our cistern, which changes, but is always someone with hands like dry riverbeds, deep-lined and sure — has told me that laughing at a flatlander's stagger is a small cruelty dressed as a joke, and small cruelties left unchecked grow into walls nobody wants to live near.

I am, I think, somewhere between child and not-child. Old enough to light the lantern on my own stretch of path most evenings — though not every evening; that honor still occasionally goes to my younger cousin, who lobbies for it with an intensity that the whole terrace finds funny and pretends not to notice, because not-noticing is itself a kind of gift we give each other here, the only privacy that exists in a place where sound climbs the open shaft like smoke. I heard, last week, an argument two terraces up about a goat that wasn't where it should have been. I have already, without being told to, arranged my morning so I won't be on the ladder-chute when the people involved are using it, won't make them feel watched in their own unresolved mess. This isn't kindness exactly. It's just how the air works here. You learn the shape of other people's privacy the way you learn the shape of the spire's lean — invisibly, constantly, by correction rather than instruction.

My hands are not yet old enough to be read the way the old hands are read. They don't yet carry the permanent dark stain of beetle-oil pressed deep into the creases, the way my grandmother's do, the way that lets anyone glancing at her palms know instantly what she's spent fifty years doing with them. My hands are still becoming. I have a single thin white scar across my left thumb from a vine-rope that slipped during a re-weaving lesson, and I am, embarrassingly, a little proud of it, because it is the first mark my body has earned toward becoming legible the way the elders are legible — toward becoming a person whose history can be read without words, just by someone taking your hand and turning it over in the lantern-light.

I do not think of myself as living *on* the spire. That is a flatlander's way of putting it, the cistern-warden says, and she should know, she has spoken with traders for forty years and still finds their language faintly puzzling, all that talk of *on top of* and *underneath*, as if the spire were a thing you stood upon rather than a thing you were a temporary, grateful part of. I think — when I think about it at all, which is rarely, the way you rarely think about your own pulse — that I am simply some of the spire's current growth. Soil filling a crack that opened up a few generations back. I will widen the terrace some imperceptible amount in my lifetime, the way everyone before me did, and someone will continue what I started without finishing, the way I am continuing what was started long before any name I could put to it.

At dusk I climb out along the vine-railing to the terrace lip and look down the long curve of the spire, the lanterns catching one after another below me like something igniting in slow motion, like a comet that has decided, against all sense, to fall upward instead of down. Goat-bells. A grain-mill somewhere groaning on its stone axle. Birds settling their last furious arguments of the day into the thousand crevices above and below. And far up, past the last terrace anyone has cut, past the place my mother's voice goes soft and careful when she mentions it — not frightened, just *careful*, the way you'd speak of weather — there is, sometimes, if the wind sits right, a low cough-call from the unclimbed dark.

I am not afraid of it. I have simply arranged my whole short life, so far, downward and around it, the way everyone here has, the way the terraces themselves do, each one built on the patient understanding that some things are not yours to finish, only yours to live alongside, gratefully, palms cupped, in no hurry at all.
