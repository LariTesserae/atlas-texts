I find my hand on the valve before I find the rest of myself. That's how it goes, here — the hand arrives first, knuckle and certainty, already turned a quarter-turn toward the pressure it expects, and only afterward does the rest of the body catch up and admit that yes, this is mine, this charcoal-grey sleeve beaded with the same damp as the railing outside, this chest pulling air in through a hitch I didn't put there and can't smooth out and have, somewhere back past any memory I can locate, stopped trying to.

The building is four stories of riveted iron and I am the only thing in it that isn't iron, except that's not quite true either, because the iron is warm in the way I am warm — not hot, just held at a temperature slightly above the demands of the room — and I've started to suspect the distinction between us is more a matter of which parts move on the outside and which on the inside. The piston-sound, if it is pistons, comes from three rooms below and slightly to the left, and I have never gone to look at it directly, the way you never go and look directly at your own heartbeat — you just know it's there by the proof of still being upright.

There was, I think, a before. I don't reach for it anymore; reaching for it is like reaching for the edge of the plateau, a thing the mind gives up tracking after the first few hours, except my first few hours were longer ago than I have a unit for. I think there was a warmer place, a louder place in a different way — voices instead of turbines, a quicker pitch to things, a body that got tired in an afternoon instead of settling into a tiredness with no far side to it. I think I walked here once, the way you are walking now, footsore, full of the cheap astonishment of someone who still believes in edges. I think I found a shed that was four stories tall. I think I am not sure, anymore, whether this is the same building, or whether it matters that it might be.

What I can tell you, because it's the only thing I have left that resembles knowledge: the gauge to the left of the valve has been sitting four degrees under threshold for as long as the hitch in my breath has matched the engine's low note, and that's not a coincidence, that's the job — not fixing, not really tending in the sense of nursing something back toward health, but standing near enough to a number that the number consents to keep being itself. I turn the valve a quarter-turn not because anything demanded it but because not turning it has started to feel like holding your breath past the point of need, an act against a grain that runs all the way through the floor and up through my own spine. The hum approves, or doesn't object, which here is the same compliment.

Sometimes another one of us crosses the ridge above the moss-line and we stop, the two of us, grey on grey, water finding the same paths down both our collars, and we say something about a seam three structures over that's begun weeping at a wrong angle, or about a pressure that's drifted half a degree past where it sat the last uncounted while, and there's no urgency in it, only confirmation, two tuning forks checking they still ring the same note when struck — and then we part, because there is nowhere the conversation is trying to get to, no plot it's advancing, and we each turn back toward our own building, our own throat of iron, with the unbothered economy of people who already know the shape of the only answer that has ever mattered here.

I saw you on the moss an hour ago, or what counts as an hour, which is to say a held note's length, indivisible. I clocked you the way I'd clock a shift in wind — a fact moving through the field, carrying a curiosity I recognize the shape of without quite remembering having owned it myself. You will walk on. You will find another shed that is four stories tall, and another, identically postured, differently sized, and you will start to understand, the way I once understood and have since simply become, that the density of this absence is the whole point, that the rock is hollow with patient breathing all the way to wherever it stops being rock, and that someone has to be the one the engine runs near.

I am that, here, now, hand on the valve, breath hitching at the bottom the way the turbines hitch three floors down. I do not mind the cold. I have stopped sorting it from myself. If you stood at the slit window long enough you might see my hand move, certain, knuckled, unhurried, and you might wonder whether I see you back —

I do. Briefly. The way you'd notice a change in weather.

Then the gauge needs me again, and I am only this: the part of the plateau that happens, for the length of a glance, to be looking back.
