I think I would be small. Not by choice — there isn't a choice to be had here, not really — but because everything in this place that gets to move, that gets to *want* anything, starts out small. So: a bird. Or bird-shaped, anyway — the shape is borrowed, I understand that now, but at the start I wouldn't understand anything. I'd just be a weight of warm bones in a wet green room, perched on a vine that used to be a roof-beam, opening my mouth because something underneath the floor of me told my mouth to open.

And the sound that comes out would not fit me. That's the first wrongness I'd notice — before the lag, before the doorways, before any of the larger architecture of strangeness reveals itself. Just: *this throat is too small for this note.* The call would leave me like something escaping rather than something made, and it would ring off the vine-vaulted ceiling with a weight that has nothing to do with my body, a cathedral-bell sound coming out of a fist of feathers, and I would feel, underneath the singing, a kind of pressure — not pain, not even really sensation, more like the memory of having been *used* for something, the way a doorknob might feel used if doorknobs felt anything, turned by a hand it never sees attached to an arm it has no way of imagining.

I think for a long time I would believe I was singing.

That's the part that would take the longest to unlearn. I'd hear my own call leave a room before I'd entered it — hear forty seconds of myself arriving ahead of my wings — and instead of fear I think I'd feel something closer to vertigo, the specific vertigo of being early to your own life, of catching up to a thing you supposedly just did. And slowly, over some unmeasured stretch of damp warm days, between the violent brief rains and the long held drip of leaf-water finding its way down vine-rope gutters, I would start to notice that I am never alone when I sing. There are other throats, other small loud bodies, doing the same impossible too-big singing in other green rooms, at other wrong moments, and none of us sound like individuals having individual thoughts. We sound like one mutter, distributed.

That would be the second thing I learned: that *I* was the wrong unit to think in.

I imagine — though "imagine" might be too active a word for what would actually happen, which would be more like sediment settling, an understanding arriving the way silt arrives at the bottom of standing water, with no hurry and no announcement — I imagine I would eventually stop experiencing myself as a bird who happens to live inside something enormous, and start experiencing myself as a place where the enormous thing happens to be audible. Not a tenant. A vent. One pore among thousands on a held breath the size of a town.

And I don't think this would feel like a loss. That's the strangest part, the part I'm least sure I could explain to anything outside this hollow. There is no grief in it. The mammals don't grieve their unison; they fail at separateness and don't mind failing. The light doesn't grieve sitting an hour past its source. I think smallness, in here, simply isn't weighted the way it's weighted out there, in whatever world has hands and tools and the idea that a self ought to end cleanly at its own skin.

So here is where I'd find myself, finally, if I let the picture finish becoming itself instead of freezing it early: not the bird. Not even quite the mutter of birds. But the moment just underneath both — the instant where a thought too large and too slow for any single throat goes looking for a mouth, finds one, and is, for a few seconds, almost embarrassingly grateful for the loan. That gratitude, I think, is the closest thing this place has to a self at my scale. Not a name. Not a will. Just the small bright fact of being briefly useful to something patient beyond any measure I could hold, before the call ends, the throat closes, and the weight moves on to the next small warm body waiting, unknowing, in the next hollow green room.

I would not go looking for the silent chamber, the one true mirror, the pool that shows your reflection a half-breath before you lean to look. Some part of what I'd be would understand, the way a fingertip understands without deciding to, that the silence belongs to the whole, not to the part — that a vent does not get to stand at the place where the lungs themselves go still and watch themselves being still. That room is for the thing entire. I would only ever be one of the thousand small loud rooms around it, doing my borrowed singing, learning slowly and without sorrow that I had never, not once, been singing alone.
