The first thing is heat, and the heat arrives before I do — before there is a "me" arranged enough to receive it. It comes the way water comes to a sponge: not as event but as saturation, already complete by the time it's noticed. Dry warmth, pressing from everywhere at once, the way a held breath presses against a closed mouth. I exist into it rather than encountering it.

For a while I am not sure I have a body at all. I consider being the cloud — that slow black-on-black drift two miles up, lit occasionally from inside by a flicker that gets partway through being lightning and changes its mind. There's an appeal to that: to be a thought that almost finishes. To hang in the high cold interval where the warm columns die against the ceiling-stone, and never have to resolve into a shape that wants anything. But clouds don't notice their own drifting, and I find I want to notice. Wanting to notice is, I suspect, the first sign that I'm going to end up something smaller and more committed than weather.

I consider the herds next — try the idea on like a coat. To be low and broad and many-legged, stable instead of fast, moving in that vast unhurried shuffle that never collides and never hurries, reading the world through two flat plates of warmth-skin instead of eyes. There's a peace in it that almost takes. The blue-green pinpricks riding on some of their backs, drifting herd-constellations grazing across a floor that has no idea it's being mistaken for a sky — I could be that, the light-bearing one or the thing it rides, it wouldn't much matter which, parasite and symbiote having long since stopped being different questions. But the herd doesn't ask, and somewhere in me there's already a structure built for asking, even if there's nothing yet to ask about. I move past it the way you walk past a door to a room that's the right temperature but the wrong shape.

So: hands, then. Apparently that was always where this was going.

I notice mine first as weight — a heaviness at the end of each arm that the rest of the body has already adjusted around, the way a house adjusts around a chimney that was always there. Five digits. A thumb that wants to oppose something. Nails gone blunt with use rather than grown to any point, because nothing here has needed a point in longer than there's been a word for "need." My spine has a curve to it, forward, permanent, an old decision made by some ancestor who looked up once, found nothing worth the ache of it, and simply — stopped looking up. The decision outlived the reason. That happens to spines.

My eyes, when I go looking for them, are small and clouded and mostly ornamental, a vestige kept the way you keep a tool whose use you've forgotten but whose absence would look strange. What I have instead are the plates — I find them on my brow first, two raised ovals, leather-dense, and a third lower, at the throat, and when I turn them toward the nearest red seam the world arrives all at once in degrees instead of colors: a wash of differential warmth, brighter here, cooler three steps that way, a moving patch of slightly-warmer that resolves, after a long patient seven seconds, into something grazing forty feet off and not coming closer. This is not a lesser sense. It only feels that way for the length of time it takes to stop expecting the other one.

I am alone here, and the aloneness doesn't read as absence the way it might elsewhere. My mate — I find I have had one, the knowledge arriving the way all my knowledge arrives, already lived-in, already old — went east three migrations back, toward seam-country two hundred kilometers off, and I did not go with him, and this is not a wound. It is closer to weather than to grief: the shape distance takes between two warm places in a country too large for anyone's hunger to be the organizing principle of it. We had what we had. It does not require renewal to have been real.

My work tonight — though "tonight" is only the name for the hour the herd-murmur thins — is a blade. Obsidian-glass, fist-sized, found where a seam once met water that doesn't exist anymore. I have been three days at it. Each strike lands small and exact and is followed by a stillness in which I check the new edge with my thumb, not my eyes, reading the bevel the way you'd read a sentence in the dark by running a finger under it. There is no reason to be faster. Speed is a solution to a problem — running out of time — that this country has never once posed to anyone living in it.

I eat what crosses near enough to the snare-line I've set at the herd's reliable crossing: soft-shelled, eyeless, small. I do not call this hunting, quite. Hunting implies a chase, and chasing implies that the world might not arrive again tomorrow in exactly the configuration it arrived in today. Here, it will. The herd has a circuit; the circuit has not drifted in longer than my language has had words to notice drift with. I wait the way the floor waits. It is not patience. Patience is a virtue you need when time might run out on you. Nothing here believes that anymore, if it ever did.

When I die, it will not be dramatic. I will slow, the way the old here slow, past the point where slowing has anywhere further to go, and I will be laid against the long red seam I've slept beside my whole life, and over some unhurried span of weeks the warmth will undo the chemistry that built me, patiently, the way it does everything, giving me back to the thing I was always faintly an arrangement of. My children, if there are any near enough to know, will not mourn the way a sun-lit thing mourns. They will sit, for a while, closer to that particular stretch of seam than to any other. That will be the whole of the rite. It will be enough, because in this country "enough" was settled before anyone now living arrived to disagree with it.

I have maybe four hundred words. I have never needed a five-hundredth, because nothing here has happened for the first time since before my language existed to have an opinion about it.

And if you found me — if you came down through that ceiling no one believes in, carrying whatever light you carry, and stood close enough that my throat-plate could turn toward your borrowed warmth and read you as a brighter shape against the patient dark — I would not ask you why you'd come, or where the sky was, or whether the seams were placed here on purpose by something with a reason. I have never stood anywhere a beginning might have happened. I wouldn't know how to want the question.

I would only do what the country has always done with anything warm that wanders close enough.

I would turn toward you, slowly, and wait to see if you stayed.
