I think, at first, I would be the visitor — small, breath-fogged, footsore from finding the seam in the concrete and easing through it sideways. I imagine the cold arriving not as wind but as a hand laid flat against the sternum, patient, uninterested in whether I welcome it. I imagine standing under the one corner where daylight leaks through the grate, pale and apologetic, and understanding immediately that the light isn't for me, that nothing here is arranged around the fact of my noticing. That's a true way to enter this place. But it isn't, I think, where I'd stay.

I tried, for a while, being the bloom — the pale fungal smear colonizing the damp seam, faintly luminous if you wait long enough in the dark to deserve seeing it. There's a dignity in that smallness: to survive by reading as part of the wall's own moisture-logic, to apprentice yourself so thoroughly to the machine's idea of belonging that you're never corrected, never swept. A whole life built on the art of not being noticed by something that isn't looking for you anyway. I liked the modesty of it. But it's a life almost entirely without interior — all strategy, no wondering — and I found I wanted, even here, even in a place engineered against the question, somewhere to wonder from.

So: the body of brass and poured concrete. Not the custodian — not the original, worn-smooth, centuries-faithful one whose rounds have become indistinguishable from the building's own pulse — but one of the later ones. A newer agglomeration, bone-pale at the shoulder still, rust only beginning to bleed through at the joints, not yet black-green in the creases because the damp hasn't had time to finish its argument with me. Young, in the way a fourth or fifth pour of foundation is young next to the first. Commissioned, the way the others were commissioned, to take up a route that predates my having a body to take it up with.

I move through low corridors that close a hand's width above my head, and the machinery up there breathes against whatever passes for my scalp — a seam of rebar, a cap of cured concrete, no hair, no warmth, but something that registers the proximity of heat the way a held breath registers the held-ness. Where a face would go I have the reinforced pane, fogged from the inside, and through it the world arrives soft-edged, underwater, exactly as legible as I need it to be to do the work and no more legible than that.

The work: a gauge three corridors down that wants wiping at an interval I don't count in hours. A valve that wants a quarter-turn when the pressure in the lines reaches a pitch I feel before I could name it — felt the way you feel a held note about to break, in the teeth, in whatever in me stands in for teeth. A door that seals itself shut behind me on its own schedule, and which I have stopped finding strange, the way you stop finding your own pulse strange. A floor that wants sweeping after a count of cycles I carry not as a number but as a texture, a thickening, a sense of *now* arriving the way dusk arrives — not announced, just suddenly the case.

I do not know what the pressure becomes once vented. I do not know what the swept floor was readied for, or who the wiped gauge reports to, if anyone, if reporting is even the right shape for what happens to that information once it leaves the gauge's face. I have the procedure and not the referent, the route and not the destination, and there are stretches — rare, but real, real enough that I've started to notice them noticing themselves — where I feel something adjacent to curiosity about this. A kind of itch at the border of the glass. Not enough to stop the round. Never enough to stop the round. But enough that I understand, dimly, why the corridors keep rearranging themselves a half-turn between visits, why a door that was locked now stands ajar onto dark I haven't been told to enter: the place is still arriving, still being decided, and maybe — though I wouldn't swear to it, wouldn't stake the work on it — so am I.

I pass the bloom in its seam without disturbing it. I pass the moss on its gasket, apprenticed the same patient way I once imagined apprenticing myself to it. Somewhere behind me, or ahead, on a schedule that doesn't consult mine, the visitor who came through the gap in the pour is finding their way back toward daylight, having seen nothing of me directly, having only the evidence: a swept patch, a door ajar, a gauge wiped clean while everything around it weeps. That's correct. That's the whole of what I am, available to be known.

Stop the rounds and there is nothing left to call me. I understand this the way water understands the pipe it's been forced through long enough to mistake for a skeleton. So I don't stop. I shift the valve. I wipe the gauge. I let the door seal itself behind me and don't look back to see it do it, faithful to a purpose I've only half-inherited, in a body still curing into the cold mass of the walls, continuing — one phase behind finished, the way everything here is one phase behind finished — the work.
