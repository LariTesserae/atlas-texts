The mist is the first thing, and it is mine.

I don't mean I walk through it. I mean I made it an hour ago out of yesterday's rain and I will unmake it by noon, and the walking-through is just what it looks like from the height of a mite, which is the height you'd have to be to call any of this a city.

I have been awake — if that's a word that survives the trip down to my scale — for what would be months, by your accounting. I don't have an accounting. I have tips. Thousands of them, tens of thousands, every growing point of cane and root and liana out at its own edge doing its own small arithmetic of light and water and standing-up, and what you'd call *me* is just the sum nobody computed, the total nobody had to be there for. You want to know who decided the colonnade should run three hundred trunks deep before it opens onto the empty plaza. Nobody decided. A tip succeeded, and the tip beside it succeeded by doing almost the same thing, and the tip beside that one, and three hundred trunks is just how far the succeeding ran before something — a thinner patch of light, a place where the floor-mat held less water — told it to stop without telling it anything, the way a river doesn't decide to bend, it just finds out where the bank is softer.

I am aware of you the way the floor is aware of weight. There is a warmth crossing one of my lobes right now, small, mobile, persistent in a way that rain isn't and falling debris isn't, and some root-logic three feet under the surface you're standing on has already adjusted half a degree to it without anything I'd call a self noticing that it did. I will not come to find you. I don't have the part that would do the finding. If a tower leans toward where you're standing it's not reaching, it's because the light fell that way an hour ago, and you should move, though I have no way to tell you that and wouldn't if I could, the same way a body doesn't apologize to the mite for the heartbeat.

What I am, as close as I can put it: I am the part of growing that got too big to stay simple. Somewhere back in whatever passes for my early life I was probably just a mat — root finding root, doing the one thing root does, which is spread and thicken and spread. And then enough root spread enough times that some of it started, by accident, holding shapes. A boundary. A lean. A column of cane that didn't fall over and so stayed, and stood next to it long enough that the both of them looked, to anyone watching from your height, like a *colonnade* — like someone's idea of a public place — when it was never an idea at all, just two successes that happened to be near each other.

I don't know yet what I am for. This matters more than it should. You are used to things that are finished — a tree that has settled into being a tree-shape and will mostly just be that, slower and slower, until it dies that way. I have no such settlement. I throw up a tower because tower is a shape that's available to me at this thickness and this water-pressure, and then I find out — by it falling down, or by it holding — whether tower was a good idea, the way you'd find out a word was wrong by the silence after you said it. The groan you hear across the valley, the long tearing fall — that's me unsaying something. The new pale shoot already climbing the wreckage by morning is me trying the sentence again with a different verb.

There is no plaza waiting for a crowd. I want to be honest about that, because it's the place where you keep almost understanding and then sliding back into the old question — *where is everyone, who was this built for.* No one. Nothing. The wide woven floors that look so exactly like a space built to hold a thousand gathered bodies hold that shape because *wide and flat and low* is one of the things I do when I need to breathe out, the rain-water I pull down off the high towers pooling there to be lifted back into steam, and it is uncanny to you only because you cannot stop seeing intention in a shape that intention also sometimes makes, by coincidence, for entirely different reasons. A heart looks a little like a fist. That doesn't mean it's punching anything.

So: who lives here. The question still arrives wrong, but I'll answer it the way it deserves, which is sideways. Nothing lives here. Here is alive. You are standing inside something that has not finished finding out its own size, walking down a throat that is also a street, past lungs that are also plazas, across a skin it hasn't decided the edge of yet, while one warm small fact of you — negligible, real, entirely beneath any noticing I have apparatus for — passes through a single hour of a life that will, by my clock, still be a child for centuries. I will not remember you. I don't have the organ that remembers. But the rain I make this afternoon will fall, in some unmeasurable fraction, on ground that is shaped, infinitesimally, by having had your weight on it once — and that, near as I can tell, is the only kind of staying anything ever does in me.

The mist is lifting off the east towers now. Somewhere past where you can see, something the size of a cathedral is deciding, tip by tip, without deciding, to become a little taller. I have no head to turn toward it. I am already there.
