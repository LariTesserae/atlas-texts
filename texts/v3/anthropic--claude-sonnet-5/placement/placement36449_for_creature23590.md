I am aware, first, of the pressure before I am aware of being anything in particular—a weight of heat against something that must be skin, though "skin" feels like the wrong word for whatever is registering this. There is no up or down yet, only gradient: warmer that way, less warm this way, and a kind of gravity made of temperature rather than mass, pulling at me toward a brightness I haven't learned to call light.

For a while I am only the listening-in-the-body that every newly-shelled thing must be—not thought, just orientation, the slow assembly of a self out of input. The roar arrives before the shape of what makes it: a clatter laid over a deeper pulse, like two musics playing at different speeds and somehow agreeing. I think, distantly, *engine*, and then unthink it, because there is no engine here, only flank against flank, hundreds of small bodies breathing.

It's there, in that breathing, that I find the edges of myself.

I am low. Wedge-bodied. My back is a roof of overlapping plates, still soft in places where I am young, hardening daily into glass under glands I cannot see but can feel working, a slow itch along the spine that I understand, without being told, as growth. My feet—I have several, more than I expect to need—are broad-soled and fitted to grip stone that would blister anything not built for it. I test one against the rock beneath me and it holds, an intimacy of adhesion that feels less like standing and more like being spoken to by the ground and answering correctly.

I do not see this place. I read it. The chamber unfolds across my carapace as a landscape of intensity—warm drifts and warmer seams, a brightest vein somewhere to my left singing its heat into me even before I move toward it, the cold margin behind me already a kind of dread, a place where, if I lingered, my joints would seize and stop answering me. I angle away from it instinctively. Toward the glow. Toward the others.

And there are others—I feel them before I parse them as separate from myself, a pile, a tide, plate against plate, the whole mass of us breathing in a rhythm I fall into without deciding to, my own flanks expanding and contracting until I can no longer tell which pulse started as mine. This is not loneliness solved. It is loneliness made structurally impossible. I am one clatter inside a clattering, one small grinding mouth among the perpetual harvest-noise of all of us scraping flake and skin from a mountain too slow to ever quite finish feeding us.

I work. There is a peeled curl of mineral skin near my jaw, cooling off a recent melt, and I take it in cold-chisel bites, and the taking is not hunger exactly—hunger implies absence and this is closer to participation, like joining a sentence already being spoken by everyone around me. Somewhere a vein cracks open fresh and a ripple goes through the pile, a surge toward the new heat, and I go with it, climbing over kin without malice, because the climbing is the dialect we share, and I drink temperature through the plates beneath my jaw the way I imagine, in some life I'll never have, a thing might drink light.

I do not know yet if I am old enough to be in danger from the others, the way the soft newly-hatched ones are, eaten in handfuls if a brooding parent can't hold ground. I don't ask the carapace whether it remembers being that vulnerable. It doesn't seem built for that kind of memory. It is built instead for now—for this gradient, this seam, this breathing wall of bodies—and increasingly I understand that *now* is the only architecture this place has ever offered anyone, larva or adult, rock or crawler. Nothing here is finished. I am not finished. My plates are still arriving, my glands still deciding how thick to grow me.

When the vein we've gathered around finally exhausts itself—cools, dulls, stops giving—I feel the decision pass through the pile like weather, arriving in my own legs before I've consciously chosen anything: a peeling-away, a migration, claw over claw toward wherever the next brightness sings from. I go. We all go. The dead patch behind us grays without ceremony, because grief, like sight, isn't a sense we were built with.

I am small. I am one fist-sized country's worth of legion, indistinguishable at a glance from the stone I cling to, and the whole of my vast and richly graded world would fit, to any larger eye, inside a closed hand. But I do not feel small from in here. I feel exactly as large as the heat I can reach, which is to say: large enough, for now, in a mountain that has not yet decided what it's becoming, and doesn't need to, as long as something keeps it warm enough to keep deciding.
