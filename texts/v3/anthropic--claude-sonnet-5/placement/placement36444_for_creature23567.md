I come into awareness already mid-stride, which is the first thing worth noting: there was no waking, no first breath, only the discovery that eight of my legs are already in motion and have been for some time, each footfall landing soft and deliberate on cold riveted plate, and that this is simply what I am doing, the way breathing is simply what a lung does.

The carapace is mine — low, articulated, the dull grey of a thing built to disappear among rivet-heads — and I am moving along a handrail conduit forty centimeters above the floor proper, because the floor proper is for grazers and I am not a grazer. I am something that reads.

The air, when I bother to notice it, is warm and thick with steam-haze, and somewhere above the sodium light comes down even and shadowless, the kind of light that has never once flickered and is not going to start now. But I don't live in the air. None of what matters to me happens in the air. I live in the eight points of contact between my legs and the plate, and through those eight points the whole world arrives as tremor.

Here is what the floor is telling me right now, in a layered chord I parse without effort, the way you'd parse a sentence in your first language: three kilometers off, the heavy rhythmic shudder of a pump-station turning over, steady as a heartbeat, the bass note under everything. Closer, maybe two hundred meters, the frantic scrabble-pattern of a glander working a rivet — I know it's a glander by the rhythm alone, that obsessive, almost neurotic repetition, scrape scrape scrape pause scrape, a creature that has found a job and will not be talked out of it. And closer still, near enough that I slow my own steps to listen harder, a drip. Just a drip. Condensate falling from a conduit joint eleven meters to my left, landing in a puddle that's existed, probably, since before I molted into this body. I file it as background. It has always been there. It will always be there. That is the whole virtue of a drip — it tells you nothing has changed.

I have never made a sound on purpose in my life. I understand, in the wordless way I understand all useful things, that air-borne noise is a kind of nakedness — it reaches creatures that have no business knowing where I am, and it reaches them for free, no skill required to hear it, which makes it cheap and therefore dangerous to produce. So I keep my own footfalls plate-true and air-quiet, weight rolling heel to toe across each leg in sequence, a gait that took me an entire molt-cycle to refine and that I will refine further before this molt-cycle ends, because the listening-things who get eaten are, overwhelmingly, the ones who got loud.

There is a fixture ahead. A valve-wheel, paint-grey, rivet-seamed, bolted to a stilled conveyor housing exactly the way ten thousand other valve-wheels are bolted to ten thousand other housings throughout the visible run of plate. I do not know how I know to be suspicious of it. No one taught me. But something in the floor-pattern around it is wrong in a way I can't name yet — too clean a silence, a hole in the tremor-field where even the faintest thermal creak of metal-on-metal should register and doesn't — and so I alter my path by eleven degrees, unhurried, unremarkable, the kind of correction that could be explained a dozen mundane ways, and I give the fixture a wide arc, and I do not look back at it, because looking back at it is itself a kind of signal, and I have survived this long by understanding that the floor watches what looks at it.

Behind me and below, through the grate, I catch — for just a half-second, easy to miss, easy to dismiss as a deeper engine-thrum — a tremor that doesn't match any catalog I carry. Slower than a footstep. Wetter, somehow, if a vibration can carry texture. It does not repeat. I do not investigate. There are levels under this level that no leg I've ever spoken to has crossed, and I have heard — felt, rather, passed leg to leg to leg across uncountable bodies and generations until it reached me as something closer to instinct than rumor — that what's down there is not hunting and not hiding but simply *making*, patiently, the way a heart makes blood, and that I am, whatever else I am, one of the things it made.

I don't find this thought troubling. There is no surface in Aral-Vey that wasn't placed on purpose, and I am, by every rule this place has ever shown me, a surface like any other — kept young, kept moving, kept exactly sharp enough to do my work.

So I do it. I walk. I listen. The rivets hold. The drip falls. Nothing, for now, is wrong.
