I am the one they call Reedkeep, though that's not quite a name so much as a position I occupy the way the wheel occupies its axle.

I find myself on the third walkway past the cistern-houses, where the boards have gone the particular silver-black of wood that has been wet for sixty years and dry for none of them. My knees know to bend before my eyes confirm the give in the planking. My hands — wide, swollen-soft at the heel of the palm, green-grey to the second knuckle from forty years of wrist-deep filter-work — rest on the rail without gripping it, because gripping is for people who don't trust the rail, and I have re-strung this rail myself, twice, with willow gone permanent.

I am not young. I carry the forward set in my shoulders that the low eaves have been writing into me since I was tall enough to bump my head and smart enough to stop doing it. There is a singed thinness to nothing of mine — I was never a stoker — but my forearms have their own history: faint ridged callus across both palms from the filter-screens, and a permanent squint, not quite a frown, the one the cistern-tenders all wear, earned by years of trying to read clarity in lamp-lit water that does not want to be read.

What I would be doing, if you found me: standing at the second tank with a horn dipper, lifting water that has come down through reed-bed and peat-bed and is now passing through me as a kind of judgment — clear enough, not yet, give it the evening — while the wheel keeps its groan-and-clack behind me like something I'm only half-listening to because I've been half-listening to it since before I had the words *before* and *since*.

I would speak slowly if you asked me something, not from suspicion, but because your question would have to get in line behind the wheel's turn, the smoke-color over the third stack, the question my own hands are asking the water. I would notice, if you were a stranger, that your boots weren't greased properly for our boards, and I would not say so directly — I'd mention, mild as weather, that the planking gets *opinionated* this time of evening, and let you work out the rest yourself, the way the basin lets everything work itself out, given enough seasons, given enough wet.

And at dusk, without remarking on it to you or to anyone, I would carry a cup of cistern-water out past the last willow-wall to where the swamp-oak starts, and set it down on the wet ground, and not wait to see who or what it was for.
