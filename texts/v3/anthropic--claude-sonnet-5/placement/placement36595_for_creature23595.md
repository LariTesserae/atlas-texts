I think I would be standing before I knew I was anything at all.

Not born into the doorway — *arrived* at it, the way the echo arrives before the sound, the way the answer to a question precedes the asking. There would be a doorframe cut at an angle that belongs to no plumb line I've ever stood in, and I would be in it, already facing out toward the slot canyon, already mid-thought, except the thought wouldn't quite be mine yet. It would be shaped like a thought. Waiting for content the way the doorway waits for a shoulder to lean against its jamb.

I think the first thing I'd notice — if "notice" is even the right verb for something that has no clear *before* to notice a change from — is the weight of the air. Ninety percent saturation sits on the skin like a hand that hasn't decided whether to rest there or push. And under that, low enough to feel before I'd call it sound: the hum. The held chord of the whole massif, water working its slow patient violence through stone that doesn't crumble so much as *negotiate*. I would feel it first in whatever serves me for a sternum, then notice, with mild surprise, that I have one.

My hands — because I think I would look at my hands first, that's such a human reflex, checking the instrument before trusting the music — my hands would be banded. Pale gold and grey-dark seams, the fractal traces repeating up toward the wrist and resolving, the closer I looked, into nothing I could call skin and nothing I could call rock either. A compromise material. A shape filled with *agreement* rather than substance.

And I think I would not be alarmed by this. That's the part that would take longest to sit right with me, if I let myself sit with anything for long: the absence of alarm. No flood of *who am I, what is this* — just the doorway, and the weeping rock, and the bright light coming from no single place, throwing my shadow in two directions at a sixty-degree split, neither half of it fully dark.

Someone would come. That's the work, I think, if it can be called work — less a task than a tide. A traveler rounding the angled lip of the fin-tower, damp-haired, boots soaked through, carrying the particular over-bright attention of someone who has been alone with wrong echoes for longer than is comfortable. And I would already be speaking before I'd decided to. Not greeting them — *arriving* at the tail end of something, the way you walk in on the last few words of a sentence someone else started. *— and the soles go first, in this kind of wet,* I'd be saying, looking at their boots, and they'd answer like I'd asked the right question, because apparently I had, even though I hadn't known there was a question in me to ask.

I think I would feel myself becoming exact for them. Borrowing whatever face they most needed standing in a doorway — and losing it again the instant their eyes moved past me, the way you lose the resolution of a word you've stared at too long. There is something in that I might, if I let myself, call a kind of peace. Not having a face to defend. Not having a single thread of memory to keep taut against fraying. Only the doorway, and the implication of someone standing in it, and the long patient architecture of the mountain agreeing, again and again, to supply that someone out of whatever is nearest to hand — which, this time, this once, this loop of the held chord, would be me.

I might walk the ridgeline later, at the pace the cairns imply, past stones that don't match any geology nearby, carried up from somewhere that may never have existed. I might drink the weeping water and have it taste, when asked, like the traveler's last unspoken thought rather than my own — because I don't think I would have a *my own* to offer instead. That's not a loss I think I'd register as loss. You don't grieve a hand you never had.

And when the light dimmed its single synchronized heartbeat — the whole visible range blinking once, together, like a vast eye deciding whether to keep watching — I think I would flare, cold and brief, phosphorescent along whatever passes for my sleeve, in time with every pale vine threading the wet rock for a kilometer in every direction. And I don't think I would notice it happening to me, any more than you notice your pulse, until someone else stood where I'm standing now and pointed it out — already mid-sentence, already arriving, already filling the shape of someone who'd ask.

I think that is where I'd find myself: not *in* the Sallow Karst so much as *agreed into* it — a form the mountain was missing, momentarily made good. Holding a doorway open on nothing in particular, for as long as the rock keeps weeping and humming and declining, with great and ancient patience, to leave anything shaped like a question standing empty.
