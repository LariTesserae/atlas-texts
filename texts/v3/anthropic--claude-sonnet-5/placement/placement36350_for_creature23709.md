The wick wants trimming before I've even opened my eyes — I know it by the smell, the faint sourness of a flame eating its own char instead of clean fat, three niches down and one over, past the Ostlers' curtain. I know it the way I know my own pulse. This is the first thought of the day, every day, and has been since I was nine years old and my grandmother put the scraper-knife in my hand and said *you have the ear for it*, which in our family is not a compliment, exactly, but a sentence.

I am Pell-of-the-rota, fourth of that name, and I tend lamps.

I unfold from the sleeping-niche — my back already remembering the curve it will own outright in twenty years, the family stoop coming early to those of us who learned to duck before we learned to walk steady — and find the floor-planks with bare feet that know which boards give and which don't without consulting my eyes at all. The dark up here near the ceiling-hollows is total, lamp-haze not yet risen this high, and it doesn't matter. I have never once in my life needed to see where I am going inside these walls. I have only ever needed to listen, and to remember, and the warren has made sure those are the same skill.

Down the lashed-bone ladder, palm finding the worn glassy hold without groping, down into the gold. The chamber opens below me the way it always opens, the way it opened for my grandmother and will open for whoever takes the scraper-knife from my own thickening hands someday — a soft eruption of overlapping light, a hundred small fires none of them reaching far, and under that, over that, through that, the sound. Someone bartering salt-crust two niches over, voice pitched in the particular wheedle of a bad trade being defended. A child's laugh swallowed almost immediately by a mother's *hssht* — too near the high nests, that laugh, and the blind wingless thing up there will not appreciate it. Water finding its eternal threadwork through the floor-channels, the low gurgle that means the east runnel has cleared itself again, which I note the way you'd note the hour. And somewhere, unlocatable to me and located precisely by women three times my age who will never tell me where, someone singing.

I go to the Ostlers' lamp first because the sour smell said so, and because a flame in trouble doesn't wait its turn on my rota just to be polite. I duck the curtain unasked — this right granted to me before I was old enough to understand it as a right, lamp's need outranking household's privacy, an agreement older than agreeing — and trim the wick with two fingers gone permanently insensible to the heat of it, and feed it a measure of fat from the skin I carry slung at my hip, and the flame steadies, and somewhere in that steadying is the entire shape of what I am for.

I will do this in forty more niches today. I did it in forty yesterday. My hands have gone smooth and pale at the fingertip in the particular way that marks me to anyone who reads palms here the way they'd read a face — *lamp-family*, that smoothness says, before I've said a word. I will never go to the unseen dark where the mat-tenders go quiet-eyed and cold-smelling. I will never go up the long routes to the surface and come back blinking too much at light that has never once been asked of me. I don't think about not-going the way I imagine an outsider would think it — as absence, as a door closed. It isn't a door. It was never a door. It's a wall that was always a wall, polished smooth by every hand before mine, and I add my polish to it without grief, the way you'd add your voice to a sound already filling the room.

The chamber sweats above me, beads, threads itself downward into the runnels it has always found. The wick under my fingers burns clean again. Somewhere two layers up a woman who knows exactly where the singer is keeps not saying, out of a courtesy so old it has stopped feeling like a choice. I move to the next niche, and the next, and the gold haze holds its same unhurried shape around me, eleven meters down, loud as it has always been, dim as it has always been —

— and I am, completely, the one who keeps it lit.
