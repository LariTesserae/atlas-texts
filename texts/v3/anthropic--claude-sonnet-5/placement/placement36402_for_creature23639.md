The fog sits at my chest, the way it always does before noon, and I know without checking that it's the fourth hour because the south chimney-vines have just started ticking — condensation letting go of the leaves in that particular rhythm that means the foundry below has banked its fire correctly. If they hadn't, the ticking would be wrong, arrhythmic, and I'd already be moving. But it's right today. Tuesday's weather, holding.

I'm called Wren-who-minds-the-seventh, though half the terrace just says *the vine-woman* when they're being lazy, which is most of the time, language being one more thing we economize. My hands say tannery before anything else does — tea-brown to the wrist, a stain that doesn't wash, won't ever wash, has stopped trying to wash — but that's an old trade, a girlhood trade. What I do now is stand on a narrow iron walk bolted to the foundry's south face, three terraces up from where I was born, and tend forty feet of vine trellis that has nothing to do with vines, not really. The vines are real. The vines are also instrument.

You learn a chimney's voice over years the way you'd learn a parent's cough. This one runs low and steady when the draw is clean, a sound underneath sound, something you feel through the iron rail more than hear. When the draw shifts — when slag builds wrong in the throat, when a valve downstream starts giving up degrees it shouldn't — the pitch climbs a half-step before any gauge down in the boiler-room registers it. I am, by accident of standing in exactly this spot twice a day with a pruning hook in hand, the first warning system the wheel-house crew has. They know this. Nobody asks me to do it. It's simply understood, the way it's understood that the boy six terraces down with the oxen knows the fodder-rotation better than the greenhouse log does, the way it's understood that everyone here ends up doing two jobs because the works was never built generous enough to allow for one.

This morning the pitch is clean. I work the trellis anyway — cutting back the leaf-growth that's gone shaggy over the wet week, checking the wire ties haven't rusted through, the small unglamorous maintenance that is most of what tending anything actually is. Below me the street is doing its quiet morning version of itself: someone hauling two buckets on a yoke, breathing through the nose, not hurrying, not needing to. A door left unbolted three rooms down because someone came off the night repair crew soaked and needed a floor, and found one, and no one will ask whose floor it was. The stair-tunnel two buildings over drums faintly — not rain, just the runoff finding its scheduled drip, the ceiling keeping its appointment.

I don't expect the chimney to stay clean all season. I don't expect the ninth-terrace wall, three folds west of here, to get properly repaired before the thaw — it's been down six months already, traffic rerouted, and that's simply where it sits now, a landmark in time rather than a wound. What I tend, with the only kind of devotion that makes sense at this elevation, is *held*. The trellis held through last week's hail. The draw holds clean today. That's the whole of the inheritance, passed terrace to terrace, hand to stained hand: not a finished thing, never a finished thing, just the unbroken low-grade attention that keeps the held things held a little longer.

The fog will burn off in patches by noon. It always does, a little. Never all the way. I keep cutting, listening with my palm flat on the warm iron, and somewhere below the foundry breathes on, and somewhere above the cable-cars vanish upward into cloud I have never followed and don't especially want to. This is the terrace I know the voice of. That's enough to be useful, and being useful, here, is the only permanence anyone's actually offered.
