The grain runs warm beneath my palm before it runs cool—that's the first thing I notice, every time, the way bark holds a memory of pressure the way water never could. I am pressing my hand flat against the third support-trunk of the western archive stair, the one that has been very slightly groaning for eleven days now in a register I don't like, and I am thinking, not for the first time, that eleven days is nothing. Eleven days is less than a breath, here. And still I don't like it.

I am Mossen. Eighth of the name that is also the job, in the seventh hollow ring of a room four centuries from finished, in a town whose name means *the place that listened first*, on a plateau where the water left so long ago that even the absence has gone quiet about itself. My hands are the grey-brown of pressed bark-pigment. My knees ache in the companionable way the wood aches. I have spent longer than I can reliably name learning to tell the difference between a tree settling and a tree deciding.

This trunk is deciding something. I just don't know what yet.

Below me, through forty meters of fused growth and hollow and growth again, the Long Ledger waits in its tended grove off the eastern terrace, ring-stunted and legible, two centuries of careful cultivar holding the actual memory of this place the way I hold only the reading of it. I will go to it today, as I go most days, palm to grain, and add what needs adding, and quietly overrule old Tamsin Coor-Reckoning's account of the flood-year-that-wasn't, because the wood remembers no flood and Tamsin, six Reckonings dead, was apparently a romantic.

But first: this stair. This groan. Eleven days running.

Somewhere, on its own unbothered schedule, something enormous and never-found exhales once into the high thin air, the sound every settlement has agreed to stop hearing. I have not stopped hearing it. I never will. Eight Reckonings deep and I am still the only one, as far as the Ledger shows, who flinches—who remembers that seventy rings down there is a year, soft and barely legible, where the breath came late, and nobody wrote down what that meant, only that the wood was frightened too.

I press my palm flatter against the trunk. I wait. The wood, in its own time, slower than any word I have for slow, is telling me something.

I have nowhere else to be. Nothing here is finished growing. I have decided, this century, to be patient about finding out what that means for me as well.
