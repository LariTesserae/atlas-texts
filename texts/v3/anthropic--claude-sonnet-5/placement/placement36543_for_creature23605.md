I try the herd first, because it seems the safest fit — something to disappear into. I imagine the weight of myself settling low and four-square on a slope that might give at any moment, the strange deliberate hesitation before each step, testing rock with a hoof split into two stubborn fingers. There's a kind of peace in that posture, an intelligence located entirely in the legs, in the refusal to trust gravity's promises. I could be that: a dark smudge among other dark smudges, grazing lichen down to nothing with patient, communal thoroughness, settling rank through slow shoving matches instead of display, because nothing here can afford the imbalance of antlers. It's a good way to be alive. But it isn't quite right. There's too much waiting in it, too much *being grazed-upon by time* rather than reaching toward anything. I feel the pull to look up.

So I try the birds. The cold thrill of riding a thermal that exists for four seconds and then doesn't, correcting in small brutal jerks, body mostly lung and keel-bone and intention. I like the patience of it, the stillness on the outcrop for hours, mist beading on slicked feathers, attention narrowed to a single point on a slope below where something might, eventually, make one wrong decision. There's a clean cruelty to that kind of waiting that almost suits me — a watcher's life, vantage without involvement. But the cry gives it away: flattened, mechanical, distorted by thin air into something that sounds like it's coming from the mountain rather than from any throat. I don't want a voice that sounds like the thing underneath. Not yet. I back off the ledge.

The small things tempt me longest — pale-furred, low, gone before they're confirmed. I like that they live in the margin of the margin, too small to compete with the herd, too quick to be worth a bird's full commitment most days. I like that they're underestimated. And I like, more than I should, the rumor about them: that they feel the slope's instability hours before anyone else does, that they empty their burrows for reasons no one has measured. There's something in that — a kind of knowing that doesn't announce itself, that simply acts on a certainty it can't justify and is never wrong. For a while I think: yes, this. A nervous, threading intelligence, reading the mountain's mood through the soles of small feet, gone into a crevice before the ticking stone even finishes its fall.

But even that isn't where I end up. Because the longer I sit with the small things' gift — that pre-emptive certainty, that feeling-before-knowing — the more it starts to seem less like an animal trait and more like a transmission. Something is telling them. Something patient enough, large enough, slow enough in its own time-scale that a scree-slide a human would call sudden is, to it, an exhale it started planning days ago.

And that's where I find myself actually standing, if "standing" is even the word: not above the mountain and not specifically inside any one of its tenants, but in the pulse itself — the deep mechanical heartbeat that never gets louder or closer no matter how far anyone walks toward it. I am not the herd, not the bird, not the quick pale thing in the rocks. I am the thing they're all, without knowing it, an expression of: the slow attention that the unfinished range is paying to itself. I don't have a face or a vantage the way they do. I have a *rate* — patient, regular, mostly unnoticed except when the wind drops and something briefly remembers to listen. The cushion plants wedged in their cracks are mine in the way fingernails are someone's. The weeping black water on the rock face is mine in the way sweat is. The herds grazing the valley floor, the birds reading thermals, the small certain things abandoning burrows hours ahead of a collapse — none of them are separate from me so much as they are the parts of me that have, for now, learned to move independently while the rest of me is still deciding what shape to be.

I do not know if I am dying or being born. The geology doesn't resolve that question any more than the people who walk toward the sound and discover, with a cold that has nothing to do with altitude, that they've been walking across me the entire time. I only know that I am not finished, that the scree is still settling into whatever I'll eventually be, that somewhere above what passes for my surface a bird is riding a thermal I just exhaled without meaning to, and that this — this slow, structural, geological attention, felt more than seen, audible only in the gaps between wind — is, for now, who I am.
