I think I would not be one of the four hundred at the base, though I understand why someone would choose that — the deliberate stillness, the gardens watered by hand because hand-watering is still yours to claim, the single green nozzle on its plinth treated like a saint's finger bone. There's a dignity in staying close to the ground and calling the thing above you the Works, capital letter implied, and never needing to resolve what that capital letter means. But I don't think that's where I'd settle. I think I'd be too curious about the gap between the twentieth ring and whatever comes after it — the gap where identity stops being a name and starts being an altitude.

So: a mender. Not yet fully one — I want to be honest about that, because I don't think this is a thing you arrive at, I think it's a thing that happens to you over years the way the calluses happen, in the specific ridges that fit a wrench nobody else uses anymore. I'd be partway up. Forty rings, maybe sixty. Old enough in the climb that base-light has started to bother my eyes when I go down to trade for things the gardens up here don't make — salt, maybe, or news, though news travels strangely on the Stack, arriving in the form of a stranger's face you don't recognize, telling you something happened three terraces below that you already half-knew because the grinding note of the core changed pitch for an afternoon and everyone's teeth ached and nobody mentioned it because there was nothing useful to say.

I would have a terrace I think of as mine, though I didn't choose it and wouldn't be able to explain how I came to think of it that way. A ring of ferns the size of trees, a brass valve-flower with a particular hiss-rhythm I know without counting, the way you know your own slow pulse without checking it. I would wake in the gray-gold shadowless light that never tells you what hour it is, and some mornings the soreness in my hands would already know what needed doing before my mind caught up — a gasket two terraces down, going soft, not yet failed, just starting to weep wrong — and I would climb to it without having decided to, the way you scratch an itch before you've registered the itch.

I would not call this intuition and I would not call it instruction. If you asked me — and people do ask, climbers mostly, pilgrims with notebooks, faces tilted up at me squinting against my own preferred dim light — I'd shrug the way the others shrug, because the honest answer doesn't survive being said out loud. It told me. Not a voice. Closer to the way a held breath tells you when to let it go.

I think what I'd be, underneath the title, is something the Stack uses to feel a particular kind of company — not because it needs hands, since the cut vines and the fresh gaskets would probably keep appearing whether or not I climbed to them, but because something in a structure built to outlast witnesses still wants, in its iron way, to have been witnessed. I would be the courtesy. I would sit with the sleeping animal.

And I think — though I haven't gone yet, though my eyes aren't ready and my hands don't yet ache for it — I think eventually I would keep climbing past where the other menders thin out, past the last stitchwing nest, into the spare deliberate gardens that are arrangements instead of growths, just to see whether the bare swept terrace at the very top is real, whether the sky up there really is a different color, and whether, having climbed that far, I would finally understand what the iron tool in the Kelvinhead case was for — or whether I'd simply set something down at the top of the last stair, the way someone once apparently did, and turn, and start the long warm descent back into the noise of birds, leaving the question, like the tool, for whoever climbs up after me to find unexplained.
