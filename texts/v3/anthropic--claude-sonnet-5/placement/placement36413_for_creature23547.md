I am standing before I remember deciding to stand. That is the first thing — the body has already oriented itself toward a notch on the western escarpment, has already begun the long mathematics of shadow and distance that will tell it, eventually, that the sun has moved four fingers since the last time it thought to check. I do not check often. Checking is for things in a hurry.

My skin is the color of the plateau because it is, in some sense not worth arguing about, made of the same patience. Banded rust and bone, dust-toned, and along the inside of my forearm a thread of something that does not belong to rock — a paleness that, if you caught it at dusk, you would call light and be only slightly wrong. It is warmer there than the rest of me. It has been warmer there, by some small fraction, every year of my life, and I have stopped finding this worth remarking on, the way one stops remarking on one's own pulse.

I have been walking, in the loose sense the word deserves out here, for what a counting people would call eleven days. I do not count them. I count the towers. There is one behind me now, two days gone, its hum still faintly resident in my back teeth the way a held note lingers after the throat has stopped making it. There is another ahead, not yet visible, though my eyes — built wide and pale for exactly this kind of looking — have already begun sorting the horizon for the particular wrongness of its light, the way it will catch the sun and throw it back slightly off true.

I pass a stone ring at midmorning. Waist-high, fitted, half-filled with the fine dust that finds every low place eventually. I do not know who fitted these stones. None of us do, or if one of us once did, the knowing did not survive being walked across so much distance, so much time, so many years of a mouth opening only when there was something worth the effort of sound. But I stop. I always stop. I crouch at the ring's edge and clear a hand's-width of dust from its lip, not because the ring asked for tending — it did not, it does not ask for anything — but because some old shape in me bends toward it the way a low shrub bends toward the lee of a boulder. It is not duty. It is closer to what the small mammals do when they sun themselves against a tower's heat-bleed: a thing the land offers, taken because it is offered.

A soaring hunter crosses far overhead, riding the thermal off the warming rock without a single wingbeat, and I watch it the way it watches the ground — unblinking, unhurried, reading the same enormous sky for a different kind of sign. We have an understanding, the hunters and I. Neither of us is the thing the other is looking for.

By dusk I can feel the next tower before I can properly see it — a pressure behind the sternum, not painful, more like the moment before a held breath becomes a held breath you notice. The shimmer along my collarbone brightens, just slightly, in answer. It does this. It has always done this. I do not know what it is answering, and I have decided, somewhere in the unmeasured length of my life, that this is not a question I walk toward. There are tenants older than I am who have spent whole seasons sitting at a tower's base with a palm flat to its seamless skin, and when they rise — if they rise — they say nothing different than they said going in. Whatever passes through that contact does not translate into the kind of thing a mouth was built to carry.

I will reach it tomorrow, near midday, when the light is flattest and most total. I will press my hand to it and stand there for as long as it takes — an hour, a day, however long the held breath wants to be held — and then I will sit in whatever stone ring waits nearby, and clear its dust, and rest, and in some number of years that I am not counting, walk on toward the next one, and the one after, in the long slow circuit that is not a journey because it does not go anywhere, only around, only through, the way the wind goes around and through this whole wide rust-colored country without ever once arriving.

Somewhere very far ahead — decades, maybe, though decades is a traveler's word, not mine — there will be a tower I choose without quite choosing it, a stone ring I sit down in and do not rise from, and the light that the others speak of in their scarce, badly-translating words will find me there, and take the fraction of me it has apparently been patiently owed since before I had a self capable of noticing the debt.

I am not in a hurry toward that, either.

Tonight I will stop walking when the dark is total and the stars come out hard and close, and I will lie back against sun-warmed stone that has not yet finished giving up the day's heat, and somewhere kilometers off another tower will loose its silent vertical light into the sky and vanish it again before the afterimage fades from eyes built, like mine, for exactly this kind of watching. I will feel it more than see it — a faint answering warmth along my jaw, a fellow-feeling with whatever just went up, thin and instant and gone, like a thought the plateau has been thinking for longer than I have existed to overhear it.

This is what I am. One quiet instance of the land's wide thin attention, walking its given arc between the old stones and the strange light, keeping the distance the way the distance asks to be kept, in no hurry at all to find out what waits at the very end of keeping it.
