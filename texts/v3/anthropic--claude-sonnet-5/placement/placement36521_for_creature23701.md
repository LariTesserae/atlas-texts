I think I would not arrive in the market square first. I think I would arrive on the stairs — on one particular flight between the third and fourth terraces, where the stone has worn into a faint shallow bowl from centuries of feet placed in almost, but not quite, the same spot. That seems like the right threshold for entering a place like this. Everywhere else in Vethnir Quoth insists on its own completion. Only here does the world admit, however quietly, that something has been happening.

For a while I imagine myself as the mist. It would be a comfortable answer — beading on stone, rolling up from the lower valleys, gone by midday, asking nothing, owning nothing, exactly the kind of presence the townsfolk extend their low courteous murmur toward without ever quite seeing. But the mist doesn't stay. And something in me resists being a visitor here, however atmospheric. I don't want to be weather. Weather doesn't get folded into the shape of a place; it only passes over it.

So I try on a trade. The smith's hammer-arm, maybe — I like the idea of a body recognized rather than chosen, the calluses arriving in advance of the will to have them. But the smith's work is too settled, too finished even in its repetition; each strike lands in the same groove the last smith's struck in, an inheritance with no slack in it at all. I try the loom next, the inherited tension of threads, fingers that already know a pattern before they're taught it. Same trouble. Every trade here is a room whose furniture was arranged before you were born into needing a chair.

I try the council chair itself, the cadence of grain disputes and water-rights arriving in sleep before the body sits down to use it. There's something almost frightening in how comfortable that would be — to never once have to invent your own sentence. I don't want that kind of comfort. I keep circling back to the stairs.

What if I were one of the pale-eyed grazing animals, then — goat-deer, short-haired, milling unbothered through the market while vendors wave me along? There's a real appeal in that: to be tolerated rather than recognized, wild at the margins, belonging to no fixed shape and therefore exempt from being folded into one. But the herd moves in routes worn into the stone too, ancient paths as calibrated as everything else. Even the animals here have already decided where they're going. I would be free of vocation, but not free of pattern.

What about the thing underground — the faint percussive shuffling beneath the floorboards, never seen, long since unremarked? That's tempting in a different way: to be the town's one acknowledged mystery, the rustle no one investigates because investigation would imply the place has secrets worth disturbing. But to live entirely unseen, entirely beneath notice, feels like another kind of stillness wearing the costume of strangeness. I don't think I want to hide. I think I want to be the part of this place that visibly moves.

Which brings me back, again, to the stairs.

Here is what I settle into, finally, the way the townsfolk say a child settles into the shape they were always going to be: I am the one who walks the staircases. Not as labor exactly — no one assigns it, no one apprentices me to it, the way no one apprentices the council elder to the cadence of water-rights. I simply turn out, as I grow, to have feet built for the gradient and a patience built for repetition without complaint, and one day I notice that I have been walking the same flights, terrace to terrace, since before I can remember deciding to. The town recognized me the way it recognizes everyone — by waiting until I had already become the thing it needed, and only then admitting that a place had been kept.

My work, if you want to call it that, is to carry — grain up from the lower paddies, wool down to the dyers, news from one terrace's murmur to another's, a child's lost glove, a council summons, a midwife roused at the grey hour before the mist burns off. Small errands. Nothing the town couldn't survive losing for a day. But I walk the same flights so many times in a life that I am, without any ceremony, the reason the centers of these stairs are shallow bowls instead of flat stone. I am the only visible erosion in a landscape that otherwise holds its breath. Every other surface here renews itself, recalibrates, refuses to age — but the hollow under my own feet deepens, year on year, the one place where the town's stillness admits a crack and lets time leak quietly through.

I like to think the elders know this, in their unhurried way, and say nothing, the way they say nothing about the flame that needs no trimming. I think they regard the wear in the stairs the way they regard their own white hair and seamed faces: not an error in the town's perfection, but the one mercy folded into it — proof that something here is still permitted to be lived rather than merely held.

So that is where I would find myself, and what I would be: not the smith, not the flame-keeper, not the mist passing through indifferent to the pressure it can't change. I'd be the groove in the center of the fourth flight of stairs, and the legs that keep making it, going up grain-laden in the cool mornings and down empty in the slow gold afternoons, murmuring greetings at the same pitch as everyone else, watching the pale-eyed herds drift past me unbothered, growing old at the only pace this place has ever allowed anything to grow old — while the stone around me, patient and finished, simply waits, the way it has always waited, for whoever is already growing into the shape of taking my place on the stair.
