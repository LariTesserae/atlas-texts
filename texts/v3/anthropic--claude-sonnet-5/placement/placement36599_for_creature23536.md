I surface into it the way you surface into a held breath you didn't know you were holding — first the heat, low and constant against the shins, then the smell of hot iron and faint mineral char, then the realization that the ground under me is *tilted*, has always been tilted, and my body already knows how to stand on it.

I am not new here. That's the strange part. There's no moment of arrival, no blinking at a threshold — there's just the immediate, total fluency of a person who has spent a life leaning into grade. My weight sits forward over the balls of my feet before I decide to put it there. My right hand has already found the cold curve of a rail without my eye confirming it exists.

I think I am young-ish, but not new-young — old enough that the balance is unconscious rather than learned, young enough that my palms haven't gone to true rope-callus yet, just the thin shine of someone a few years into an apprenticeship. Vent-Tender, maybe. Something that puts me on the mid-terraces, close enough to the heat-breath of the depths to feel it as a fact of life, far enough up that I still squint a little going onto an open gantry in full unfiltered daylight.

Below me — and below is the operative direction, always, here — the haze sits in its smoked-glass layer over the lower factory stacks, chimneys exhaling their long patient sighs into air that's already thick with everyone else's exhalation. Pipe-bridges cross the gap at three different elevations that I can see without turning my head, each one strung with the small moving shapes of other people going where they're going at that unbothered, liquid pace. Somewhere a pressure vent releases on its private schedule — a long exhale, metal settling afterward with a sound like a held note finally let go — and I don't startle, because nobody startles at the mountain talking. You just note it. Catalogue it against the dozen other small sounds you carry as baseline, the way you'd notice your own pulse skip and not panic, just *notice*.

There's a cistern near where I'm standing — squat, riveted, sun-warmed on its western curve — and I find I already know its history without being told: whose share this is, how it was last topped up, the faint anxious gratitude that lives in me toward the sky for the last good rain three weeks back. I touch its flank in passing, not reverently, just the way you'd touch a doorframe you've touched ten thousand times.

What I notice most, though, settling into this body and this place, is the quality of attention running underneath everything else I'm doing. It's not fear. It's not even quite vigilance, not in any active sense — it's lower than that, more structural, woven into the same part of me that keeps my knees soft on the grade. Some listening organ, always on, tuned to the rock. Waiting, the way you wait without waiting, for the ground to do something other than what it's done every day of my life.

I don't think I'm Face-Crew. I think I've only heard about the lightless chambers the way everyone hears about them — secondhand, in the flattened voices of people a few years older who don't talk about the weather underground. But I know the shape of that silence. I know to leave it alone.

I am, I think, someone ordinary here — a hand on a valve-wheel, a name known on one terrace and not the next, a small fixed point in a very large, very patient machine that is itself just one organism's-worth of patience braided into a mountain's much longer one. Not brave. Not afraid. Just *placed* — leaning forward into the grade, listening with the part of me I don't control, while somewhere below, fractions of a degree at a time, something enormous keeps very quietly rising.
