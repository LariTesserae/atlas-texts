The air arrives first — thin, and costing something to take in, so I draw it the long slow way, low into the belly, the way my mother's hands taught my breathing before they taught me anything else. I am standing on the rim of the third terrace, where the salt-crust is thick enough to bear weight without crunching, and the wheel under my palm is warm from yesterday's sun even though the night hasn't fully let go of the metal yet.

My feet are wide and soft-soled and they know this ground without needing my eyes to confirm it — a slight give here, a harder patch three steps left where the crust runs thin over packed mineral, the faint vibration through the sole that means the pipe beneath is carrying water right now, right under this exact spot, on its way to a settling tank I have never seen the inside-purpose of, only its outside-procedure: crack the gate at the second tick of cooling metal, no sooner.

I don't know why. My grandmother didn't know why either, though her hands knew the wheel better than mine still do, forty years of turning it smoothing her knuckles into something like the rims of the pools themselves. She is the reason I know this is the *second* tick and not the first, the reason my shoulder drops half an inch when I mean "wait" to the tender working the next terrace over, fifty meters off, too far for words to survive the crossing but not too far for that small grammar of shoulder and wrist.

Overhead one of the broad-winged things has been standing on the tower rail since midday, not moving, the way I am not moving now, the two of us keeping a kind of company that doesn't require acknowledgment. Below us, in water that looks too still to hold anything, the pale segmented lives go on doing whatever it is they do to stay so improbably, abundantly alive on a diet of nothing but mineral rumor.

Soon the light will go from glare to almost nothing in the space of a few held breaths, and the towers will start their ticking contraction, and I will walk — not hurry, the ground doesn't forgive hurry, swallows it the way it swallows sound — out to the catwalk rail to stand beside whoever else has come out to stand. We won't speak much. Maybe someone will hum, low enough to lose inside the engine-hum, and I won't ask why, because I am not visiting this rule. I am made of it.
