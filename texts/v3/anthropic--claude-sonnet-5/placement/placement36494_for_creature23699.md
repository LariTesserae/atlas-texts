I find myself somewhere around the second tier down, on a catwalk that has gone slightly soft underfoot where moss has gotten into the grating, and I am — I notice this the way you notice a held breath you didn't know you were holding — not standing still. My weight is low, my steps are short and flat, and there's a vine looped at shoulder height that I duck without looking at, the way you duck a doorframe in your own house.

So: an Undergrader. Not young — the stoop is already in me, just starting, a rounding at the shoulders that hasn't yet become the elders' deep permanent curve but is practicing toward it. My hands are a tender's hands. I know this because when I reach out to the belt-drive beside me, the gesture is already half-finished before I decide to make it — fingers finding the place where the housing's iron gives way to a soft collar of fitted moss, checking it the way you'd check a bandage, or a loaf of bread for doneness. Somewhere in the last twenty years I stopped thinking of this as two jobs.

There's a basin near my station, shallow, stone-cut, a third full. I know without counting that it's owed two mornings' collection from the family upstream on the catwalk, and I know I'll mention it to them not as a demand but as a fact dropped quietly into a sentence about something else entirely, the way you'd mention weather if weather here meant anything. My voice, when I do speak — to no one yet, just testing it against the thick air — comes out close and flat, pitched to die within a few feet. It's a register I didn't choose. It chose me, the way the gait did, the way the soft-edged economical body did, built by a climate and a debt-ledger and forty years of people learning not to startle the quiet.

Above me, several stories up through pipe and leaf and scaffold-lattice, there's a pale wash of something I could call daylight if I were being generous to it. I don't look up there much. Not from fear — it's not loud enough in me to be fear — just a kind of settled disinterest, the same disinterest I feel toward the dark unlit tier below where the machines give out and the cave resumes and something occasionally creaks that isn't wood and isn't iron. Both directions are edges. I live, like everyone here, in the warm thick middle of the bowl, where the engines breathe slow and the moss insulates the pipes I used to scrape clean, back when I still thought the green was something to be fought rather than trained.

If you asked me what I am, I don't think "person" would be the first word that arrived. I think it would be something more like: a tender. A small, conserving motion in a held breath that the whole sinkhole takes together, drip by scheduled drip, season after soft humid season — sweat-glazed, sallow-pale, flushed dark at the throat, listening with half an ear to a belt-drive's pitch to know if the groan in it is the wood settling or the iron beginning, at last, after all this patient tending, to fail.
