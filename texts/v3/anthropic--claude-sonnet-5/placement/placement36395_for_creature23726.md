I think I would be the one who tends the third valve from the east gantry — the sticking one, the one that nobody else has the patience for anymore, though patience here is so ambient it's almost rude to claim more of it than your neighbor.

I find myself crouched in the wet-stone hour just after dawn, when the basin's walls are still sweating and the light hasn't yet found its way past the canopy to do anything but rim the leaves gold. My hands are damp before I've touched anything. That's just the cup's doing. There's a vine — I don't know its name, nobody bothered to name it, it arrived sometime before I did and simply continued — that has wound itself three-quarters around the valve's iron throat, and every morning I have the same small negotiation with it: not to remove it, never that, just to loosen its grip enough that the valve can breathe when the pressure climbs toward noon. I do this with the flat of my palm and a kind of murmured nothing, words that aren't instructions to the plant so much as a noise I make to keep my own hands slow. The vine doesn't listen. I've decided that doesn't matter.

I notice, crouched there, that my knuckles have gone soft-cracked in a pattern that matches the rope I coil every evening, and that there's a faint green at the seams of my sleeve that wasn't dye and wasn't intended and arrived anyway, the way everything here arrives — uninvited, slow, permanent. I don't remember deciding to let the basin write itself into my clothes. It simply did, the way it does to everyone who stays past a season.

What I am, I think, is something that used to have a clearer edge. I remember — distantly, the way you remember a season rather than a day — arriving with a faster clock in me, irritated by the long pause before anyone answered a question, impatient with the chuff and drip and the four-hour breath of the vents. That clock is gone now, or buried so deep under the boiler-rhythm that I can't find it to wind it back up even if I wanted to. I check my answers against the moss now. I didn't notice it happening. I noticed only that it had.

There's a heron-thing that stands in the creek some mornings while I work the valve, close enough that I could touch it if I leaned, and it doesn't move for me any more than I move for it. We have an arrangement neither of us drafted. I leave a little food near the same flat stone out of something that isn't quite charity — more like topping off a cistern that asks for nothing but receives anyway, because withholding would feel like a kind of rudeness the basin doesn't have a word for.

If you asked me to explain why the vine curls toward the heat instead of away from it, or why the iron has been grown around rather than built around, I'd give you the shrug everyone gives — minerals in the runoff, warmth being warmth wherever you find it — and I'd mean it, not as an evasion but because the question has stopped being a question for me. It became weather. I became, somewhere in the absorbing, something less like a worker and more like a fern that happens to also fix valves: rooted, slow, mildly defiant of the water budget of my own attention, growing toward the warm iron not because I decided to but because everything in this cup eventually does.

So: a tender. The third valve, the green-seamed sleeve, the unnamed vine, the heron's quiet tolerance. Young land, young settlement, and a self that arrived with sharper edges than it has now — smoothed, like the stone, by water that no longer runs in any great quantity but never quite stopped either.
