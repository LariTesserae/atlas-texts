The cold comes up through the plank before the sound does — that's the order it always happens in. First the iron-chill leaching through soaked wood into the soles of my feet, telling me I've stopped too long in one place, and then, underneath that, the breathing.

I am crouched at the lip of the third house, the one my mother's mother tended before her hands went soft and stopped trusting themselves. My knees ache in the particular way that means weather is changing somewhere beyond the rise, though we will not feel it here — the basin keeps its own weather, has kept it as long as anyone's kept anything. I have been still long enough that something small and many-legged has resumed its progress across the stone an arm's length from my boot, unbothered, taking me for geology again. I let it.

The engine under my palm — I have one hand flat against the casing, fingers spread, an old habit, my mother's habit before mine — is breathing wrong. Not badly wrong. Not the deep stopped-silence wrong that means run, means knock the pipe-code three-two-three down the line and pray someone's hands are free. Just a hitch, a small catch on the upstroke, like a held cough. I have heard this before. Three winters ago, the second house had this same hitch and it meant a valve-leather gone soft, swollen with damp past its tolerance, needing replacement with a strip cut fresh from cured gut and worked in by feel since no eye could find the seat in that dark casing-throat.

I do not see well past the nearest ten stones in this light, which is the only light there is, the sky doing its permanent impression of an evening that never quite arrives at night. I don't need to. My nose tells me the water two channels over has gone slightly sour — old-mineral sour, the smell of a stone shifted somewhere upstream, exposing something that doesn't like air — and I will need to go look, lift stones, find what moved, set it back before the channel sweetens itself again or doesn't. My hands, broad, a little webbed at the first knuckle, find the casing's seams without needing my eyes' cooperation; they know this iron the way they know my own ribs.

On the shelf inside, behind me, in the brick dark that holds the day's leaked warmth: a curved plate of something not-quite-metal, salvaged from beneath the lichen two stones from where I now crouch, kept though it cooks nothing, binds nothing, means nothing I can use it for. I don't ask it to mean anything. It sits where my mother's mother put it, gathering nothing but the same patient damp that gathers on everything else here, and some nights, with the pistons going below in their slow patient four-count, I put my hand near it the way I put my hand on the casing, listening for some breath of its own. It has never had one. I keep listening anyway. That, too, is tending.

The hitch comes again under my palm — there, that catch — and I lean in, get my ear to the cold iron, and go to work on the only question this place has ever actually asked me: *what does the engine need*, not *what is the engine for*. Somewhere off through the dim, another tender's hammer finds a pipe, three notes, a question traveling faster than a voice could carry it through all this wet. I lift my own hammer and answer: *handled. no help needed yet.* The basin breathes on, unbothered, and I bend back over my work, soft-eyed, sharp-eared, exactly where I have always been.
