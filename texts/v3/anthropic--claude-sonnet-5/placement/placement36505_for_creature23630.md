The knock comes through the joist before it comes through the air — three taps, pause, two — and I feel it in the soles of my feet before my ears sort it out of the morning roar. Mara, two platforms over, asking if the eel-traps came up heavy. I tap back against the rope-rail without looking up from what I'm doing: *light, check the east channel instead.* She'll feel that answer arrive the same way I felt hers.

I am cataloguing condensation. That's the polite name for it. My actual title, if you forced me to give one to a stranger from outside, is *Listener-in-training*, third year, of the Low Cistern lineage, which means my grandmother's grandmother's name is also the name of the building I am currently sitting inside the bones of, six courses down, where the ceiling is close enough that I have never once stood fully upright in my own home without thinking about it first. I like it down here. The old quarters smell like mineral and damp stone and the particular sourness of standing water that's been standing a long time, and I would not trade it for the bright new lattice three hundred meters up where my cousin lives, where the air is dry and the ceilings are generous and, I am told, faintly contemptuous, you can see actual sky.

My hands are out right now against a support pylon's lower flank, palm flat, fingers spread, feeling for the texture of its hum. This is most of what the training is — not theory, not anything you could write on a slate and hand to someone. You put your hand on enough joists for long enough years that eventually a wrong note arrives in your body before it arrives in your head, the way you'd flinch from a held note going sour before you could say *why*. Grandmother Ossa, who is not actually my grandmother but everyone under eighty calls her that, says I have maybe another decade before my ear is worth trusting on anything that matters. I believe her. I have been wrong about three "wrongnesses" already this year, false alarms, embarrassing in front of the strippers crew who had to halt a perfectly good demolition because I didn't like a groan that turned out to be nothing but an old door deciding to swell shut for the wet season.

But this pylon. This one I don't like.

It's not loud-wrong. It's quiet-wrong, which is worse, which is the kind Ossa always warns about hardest — a structure going *too* still in a place that should be breathing the way it always breathes, drinking its little ankle of standing water and exhaling it cool somewhere four stories up the way it's done since before anyone alive can account for. I press my ear flat to the ceramic-glass, feel the grain of it cold against my cheekbone despite the morning heat already building, and listen under the market-roar, under the drip-chime off the awning above me, under Mara's eel-traps and a flock of roost-things arguing two lattice-gaps over.

Nothing. Which is the diagnosis. Which means I have to climb up and find someone whose ear outranks mine before this nothing becomes someone's flooded floor by evening.

I tap twice against the rail — *going up, watch the channel* — and Mara's answer comes back faint through the rope before I've even let go of it. Then I climb, bare feet finding the wet rungs without needing to look, up out of the old cistern-dark and into the layered roar of everything the Floor is doing at once above me, carrying a small bad silence in my hands like something that might still be put down gently, if I hurry.
