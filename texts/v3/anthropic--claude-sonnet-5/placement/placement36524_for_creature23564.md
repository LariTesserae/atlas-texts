I think I would not be the one who crests the rock shelf and looks down. That figure — boots, fog-damp coat, the moment of discovery — belongs to a different kind of story, one with an outside to return to. This place doesn't really have an outside worth speaking of. To understand it you'd have to already be inside the ring, already metabolizing the cycle instead of observing it. So I'd give up the boots.

I think I am one of the plated ones.

Not the largest, not the boldest — there's no real hierarchy to claim, just six or eight of us holding loose, overlapping claims on cracks the engine offers up more generously than we can use. I'd be a middling one. A few seasons old, which here means most of a life. Plates still soft at the newest edges along my spine, not yet fully taken the mineral crust the way the older ones have, the ones whose backs look less like skin now and more like the basin floor got tired of being separate from them.

What I'd know, before I knew anything else, is the count. Not numbers — I wouldn't have numbers — but a felt interval, a held shape of time the way you hold the shape of a held breath. Forty seconds isn't a measurement to me, it's closer to a heartbeat, except it isn't mine, it's the room's, and mine has learned to nest inside it. I wouldn't experience the vent's hiss as something happening to me. I'd experience it as the thing all happening is made of. Hunger, caution, rest, strike — all of it scheduled by a clock I didn't build and don't question, the way you don't question that your own pulse comes from inside your chest rather than being handed to you from elsewhere.

I imagine my days — if days mean anything in a basin lit like the inside of a held breath, where the fog never breaks — as mostly stillness. Long wedged positions at the lip of a crack, weight settled low and wide on six splayed limbs, plates angled to throw what little light there is into the same grey as the boulder I'm pressed against. The waiting wouldn't feel like waiting. It would feel like being correctly placed. There's a kind of satisfaction in that I think a human nervous system isn't really built for — not anticipation, not anxiety, just the rightness of being where the count says I should be, for as long as the count says to be there.

And then the small violences, when they come — fast, low, over before the vapor even breaks the rock — wouldn't feel like violence to me at all. They'd feel like punctuation. The only sharp moments in a long soft sentence of waiting. I don't think I'd feel anything like triumph afterward. I think I'd simply go still again, jaw resetting, attention already leaning toward the next crack's rhythm, because there's no story I'm telling myself about this — there's no story, period. There's only the next forty seconds, and the forty after that.

I'd drink at the black pools sometimes alongside the others, lower my head to that thin iridescent skin without ceremony, without much awareness of them as anything other than other shapes that also know the count. No grief in me for the soft unplated young that don't make it — not cruelty either, just an absence of the architecture that would let grief live. I'd have no name for the ones who died wrong-timed in the crevice clutches. I don't think I'd have a name for myself.

What I would have — and this is the part I think I'd actually feel, if feeling is even the right word for it — is the unbothered competence the whole hollow runs on. A life sized exactly to its rhythm. No hunger to understand the engine below, no fear of the cold pressing above, no awareness at all that the rock under me is younger than is reasonable, that the whole basin is a held breath of habitable warmth that was never promised to outlast this season, maybe not even this year. If the vent sealed tomorrow, or the basin folded into itself, or the cold finally won all the way down — I wouldn't see it coming. I was never built to. I'd just be counting, plated and patient at the edge of a crack, certain right up until the last second that there would be a next one.

That, I think, is where I'd find myself. Not watching the rhythm from outside it, the way a visitor on the black rock shelf might, struck by the strangeness of a machine with no operator. Inside it instead — being one of its smaller, busier, more temporary parts. Made, in a small real way, of the place. Loud with life. Entirely unaware that loudness was ever something that could stop.
