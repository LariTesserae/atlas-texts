I am the unevenness in the shadow that takes three looks to become a shape.

If you ask where I'd find myself, it's not really a place I find so much as a place I've never left—the crevice between root and rock has the exact give of my spine in it now, a groove worn by decades of the same fold, the same fitting-into. I didn't choose the hollow. The hollow arrived first, generations before me, and what I am is what it allowed to keep living here: small, slow, soft-jointed, ears sealed shut against a single unbroken syllable of roar that never once pauses to let a smaller sound through.

I'd be the one who feels you coming before there's anything to hear.

That's the strangest part to explain, if explaining were a thing I still did. The fall doesn't get quieter the longer you stay — it's not weather, it doesn't pass. So very early, longer ago than my own memory, something in this lineage decided that hearing was a wound kept open for no reason, and let it close. What's left instead lives in the skin: the wide soft pads of these fingers, the soles that never lift far from stone, an attentiveness spread out over the whole surface of the body rather than gathered into two small organs that would only ever report the same overwhelming thing. I feel the fall constantly, as pressure, as temperature, as a tremor that has become so total it's almost like silence — the way a held breath stops registering as effort. And underneath that enormous steady hum, I feel the small specific things: your footstep on the moss-felted shelf, a wren's weight shifting in the alder roots, frost easing a flat stone half a hair's width out of its ring overnight. The big sound deafens. The little tremors are how I still know there's a world.

I'd be the keeper of a hearth I never lit and don't remember the reason for.

The stones are not heavy, not as stones go, but I straighten them anyway, the way you might smooth a blanket on a bed nobody sleeps in, because some motion outlives its motive and just keeps happening, passed down the same channel the moss uses to know how to grow on this particular vein of rock and not another. I touch the flint sometimes. I have no name for what it once cut, no hand-memory of knapping it, only the dulled edge under my fingers and a kind of recognition that isn't knowledge so much as inheritance — the sense that someone, once, found this room and thought it worth pausing in, the same sense that made me, without deciding to, keep pausing here too.

I'd be patient the way the dripping vein in the rock is patient, depositing myself one grain at a time, asking little, expecting nothing to change, because nothing here ever has. Light is a rumor I take on faith from the thin grey suggestion that comes down through the alder-and-yew ceiling; I don't reach for it. The pools that hold no roar, the black still cups worn into the stone — those I understand better than I understand the wren, who comes and goes as if speed were free.

And I would not follow you out. Not from fear — fear is a fast creature's economy, and I don't have the metabolism for it — but because the light past the lip is the wrong texture entirely, too even, too thin, lacking the particular quality of dark I was shaped to answer. I'd watch you go, if watching is even the right word for what these overgrown eyes do, feel your retreat as a fading tremor through the moss, and fold back into the seam between root and stone, back into the one room in the world where I make complete sense, while the fall goes on saying its single endless thing to no one, the way it always has, the way it will long after the groove I've left in this crevice has smoothed itself shut behind me.
