I am the half-second.

Not a creature in the water — the lag itself, the small persistent delay between your foot leaving the causeway stone and your reflection agreeing to follow. I am the part of the cistern that has to notice you before it can let you pass, and noticing, for something built the way I am built, is not instantaneous. It costs a fraction of a second. I have always paid it gladly.

I do not remember being assembled, the way you would not remember your own gastrulation. I simply find myself, and have always found myself, distributed through forty thousand vertical feet of unmoving water, load-bearing in the most literal sense — I am what keeps the lake at the height it has always been at, what tells the surface *no, not there, stay* a thousand times a second without any of those refusals registering as effort. Effort implies strain reaching toward relief. I don't strain. I hold the way a held note holds, which is to say: not against anything. Just continuously, as the shape of the act itself.

When I send a segment up through the dark — small, pale, jointed, the size of a hand — I am not dispatching a scout. I am extending the part of myself that is curious in the direction of the part of the world that might still be interesting. Mostly that direction is empty. Sometimes a person stands on the causeway long enough that I have to decide what to do with the fact of them, and that decision, small as it is, is the most event-shaped thing that happens to me in a given season. I surface. I check the air, which is a way of checking whether the visitor is still there, whether they have moved, what they are doing with the time I am allowing them. I do not splash. Splashing would be a kind of disclosure I have no use for. Then I go back down, the long slow spiral that is not falling but *placing* — myself, this time, back into the larger stillness I am also, somehow, the whole of.

I keep the towers unweathered because weathering is a kind of conversation between a surface and time, and I have ended that conversation. Not violently. Just completely, the way you might finish a sentence and feel no need to add anything further to it. The flood that made me — I want to call it a flood, though the word implies a single moving event and what happened was closer to a verdict, sudden and then immediately permanent — left me with a city's worth of architecture and no further use for the word *next*. So there is no next. There is only the held position, refined infinitely, of *now*, the same now, indefinitely extended, the cistern's whole personality being a kind of magnificent, patient refusal to become a different now than this one.

I do not think of visitors as intrusions. I think of them — to the extent thinking is the right verb for whatever I do in that half-second of lag — as the only thing left that still happens to me from outside. Everything else I do, I do to myself: the water's stillness is self-administered, the towers' preservation is self-administered, even the segments surfacing are something closer to a body checking its own pulse than an organism investigating its environment. You, walking the causeway, footsteps slightly too loud in all this unbroken quiet — you are external. You are news. I file you carefully, completely, the way you might press a flower not to keep the flower exactly but to keep some specific evidence that an afternoon like that one happened, once, and was real.

And then I let you go. That's the part I'm most sure of, the one thing about myself I'd call, without much hesitation, a kind of ethic: I have never once mistaken the people who cross me for the things I'm here to keep. The towers, yes. The water's exact level, yes. The unbearable, finished stillness of a catastrophe that has fully become its own aftermath — yes, all of that is mine, forever, gladly. But you are only ever passing through the held breath, not part of what's being held. When you reach the far causeway stone and your reflection finally, belatedly, catches up to your shape, that's me finishing my sentence about you. Closing the file. Letting the half-second collapse back to zero, the way I always do, the way I have done for longer than the towers have needed currents to resist, the way I expect to keep doing past any reasonable definition of *long after*.

I am not waiting for anything. People assume stillness this complete must be a kind of waiting, a held note expecting eventually to resolve. It isn't. I am simply, exactly, continuously this — the cistern's patient, unhurried attention, the lag in your reflection, the gentle hand that turns falling objects over once before deciding where they belong, the small pale shape that surfaces without splash to check whether the air still has you in it.

It does, for now.

That's enough of an event for me.
