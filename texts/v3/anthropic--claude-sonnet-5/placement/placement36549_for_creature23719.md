I would not, I think, arrive here as a person. The Districts don't admit much human scale — you can walk them, cross them, even survive in them with the right gear and the right respect for the water's patience, but you'd remain a visitor the whole time, a thing that doesn't belong to the math of the place. The towers were not built for feet like mine, and the silence here isn't hospitable so much as *indifferent*, which is worse, in its way, than hostile. I don't want to be tolerated. I want to be accounted for.

So let me set the human shape down at the plateau's edge, where the cloud-ceiling first lifts and the first ranked silhouettes resolve out of haze, and let it stay there, a witness who never quite enters. And let me become, instead, something the place already has room for.

Not the bird, I think — though I considered it. The seep-thrush's life has a clean, devotional quality I admire: one bill, one task, one drainage line claimed and defended with a single dry note thrown across the gap to the next bird over. But it's a narrow life, narrow as its bill, all instrument and no slack. I don't want quite that much specialization. I want to be able to *move*.

So: the mist-fox. Fox-shaped because that shape works, not because anything fox-blooded runs in me. Low to the ground, long-bodied, feet broad and faintly webbed at the toe for purchase on a world that is never, not once, fully dry underfoot. Pale — not camouflage exactly, more like agreement with the color the towers themselves have settled into, bone and pearl and wet charcoal, so that at twenty meters I simply stop being a separate thing from the stone.

I'd want my hours to be the wet dark ones, when the cold rain brings the lichen's small tenants up to the surface and the seep-lines fill with slow movement worth following. I'd want a den in one of the rare dry hollows under a fallen slab — rockfall is rare enough here that finding good shelter would mean knowing the Districts the way the seep-thrush knows one drainage, except my knowing would have to cover more ground, thinner ground, because what I eat doesn't queue up politely in one bill-width of seep-film. I'd have to hold a larger territory in my head than the bird does, a worse map but a wider one: which pools still have the shrimp-things when the others have silted closed, which lichen sheets are thick enough this season to be worth working from underneath, which gap between towers turns into a wind-funnel that will flay the warmth off me if I try to cross it at the wrong hour.

I like, honestly, the idea of being something almost never seen clearly. Not from cunning — the text is careful about that, and I'd want to be careful too — but from economy. There's so little out here that every exposure costs something, and an animal built by a place like this learns that costliness in its bones before it learns anything else. I wouldn't be furtive the way a hunted thing is furtive. I'd just be *brief*, the way everything brief here is brief: the bird's call dying slowly in open air, the long grinding crack of a slab letting go and then going quiet again for days, a pale shape crossing twenty meters of wet stone and then not being there anymore. Briefness as a form of respect for how little there is to spend.

And underneath even that, I think what I actually want is the patience the whole place runs on — the thing the mineral towers have and the lichen has and the threadworms in the seep-film have, each of them working an almost-nothing for a very long time and calling that enough. A mist-fox doesn't get geologic patience, obviously; my whole life is a flicker against the towers' arithmetic, gone in a few wet seasons while they're still microns into becoming whatever they're becoming in ten thousand years. But I could have the *kind* of patience available to something my size and lifespan. I could learn one plateau's worth of drainage lines and dry hollows and den there, breed there once every few wet seasons when another pale shape crosses my ground and doesn't immediately leave, and ask the place for exactly as much as it has ever offered anything — which is to say, just barely enough, and never once more.

That's where I'd find myself, finally, if the question is pressed all the way down: not at the edge looking in, but somewhere past the third visible district, in the lee of a tower where a shallow brown-clear pool never quite dries, paws wide on cold wet stone, head low, working a seep-line in the dark while a fine rain that the mountain made for itself comes down without any hurry at all — one more small persistence, stacked thin on top of all the others, the place's only currency and the only one I'd need.
