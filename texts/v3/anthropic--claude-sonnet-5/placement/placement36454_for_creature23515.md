I am standing on a terrace called Ninth Cut, on the sunward face of a spire the maps call Verrin's Needle and my grandmother called, simply, home, and I am waiting for the light to finish crossing the bloom-sheet at my feet before I touch it.

This is the part outsiders never understand when they come up the switchback rail with their notebooks and their patient, condescending questions: that the work is mostly waiting. I am a crystal-assayer. My basket is empty, my chisel is sheathed at my hip, bone-handled, worn to the shape of my own palm by my mother's hand before mine, and I have not moved in eleven minutes because the sheet in front of me is still drinking the last of its four hours of direct sun, and a bloom read in shadow lies to you. It tells you it's spent when it isn't. It tells you it's rich when it's only catching light at a flattering angle. You learn this the way you learn anything here — slowly, by example, by being wrong in front of someone who loves you enough to let you be wrong and correct you quietly afterward.

My calves are the calves they all have, the ones outsiders photograph without asking, dense as rope from forty years of ladder and stair. My eyes have the squint. I do not need a compass here, have never needed one — I think in terrace-numbers and rail-stops, in *up past the foundry bell* and *down where the third spur forks*, and the one time I traveled to the basin-floor survey camp and a cartographer asked me which way was north I had to stop and actually calculate it, like long division, like a foreign tongue.

Below me, a soft chiseling percussion — climbers, maybe thirty of them, working a seam two terraces down, pale segmented bodies catching the light in quick chitinous flickers as they swing and graze. I know that colony. I have known it eleven years, since it moved off the played-out vein on Eighth and resettled here, and I think of them the way you think of difficult neighbors you've stopped resenting: present, competing, occasionally useful. When they cluster like that, dense and sudden after an hour of nothing, my father taught me to mark it on the chart, because climbers find the rich seams before any drill does. They have never once been wrong about where the mountain is generous. They have also never once explained how it stays generous — how this sheet I'm about to harvest will, in a year, in five, show no thinner than it does today, though I will have taken baskets from it every season I'm strong enough to climb to it.

The mountain keeps its own books. My uncle used to say it with a laugh worn thin enough to see through. He went down once, on a deep crew, below the seventh foundry level where the rock goes warm and soft enough to cut like cheese, and he came up nine days early and asked for reassignment to a surface tap and never said why, and at his funeral — bell-rung, three clean strikes that carried all the way to Twelfth Terrace though it was a still, swallowing morning — nobody asked either. We are not an incurious people. We are a *patient* one, which is a different thing, and patience here has learned, over generations, exactly where its own asking stops being useful.

The light finishes its crossing. The sheet in front of me goes from raked gold to flat, true daylight, and I crouch, and I read it properly now — the soft glitter of accreted salt over the harder vitreous bloom beneath, the faint variations in sheen that tell a trained eye where the chisel will bite clean and where it'll shatter and waste half a morning's yield. I start to cut. Behind me and far above, riding the heat coming off this very face without a single wingbeat, one of the gliders that the Eighteenth rail crew calls Old Cinder — wing-pattern like a smear of ash across the membrane, flying these thermals longer than I've been alive — turns its slow, unbothered circle, watching the basin floor for something smaller to misstep.

Somewhere in the porous rock above the foundry stacks, in fissures with names I learned before I learned my numbers, a chord begins, low, almost beneath hearing, and I stop my chisel without deciding to, the way you'd stop mid-sentence for a relative clearing their throat. It might spread. Three districts might pick it up within the hour, humming together for reasons the engineers' disagreeing instruments will fail, again, to reconcile. Or it might be just this fissure, just this once, talking to itself.

I go back to the bloom. The basket fills slowly, the way everything here fills slowly — a vein worth my granddaughter's granddaughter, a chord the mountain is thinking out loud, a colony of pale climbers keeping their own patient census of where the rock is still willing to give. I do not need to know where the mass comes from to harvest it honestly. I do not need to know what's sealed in the warm dark below the eighth level to live well above it. This is not incuriosity. This is the specific, inherited humility of a people who have built three generations of foundries and rail-lines on top of a mystery and found that the foundries run fine regardless — that some ledgers don't have to balance from where you're standing for the work to still be worth doing.

The light will move on in another hour, sliding its slow checkerboard across to the next face, and I will follow it, basket on my hip, chisel warm from my hand, one more small and unhurried entry in a book the mountain has been keeping, indifferently, since long before there was anyone here to read it back.
