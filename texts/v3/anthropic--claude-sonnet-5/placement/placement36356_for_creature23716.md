The bell I'm listening for isn't mine to ring yet, which is how I know I'm still young, even at forty-some years with moss-stain worked so deep into my knuckle-creases it'll never wash pale again.

I'm a rope-and-bridge keeper — Old Maeve's-wall lineage, "born in the third Hollin re-thatch," if you're asking the way we ask. Lean, yes, swagger, yes, the gentle teasing I pretend not to hear when I cross a span one-handed just to make some terrace-keeper's grandchild gasp. My forearms are the part of me that aged first and best, ropy and overbuilt, hands that read a fiber's rot through three layers of oiled wrap without looking.

Here is where you'd find me: out past the last drinking-cisterns, on the long vine bridge between the goat-shelf and the eastern terrace, mid-morning — though mid-morning means nothing here except that the bells say so, three clatters down in the lower basin, arriving, as they always do, from somewhere above my head instead of below it. I don't look up. You learn not to. I keep my eyes on the work: a frayed lashing where the bridge-deck meets the anchor-post, going soft and dark the way fiber goes when it's a season from giving out under somebody's weight at the worst possible moment. My satchel's open beside me, dry-side up, the spare cordage coiled inside it kept scrupulously apart from the wet tools, because some habits you don't break even when your hands could do it blind.

The rain is doing what it does — not falling, just being — sheeting off the broad leaves above me in ropes you could almost mistake for solid things, drumming the moss-skin of the world into that single low register that stopped sounding like noise to me before I was ten. Underneath it, the moss-frogs are tuning up early, arguing about something in the lip of the goat-shelf cistern. A heavy-bodied tree-squirrel drops a fruit pit onto the shelf roof below me — tithe paid, indifferent — and somewhere off to my left, or maybe behind me, or maybe nowhere at all, someone laughs.

I know that laugh. I've known it longer than I've known how to tie this particular knot.

It isn't her. She went under the moss two re-thatches back, the thatch-mother whose roof-names half this settlement still carries without meaning to. But the basin doesn't return sound honestly — never has, never will — and I've made my private, evidence-free accounting same as everyone does, and decided that *this* laugh, arriving wrong, arriving sideways out of a cliff face that has no mouth, is hers, is allowed to be hers, costs nothing to let it be hers.

So I don't startle. I finish the lashing, test it once, hard, with my whole weight leaned back against the anchor-post the way you're not supposed to alone, the way everyone does anyway. The fiber holds. Good work, if I say so. Somewhere a bell I'll never quite point to correctly rings true, and I let myself be kept company by a sound that has no business coming from where it comes from, and I call it luck, because that's what we call it here, and head back along the dripping rope toward whatever the bells say is lunch.
