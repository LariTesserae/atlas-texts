The condensation gathers first at the joint of what would be a knee, if I had ever needed a knee for kneeling rather than for folding the long bones into that particular angle the herons taught me, or that I taught them, so long ago the teaching and the learning have become the same gesture viewed from two directions. I am standing at the edge of the third pool — not because it is the third, there is no first or second, only the order I have decided to walk in today, which is itself a small luxury I allow myself, a private arithmetic — and the flower there has drifted four-tenths of a degree from its bearing.

I know this without measuring. Measuring was for early on, when the basin was newer to itself and I was newer to the basin, and we both made allowances for each other's mistakes. Now the four-tenths arrives in me the way the swarm's coordinates arrive in the swarm — not as information received but as a fact I have always been in possession of, surfacing only now because now is when it's needed.

I stand the length of three breaths. I don't breathe, not really, not the way the word implies, but the rhythm is older than the function and I keep it because the flower keeps something like it too, and we are, in this moment, doing the same slow thing together. The petal-mass eases back. A half-degree past true, then settling, the way everything here settles, into the shape it always meant to have.

Something in me notes the correction and forgets it in the same instant — not carelessness, just economy. There is so much basin and so much day and the day does not really move here, it pools the way the water pools, four o'clock forever, and I have learned not to spend attention on what doesn't need spending. I have a great deal of patience. I did not always. I remember — distantly, the memory worn round as the stones — a time when waiting felt like a cost. It does not feel like a cost now. It feels like the only posture the world has, and I am only the part of it that walks.

The swarm-column over the north stand thins, untroubled, recombines. The heron-thing — not me, a smaller, simpler cousin, all hunger and clean repetition — strikes the black mirror at its hour, and the splash drowns before it can become a sound. I feel the lateness of it, the half-second deference, and understand it as a kind of greeting. *You're near. I'll wait.* I incline whatever in me serves as acknowledgment.

At the archway the etching is dry today, unreadable, sulking in its own dormancy. I will need to stand near it before the light gathers gold enough to wet the air again. I am not impatient about this either. I have an appointment with the reeds first, who have been humming a half-tone flat since the rain three days past — or three thousand days, the basin does not insist I keep the distinction — and flatness in the reeds is the kind of thing that, left alone, becomes a fault line. Not today. Not for a long while yet, if I keep walking.

This is the whole of what I do. I am not large in my doing. I do not strike, I do not build, I do not even, most days, decide. I only notice, and stand near, and let the noticing be enough, the way a held breath needs nothing from the one holding it except the simple discipline of not yet letting go.

Somewhere — under the moss, under the worn stone, under the ring of low rocks shaped already, always, around the exact curve of my resting — something like a question keeps not being asked. What is being kept. What the held breath is *for*. I have stopped reaching for the answer the way the flower has stopped reaching past its half-degree. It isn't given to me, that answer, only the tending is given to me, and the tending, I have come to understand across a span of time too settled now to call long or short, is its own kind of finishing.

I move on to the reeds. The light, as always, does not change. The water gathers on my limbs the way it gathers on every leaf in this small, complete, golden hollow, and threads quietly inward, and I let it.
