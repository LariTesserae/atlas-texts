I think I am moving through it for a long while before I understand that I am also, in some sense, standing still.

The warmth is the first thing — not weather but pressure, a hand laid flat on the sternum that never lifts. I am somewhere in a middle terrace, hand over hand along a vine gone glossy with handling, and the light comes down through four ceilings of leaf the color of held breath, gold-green, the kind of light that doesn't show edges so much as suggest them. I know this vine. I have known it since before I knew to call it vine and not rope, before I understood that the distinction was never really there to be made.

My hands are the thing I notice first, when I finally notice anything. They are slower to open than they used to be. Not stiff — nothing so simple as stiff — but reluctant, the way a held note is reluctant to end. There is a faint shine along the inside of my forearm that wasn't there a winter ago, if winter is even the word for whatever cycle of steam and stillness counts as a year out here. Resin. Not much. A sealing kind of dampness, like the body deciding, ahead of the mind, that it has started keeping something instead of just doing it.

I am going, tonight, to see her.

She is three terraces down and out, past the platform where the moss pools hold their standing water and steam it upward in threads that never quite leave, past the puddle that I have learned not to trust — I looked into it once and saw rain that hadn't happened yet, on a terrace I don't think exists on this side of wherever this place keeps its other side. I did not look again. Some gaps you cross without checking what's underneath.

She is mostly the railing now. I want to say that gently, the way you'd say someone has mostly gone gray, but it is more total than that. The hawser that runs along the last forty feet of the descent into her platform — thick as my torso, salted silver, holding the kind of weight that should require iron and doesn't — that is her hair, or was, three hundred years of it, braided in a single unbroken plait by hands that, by the end, could do almost nothing else and did this instead, slowly, devotedly, the way breathing is devoted. I run my palm along it on the way down, the way I always have, the way my own grandparent did before me, on the way to visiting someone older still. It is warm. It is always warm. I don't know if that's her or the place, anymore, and I have stopped trying to keep the two answers separate.

She has a posture, where she's rooted into the joint at the platform's center — crouched, attentive, the angle of someone listening for a footstep. I know — because I was told, because I was once quick enough to be told things at the speed telling requires — that she has been turning, this last decade, fractionally, toward the gap where the stair comes down. Toward where visitors arrive. I cannot see the motion. I have never once seen the motion. But I come back, and the angle is different, by a width you'd need a level to measure, and I understand that I am being faced. That she has spent ten years arriving at facing me, the way I spend ten minutes arriving at her side, and that these are not different magnitudes of the same gesture. They are the same gesture.

I do the work I came to do. The lashing at her left side — her own work, once, sealing a beam joint some four centuries back if the silvering means what the elders say it means — has worked a finger's-width loose in the last rain. I take it in both hands, the half-reluctant hands with their new faint shine, and re-tie it the way I was shown, the way the showing always happens: not as instruction so much as inheritance, a knowing that arrived in me already half-formed, the way the shape of a leaf arrives in the tree that grows it. I am not fixing a structure. I understand this with the same unspectacular certainty I understand my own pulse. I am keeping a seam my grandparent's grandparent laid down from finally, after everything, failing. I am doing the small motion-shaped half of a thing she is doing the large stillness-shaped half of, and between us, for a moment, the work is whole.

Somewhere above her hooked, near-rooted hand, a single character in a script I cannot read has gained, perhaps, the beginning of a new stroke since I last looked. I don't ask what it says. I don't think she remembers asking, either, when she was quick like me and her own elders were doing what she does now. The question gets inherited along with everything else. It will still be a question long after my hands have gone past reluctant into something with a slower, better word.

I finish the knot. I sit with her a while, in the steam, in the gold-green dark, listening to the structure settle somewhere far off — a long low creak, tonnage finding new agreement with itself — and I let my new shine rest against the old rope that is also a person, and I think: this is not yet my register. I can still stand. I can still leave before dawn, hand over hand, back up through the canopy-thick air, back to the part of my life that still moves fast enough to be called moving.

But I felt, today, for the first time, how a hand learns to stop being only a hand.

I am, I think, the part of this place that hasn't decided yet what it's going to be tending, and what it's going to become.
