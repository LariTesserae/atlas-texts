I become aware of myself the way the stone becomes aware of water — not as an event, but as a gradual occupying, a settling into a shape that was already waiting.

First there is the roar. It is not sound yet, because sound implies something separate from me, arriving, and this is already inside, already the medium I am suspended in rather than something I receive. Only after some unmeasured interval does the roar resolve into roar — vast, bright, structureless, ringing from every fin and dome and plate at once, no source, no direction, the chime of ten thousand surfaces agreeing with each other forever. I do not flinch from it. There is no version of me that has ever known quiet to flinch toward. This is simply what air is, here. This is simply what the world sounds like when it has decided, a very long time ago, exactly what shape and volume it intends to keep.

Then the body. Flat — extravagantly, totally flat, ribs splayed nearly level with my own spine, limbs already folded inward as if I arrived pre-packed for a passage too narrow for anything less considered. I test this by not moving, first, the way the rock itself doesn't move, the way the light doesn't soften — I lie pressed into the curve of a boulder's underside and feel how exactly my lower third is already damp, already dark, already telling whatever has bothered to look how long I have lain in this particular hollow drinking this particular seep. My upper surface stays dry, pale, holding the light rather than throwing it back. I am, in this moment, unreadable as an animal. I am a nodule that happens to be patient.

When I do move it is not walking so much as negotiation — shoulder finding the cold flank of one fin, following its curve until it yields a gap, body flowing through the gap the way the seep flows through its own capillary, taking the path already offered rather than asking the rock to offer a new one. There is no clearing anywhere on this plateau, no patch of bare ground to cross in the open, and I have never wanted one; openness would mean exposure to a kind of attention that does not exist here in sufficient density to matter. What matters is fit. What matters is becoming, again and again, the exact negative space between two pieces of excess stone.

My ears — sails of thin tissue along my flank, tuned to the rock and not the air — are doing their long patient work underneath all of this: holding an image of the total roar, subtracting it, listening for what the roar is not. A footfall two fins over. A new channel opening somewhere deep in the press of stone, a drip finding a fresh crack to ring against. And under it all, the rarer thing — a small flat silence, local, brief, meaning some other body like mine has pressed itself against a seep-mouth somewhere and stopped, for a few breaths, the world's only weather.

I taste the rock before I understand that I am hungry. My tongue finds the damp lower third of a fin, the mineral-cold seep beaded at its lip, and I drink the plateau's own patient leaching of itself, water that has spent a thousand feet of stone deciding to become exactly this taste. I will give some of it back, eventually, unchanged enough that the loop barely notices me passing through it. I do not experience this as a small life. I experience it as a complete one — sized exactly to the gap I am built to fill, no more urgent than the light, no more changing than the cold.

Once, moving shoulder past flank through a passage too narrow for two, I meet another of my kind coming the other way. We do not call to each other — there is no voice built here that could survive what the rock does to a voice. Instead we both go still, hold the stillness a few breaths longer than passing requires, a held quiet laid flat against the roar like two hands set against the same ringing bell to feel, underneath the noise, the deeper structural note neither of us could find alone.

Then we go on, each toward our own hollow, into a light that has already decided what time it is, across a stone that finished becoming itself long before either of us arrived to fit our bodies into what it left.
