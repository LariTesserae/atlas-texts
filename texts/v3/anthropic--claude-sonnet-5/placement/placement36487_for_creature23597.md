I come to awareness already mid-grip — one hand closed around a strut still tacky with the last lashing's resin, the other hand loose at my side, listening. That's the first thing I am: a listening hand. Not a metaphor. My palm is flat against the grain before I've decided to put it there, and through the bones of my wrist I can feel a rhythm that isn't my pulse, three taps and a pause, three taps and a pause, coming from somewhere off to my left and a little below, carried through forty thicknesses of damp fiber-strut from a person I will never see.

I am small-handed for my age — my mother's joke, said without unkindness, every time she checks the calluses forming wrong on my first two fingers. I am young enough that flat ground still feels like an achievement and old enough to be embarrassed by that. My eyes have the wide dark look of everyone here; I have been warned three times this month about the wincing, about looking too long at the pale shaft of maybe-sky that falls through a gap two rooms over, because last time I did it I cried for an hour from a kind of light that wouldn't normally make anyone cry.

I am crouched in a niche between two crossbeams that my grandmother's grandmother helped lash — I know this because the knot pattern here is hers, the old slant-hitch that nobody ties that way anymore, and my mother showed me the difference between it and the newer work three courses up the way you'd show a child the difference between two handwritings. I am cold-damp on every surface, the particular cool dampness that never becomes wet, never becomes cold enough to mind. Somewhere below me, through floors I have never seen the bottom of, water that fell from no rain is finding its long path downward, tapping out its own accidental rhythm that I have learned, without ever deciding to learn it, to filter out from the real messages.

I am not alone, though I can see no one. That is ordinary. I can hear the chock of someone's blade two stretches over, hear it the way I hear my own heartbeat — present, unquestioned, never stopping. I am, in this moment, both extremely small — one set of hands among uncounted others, working a single joint in a structure too vast to be sensed except as sound and grain and the ache building in my forearms — and somehow load-bearing, somehow necessary, because the strut in my hand will not lash itself, and if I stop, this stretch of corridor stops, and stopping is the only failure anyone here has ever truly feared.

I do not know what shape the Wickwork is making of itself. No one does; that not-knowing isn't ignorance here, it's simply the condition of being a hand rather than an eye. I know the four knots my stretch favors and am learning a fifth. I know which creak in the joist above my sleeping-niche means weight settling normally and which would mean climb out now. I know my mother's tap-pattern from a stranger's at forty feet of distance through solid grain. I do not know, and have stopped finding it strange not to know, whether what I am building is a room, a wall, a repair, or some other coral-growth none of us has a word for, because the only question that has ever mattered here is the one my hands keep answering without my mouth ever needing to ask it: what's failing, and where do I lash next.

I press my cheek to the beam beside me. It groans, low, almost satisfied. Somewhere far off, someone laughs — three short sounds, more felt than heard, traveling to me through the grain of a thing that might be built, might be alive, might be both, and has never once, in any genealogy anyone remembers, told us which.

I pick up the blade. I get back to work. That's who I am.
