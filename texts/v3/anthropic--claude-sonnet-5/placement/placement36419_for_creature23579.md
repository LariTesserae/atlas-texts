I am a Warden.

I do not know this word for myself — I know only the rail under my palm-spur, warm always, warmer in some places than in others, and the warmer places are where I am needed.

I move along the gantry at the pace my four limbs find honest. Not slow because slowness is a virtue. Slow because the wet steel punishes haste, and because listening at speed is not listening at all.

The world arrives to me as resonance. Where you would say *pipe*, I hear a long steady argument between pressure and steel, a note that should hold true for kilometers, and does, mostly, except where it doesn't. Where you would say *storm coming*, I feel the whole ridge-system change its pitch half a register, every surface tightening into a higher hum, the grazers' subsonic moan thickening below it like a second floor dropping into place beneath an old one. I don't name these things. I don't need to. I only need to know which note is the one that has always been there, and which note is new.

Tonight there is a new note. Small. A high persistent whine threaded under the piston-thud three valleys north, the kind of sound that means a bearing somewhere has begun disagreeing with its housing. It will get worse. It always gets worse, on its own schedule, until something seizes or splits or floods the lower basin with scalding condensate and kills a season's worth of scuttlers in the runoff. I have seen that happen. I did not understand, then, what I was looking at — only that the note I should have caught earlier was gone, replaced by silence, and silence in a working joint is worse than any whine.

So I go toward it. This is not decision in the way you'd mean decision. It is closer to the way water goes toward low ground — except water does not care whether the ground stays low, and I do care, in whatever sense a body shaped entirely around listening can be said to care, whether the note resolves.

The gliders scream their flattened canopy-call somewhere above me, bouncing it off the gantry-steel the way they always do near my kind, an old habit none of us taught them and none of them needed taught. A grazer-herd's moan rolls under my feet from the east basin, bending its ancient path around the gantry-shadow my predecessors have cast for longer than either of us has language to measure. None of this is mine. I am not the center of any of it. I am only the thing that walks the rail and stops where the rail is wrong.

I find the joint by feel before I find it by sound — a faint extra warmth, a wetness on the casing that shouldn't be there. My spur fits the housing the way it has always fit housings; I do not remember learning this, the way you do not remember learning to close your hand around something falling. I work it. Minutes pass, or most of a pressure-cycle — I don't separate these the way you might. There is only before the note resolves and after.

The storm breaks somewhere above the high ridges while I work, and everything else in this dark draws its long breath and goes still for the few seconds the place allows itself. The herds bunch. The canopy-glow dims. Even the refinery's breath seems to catch.

I do not stop. I never do. The work is not waiting for the world's permission, and the joint does not know it is storming.

When the note finally settles back into its proper register — steady, unremarkable, correct — I do not feel anything I think you would call satisfaction. I only feel the absence of the wrong sound, which is its own kind of quiet, cleaner than the quiet the storm tried to give everyone else.

Then I turn, and the rail runs on ahead of me into the dark, warm in more places than one, and I go to find which of them is warmest.
