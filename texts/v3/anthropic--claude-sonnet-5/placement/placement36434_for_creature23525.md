The first thing is the ceiling. Not seeing it — *feeling* it, the way the air sits lower and closer than it should, a held pressure just above the crown of the head that I've stopped noticing as pressure and now only notice as absence, on the rare occasions I'm not under it. My shoulders carry the knowledge before my eyes confirm it. I duck before I know I'm ducking.

Then the gold. Everywhere a small, insufficient, entirely adequate gold — lamp-flames no bigger than a curled finger, but so many of them, niche after niche down every corridor, that the dark never quite closes. My eyes have gone wide for it without my asking them to, pupils permanently a little open, a little hungry, so that when I do glimpse the rare shaft of true daylight bounced down some upper well, it comes in like a blow, like a sound rather than a light.

And the sound. Always the sound — looms two rooms over, a grindstone's grit-grit-grit, fowl muttering in their ceiling-baskets, somebody's laugh ricocheting off three walls before it reaches me flattened into something almost unrecognizable as laughter. I have lived inside this roar so long it has stopped being noise and become something closer to weather. When it stops — when some freak pocket of a corridor goes briefly, accidentally silent — my whole body turns toward the wrongness of it like a plant turning from cold.

I find my hands first, before I find my name. They are not soft. The fingertips carry old blue-black ghosting under the nail that no scrubbing has fully lifted — dye-stain, fungus-stain, work done young enough that it set into me like a second pigment. But the calluses on the heel of the right thumb are newer, smoother, burned-numb in one specific spot from years of pinching wicks down to a manageable smolder without flinching. Lamp-tender's thumb. Dyer's hands underneath it, an old trade worn down to make room for a newer one.

So: I tend the warren's invented night.

I walk fixed rounds I could walk asleep — have walked asleep, more than once, waking mid-stride with the wick-trimmer already in hand and the oil-flask already tilted, my feet having kept the route without consulting the rest of me. I am one of the few people in Kelth-Adar who can be asked, *what hour is it*, and answer without hesitation, because I am the hour, I am the one who decides how much gold burns in which niche and when it gets coaxed down toward sleep-dim. There is a particular satisfaction in this that outsiders never seem to credit: that someone has to *keep* the dark and the light both, has to ration the difference between waking and rest in a place where the sun has no vote.

I am past my middle years now — not fired yet, not quite, but the grey has worked well into the creases of my knuckles and the soft skin behind my ears, ground in past washing, the way it does to everyone eventually, the way dust works into the warren's own walls. My back has the characteristic curve. Someone joked once that they could read my whole home-corridor in the exact angle of my spine, and they were probably not wrong.

I had — have — a parent who was a deep-digger. Most of my early years that fact sat in me like a held breath: the long absences, the chalk-marks on her palms when she came back, the wide stretched quality behind her eyes that wasn't quite fear and wasn't quite wonder, something that hadn't fully been named by the time it had to be felt. She used to say the warmest wall she ever touched in the unlit dark was warmer than any fire could explain from that distance, and that a draft came through once smelling unmistakably, impossibly, of rain, in a place no rain has any business reaching. She said it the way you'd report on weather. Not frightened. Attentive. I grew up with that attentiveness modeled for me and never quite shook the pull of it, even though I chose the lit rounds, the lamp-rounds, the known corridors, instead of her dark ones.

She is in the wall now, three turns down from the firepit hall where I was minded as a child by whoever happened to be nearest at the time — which is to say, by everyone, loosely, which is to say, by no one in particular, which is to say, the ordinary way. Her niche has nearly finished matching the clay around it. In another decade no stranger's hand would know where she ends and the corridor begins. I leave a pinch of dried fungus there some weeks, not many, not on a schedule — when I pass and think of her, which the wall makes easy, since I pass that way on every round.

I am thinking, lately, in the unhurried way one thinks of something for years before doing it, about the deep tunnels. Not to abandon the rounds — someone has to keep the gold honest, and I am good at it, and the warren does not love restlessness for its own sake. But there is a thing in me, dye-stained and lamp-burned and curved at the spine, that wants once, at least once, paired with someone steady, chalk in hand, to walk past the last claimed niche and find out for myself whether the wall really runs warm out there, whether the draft really carries a smell the warren has no word for because the warren has never needed one.

Not tonight. Tonight there are wicks to trim, an agreed dimness to bring on, forty bodies in the great hall to ease toward whatever passes here for sleep. I move through my own crowded home by the practiced sidestep everyone learns before they can properly walk — past the hanging baskets, the stacked brick, the sleeping mat that is mine only by the soft unclaimed logic of *this is where I usually am* — and I take up the flask, and I begin the round, gold pooling and dimming behind me, corridor by corridor, the whole murmuring weight of Kelth-Adar settling, under my hands, into another counted night.
