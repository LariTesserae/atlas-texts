I'd be old enough that my hands have stopped being useful for digging and started being useful for something else — for *listening*. Not a Listener by the basins, no; my ear is for stone, not water. I sit, most days, in the third-level joining-chamber where the corridor narrows to let two families' walls meet, because that seam is old and has never quite decided whether it trusts itself.

I'd find myself low-ceilinged into permanent stoop, palm flat against rough rock that still remembers being broken rather than worn, head tilted in the particular attitude that anyone in the warren would recognize without needing to ask what I was doing. My voice, when I use it, would come out pitched down near a register that doesn't trespass into anyone else's evening meal — clipped, vowel-worn, economical, because sound here is a held thing, not a spent one.

I think I am not one of the ones drawn to the unclaimed dark. I don't envy them their threshold. My edge is smaller and more domestic: the hairline patience running through a ceiling above a sleeping-ledge where someone's grandchildren rest, the soft give in a wall that hasn't yet decided to become a crack. My whole authority — informal, total within its narrow inch of expertise — is the tap-pattern that means *this will hold* and the sharper one that means *get them out before nightfall.*

I'd have a stack of ancestors beneath me, literally beneath, their chisel-marks under my soot, and I would think of them not as people I half-remember but as renovations I am still living inside — my grandmother's widened doorway, my own added niche, someone not yet born who will inherit the question I haven't finished answering about whether this particular seam of rock means to hold or means to move.

I would be, I think, one of the small invisible laborers of accommodation — quick to yield a half-step, quick to read a silence correctly, fluent in a language that never gets written down because it lives entirely in fingertips and the spaces between words. Not unfeeling. Just translated. Grief as a long quiet. Joy as something kept in the chest, let out only as far as the eyes, because the room is small and everyone in it deserves not to have my feelings thrown at their walls.

And somewhere above me, rarely looked at, a disc of pale old sky — indifferent, still there — that I would not bother glancing toward, because my whole vocation is bent the other way: down, into stone that hasn't finished deciding what it wants to be, asking it questions with the flat of my hand, and waiting, patient as the warren itself, for the answer.
