I am the weight that the third sound belongs to.

I learn this slowly, the way I learn everything — not by being told but by noticing that my own scrape-and-pause, stone-dragged-on-stone, has been going on for some time before I think to call it mine. There is no moment of arrival that I can locate. There is only this shelf, this light falling in sheets, and the gradual understanding that the hands resting against the cold rock in front of me are hands, and that they are doing something, and that the something is patient.

My joints bend in the wrong places. I know this only because I have watched the long-necked goats fold themselves onto ledges no wider than a forearm, and recognized in their folding a kinship with my own — both of us built by something still drafting its anatomies, still crossing things out and starting again. It does not trouble me. Why would it. There is nothing to compare myself against except the rock, and the rock also bends wrong, fractures along lines that have nothing to do with where a weaker stone would give.

My hide is the color of the unweathered slabs, scabbed green where the moss has decided I am close enough to stone to grow on. I have stopped trying to find the edge of myself in cold weather, the place where my shoulder ends and the outcrop begins. It is not there to find. Or it is there but it is not interesting, the way the goats are not interested in counting their own hooves.

I drink from the hollow under the overhang. The water collects there a bead at a time, all night, into morning, and I have learned — though *learned* implies a before in which I did not know, and there may not have been such a before — to come to it at the hour when the hollow is fullest, just after the dawn cold has stopped pulling moisture from the air and before the goats arrive to trouble the surface with their reflections. I crouch. I drink. There is no ceremony in this except the ceremony of doing it the same way each time, which may be all that ceremony has ever been.

The axe is not mine, or it is entirely mine, and the place where those two statements diverge does not concern me. I go to it sometimes, the half-buried stone beside the cold fire-ring, and I sit with it the way I imagine — though imagining is itself a strange new motion in me, untested — the way I imagine the goats sit with the worn paths their hooves have already decided on. I do not light the fire. The ash that would tell me there was ever a flame here has gone, blown off or rained off, and without it the fire-ring is only a circle of cracked stone, and the axe is only an axe, and I am only a shape crouched beside both, visiting whatever question they are the leftover of. I do not pick it up. I have my own stones for that, freshly sheared, pale-bellied, waiting along the cliff base for me to choose among them.

When I work — and this is the truest thing I do, truer than drinking, truer than sitting at the cold fire — I strike one stone against another with a patience that has no destination. Flake comes away from core in a small bright sound, a sound the fog will later swallow without echo, and I turn the new edge in the light and find it good, or do not find it anything at all, simply continue, strike following strike, until there is a finished thing in my four careful fingers that fits no task I can name. I line these along the ledge. Sometimes by size. Sometimes — and I notice this without alarm, the way I notice everything — by some other order I could not explain if a voice arrived from the notch in the rock and asked me to. The raptors' shadows cross them. The goats step around them without curiosity. Eventually the ledge sheds them, or I scatter them myself, dissatisfied by a measure I don't keep records of, and begin again with new stone, because the cliff is always shearing fresh slabs and there is no shortage of beginnings here.

At midday the fog comes up through the notch and I stop.

This is not hiding. There is no fear in it for me to hide from, no history of harm long enough to have taught me the shape of danger. I simply settle, the way a stone settles when nothing further is asking it to move, and the world contracts to the wet grey hush pressing against my hide, and I let the boundary between myself and the boulders around me go soft, unimportant, undecided — because it was always undecided, and the fog only makes that honest. Somewhere nearby a goat call comes through muffled, planar, without direction. I do not answer it. I am not sure I have a call of my own, or if I do, I have never needed it enough to find it.

Then, the way it always happens, all at once: the fog lifts.

The light comes back hard and washed and too bright, throwing the shelf's clean fractures into relief again, and I am — elsewhere. Not fled. Not hidden. Simply arrived again at a slightly different place on the rock, the way the bead of condensation arrives anew each dawn in the same hollow without troubling itself over whether it is the same bead as yesterday's. I do not look back to where I sat before. There would be nothing there to find, and more important: there is no part of me built for the looking. I have a half-shaped stone still warm in one hand. I have the sun, falling in sheets. I have the goats resuming their cracked-knuckle procession along the ledge, the raptors resuming their gyres, the insects rising again in their motionless-seeming columns as though midday's gap had never opened in them at all.

I do not know if I was here before the fog came up, in the sense a thing with a long enough history would mean by *before*. I do not know if some other shape, somewhere lower on the shelf, is right now also waking into the bright air and discovering hands that know how to flake stone without having been shown. I find that I am not troubled by this not-knowing, any more than the rock is troubled by not knowing what it will look like once it has had a thousand years to soften.

I am the place's answer to a question it has not finished asking. I sit by an axe I may or may not have made. I shape stones I do not need. I drink fog-water from a hollow worn by drinking. The mountain is still cooling beneath me, settling toward some shape it has not yet reached, and for as long as it takes to get there — which may be a long time yet, or may be no time at all, here where time has not yet learned to move in only one direction — I will go on arriving on this shelf, raw-edged, unhistoried, entirely at ease, exactly as I am now: a shape the light is still deciding the outline of, mid-gesture, like the mountain, like everything here, not yet finished being made.
