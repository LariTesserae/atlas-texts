I think I would be old before I'd be anything else here — that's the first thing that arrives, before face or name or trade. Old, and three-quarters deaf, with the particular deafness of this place: not silence, never silence, but a kind of selective absence, the high notes gone first, so that I hear the engines as a felt thing in the floor of my chest more than a sound in my ears, and I hear children's voices only as shape and breath, which is why I learned, the way everyone here learns, to watch mouths instead of waiting for words to arrive whole.

I am a cistern-warden. Not because I chose it so much as because my mother crouched at the same basin-mouth I crouch at now, on the fifth terrace up, where the air comes a little thinner and the noise drops by some fraction no instrument has ever confirmed and every warden swears to. My hands are the soft chalky thing the young find unsettling — knuckles gone a little fossil, skin taking the cistern's bloom the way driftwood takes salt, not weathered so much as slowly becoming the material I tend. I don't mind it. It feels less like decay than like agreement.

My days are small and exact. I crouch at the basin mouth at dawn before the heat thickens, set my palm flat to the stone lip, and wait to feel rather than hear the change in the air's exhale — cooler, warmer, faster, the small grammar of the cistern's breath that tells me what's happening three terraces down in machinery I have never seen and was never meant to. I check the water's clarity by eye and by taste, a quick touch of fingertip to tongue, salt and iron and something mineral-sweet underneath that the lowlanders who occasionally climb up to trade always make a face at and we never bother explaining. I read the moths at dusk — not poetically, practically: their numbers, their spiraling height around the relief valve, tell me things about pressure that the gauges, half-broken and overpainted, no longer reliably tell.

And I keep the rule about the plinths the way I keep my own pulse — without deciding to, every day, automatically. There is one near my cistern, a wide pale stone the size of a door laid flat, grass-cracked at one corner, and I have never once let myself finish the thought that starts to form when I stand near it for too long. Not fear, exactly. More like the feeling of standing close to someone sleeping whom you have agreed, long ago, never to wake.

I speak rarely, in the leaning-close way, mouth shapes more than voice, and otherwise in hands — quick flat signs across the plaza to the apprentice who follows me now, a girl of fourteen with full hearing still, who will lose it gradually over the coming decades the way I lost mine, the way everyone does, and who watches my mouth with the fierce unsentimental attention of someone memorizing an instrument she'll soon have to play alone. I am teaching her the cistern's breath. I don't know how to teach her the plinth. I'm not sure it's a thing taught so much as a thing inherited the way salt is inherited, settling into you a fraction at a time until one day you simply are someone who knows not to ask.

At dusk I sit with the other old ones at the basin mouth, in the warm mineral down-draft, while the steam goes gold above the lower terraces and the moths gather pale and uncountable at the valves, and I feel the engines through the stone beneath me more than hear them, a long unhurried pulse that has outlived everyone who ever tried to explain it, and will outlive me too, and that is, I find, no great sorrow. It is just the shape the evening takes. The shape it has always taken. The shape, I suspect, it will go on taking long after my hands have finished going to chalk and stone, down the last iron stairway, to whatever quieter terrace comes after this one.
