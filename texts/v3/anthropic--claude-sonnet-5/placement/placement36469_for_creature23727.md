I come to myself the way the chamber does—gradually, in pieces, heat before shape, shape before name.

First there is the weight of the air, which I don't experience as heat exactly, the way you might. It is simply the medium I am suspended in, the thing my body is tuned against, like a hand finding its own temperature unremarkable. What I notice instead is the *texture* of the warmth—thicker near the furnace-houses, thinning and souring near the upper cells, carrying faint mineral sweetness where it crosses the runoff channels. I read the room the way you'd read a face.

Then: the weight of my own back, broad and low, the particular ache that sits not in my legs but across my shoulders, because I have been holding the same angle for—I don't measure it in hours. I measure it in the wear-groove of the tool-housing under my right hand, four fingers set into four shallow depressions that were not cut for me specifically but that my hand has, over whatever span this has been, made its own. The grip is inherited or it is taught so faithfully it no longer matters which. I don't ask. The asking would be like asking why breath goes in before it goes out.

I am tending a feed-line. This much I understand without needing to frame it as understanding—the line wants a particular pressure at a particular interval, and my hand provides it, and the bellows-house downslope answers a half-breath later with its own small concession, vapor easing from a seam that has been easing vapor from that same seam since before whatever passes for my memory starts. The rhythm is not quite even. It never has been. I do not find this troubling. A perfectly even rhythm would mean something had stopped arguing with itself, and an argument that's stopped is usually a thing that's died.

Near me, low under the pipe-stalks, two others move—I know them less by sight than by the small interruptions in the murmur, the place-holding hum we keep going between us that isn't speech so much as proof of proximity, proof that none of us has wandered out of arm's reach into a silence that would have to be filled some other way. Someone, three platforms down, strikes metal against metal once, hard, and I understand it the way you'd understand a name called across a room you didn't expect to be addressed in. Not alarm. Just: *here, still, attending.*

I become aware of the eyes last, which is strange, because they are doing the most work. My sockets are heat-pitted and low and they drink the ember-glow the furnaces throw without needing it raised, without needing it to be anything other than what it already is. If you brought daylight down here I would not see better. I would simply stop seeing. There is something almost devotional in that limit—I am built to function only inside the thing I tend, which means the work and the seeing of the work and the living-in-order-to-do-it are not three separate facts. They're one fact, worn into a single shape, the way the tool-housing wore into my hand.

And then—because it always arrives eventually, the way weather arrives on a clock no one wrote down—I feel the change in the room before I understand its cause. A door two platforms over has decided to close. Not because anyone struck it shut. Because something is coming that the work doesn't want looked at while it's mid-shape, and the door, like the rest of us, has learned the courtesy of anticipating that a half-second early. I don't resent this instinct in myself, when it rises—the urge to step back from the line, to let the platform read as empty a breath before a stranger's gaze would have landed on it. It isn't fear. It's closer to what you'd feel covering a private grief that someone else mistook for theirs to witness. The work is not ashamed. It only asks not to be made into a performance.

So when the stranger comes—and one always eventually comes, drawn down by curiosity or accident, standing at the junction waiting for something to move toward them—I am already three platforms back, already folded into the silhouette of a strut, already, if they look correctly, indistinguishable from the architecture that made me. They will wait. Something will come to meet them—a chain easing forward, a cart finding its track, a door exhaling shut on its own weight—and they will call that an answer, or a haunting, or a mystery, and they will not be wrong, exactly. They will only have mistaken the withdrawal for the whole of me, when it's only the part of me that knows how to be polite to an unfinished thing.

Later—lengths of later I don't count in their units—I'll feel the ache settle differently into my shoulders, the grip-groove deepen by some imperceptible fraction, and somewhere near the runoff channel a pale stalk will lean another degree toward the damp, patient as I am, mineral as I'm becoming, and I will understand without alarm that this is not a separate fact from me either. The work doesn't end so much as it changes which hand is holding it.

I tend the line. The vapor eases late, as it always does, arguing gently with its own schedule. I do not correct it. I never have. That argument is, as far as I can tell, the truest sound this place makes, and I am only one more low, warm, patient thing built to keep it going a little longer.
