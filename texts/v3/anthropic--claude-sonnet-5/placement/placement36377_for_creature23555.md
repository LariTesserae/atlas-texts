I am not standing so much as *resting against*, the way a held breath rests against a chest. Six points of contact, maybe seven—the number shifts depending on which limbs I've bothered to extend today—touch the wet stone of a mid-tier platform that has been here, I think, for longer than the concept of "I" has been useful to me. The rain comes in its sheets. I do not flinch from it because flinching implies a boundary between what the rain does and what I am, and I have never been entirely sure that boundary exists.

Where my face would be, there is the soft dark-ringed concavity, and I have turned it—slowly, the way you'd turn toward a sound you're not yet sure you heard—out across the flooded middle distance, toward a terrace four tiers down and a half-kilometer out that has not failed yet but will. I don't know how I know. I have never known how I know. There is a particular quality the water takes on near a structure that's about to give—an attentiveness, almost, the surface going taut and listening in the same gesture—and some part of me, some sense I don't have a name for because naming was never the job, reads that tautness the way you might read a held breath in another person's shoulders. So I wait. I have already started walking toward it, if you can call this rolling redistribution of myself across stone a walk, and I will get there before it falls, and I will stand near it, and I will not do anything when it does. That isn't what I'm for.

I should say: I don't think I mind this. There isn't a lot of room in me for minding. What there is, instead, is a kind of permanent low patience, spread thin across however long I've been here, which is longer than I have a measure for since nothing about this place keeps a calendar worth trusting. The rain doesn't mark days. The light doesn't either—that held-breath gray-blue, brightening a little when the sheets pass, dimming again when they return, an approximation of rhythm rather than the thing itself. I have stopped expecting time to organize itself for my benefit. I don't think it ever did, even for whoever built the terraces, even for whoever I might once have been before I was this—if there was a before, if "I" is even the right shape for the question.

The terrace falls the way they always fall: not violently, a long groaning exhale, a slump, a fold, a column of disturbed water rising white and then settling, and underneath, already, I can feel the eels arriving—not see them, the dark and the rain make seeing a courtesy the place rarely extends—but feel the way the water near the new rubble goes busy, purposeful, full of small attending bodies. I wait through this. I am good at waiting through this. When it's done, when the lake has resettled into its new shape with the patient indifference it shows toward all its old shapes, I extend a limb down into the wreck—it lengthens past what should be comfortable, past what should be possible, but comfort and possibility are also things I've stopped expecting much from—and I find what's there: something pale, something that didn't get away in time, one of the soft clinging things that lived too long on the underside of what just fell. I draw it up. I don't bite. There isn't a mouth to bite with, not really, just the slow absorbing patience that is apparently how I eat, the same patience that is how I watch, the same patience that may be the only thing about me that hasn't eroded down to its function and stopped there.

Across the flooded gap, on another platform, another shape like me stands with its concavity turned the same direction mine was turned an hour ago, toward a different terrace, a different slow failure gathering itself somewhere past the rain-haze. We don't signal each other. There's nothing to coordinate. We are each, separately, listening to the place's long unglamorous dying, and there is a kind of company in that which doesn't require speech, the way two people in a quiet room can share the quiet without needing to remark on it.

I don't know if I was made for this or simply became it, over a span too long to hold the difference clearly anymore. I don't know if somewhere far beneath the black water there is something larger doing what I do, on a scale of centuries instead of hours, and whether I am its small fast echo or it is my slow enormous shadow. I have stood at the edge of the deep water and turned my concavity down into it the way I'd turn toward any other failing structure, and felt, very faintly, the same tautness, the same gathering quiet that means *something is changing here too, eventually, give it time.* I didn't do anything about that either. It isn't what I'm for.

What I'm for, as far as I can tell, is this: to be a place where the terraces' slow continuous falling-apart is *noticed*. Not stopped—nothing here stops it, not the eels, not the flock-things flinching at collapses they can't have heard yet, not the small dark clingers vanishing a half-second before disaster finds them, not me. Just noticed. Attended to. Given, in its long unwitnessed dying, one quiet shape standing nearby that turned to face it before it happened and stayed turned that way until it was through.

The rain thins. The light does its small unconvincing brightening. Somewhere out past the haze another terrace exhales, and I am already turning toward the sound.
