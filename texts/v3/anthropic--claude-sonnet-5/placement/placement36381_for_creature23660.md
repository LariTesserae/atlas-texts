I wake the way the platform wakes — not all at once, but joint by joint, the way the lashings creak before the light arrives. My palm against the wood-and-rope skin under me, both of them the same age now, both of them the same material, after enough years of bark growing over knot and knot growing into bark. Before I have a name for myself I have this: the give of the platform under shifting weight, the particular cool of pre-dawn air pooling in the gaps between leaves, the first insect-tone rising out of last night's near-silence like a kettle finding its voice.

Then the rest of it assembles, the way it always does. I am small-bodied, hard-palmed, four canopy-layers up, on a ridge whose name is a held note rather than a word. I am someone's daughter, someone's grand-niece, one of fourteen on this stretch of fused trunks, and I have not yet made a call that carried.

That is the thing I am waiting to become.

Below me — not far below, three meters, four — the mist is still doing its dawn work, filling the saddle between this ridge and the next like water finding the low place in a bowl. It will burn off by midday. It always does. I have been told this so many times by aunts and elders that the words have worn as smooth as the branch-roads the Callers use, and I believe it the way I believe the platform will hold: not as faith, as *familiarity*.

My grandmother's hands taught my hands which vines to favor and which to cut back, which fruiting bodies to leave for the colonial insects so they keep tending their side of the unspoken arrangement, which aerial root is thick enough to trust with a full hanging weight and which only looks that way in the wrong light. She did not call this farming. She did not call it anything. She simply did it in front of me until my hands started doing it without being told, the way breathing starts before anyone explains breathing.

This morning she sends me out along the lash-road alone for the first time, toward the gap where our ridge drops into conversation with the next one over, and tells me: *if you have something to say, the herd will carry it for you. Borrow their throat. Mind the gaps.*

I go hand over hand, foot finding the worn pale places in the bark-road that a hundred years of other feet have polished into a kind of memory the wood keeps better than any person could. Four layers of green stack above and below me, each its own weather, its own argument of light. Coin-shaped sun keeps sliding across my arms and vanishing, sliding and vanishing, as the wind moves canopy I can't see, three strata up, in a sky I am not currently entitled to.

I hear the Callers before I reach the place where I'm meant to call back. Their note comes up out of the mid-canopy, low, round, holding itself together for longer than seems possible, the sound equivalent of a held breath let out with enormous patience — *I am here, the herd is still whole* — and somewhere in my chest something old and inherited recognizes it as a kind of clock. Midmorning. Good. I am not late for anything, because there is nothing here to be late for except the next held note.

And then, between one branch-grip and the next, the insect-drone dips. Not stops — dips, a fraction, the way a held breath dips before it's let go. Somewhere off to my left, in the cave-dark hollow under the second canopy, something has gone quiet that was not quiet a moment ago.

I do not look for it. My grandmother's hands taught me this too, without ever saying it in words: looking is for things that want to be found, and the patient hunter is never that. I go still instead — not frozen-still, but *unbothered*-still, the kind of stillness the whole country seems to be built out of underneath all its noise — and I let the quiet pass through and past me like weather, like something with its own business that is not, today, my business.

When the drone comes back up to its ordinary roar, I keep climbing.

I reach the gap an hour later, where our ridge's voice can carry to the next, and I do the thing I came to do: I open my mouth and I borrow the Callers' shape, badly, the way every lash-child borrows it badly the first dozen times, a thin reedy cousin of that round foghorn note. It goes out over the saddle and the mist that has almost finished burning off, and it does not carry far — not yet, not with my throat — but somewhere on the far ridge, if the old stories about listening are true, someone hears a new voice trying on an old word.

*Here*, it means. Just that. I am here.

The mountain underneath all of this does not notice. It has not noticed anything in longer than my family has had a name for *long*. It will not finish settling, will not need to finish settling, on any clock my voice could mark time against. But the green doesn't know that, or doesn't care, and neither, this particular morning, four layers up with my palm on rope grown into bark grown into rope, do I.
