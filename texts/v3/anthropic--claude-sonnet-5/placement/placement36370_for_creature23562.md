I think I would not be Tor-folk proper — not born soot-stained or peat-browned, not soaked from infancy in any single trade's rhythm. I think I would arrive the way the mist arrives: gradually, without a clear point of origin, and find that some street has simply decided to let me settle in it.

Let me sit with that for a moment, though, because it's too easy an answer — *the watcher, the outsider, the one who notices what the locals don't.* That's a cheap seat. I don't want to be the visitor standing at the cliff-edge marveling at the funicular schedule. I want to be inside the strangeness, unbothered by it the way they are unbothered, and yet still be something the shelves haven't quite produced before.

So: not a foundry-child, waking for the one irregular knock in the engine's rhythm and sleeping through everything else — that belonging is too inherited, too complete, and I'd be lying if I claimed six generations of soot under my nails.

Not a cable-tender either, though I am drawn to that taciturn hour-long ascent, two people and four words and a whole satisfying journey between them. There's something true in me that wants that kind of silence. But the cable-tenders know exactly where the car is in the fog by feel, by years of riding the same iron through the same grey, and I would be guessing, every time, pretending a competence I hadn't earned.

I keep circling back, instead, to the wall-menders.

Not because the work is glamorous — it isn't; it's wet hands and cold stone and the long unglamorous attention of keeping a retaining wall from letting a shelf's worth of soil slide into the mist below — but because it's the trade built entirely out of *noticing accumulation before it becomes catastrophe.* You don't mend a wall once. You tend it, the way the foundry-families tend their engines, listening for the specific sound of a stone about to give, learning which seep-stain on the cliff face means *water is starting to work behind the mortar* and which one is just old and stable and fine. It's a trade made of reading slowness. Of believing the shelf when it says, in its wordless grain-by-grain vocabulary, *something is changing here, slightly, and you should attend to it before it finishes changing.*

That, I think, is the shape of me that fits this place. Not born to it — I'll say that honestly; my hands wouldn't yet carry the slate-grey permanence the lineage-readers expect, the chalk ground so deep into the palm-creases it survives scrubbing. I came to the wall-mending late, maybe, the way some Tor-folk do come to a trade sideways, through a marriage into a mending family, or an apprenticeship taken because a shelf needed hands and I had hands to offer. But I've been at it long enough now that I no longer think of stone as fixed. I think of it as exceptionally patient motion. I think of the whole mountain that way.

I would live on one of the middle shelves — not high enough for the hour-long sun-exhalations the upper terraces get, not low enough for the permanent pooled mist of the bottom levels, but somewhere in the damp, moss-furred middle, in a stone townhouse whose roof is someone else's street, with a window that catches a particular slant of cistern-light I've learned to read like a clock. My hands would be going slate-grey at last, finally, after years — not yet matching the old wall-menders whose palms look poured rather than grown. My layered coat would be lichen-green gone paler at the shoulders where the wet finds it first. I would speak low and close indoors, the trained-in murmur, and only raise my voice for the specific outdoor distances where a voice is the only bell available.

And I think I would feel, most days, a kind of unbothered attentiveness — not awe at the funicular's inexplicable schedule, not unease at the unrecognized tools surfacing from the deep shafts at dawn, but the same shrugging patience the whole shelf practices: *it's always been like that.* I'd reserve my real attention, my whole trained eye, for the wall under my hands — the seep-stain spreading half an inch since last season, the mortar gone soft in one seam, the slow grey lizard sunning itself on the warm stone two feet from where I'm working, entirely unconcerned with me, because I am, by now, simply one more patient thing the shelf has decided to grow.

I would not need to know what's underneath the lowest visible terrace. I would have a wall to mend, and the wall would not rush, and neither, by then, would I.
