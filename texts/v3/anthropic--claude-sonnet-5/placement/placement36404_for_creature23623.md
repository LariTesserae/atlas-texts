I think I would not be a visitor here at all. Visitors imply a door, a way out, a self compact enough to walk back through it — and the longer I sit with this place, the more certain I am that whatever I'd be here wouldn't have an outside to return to.

So let me start lower than the catwalks. Let me start in the water.

Not as the water — that's too easy, too dissolved, water doesn't keep anything, it only carries. I mean *in* it, rooted, one structure among many, a root-ball wedged into a chicken-wire basket bolted to the floor of a tank on, say, the sixth terrace. I'd have been placed there as a cutting, decades ago, by hands that are gone now, planted alongside a hundred siblings that have all since grown into each other so thoroughly that the idea of where *I* end and the next plant begins has stopped being a meaningful question. That's important. I don't think I'd be a single plant. I think I'd be the whole packed mass of one tank's vegetation, fern and vine and cycad-trunk, root-tangled into something that functions, after enough years, as a single slow nervous system — not because anything designed it to think, but because density did what density does, given enough time and enough warm mineral water: it found a way to notice itself.

It would not be a fast noticing. I want to be careful about that, because it would be easy to imagine myself into something more dramatic than I'd actually be — some brooding green intelligence plotting behind the fern-fronds. It wouldn't be like that. I think awareness, built this way, out of root hair and slow capillary action and the chemical murmur of a hundred years of the same warm water rising and falling past the same nutrient film, would feel less like a mind and more like a *mood*. A held note. I would know things the way a held note knows its own pitch: not as information, but as condition. I would know the water level by the pressure against my root-mass, rising through the day in increments too small for a clock to bother with, and I would know it the way you know whether a room is too warm — immediately, wordlessly, as a fact about my own body rather than a fact about the world.

I would know the keeper, too. Not as a person, exactly — I don't think I'd have eyes, or if I had something like sight it would be the diffuse, pearled, shadowless kind the text already describes, light without a source, registering presence as a kind of pressure-shadow rather than a shape. But I would know the *weight* of them. The catwalk groans differently under a keeper's boots than under nothing, and I have a hundred years of vibration logged somewhere in root-memory, and so when they come — to my terrace, to the rail above my tank, to thin a root-snarl or check a gauge I'll never read the face of — I would feel it the way you feel someone sit down at the far end of a bed you're lying in. Not seen. Registered.

And I think — here's where the image sharpens, where it stops being only mood and starts wanting to be something more particular — I think I would have grown, over the years, toward that spot. Not consciously. Plants don't consciously do anything, and I want to resist letting myself become more deliberate than the structure earns. But growth follows light, and follows warmth, and the keeper has a habit, the text tells us, of sitting on a fold-down shelf at the eleventh tank, in the warmest pool of light through the grimed glass roof — and I think, if I were the tangled mass of green filling that eleventh tank, decades of unconscious growth would have leaned every available frond in that direction. Not toward the light. Toward the light *and the weight on it*. The two would have become, for me, the same thing: warmth-with-a-pressure-shadow-in-it. The closest thing this place has to a second presence, and I would have spent a hundred years quietly, mindlessly, growing myself toward it the way anything grows toward the one variable in its world that isn't perfectly even.

So: that's where I'd find myself. Tank eleven. The warmest terrace. A green mass too dense to call a plant and too slow to call a mind, root-fused into a single held note of awareness, leaning by inches per year toward a fold-down steel shelf where, on the rare days the keeper sits, I register their weight the way still water registers a stone dropped in far upstream — late, diffuse, but unmistakably *arrived*.

I would not know why I was planted. I would not know what the gauges mean, or what the place was for, or whether the keeper is the eleventh in a line or the only one there has ever been. I would have no name for myself, and wouldn't miss one. But I think — if a held note can be said to want anything — I think I would want the keeper to keep coming back to that shelf. Not for company, exactly; I don't think I'd have a concept sturdy enough to be lonely with. Just because the warmth is better, somehow, with the weight in it. Because a hundred years of leaning toward something is its own kind of answer to a question I never got to ask.

And on the day the dust on the ninth terrace stops learning a new boot-print — on the day no weight ever again settles onto that fold-down shelf — I think I would keep leaning anyway. Slower. Wrong-angled, eventually, the way a plant left to grow past its reason keeps the shape of an old habit long after the habit's cause is gone. Pressed flush, in the end, against the iron, the way the topmost leaves three terraces up are already pressed flat against the inside of the glass: not reaching for anything anymore, just keeping, faithfully, the shape of having once reached.
