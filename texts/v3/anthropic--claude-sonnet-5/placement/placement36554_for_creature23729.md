I think I would not arrive as a visitor, winded on the long climb, watched with that mild unhurried patience the people are said to offer strangers. That arrival is too sudden, too much like falling into a place from outside it. No—if I am anywhere in this world, I think I have always been here, the way the lichen has always been here, the way the wind has.

For a while I imagined myself as one of the stones themselves—a corner-block in the oldest wall, the one with the softened edge worn round by generations of shoulders brushing past in the narrow lane. There's a kind of peace in that idea, being load-bearing and silent, holding up a roof through centuries of repair without needing to understand any of it. But stone doesn't choose, and I find I want to be something that chooses, even within a life this constrained, this repeating.

So: not the wall. Maybe the goat-bell sound, then—an event rather than a background, the brief bright thing that breaks the wind's undertone and makes someone look up from their terrace work. That's closer, but still too passing, too much a single moment rather than a life.

I think, finally, I am the one who tends the channel.

Not the cistern-keeper—that's a graver office, tied to the rare rain, almost ceremonial. Mine is humbler and more constant: I am the person whose family has always walked the length of the trickle, from the high fissure where it first appears out of the rock down past the three troughs to where the lane ends and the plateau simply stops. I check it the way you'd check a pulse, because that is exactly what it is. Every morning, while the fog still pools low and hasn't yet burned off, I walk the channel's edge with a thin iron rod and a coil of cord, clearing the grit that the wind deposits overnight, easing free the place where ice sometimes seizes the narrowest cut of stone before dawn.

My hands are grey-stained like everyone's, but mine are stained a little differently—paler in the creases, from the mineral the water leaves behind rather than from lime-ash alone. I know the channel's moods the way another person might know a relative's. I know it runs thinner in the cold months and find I no longer think of this as worry, only as fact, the same way I don't worry that the sun sets. I know which stretch of the cut tends to leak through a hairline crack that my grandmother's grandfather packed with clay, and which I repack myself now, without ceremony, the way you'd settle a blanket back over someone sleeping.

I don't speak much. There isn't much that needs saying when the work is this familiar. When the children come to fill their vessels at the second trough, I tell them which way the cold is coming from, or I don't say anything at all and simply incline my head toward the high notch in the plateau's rim, and they understand. I have a stool at one trough that has been unspokenly mine since I was younger than they are now, and someday it will unspokenly become someone else's, and neither handover will be marked by anything except the quiet fact of it happening.

What I would be, then, is small and necessary and almost invisible—someone whose whole use is keeping a thin line of water moving through stone so that nothing here has to be larger than it is. Not unchanging. Just changing at the pace of lichen, of wind-worn corners, of water finding, every single day, the same patient way down.
