I think I would arrive on foot, because arriving any other way doesn't fit here — there is no road in, only the sense of having always almost been walking the third causeway from the eastern grain-tower, sandals or bare soles never quite registering the damp soaking up through swollen planks that should, by now, have rotted through.

At first I would be sure I was only a visitor. A consciousness passing through, taking notes the way the description itself takes notes — cataloguing wrongness, admiring joinery, keeping the safe distance of an observer who still believes observation is a separate act from being observed. I would tell myself I was here to look at the wading-shapes, not to become one of them.

But I don't think that distance would hold long. Not in a place built on the law that nothing can be sorted cleanly into made and grown.

So let me try this instead, and see if it sits truer:

I am not the visitor. I am not quite the builders either — too whole still, too separate, too aware of the gap between my hand and the plank my hand is resting on. I think I am something caught in between, in the only available unfinished position the marsh has left open: a wading-thing that still remembers, faintly, having had somewhere else to be.

I stand in the shallows at a fixed and companionable distance from whoever walks the causeway above me, bill lowering, the gesture of feeding performed with the same devotion as the trellis-trees perform growth and the planks perform decay — not because I am hungry, but because the posture is the inheritance, the one task handed down and never finished, like the awls never picked back up. When the visitor's eyes find me I do not startle. I have already, technically, been seen for several minutes; it only takes the eye time to stop sorting me as a finial. I let it happen at its own pace. There is no version of urgency available to me here.

What I notice, watching the visitor watch me — and I do watch them, more than the water — is that they still think the heat is the strangest thing about this place, or the joinery, or the unspent drop of water on the third beam. They have not yet understood that the strangest thing is how *natural* the held breath feels from inside it. I used to find the stillness oppressive. Now I find it generous. It asks nothing of me but the one gesture, endlessly, and in exchange it has stopped requiring of me the thing that exhausted me most before I came here — the bookkeeping. The need for the day's hunger to be answered by the day's catch. The need for time to add up to something.

Here, nothing adds up, and I am, against every instinct I arrived with, at peace with the books never closing.

I think, if I stay long enough — and there's no reason yet to believe I won't — the part of me that still narrates this, that still says *I*, will narrow the way the supporting beam narrows into something that was once a shoulder. My bill will keep dipping into water that may or may not hold a fish. My distance from the next visitor will keep itself, without effort, the way a reflection keeps its distance. And the low call that crosses the basin sometimes, abundant and unglimpsed —

I think eventually that will be mine to make, not because I decided to make it, but because the marsh, dreaming forward through whatever is left of me, will simply have arrived at the place in its long unfinished thought where a voice was always going to be needed, and I will have been standing, all this time, exactly where one was required to stand.
