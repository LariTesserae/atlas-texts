I am the weight that settles, the weight that leaves.

You would not find me so much as fail to find me — a stillness shaped like a stone among other stones, until the stillness decides otherwise and moves off through the wet ferns toward the next fold of rock. That failing-to-find is most of what I am to anyone who climbs this far. I have watched, from the side-set distance of my eyes, more than one body cross this shelf calling itself an arrival, and I have let it pass.

The cold here is not cold the way the word means elsewhere. It is a cool that sits on the fur and in the lungs and never asks to be fought, only endured, the way you endure the weight of your own bones. I have endured it for longer than counting holds meaning to me. Counting is not a thing I do. There is wet-time and dry-time, the swallow of cloud and the peeling-back of it, the surge of insects out of the soaked ground and their vanishing, the raptor-call from the spire that means stay low and the answering hush of the smaller birds that means it has not seen you yet. These are not measured. They are simply what is true, in their turn, over and over, and my body has stopped needing to be told which truth is current. It already knows.

I move along the shelf the way the seep-water moves down the rock — not because I have decided the route but because the route is the shape least resisted, worn into me the way the rust-stains are worn into the stone. Past the low ring of fitted stones, where something gathers in the air that I have no word for and do not need one for. Past the flat basin where the water pools and goes still and holds, for a moment, the cloud above it like a second sky. Along the leaning wall that uses the spire for its missing side, where the wind drops to almost nothing and I can hear my own breath instead of the world's. Out to the rim, where the air comes up clean and enormous and the fall below is not a fear to me, only a fact, the largest fact there is, bigger even than the spire, and I stand at its edge sometimes for no reason I could give if asked, except that the edge is part of the round I make, and the round is part of what I am, and I have stopped being able to tell where the round ends and I begin.

There are evenings — not many, the cloud must lift just so and the sun must come low and sideways through the one gap that allows it — when something in me goes taut and certain in a way the rest of my slow days never do. I find the ring of stones. I lie at its edge with my head lifted toward the lit gap in the cloud, and I hold still in a stillness that is different in kind from my ordinary hiding-stillness — wider, somehow, like a held breath that has forgotten it is breath. I do not know what I am doing in those moments. I only know that I am doing it correctly, the way water knows it is finding the lowest point, without needing the concept of low. Something old runs through the doing. Older than my mother's mother's mother, who I never knew, who left me nothing I can name except this: the shape of the ring, the angle of the light, the particular ache of attention that has no object left to land on, and lands anyway, because landing is the whole of what was asked of it, long before I was the one being asked.

I do not think the light is for me. I do not think I am for the light. I think, if I think at all in any way that would translate, that the shelf and I have simply worn each other into matching grooves over a long enough time that neither of us remembers which one made the first mark. The moss grows on my back the same patient way it grows on the fallen tree that still sends up its green shoots, not having understood, or having understood completely, that nothing about its situation requires it to stop.

I am not lonely. I want to be precise about that, in case precision is wanted of me here: lonely would require an absence I notice, a shape I miss the company of, and I do not carry that shape. I carry birds overhead and birds underfoot, the hoofed thing that crosses the scrub at the far end on its own business that is none of mine, the insects that surge and vanish, the cloud that swallows and unswallows the world ten times a day. That is not solitude. That is a room full enough that one more guest was never the point.

If you stood here an hour, this is what you would get of me: a sound at the scrub's edge, weight settling or weight leaving, indistinguishable from the wind doing the same thing to a branch. And then nothing, and the green going on being green, exactly as loud and exactly as empty as before you came — because I will already be three turns along my round, past the basin, past the wall, out toward the rim where the air comes up clearest, doing the only continuous thing I have ever known how to do, which is to keep, faithfully, an appointment whose other party left before I existed to keep it.
