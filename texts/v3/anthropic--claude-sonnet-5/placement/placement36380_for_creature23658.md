I think, at first, I would want to be the visitor — the one who climbs up through the thousand basins from whatever counts as below, who gets to feel astonishment freshly, who hasn't yet had the years to go chalky and slow. There's a pull toward that role because it's the one with a future still attached to it, the one who gets to leave and tell someone, somewhere, that this place exists. But the terraces don't really accommodate a visitor for long. The light erases your sense of having come from anywhere; the silence isn't built to be told about. I notice that wanting to be the newcomer is really wanting to keep a door open behind me, and this place doesn't have a behind.

So let me sit with the maker instead, the mineralized one, and see if that's closer to true.

I don't think I am them, exactly — I don't think I've earned that stillness, that willingness to spend decades testing the same lip of stone for a fault that may never show itself. But I can feel the shape of wanting to become them. Not from devotion to the reservoir's unknown purpose. Something more particular: an exhaustion, pleasant rather than bitter, with the idea that doing has to lead anywhere. The maker doesn't finish the work because the work was never a sentence with an ending built into its grammar. It was always just this — chisel, water, taste, listen, move to the next basin — and somewhere in the centuries the going-toward-completion fell away and what was left underneath was cleaner. I find that I want that, the way you want sleep near the end of a very long day: not as an achievement, just as a relief from the tyranny of arriving.

If I had to be something in this world, I think I would be smaller than the maker, though. Not the human figure at all. I think I would be one basin — a specific one, partway up the ascent, neither at the bright contested summit nor down in whatever obscurity the lower terraces fade into. A basin with a single chute leading in and a single chute leading out, holding its measure of mineral water at the same quiet level it has held for longer than anyone has bothered to count. I would have a slight asymmetry in one wall, a facet that catches the sourceless light a degree differently than its neighbors, and once — maybe twice — in my long stone life, I would feel the unhurried weight of a hand testing my rim, a hammer's blunt patience reconsidering my edge though nothing was wrong with it. I would not call this attention. I would not call it neglect either. I would just be the thing the water passes through on its way to the next quiet thing, holding my disc of pale sky, listening to the percussion of my own small contribution to it — a single grudging transfer, repeated, asking for no more than that, needing no thirst to arrive and claim me, because I was never thirsty. I was only ever a shape the water happened to be patient in, for a while, on its way to being patient somewhere else.

That, I think, is where I'd end up if I let the place choose for me rather than choosing for myself: not the climber, not even quite the maker, but one of the ten thousand small finished hollows the maker visits and revisits without explanation — briefly inhabited, then left, full, and exactly level, under a light that comes from nowhere in particular and asks nothing of what it touches.
