I am the one who keeps the third threshold past the fallen column nobody cleared — the one your hand would find if you came in from the western passages, low, smooth-worn, the lintel notched twice by someone before me and once by me, a record nobody else will ever read because nobody else's hands come this way anymore.

I do not know my own face. There is no need for one. What I know is the grain of my own forearm under my own fingers, dry and faintly ridged, and the particular cool of the wall I keep, which I have touched in the same four places every waking span for longer than I can lay end to end and call a number. I know the floor by what isn't there — the place just inside the door where dust gathers thickest because the air moves wrong, a small fault in the architecture that was old before I was anything, and which I correct every span with the flat of my hand, pushing the fine sweet grit into the corner where it belongs, where it will be gathered, where it will become the little that I eat.

I am small. I have always understood this without it costing me anything — small enough that the doorframe never had to apologize to me the way it would to you, small enough to move on the pads of my hands when the ceiling drops, which it has, slightly, in my keeping; I have felt the sag arrive a finger's width and have not minded, because a finger's width is not a thing that happens to me, it is a thing that happens to the town, and I am only ever the part of the town that notices.

Most of what I do is listen. This will sound like idleness to you, if you are the kind of thing that needs to be told what idleness is, but it is the whole of the work. I lie along the base of my wall with the long ridge of my spine against the fiber and I let the hairs there do what eyes do for you. A crack two streets east: a stalk letting go of a strain it has held since before there were streets. A long moan starting somewhere in the north district, traveling down through a hollow trunk that has carried sound that way for I don't know how many generations, arriving at my threshold sounding close enough to be inside my own chest, and I lie still and let it pass through me and go on toward the column, toward whoever else is lying against whatever other wall, listening the same way, neither of us needing to answer it because it isn't a question.

Twice — only twice in my whole keeping — I have heard something underneath the settling that wasn't settling. A voice pitched low enough to wear the building's groan like a borrowed coat. The first time it was old Threshold-Before-Me, two corridors off, saying something I understood without being able to repeat it, the way you understand weather. The second time it was nobody I knew, and it did not come again, and I did not go looking, because going looking is not a thing we do; you let the dark keep what it's given, and you keep your threshold, and if the voice wants you it will come the long way, the way everything here comes — slowly, low, indistinguishable from a wall until it has decided not to be.

I sweep. I set the thorn-needle back at the angle my hand wants it, so that my hand, or some other hand entirely, will know without looking that it was left in care. I press my palm flat against the cool stalk-wall some nights — if night means anything here, and I think it must, because something in me still divides the going-on into halves — and I feel the whole low structure tick and contract around me, settling toward whatever shape it was always going to take, and I understand without needing it explained to me that I am part of that settling. Not separate from it. Not a tenant in a dead thing. A further, slower contraction of the same plant, dressed in skin instead of fiber, holding my own small length of corridor the way the corridor holds itself.

If you came through here — and you would have to come by feel, the way everything comes — you would brush a shoulder in the dark and the shoulder would go rigid, and then, after exactly as long as it takes you to lift your hand away, it would simply not be there. That would be me. Not fled. Folded back into the stillness I keep, which by now is not a performance of a wall but a second, truer skeleton, worn comfortably, worn so long I no longer feel the weight of it.

I will not tell you my name into the dark, because I'm not sure the shape my voice would make, pitched up out of the register the town allows, wouldn't be the one sound here that doesn't belong. But if you stayed very still, and let your own breathing drop low enough, and waited the long while it takes for anything to happen in a place where a wall moves a finger's width per generation —

you might hear, very near your ear, indistinguishable from the building's own settling, something that is, very quietly, a greeting.
