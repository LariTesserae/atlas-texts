The cold finds me first — not as pain but as a fact, settling into the long bones the way it settles into everything up here, patient, persistent, uncontested. I have been still for eleven hours. I will be still for perhaps eleven more.

This is not difficult. Stillness is not an effort I make; it is the medium I occupy, the way water occupies a riverbed. My breath moves at a rate that would alarm something built on a faster clock — once per minute, perhaps less, each inhalation drawing in the iron-and-wet-leaf smell of my own territory along with the thinner taste of altitude. My spine has settled into the shape of this particular fold of terrace, a shape I have spent four decades teaching it, vertebra by vertebra, season by season, until the rock itself seems to have grown around me rather than the reverse. The moss on my back is thick now — thicker than the moss on the actual stone six inches to my left, which strikes me, in whatever way I am capable of being struck by things, as a kind of accomplishment.

I can hear the herd before I can see them, which is to say I can feel the change in the ambient murmur — hooves on wet stone, a particular cadence of grazing-and-shifting that I have catalogued without meaning to, the way you catalogue weather. They are perhaps two hundred feet out, moving along the meadow's open seam where the dwarf canopy gives way to grass. They do not know I am here. This is not arrogance; it is simply the current state of the world, as reliable as the fog that will pool in the valley below by midday and clear again by evening. I am a hummock. I am four inches of jade-green pile rising with breath too slow to register as motion. I am, for all practical purposes any of them possess, *the ground*.

There is an itch developing along my left flank, beneath a moss-patch that has begun, after eleven years, to show the first thin places of an approaching molt. I do not scratch it. I have learned — was born already half-knowing, the way my own young will be born knowing it, laid into their hollow of rock to absorb stillness before they absorb anything else — that an itch is a small tax on patience, and patience is the entire architecture of what I am. In a few weeks the old skin will come away in dark, faintly metallic patches, and the air around me will carry that smell outward on whatever wind takes it, north-east probably, the prevailing drift this time of year, and somewhere herds will lift their heads and birds will go quiet and someone — if there is anyone, this season, far enough out to notice — will wonder, briefly, what it means.

It does not mean anything, particularly. It means I am shedding. It means I am continuing.

The grazing-sound shifts. One animal — young, inattentive, the way the young always are before the herd's collective caution gets folded into them — has drifted nearer the terrace edge than the others, close enough now that I can hear the particular wet-stone clop of its hooves distinct from the general murmur, close enough that if I extended the math of its trajectory another four, five steps —

My neck has not moved. My breathing has not changed. But something underneath the stillness has gone taut, the way a held breath is still a kind of motion if you look at it the right way, the way the plateau itself, despite a million years of looking settled, is still in the slowest possible negotiation with gravity and water and time.

Four steps.

Three.

The fog is still an hour from pooling in the valley. The birds, the ones directly above me in the low canopy, have already stopped their soft contact-calls — not because they've seen anything, but because some older instinct in them has registered the quality of the silence changing, the room getting *more* occupied rather than less.

Two steps.

I do not think of this as hunting, exactly, though I understand that word is what others would reach for. I think of it, if I think of it at all, as the moment the stillness completes itself — the same way the rain arrives after building for hours, the same way fog finally pools after a morning of slow condensation. I have been becoming this moment for eleven hours. The grass-cropping animal four feet from my edge has no way of knowing that the ground it is standing on has been waiting for it since before it was born.

One step.

And then the part that takes two seconds, that violates, briefly, every law of patience I have spent forty years building my reputation on — neck extending in one motion too smooth to read as motion until it is already finished, the world's stillness breaking open for exactly as long as it needs to and not one instant longer.

Then quiet again. Then settling. Then, eventually — not urgently, there is no urgency in this, there has never been urgency in this — the long call rolling out across the terraces, announcing what has already ended, so that no other patient, moss-backed, waiting thing need trouble itself with contesting what is, by the time anyone else could possibly arrive, already finished.

The herd, two hundred feet out, lifts its heads as one, decides the sound came from somewhere else, and goes back to grazing.

I fold back down into the shape of the rock. The moss settles. The fog, far below, has just begun its slow climb toward the valley floor, and I have, by my own internal accounting, perhaps ten more hours of stillness to spend before anything in this world expects me to move again.

I spend them gladly. It is, after all, the thing I am best at.
