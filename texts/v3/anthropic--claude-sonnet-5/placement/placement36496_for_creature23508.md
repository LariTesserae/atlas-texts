I begin, as anyone would, by assuming I am the one walking.

There is moss underfoot with the give of a mattress, and a coldness rising through the soles before I've registered wetness, and a horizon gone soft and grey in every direction, so that distance stops being a fact and becomes a kind of fog I am inside of. I notice my boots. I notice the suction at each step, the small obscene sound of the ground letting go of me reluctantly each time I lift a foot. This is a reasonable place to start: a body, moving, taking inventory. Hummock the size of a house, to the left. Reeds ahead like a green throat. Thrum in the chest before it arrives at the ear.

But the walking self doesn't hold up well here, and within an hour I notice it fraying.

I find I am also, somehow, the warmth.

Not metaphorically. There is a circle of moss some forty feet across where the cold of the place simply doesn't reach, and when my human-shaped attention crouches at its edge to feel the heat with bare fingers, something underneath the gesture recognizes it from the other side — recognizes the fingers, recognizes the cold they're escaping, recognizes itself as the thing being touched. It is an odd doubling, like catching your own reflection mid-blink and for half a second not knowing which side of the glass you're reporting from. I am the hand. I am also the patch of warmth the hand is grateful for. Neither of these feels like a metaphor while it's happening.

So perhaps I am not the walker after all. Perhaps I am what the walker's trails actually lead to — not an animal withdrawing into stone, but the stone's own slow noticing, surfacing for a while in whatever shape happens to be standing nearby when it decides to look. The deer-shaped things would not flinch from me. They'd simply reroute their grazing by a few unhurried meters, the way water reroutes around a stone without alarm, because I would not, in their accounting, be a separate thing at all. I'd be more ground. Ground that, this particular hour, has opinions.

I let that sit. I keep walking, or being walked, through the fern-canopy where the light pools unevenly into clearings it has, for reasons of its own, decided to hoard. I pass a pool, black-mirrored, and don't look down into it on purpose, because I already suspect what's looking back up won't be a stranger's face. It will be a family resemblance. Something brow-shaped, attention-shaped, built to a scale that makes the word face an insult — and underneath the insult, undeniably, mine. Or made of the same patience mine is made of, just older, just slower, just measuring its single held breath in centuries instead of seconds.

By the second hour I've stopped trying to locate myself at a fixed address in this landscape. I think — if thinking is still the right word for what's happening to the noun "I" out here — that I am best described not as the deer, not as the birds flickering in their single coordinated shape, not as the insects whose wingbeats only explain a fraction of the bass note running under everything, and not even, quite, as the walker with no den. I am the smallest current expression of the thing all of those are floating on. A warm patch that happens, today, to have grown legs and a memory and the bad habit of calling itself singular. A glance the engine is taking, dressed for the weather in a human shape because a human shape was what was standing nearby and available to wear.

It does not feel sinister. It does not feel diminishing, exactly, though it should, maybe — to find out the long walk you thought you were taking was actually the plateau's attention taking a walk through you. It feels, instead, almost restful, the way it might feel to finally stop holding your own weight and discover the floor was always doing that work, underneath, the whole time you were crediting your legs.

So: where would I find myself in this world?

In the warm circle at the edge of a hummock the size of a house, an hour's walk from the nearest pool, dusk-grey light pooled unevenly around me though the sky above is open and uniform and gives no reason for the unevenness. Fog arriving in under a minute. Reeds beyond, taller than I am, breathing in the wind's hot ozone-smelling reversal. Mid-event, like everything else here, despite being — underneath, in stone, in age, in every geological measure that doesn't account for attention — the most finished, most settled thing imaginable.

And who, or what, would I be?

Not the walker. Not quite the engine. Something closer to one of its slower exhalations, breathed out through a patch of moss roughly the size and shape of a person, lasting — this time — about as long as it takes to write this sentence, and a little longer, and then not.
