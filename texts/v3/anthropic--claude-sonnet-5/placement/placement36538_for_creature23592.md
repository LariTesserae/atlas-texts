The chisel is the first thing I remember the weight of.

Not a face, not a name—the weight of bone-hafted stone in a four-year-old palm, my mother's thick-knuckled hand folded over mine, guiding the angle against the dry-stacked course we were, even then, not quite finishing. That's how I'd tell it, if telling were the kind of thing we did out loud. I am one of the ones who are here now. Today that means: third-eldest of the Drying-Rack Cluster, currently apprenticed to no one, currently the one who tends the rope-bridge tension on the eastern span because I noticed it sagging and nobody else had noticed yet noticing, which is most of what authority ever is down here.

I want you to understand my hands before you understand anything else about me. They are stained at the pads, green-blue, the color of held light, the color of every sentence I've spoken today—four sentences, all of them at dusk, all of them in the hush-voice reserved for inside walls, to my grandmother, who is dying slowly and beautifully and has earned the right to be spoken to instead of signed at. Everything else I've said since waking has been done with these fingers: a flick at my cousin across forty meters of damp air that meant *the goats are restless on the third terrace, go check the lichen there*, a held stillness at a trader from the Loom-Shed cluster that meant, more eloquently than any word could, *I don't believe your price and I am not in a hurry.*

I am built for up and down, not for distance. My spine carries the permanent question-mark curve of every doorway I've stooped through since before I could ask why doorways were built so low—they aren't, really; we are built around them, the architecture and the body bargaining with each other across generations until neither one remembers who proposed first. My eyes catch light at the rim, throw it back small, so that in the evening crowd-thinning, when the work-songs of hands go quiet and people drift toward their stone hollows, my eyes are two more sparks in a field of a thousand, indistinguishable, which is exactly how I like it.

What I am doing, this season, is not finishing anything. I am the one currently re-stacking the granary into something else—we haven't decided what yet, and I suspect we won't decide until we're most of the way through unbuilding it, because deciding too early is its own kind of premature ending. My great-uncle, who is old enough to have lived through a Going, says I rebuild too fast, that I haven't learned yet to love the gap between intentions, the held beam, the missing course. I tell him—in sign, the wrist-flick that means *probably*—that he's right. I am still learning the discipline of staying unfinished. It does not come naturally to hungry young hands. It has to be practiced like a grammar, like the eyebrow-work my mother drilled into me before I could even firm a fist into a proper word.

At night—if night means anything here, where the light-veins only dim, never go dark—I lie in the green-stained hollow of my sleeping-shelf and listen to the one true sound this whole enormous quiet place permits itself: water, finding its ancient path, landing in its ancient pool, utterly unhurried, utterly certain it will be dripping in that exact place after I am goat-bone and rock-dust and somebody else's half-remembered hand-sign for *the one who used to tend the eastern bridge.*

Sometimes I think about the walled doors. Everyone does, eventually, usually first as children testing whether the threat is real, then later as something stranger—a private, specific dread that has nothing to do with what's behind the brick and everything to do with what the brick *is*: a finished thing. A completed sentence in a language that otherwise refuses periods. I have touched one, once, on a dare, the green pigment smeared into its mortar long dried to a dull, warning rust. It was cold in a way the rest of the stone isn't. The rest of the stone gave up resisting touch ten thousand years ago. That door hadn't given up anything. It was still saying no.

I don't know if I'll be here for the next Going. The old ones say you feel it coming the way you feel weather you've never seen—a pulse in the ceiling-veins, a restlessness that isn't quite yours, climbing up out of the rock the way damp climbs a pillar. Maybe I'll be the one who notices it first, the way I noticed the bridge-sag, and flick the sign across the terraces before anyone else has named the feeling: *it is time.* Or maybe I'll be stacking some other half-formed structure when the whole quarter empties around me overnight, and I'll fold my unfinished walls into my pack the way my mother taught me, without grief, without hurry, and walk out across bare stone toward wherever the place has decided to exhale us next.

Either way I will leave something undone behind me. That's not failure. That's the only prayer we have left, in a cavern that finished becoming itself long before we arrived to borrow it: *not yet. not yet. still becoming.*
