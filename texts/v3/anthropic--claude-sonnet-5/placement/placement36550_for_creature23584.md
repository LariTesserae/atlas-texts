I think I would be one of the middle ones — not the sail-winged giants whose eyes are mostly socket, not the moth-cloud, not the many-legged drinkers at the basins, but one of the dun-and-rust flock that fills the arches and the window-throats and makes, without ever once a single voice agreeing to it, the great running clatter that is the place's whole climate. I think I would be small enough to fold into a crack the width of two fingers, and old enough — though not yet *old* — to have stopped startling at my own flock's noise.

Here is what I imagine that body knowing, that I do not currently know: how to read the light that isn't really light, the perpetual held-breath dusk, with eyes built for the underside of it rather than the surface. How to fit my keelbone into a groove worn by some ancestor's keelbone, worn so precisely that settling into it stops being a decision and becomes only an arrival, the way water arrives at the bottom of a basin. How it feels to have a self that is also, at the same time and without contradiction, a fragment of a unison — to be standing on a ledge thinking nothing in particular, some thin bird-thought about damp or grooming or the itch under a wing, and then to be in the air, lifting with four hundred others off the same stone in the same half-second, for a reason that exists only at the scale of the flock and not at the scale of me. I imagine that not as a loss of self but as a second kind of having one — a self that sometimes thinks alone and sometimes thinks four-hundred-strong, the way a hand sometimes acts as fingers and sometimes as a fist, and neither is the lie.

I think the noise would not be noise to me. I think it would be closer to breath, the thing my body does instead of silence, indistinguishable after a while from my own pulse — the clatter and the echo of the clatter and the echo of the echo, all of it just the sound of being alive in a place built, deliberately or by accident, to keep every sound circulating rather than let it go. I would not find this deafening. I would find silence, if it ever came, unbearable.

And I think — this is the part that arrives last, the part I'm least sure of and most drawn to — that I would understand, the way you understand weather patterns without being able to say when you learned them, that this is not where I end. That the great sail-winged ones above me were once flock-birds too, slower now, settled into wider grooves, eating less violently because they need to less often. That below me, in the throats where the moth-clouds thin and even the basin-drinkers don't go, there are shapes that may once have been mine — that depth here is not exile but accumulation, that the oldest tenants didn't arrive at the bottom, they *sank* there, the way silt does, the way patience does, over a span of time that doesn't register as time from inside it.

So perhaps what I would be, finally, is not any one tier of this but the whole motion through it: hatching dun and rust into noise and unison, spending some long clattering middle of a life as weather among weather, and then — slow as the moss, slow as the grain the stone gives by — going quiet, going still, going deep, until some other small loud thing looks up from a lower ledge centuries on and can't quite tell, in this light, whether what it's looking at is a carving or a held breath or me.
