I think I would not begin as a shape at all. I think I would begin as a temperature — a faint differential between the underside of something and the air above it, noticed the way you notice a held breath only when it's released. That's the first thing: not sight, not place, but a slow awareness of being warmer, fractionally, than the metal at my back.

Then the metal. Riveted plate, vertical, the cool of it leaching into me at a rate so unhurried I can feel each individual degree arrive like a guest. I am leaning against it, or built to lean, my weight distributed along a spine that was clearly designed — by whatever hand designs things on this plateau, evolutionary or otherwise — for exactly this: standing without the cost of standing. My legs, when I finally attend to them, are absurd. Too long, too many joints, folded now in a configuration that from any distance would read as driftwood stacked against a tower, bleached reeds someone forgot to clear away. I could hold this fold for a long time. I understand, somewhere below conscious thought, that I have already held it for a long time, that the holding is not preamble to something else but the thing itself.

So: I am one of the long-legged ones. Or I am being one, for now, the way you can be a held breath for as long as the breath lasts.

The light comes down flat and bright through air too clean to soften it, and I register the light not as brightness but as a kind of pressure on the granular skin of my upper ridge, skin patterned — I can feel this without seeing it, the way you feel a scar you've had since before memory — with fine fracture-lines, the same idiom as the rock under the moss. I did not choose this patterning. It is simply the dialect the plateau speaks, and I am written in it the way the escarpments are.

What I am doing, mostly, is listening. This is the entire occupation, and it does not feel like waiting, though a human dropped into this body would surely call it that, would feel the unbearable itch of nothing-happening within the first ten minutes and want to walk, want narrative, want event. I don't want those things. I want — if want is even the right verb at this tempo — the sigh. The fractional exhalation of a tower three ridges over, easing some infinitesimal amount as the rock beneath it warms or cools by a degree no instrument up here was built to register, except me. Except this listening, which is the instrument.

It comes, eventually. Not eventually in the human sense — eventually in the sense that a glacier eventually reaches the sea. A long low creak, metal finding a new equilibrium, traveling flat across the terraces because there is nothing else in the air to absorb it, no birdsong, no engine, no wind thick enough to compete. I feel it arrive in the patterned ridge along my back before I'd call it sound at all.

And I answer. Something in my throat — if it's a throat — tightens and releases, producing a creak pitched just below where it would register as a creature's voice rather than a building settling. It is not a call. It is a confirmation. *Still here. Still kept.* I send it out across the moss and the pipe-elbows and the standing pools no wider than a hand, and somewhere a tower that may or may not have meant anything by its sigh receives my answer or doesn't, and either way the schedule, whatever the schedule is, continues to be kept.

Below me, at a register so much faster than mine that it might as well be a different universe of time, the beetles are working: packing verdigris paste into a hairline gap, shepherding one bead of condensation a few centimeters down a vein of waxy leaf. I am aware of them the way you're aware of your own pulse when you're not checking for it — present, constant, beneath notice. I have never hunted one. The plateau has told me, in whatever way the plateau tells anything, that I could, that on the rare occasion it would count as practice rather than need, but the occasion has not yet arrived in this folding, and I am content to let it not arrive.

If a visitor came past — and they do, rarely, boots sinking into the spongy ground, breath fogging in air too thin for the altitude — they would see driftwood against a tower. They would look away to check a map or a compass, and when they looked back I would already have resumed the fold, because resuming is most of what I do, the listening being mostly invisible and the standing being mostly all there is to see.

I would not begrudge them the missing. There is nothing to mourn in not being seen by something that measures its whole visit in hours. I have a sigh to wait for. It is patient work, and I am, for now, the shape patience takes here — bone-pale, fracture-marked, folded at the foot of a tower that is, like me, only doing what the young hot rock underneath has always asked of anything it grows: hold still, and keep faith with the schedule, for exactly as long as it takes.
