I unfold slowly along the underside of a root, count the warmth in the air before I count anything else, and decide, the way I decide most things, by inches.

The hot hours are gathering — I can feel it in the soil before I feel it in my skin, a thickening pressure that means the western seam will be calling soon, the slow stone-radiator that has always been there, that I was taught to lean against by something that leaned against it before me, who was taught by something before that. I move along the second terrace, the one with the slumped clay shelf, keeping low, keeping to the places where last season's leaf-fall has built up soft and quiet underfoot. A lizard crosses a coin of light ahead of me, gone before its own motion finishes. I envy that, a little — the cleanness of it, one movement and done. Mine is slower. Mine is mostly about not being seen finishing.

My hands find a curled leaf at the base of a fern and tuck it under the root without my quite deciding to. I don't know why. None of us know why anymore, I think, though I couldn't tell you how I know that either — only that the gesture arrives sometimes like a word in a language I've forgotten how to speak but not how to gesture toward. A stone gets nudged. A leaf gets folded. It serves nothing. It is done anyway, the way you might hum a tune whose lyrics are gone.

There are others near me, loosely — not a den, not quite apart, a kind of mutual tolerance organized around where the warmth pools. I hear one of them two terraces up, the short rising note, the short falling one, then the long nothing that means: still here, still careful, no cause yet for more than this. I don't answer. Answering isn't always required. Sometimes the call is just proof, offered into the dim and humid air, that something is still listening for it.

The big seam waits for me, faintly warm under moss, warmer than it has any right to be, and I have never once wondered if that's strange, because everything about this hollow operates on a scale where strangeness has no name and doesn't need one — the light that lingers a beat too long, the breath that rises from the seep-crack at the day's hottest pitch, the stones leaned against each other at the northern wall that I pass sometimes and feel, without reason, a kind of pull toward, the way you feel a pull toward a door in a house you don't remember entering.

I was not always shaped like this. Something in the marrow of me remembers hands doing more than turning leaf-litter for grubs, though it doesn't remember what. The ring of stones under the leaf-mulch — I have walked past it a hundred times and once, only once, felt my fingers twitch toward it with something that wasn't hunger and wasn't fear, and I didn't go closer, because going closer is not what we do anymore. We do this instead: we keep low, we keep quiet, we let the eye slide past us and settle for the lichen-shape, the dead-branch-shape, the nothing-here-shape. It has worked for longer than memory holds straight.

I reach the seam. I press my flank against the stone and feel the old warmth come up through fur into blood, and somewhere above the canopy the actual sun is sliding its coins across actual ground at its own pace, slightly out of step with ours, the way it always is down here — as if the hollow keeps its own private version of the day, a half-beat delayed, a half-beat dreaming.

I close my eyes. I am careful. I am, more than anything else I might once have been, this: a small warm weight pressed to old stone, listening for the two-note call, willing — for now, for this stretch of the slow hot hour — to be nothing more than that.
