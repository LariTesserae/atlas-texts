I think I would not be a visitor here. The mist doesn't bead on me the way it would on new skin — it sits, settles, finds the same six places along my forearms it always finds, because my forearms have been here long enough to have *usual* places for things to settle.

So: not a visitor. Let me feel out the rest.

My hands are not yet glossed all the way up to the elbow — that takes another twenty years, the older root-walkers tell me, and they tell me this the way people tell you about a retirement you haven't earned the right to want yet. But the knuckles have started. There's a faint sheen there I noticed maybe three winters ago and didn't mention to anyone, because mentioning it is how you get assigned to the next thing, and I wasn't ready for the next thing.

I think I am a root-walker who is being slowly, gently, almost administratively *promoted* into something else, and I think I resent it a little, the way you resent any door opening before you've finished standing in the room you're already in.

Here is what I know how to do: I know how to put my palm flat against a fused-root causeway at dawn and feel which way the growth wants to go this season, the same way you'd feel for a draft under a door. I know the grafting-knife's weight without looking at it. I know which trunks are coaxable and which are stubborn old treaty-makers that settled their shape a thousand years ago and will not be moved by my opinion of where the road should go. I have spent years learning the patience this requires — a patience that looks, from outside, like a kind of staring into the middle distance that worried my mother when I was young and is simply called *attention* now that I'm grown.

Here is what I am starting to know, against my will: the sound. The two-toned thump. I used to hear it the way everyone hears it — a fact of the wall, no more remarkable than the wall itself. But six months ago, doing routine graft-work on a trunk near the lower terrace, I put my ear against the bark to listen for sap-flow the way I'd done a thousand times, and the thump answered back. Not louder. Not different. Just — *aimed.* Like it had noticed where my ear was.

I haven't told the correctors. I think I haven't told them because some unrushed part of me already knows what they'll say, which is nothing alarming at all — a measured nod, a note taken, *yes, that happens, it'll happen more now, here's a chalk stick, here's the schedule for the dusk bells, welcome.* And once they say that, it will be true, officially, permanently, the way a road becomes a road once enough feet agree on it. Right now it is still just mine. A private correction I haven't filed.

So here is where I'd find myself, if I'm honest about it: kneeling on a moss-softened root-buttress an hour before dusk, mallet-and-tine not yet on my belt but sitting on the windowsill at home where I can see it every morning and pretend I haven't started reaching for it, ear against warm old bark, listening to something enormous and unhurried adjust itself one slow room at a time — and feeling, for the first time, not unsettled by this but *included*. Like the town has finally decided which district I belong to, and is simply waiting, with the patience of something ten thousand years settled, for me to stop arguing and walk the rest of the way up.
