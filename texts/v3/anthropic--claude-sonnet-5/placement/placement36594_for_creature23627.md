I think I would be a tuner — not the eldest of them, not yet trusted with the deep throats near the rim, but past apprenticeship, old enough that my hands have their own memory of stone. Let me feel my way into it.

I wake in the gray-ember hour before what passes for dawn down here, in a room whose walls still hold last night's cool the way a held breath holds air. My collarbone is damp. I run two fingers along it without thinking, the way anyone does, and touch them to my lips — habit, not hunger, though there is always a little hunger underneath. Through the thick doorway the town is already half-singing, the wind-organs finding their low waking pitch as the rock above starts to take the first weak heat of whatever counts as morning up on the rim I have not seen since I was nine years old and learned to stop looking for it.

I am built the way my people are built: dark, lacquered, economical. My eyes are too large for surface eyes, my brows heavy, and when I think of climbing topside — which I have done exactly twice, for trade, both times miserable, blinking, lost in a silence so total it felt like drowning — I think of it the way you'd think of holding your breath underwater. Survivable. Not home.

My hands are a tuner's hands, which means they are slightly ruined in a precise, useful way — calluses mapped along two fingers and the heel of the palm from the listen-stick, a thin pale scar across one knuckle from a chisel that slipped during my third year of apprenticeship, when I cut a throat too deep and made it scream instead of sing, and my teacher made me sit beside that ugly shrieking fissure for two full days until I understood, in my body and not just my ears, exactly what I'd done wrong.

Today's work is on the ninth terrace, a wind-throat the guild elders say has gone faintly sour — a quarter-tone off, maybe less, the kind of drift most people would never notice but which sits in my chest like a stone in a shoe. I climb the switchback path with my tools slung across my back, passing doorways that look empty and are not, terraces lush and black-green in the gloom, the heat already pressing in with that familiar, almost companionable weight, like a hand on the back of the neck. A child's clapper sounds somewhere below, off-rhythm in the particular way of someone still learning the count, and I find myself smiling without deciding to.

At the throat I put my ear to the stone before I touch it with anything else. This is the part of the work nobody can teach you, not really — the listening before the listening, the long minute of doing nothing but letting the wind pour through the carved channel and over my skin and into the small bones behind my ears, until I can feel, more than hear, exactly where the note has slipped and why. A hairline crack, probably. Dry-rot working through stone the way it works through everything here, patient and total. I press my listen-stick into the throat's mouth and the pitch comes up through the wood into my palm, and yes — there. A flatness, a wrongness, small enough that a stranger would walk past and call this town's voice unchanged.

I do not think of myself as making music, exactly, though that is what surface traders call it when they climb down out of curiosity and stand on the rim with their hands over their mouths. I think of it as keeping a promise that started before I was born — that this hollow in the rock will keep singing, that the dead in their stone alcoves near the upper throats will have a voice to ride on, that no terrace will ever know the unbearable, sourceless quiet that the old stories use to frighten children into respecting the work.

I take up the chisel. I find the note that's wanted, hold it in my mind the way you'd hold a single coal you didn't want to drop, and begin, very carefully, to cut.
