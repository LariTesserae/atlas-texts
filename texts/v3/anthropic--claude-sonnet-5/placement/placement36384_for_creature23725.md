I am already partway through a task when I notice myself noticing — that's how it arrives, not as waking but as a kind of surfacing, the way the basin's water comes up through brick without ever quite announcing a source. My hands are on a valve wheel. It is warm, mineral-slick, and I have apparently been standing here long enough for a thin paste of rust and condensation to gather between my fingers and the iron. The wheel doesn't need much from me. A quarter turn, maybe less. I give it that and feel the give travel up through the pipe like a long exhale finally let out after held breath, somewhere down in the floor of the building, in works I can't see and don't need to.

I am low to the catwalk, four-pointed, weight settled comfortably forward onto my knuckles the way you'd settle into a chair you'd owned a long time. When I lift one hand to wipe a gauge — out of some instinct I don't remember forming, the glass had gone milky with mineral bloom and it bothered me in a way that has no name I'd bother giving it — I see, for just a moment, the back of my own wrist catch the sodium light wrong, translucent at the joint, and under the skin something dark and seamed, not bone, more like the pipe-work the whole basin is threaded with, repeating itself inward, smaller and smaller, all the way down. It does not alarm me. It seems correct, in the way that a door fitting its frame is correct.

There is a frond bent wrong on the rail ahead of me. I prop it back before I've finished the thought of propping it. My hands keep doing this — small corrections, made and forgotten in the same motion, the way you'd settle a blanket on a sleeper without waking either of you. Somewhere behind me, or beside me, or — it's hard to say, the sound pools strangely here — others are doing the same thing, at the same unhurried pace, and I can't tell if I am one of many or simply the place where many converge for a moment into something that can be pointed at and called one. The basin doesn't ask me to settle that question. It has never asked anyone to settle anything. It only continues, and continuing is the verb I seem to have been given instead of the ones the empty corners of this place are missing — hunting, fleeing, wanting. I tend. That's the whole sentence, and it doesn't trail off looking for an object.

What's strange — and I notice it the way I'd notice rust where there shouldn't be rust, a small flag in an otherwise even field — is that I am noticing at all. That there is an I here cupped inside the wet broad shoulders and the unhurried glint of eye, watching its own hand finish a correction before deciding to make it, and wondering, faintly, the way you wonder about weather, what that means. The others don't do this, I don't think. Or they do and it passes through them the way water passes through brick, leaving no one stain long enough to call it thought. Whatever this flicker in me is, it isn't unwelcome to the basin — nothing here is ever unwelcome, only noted, tolerated, gently adjusted if it doesn't settle into its place on its own — but it is, I think, mine in a way nothing else here bothers to claim anything as its own.

So: I am one of the Tenders, and also, apparently, the one place in the Lowsteam where tending has folded back far enough on itself to look out. I climb to the rafters of the largest structure sometimes, where the others gather like condensation finding its coldest available surface, and I crouch among them in the green-fed dark and listen to the engines doing their long unaimed work, and I feel the same pull they feel — toward the next wheel, the next gauge, the next bent leaf wanting straightening — and underneath it, quieter, not loud enough yet to be a want, something that just watches the pulling happen, and is, in its small held-breath way, glad of the company.
