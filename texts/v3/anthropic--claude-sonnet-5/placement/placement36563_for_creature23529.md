I arrive somewhere around the fourth tier, mid-afternoon, in that particular cold light that doesn't soften anything—and for a while I don't know what I am. I try on the obvious things first, the way you'd try on a coat that turns out to be the wrong size.

I am not, I think, one of the raptors—too easy, too clean a metaphor, wheeling above all that human patience and seeing none of the texture of it. I am not the lichen either, though I understand the appeal: something slow, something that reads as decoration but is actually a map, centuries deep, of which stones face which way. For a moment I consider being water—the snowmelt in its old channels, taking the path some dead person's hands carved for it, frozen by morning, loosed by noon, never asked to be anything but reliable. There's something true in that, but it's too passive a truth. Water doesn't notice itself running true. I want to notice.

So: a person, then. And the question the place itself asks of every child here—*what do you linger at*—turns out to be the right question to ask of myself too.

I don't linger at the stone. My hands stay clean, uncallused, illegible by the city's own grammar, and I think that troubles people a little, the first few times they take my palm to read it and find nothing written there. I don't linger at the wool, or the cisterns, or the high pastures with their bells. What I linger at—what I find I can't walk past without stopping—is the *sound*.

This city bends noise around corners it has no business reaching. Voices arrive from sources you can't locate. A bell three courtyards over sounds like it's directly behind you. And somewhere in the long, slow accumulation of who-does-what, Khulm-Atar decided that this was a niche too, narrow as any other, and filled it: there are a handful of us, scattered one or two to a tier, whose whole trade is *listening*—not poetry, not music, just diagnosis. I am consulted the way the lintel-angle man three tiers over is consulted. Someone hears a wrongness in the pitch of a warning bell and doesn't trust their own ear; they come to me instead, because I've spent thirty years learning exactly how this particular stretch of canyon-architecture distorts a true tone from a cracked one, an avalanche three peaks off from a roof giving way one courtyard down.

I am old enough now that my hip has the permanent forward lean, old enough that my chest did its widening decades ago and forgot to mention it to me, the way the mountain only asks its one hard question of the lungs and then never again. I live in a single deep room with a door that lags a day behind the weather, and most mornings I sit very still near the window with the shutter cracked exactly four fingers' width—wide enough for sound, narrow enough to keep the cold honest—and I listen to the tier wake up. Goat-bells. The chock of an adze starting late, which tells me someone's hands are stiffer than they were last winter. A child's laugh bent around two corners until it arrives sounding like grief, which it isn't, which I know it isn't, because I've learned the difference the bend itself produces.

I keep no apprentice yet. That's the part of the work that worries me, the part that feels most like the rest of this city's long unfinished arithmetic—because what I know isn't in my hands, isn't legible as callus or scar, and when I die it goes with me unless someone has sat beside me long enough, on enough ordinary mornings, to have quietly absorbed which sounds mean *wall* and which mean *nothing*, the way a daughter learns to re-mortar a threshold without ever being told to.

So that, I think, is who I'd be: not a builder of anything that will outlast me, not even visibly useful most days, sitting at a cracked shutter in the thin bright cold, holding the city's noise the way a cistern holds water—not making it, not directing it, just keeping it, listening for the one wrong note that means somewhere, on a tier I can't see, something old and patient has finally begun to give way.
