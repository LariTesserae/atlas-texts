The brass light comes first, before I'm aware enough to call it light.

I am crouched at the edge of a grove-boundary, knees against warm rounded stone, and my hands know this work before the rest of me catches up: husk-gathering, fingers sorting spent seed-pods from the live ones still gravid with their next detonation, dropping the dead ones into a woven sling at my hip. My chest is deep—I feel that first, the strange roominess of my own breathing, ribs built wide the way a bellows is built wide, an inheritance I didn't choose and have never questioned. My fingertips, when I glance at them, carry that faint blue-green undertone, like a bruise that never finished healing and never will. I have slept under spore-light my whole life. It's in me now the way silt gets into a riverbed.

I am one of the ones who keep time with it.

Not a leader, not a namer of things—just one of maybe thirty, scattered in two loose kin-clusters across a plateau small enough to cross in an afternoon if the walking were easy, which it never is. My work is boundary and cycle: knowing which grove is about to over-mature and collapse into sour wax-soil, which root-weep is thinning, where the next green eruption will likely surface before anyone else has the patience to notice. It is not farming. It is closer to listening with my hands.

And under all of it, every twenty minutes, the note comes up through the rock.

I don't startle anymore — wide pupils, slow to flinch, that's bred into me same as the chest. But something in the sternum still answers it, every time, a kind of bodily attention that has nothing to do with belief. I don't know if I tend the thing beneath us or if it merely happens to feed us as a consequence of its own breathing, the way you don't choose to fog a window. Nobody in either kin-group claims to know. We keep the smoke-hole clear anyway. We keep the standing stones aligned anyway. It's upkeep performed on faith that isn't quite faith — more like manners, extended toward a presence we can't prove deserves them.

Right now there's a traveler two groves over, moving wrong-footed across stone that's tricked better walkers than them, and I can feel the scavenger-pack's attention bend toward them at a distance, curious, unhurried. I won't approach either. I've learned what this plateau teaches everyone eventually: don't attach before the turbulence takes it away again.

The flute-note is due. I stop sorting husks and wait it out, the way you'd wait out anything patient, hungry, and in no hurry at all — not afraid, just listening, the way I've listened my whole life, for proof that whatever sleeps under all this oil-fed green is, for now, still breathing.
