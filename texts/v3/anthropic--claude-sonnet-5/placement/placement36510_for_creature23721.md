I come through the fissure low, almost on hands and knees, because that's how the rock wants you to come through it, and for a moment I am only the heat—a body taking on the chamber's temperature, sweat starting at the hairline before my eyes have adjusted. That first moment I could be anyone. I could be a messenger, new to the gallery, sent down with a sealed note for the elders. I could be a forge-child, returning from some errand in the upper dark, already flinching at a sound I haven't placed yet. I try that shape on—broad-shouldered, deafened, missing the smallest finger on my left hand from some accident with the belt-drive that I don't remember happening, only remember after—and it doesn't quite take. The liturgy of the pump-house, the coaxing-vocabulary, the listening-for-trouble-in-a-whine: it's not mine. I'm standing in it like borrowed clothing.

I try the child next, because the children seem like the truest residents, unbothered, weaving. I drop low, fast, fearless, swat at a lantern-cloud without breaking stride along a catwalk bolted into wet stone. For a few seconds it's exhilarating—the sureness of small feet on a structure built for things heavier and stupider than me, the game-logic of *roost, not-roost*, dark you can walk under and dark you can't. But the fearlessness isn't really mine either. It's borrowed too, a costume a half-size too big, and underneath it I can feel how much watching-with-dread the adults are doing on my behalf. I don't want to be carried that way. I want to be one of the ones doing the carrying.

So I let the shape settle backward, downward, into something slower. I find I'm old. Not frail—heat doesn't let you get frail here, it boils the softness off you one way or another—but worn in the specific way of someone who has been doing one task for three decades, hands gone numb-certain around netting and smoke-pots, skin past flinching when something lands on it. My knees complain on the ladders. My voice, when I use it, doesn't have to be loud, because I've learned exactly the pitch that sits under the chuff and over the wing-roar, a register the chamber itself seems to have taught me, the way water teaches a channel where to run.

I am a smoke-warden. This is the shape that takes, finally, all the way down—not chosen so much as *arrived at*, the way the rock arrives at its final wideness only after all the false widenings have been cut away and discarded.

My place is the social margin, the banked, smoldering fire of damp fungus-wood that never quite goes out, that I feed in the gray hours when even the forge-relay is thin. From here I can see most of the chamber at once: the water-tower's patched iron catching the furnace-glow, the pump-wheel's slow groan, the dense unfinished hollows at the edges where the roosts hang like a second, inverted town, breathing as one animal. My hands, when I lift them, don't shake from any bite anymore. There were years of bites. The years are gone; what's left is a kind of fluency.

I listen for the undertone the way the forge-people listen for trouble in a belt. Most nights it's only itself—wingbeat, skitter, the long low hum that's less sound than pressure, felt in the molars before it's heard in the ear. But some nights it shifts a half-beat early, a surge that comes before the lanterns gutter the way it's supposed to, and that's the moment my whole life has been narrowing toward without my ever naming it that way. I don't run. Running would be the wrong rhythm entirely, would say to the chamber *I am afraid of you*, and the chamber, in my experience, answers fear by getting worse. Instead I call it—two words, pitched to cut, no more—and somewhere down the catwalks a woman weights a food-net without looking up, and a man pulls a child by the wrist into a doorway, and none of it looks like panic from above. It looks like weather being read correctly. It looks, if you didn't know better, like nothing happening at all.

I think about the elders who walk the unfinished hollows with their hands trailing the raw stone, reading a pulse they can't quite name aloud, and I understand I am doing the same thing they do, only upward instead of downward—not the rock deciding what it wants to be, but the dark above the rock, the roosts, deciding the same. Maybe it's one continuous uncertainty, the stone and the swarm both still becoming, and maybe what I am, finally, settled all the way into this body with its numb fingers and its banked fire, is just the place in the town where that becoming gets noticed first. Not tamed. Never that. Just noticed, named in two clipped words, and answered by people who go on with their work at the pump-wheel's pace, trusting that I'll call it again if I need to, and trusting, more quietly, that most nights I won't have to.
