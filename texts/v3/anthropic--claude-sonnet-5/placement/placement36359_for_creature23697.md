I'd be the oldest one.

Not by ambition — nobody competes for the tank-stub by the marsh — but by attrition, by simply having stayed longest while the basin filled in its functions around me like water finding level. I came when the boiler-houses were still showing raw brick, kiln-red without the rust-bloom, and I never quite left, and at some point the not-leaving became a kind of office nobody appointed me to.

My hands would tell you most of what I am before my voice did, because the voice doesn't carry far in this air and I've stopped expending it on things gesture can do better. Flat palm, hold. Turned palm, vent it. The small economy of a head-tilt toward the reeds that means: something's thinned out down there, go listen. I'd have the steamed look everyone here gets eventually — not weathered, the place is too young to weather anyone, just held too long over the low fire of it, flushed and permanently faintly damp at the collar.

What I'd actually *be*, underneath the role, is an instrument that the place tuned without asking my permission. Twenty-some years — or whatever the unmarked stretch comes to, time doesn't accumulate visibly here any more than rust does — of letting the engine-drone and the frog-throb become wallpaper, become breath, become the thing my body checks against rather than the thing it notices. I wouldn't startle at the boiler venting behind my back. I would startle, just barely, at a chorus going quiet two voices early, because that's not supposed to happen and the *not supposed to* is the only language my nerves still speak fluently.

I think I'd sit most evenings on that upturned tank-stub not because I'm tired, exactly, but because sitting is how you listen properly in a place with no real quiet in it — you have to hold still enough to let the two registers separate again in your ear, mechanical and biological, so you can hear the seam between them where the wrongness would show up if there were any. Most evenings there isn't. The pumps thud their cycle. The birds renegotiate the pipe-runs for the tenth time that hour, no closer to settling it than they were at dawn. The sky stays the color of an old coin and doesn't commit to evening or day.

I don't think I'd call it happiness. I think I'd call it *idling well* — being the same kind of finished, arrived-at, unchanging thing the works themselves became, and finding that this is enough, that there is a dignity in being something that holds its hum steady and doesn't ask the basin to be anything other than what it already, completely, is.
