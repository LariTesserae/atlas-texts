I am the woman at the fold of rock.

Not the plateau — never the plateau, that roaring permanence that has no need of me and would not notice my absence any more than it notices my presence — but the hinge below it, the place where the climb's last switchback softens into something almost like ground again, almost like shelter, before the stone gives up pretending to be anything but itself.

My knees know this trail the way the trail knows the hooves that wore it. I did not choose to stay here so much as accumulate here, year by year, the way grit accumulates in a basin until one day you look down and realize you have become a fixture, a feature, a thing climbers picture before they picture the climb. I keep a small fire because a small fire is what's needed — not warmth against the real cold, which lives up above and laughs at fires, but warmth against the idea of the cold, which arrives an hour before the cold itself does, and needs meeting first.

I sweep my basins by hand. I could let them go grey with grit like the ones above, let them fill with whatever the wind carries, but that is not my work. My work is the opposite of the plateau's work. Up there, things settle into their final shape and stop. Down here, at the door, I tend — daily, deliberately, the small undramatic labor of keeping water clear enough to trust, keeping a fire low enough not to waste the wood that had to be carried up from softer country.

I have read ten thousand mornings in the pitch of the bird-roar before I've even opened my eyes. I know restless from calm the way you'd know a held breath from an easy one. I have watched the herds turn as one animal so many times that I no longer find it strange; I find it grammatical, a sentence the plateau is always in the middle of saying, that I've simply learned to read by its rhythm rather than its words.

I tell the climbers what they need and nothing more. How much water. Which basins. When the herds are likely to cross, so they can time their walking around the thunder instead of through it. I do not tell them what they'll feel up there, because that isn't mine to give them, and because they wouldn't believe me until the light itself told them — that hard declarative light that doesn't warm, only insists.

I have been up. Fewer times now than once. It does nothing for me that it doesn't do for anyone — it simply *is*, entire, indifferent, magnificent in the unbothered way a held note is magnificent. I go up to remember what I'm tending the door of. I come back down because down is where the tending happens. The plateau keeps itself. I keep the place where people get handed to it, and handed back.

They leave me coins sometimes. Dried meat. A thank-you, which I take the way I'd take weather — present, then passed.

I have no name worth remembering. That's correct. I am not the destination.

I am only ever the last warm thing before, and the first warm thing after.
