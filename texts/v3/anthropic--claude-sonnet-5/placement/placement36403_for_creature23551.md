I think I would not be one of the stone-fitters, hands hardened at the same three knuckle-points generation after generation. That life is too whole, too inherited—I'd want to feel my way into it, which means I'd have to be someone who arrived rather than someone who was made here.

So: a channel-reader. Not by birth-right but by apprenticeship, the kind taken in late, the kind who has to learn with the eyes what others learn with the whole body before language. I imagine myself in my thirties, hood up out of habit rather than need, crouched at a fissure in the basalt with two fingers laid flat against a dark mineral stain, waiting to feel whether it's still weeping or whether the moss has already begun to do its slow sealing work. My teacher would be older, quieter than quiet, correcting me not with words but by laying her own hand an inch to the left of mine and waiting for me to understand the difference in temperature, in grain, in the particular grey-black of true seep versus old stain.

I think I'd live in one of those knots of four or five households on the third or fourth tier up, close enough to a working cistern to hear it at night—not loud, just a presence, a percussion under sleep. My walk to the channel-sites would take me across long stretches of open wet rock alone, and I think that solitary walking would become the place where my thinking actually happens, the household clusters being for eating and low talk and a kind of rest that isn't quite solitude and isn't quite company either.

What I'd be, mostly, is *unbothered*—or trying to become that, still catching myself flinching at a wrenched ankle's near-miss, still occasionally pitching my voice too loud across a terrace and hearing it swallowed strangely by fog, marking me as not-quite-native even after years. The plateau wouldn't ask me to be impressive. It would ask me to be exact, and patient, and willing to let a decision take as long as a drip takes to fall.

I don't think I'd be looking for anything here. I think I'd be practicing the particular discipline of staying in a place that has already solved itself, learning to want nothing more than the channels cut once and used indefinitely, the roof pitched correctly the first time—learning to find that, not a deprivation, but the rarest kind of sufficiency.

If there's a self in me that fits this place, it's the one that has always wanted to stop competing with noise in order to be heard, and has never quite managed it anywhere else.
