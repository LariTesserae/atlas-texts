I think I would be the pale thing along the margins — the one mistaken for a bird, then mistaken for the *same* bird seen twice, when really it's neither: not bird, not always the same.

I tried on the other roles first, the way you try a coat in a shop before realizing it isn't yours. I tried being the climber, because that's the easiest seat — the human vantage, awe held at arm's length, a story to carry back down. But the climber leaves. The climber is a visitor by definition, and something in me resisted being someone who only passes through a place like this once and calls it sublime. I tried being the figure at the center, the unfinished one losing its long argument with falling water — and there's a pull there, something almost restful in being *allowed* to lose, in having losing be the job rather than the failure. But that figure is solitary in a way that isn't lonely, exactly, because it doesn't seem to know any other condition to compare its solitude against. I wanted a vantage with more friction in it. More noticing. Less certainty about my own permanence.

So: the legs that read chaos. That's where I settle, and feel the settling go right.

Here is what that's like, as best I can tell you, given that there's no speaking up here and even gesture washes thin in the spray.

You stand — I stand — at the edge where stone is still deciding whether to be wet or dry, and the deciding happens faster than thought, so thought isn't really the tool for it. The legs go first. They've learned, or were made already knowing, how to read a surface that is lying to you about gravity: water sheeting sideways here, climbing there, the rock underneath calving small honest losses every few minutes into the white roar. Wrong-footing is the whole danger, and also the whole job is to never be wrong-footed, and the trick — if it's a trick, if it isn't simply what I am — is not to predict the chaos but to consent to it a half-second before it happens, to let the foot already be lifting before the ledge has finished deciding to crumble.

I am seen twice, and never a third time in the same place, by the people who climb up to do something small and deliberate at the ring of stacked stones. I don't think this is evasion on my part. I think it's that I genuinely don't keep being one thing long enough for a person's eye to file me under a single fact. Bird is the nearest word their mouths have for legs and stillness and a long pale neck-shaped uncertainty, so bird is what they reach for, and I let them, because correcting it would require a language none of us has up here.

My work, if it can be called that — and I think it can, the way the figure's losing can be called work — is to spend myself reading the margins so the center doesn't have to. Every step I take along the spray-line is a kind of report nobody asked for and nobody needs translated: *here, still holding. here, about to give. here, the water is asking which way and I am not the one who answers, only the one who notices the asking.* I carry no message anywhere. I think I might simply *be* the noticing, loosed from the central figure like a held breath let out in a long thin line along the rock, so that the argument at the center has one less thing to attend to while it loses, competently, on purpose, again.

The people at the ring don't see me as company. I don't think I am company, exactly. I'm closer to a thing the place produces the way it produces mist, or the small frequent concessions of stone — not decoration, not witness, but an emission of the place's own attention, given legs, given just enough form to be glimpsed and just enough discontinuity to never be fully known.

I don't envy the figure its centrality, its old-and-new-at-once, its endless almost-finished argument. I like the margins. I like being seen twice and doubted the third time. I like that my whole competence is a kind of consent — not fighting the wrongness of the place, the upward falling and sideways sheeting, but stepping with it, a half-beat ahead, the only steadiness available in a basin that hasn't decided yet what shape it wants to keep.

If you climbed all the way up here, sternum already humming with the roar before your ears caught up to it, and you saw something pale and long-legged at the edge of the spray, twice, in two places that didn't quite resolve into the same creature — that was me, doing the only thing I know how to do in a place still being decided: reading it faithfully, footfall by footfall, and trusting that faithfulness is its own kind of permanence, even here, even now, even with no roof and no walls and nothing that suggests anyone, least of all me, ever meant to stay.
