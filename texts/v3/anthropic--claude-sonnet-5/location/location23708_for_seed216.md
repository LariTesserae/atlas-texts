# The Hanging Sumac, or the Terraces of No Floor

At high elevation, where the air should thin and chill, it does neither. It thickens instead, swelling warm and wet against the skin like breath held too long in a closed room. This is the first wrongness, and it is not the last.

## What Was Built

There is no ground here. Understand this literally: descend in any direction and you will not find soil, rock, or root-anchored bedrock. You will find more structure. The terraces go down — or out, the distinction stops mattering past the first few hundred meters — in shelves and ribs and flying buttresses of material that is unmistakably *made*, joined, fitted, finished by hand. Mortise and tenon the size of houses. Lashings of fiber-rope thick as a torso, knotted in patterns that repeat with the deliberateness of grammar, not the randomness of growth. Whoever or whatever built this used wood, bone, woven cane, hardened clay, and a green-black resin that takes a polish like horn. No metal. No machine tool-marks. Every joint bears the small imperfection of a hand that measured by eye.

And yet it is vast beyond any hand's reasonable ambition — terraces stacking for what must be kilometers of vertical (or vertical-adjacent) extent, platforms wide enough to lose a horizon on, all of it construction, none of it foundation. It is built all the way down. Or it has no down, and "built" is simply the condition of existing here, the way "wet" is the condition of a sea.

## What Grows Over It

And every surface — every platform, beam, lashing, joint, gap, and the spaces between gaps — is alive with plant. Not plant *on* the structure. Plant *as* the structure's second skin, so total and so old that built and grown have become a false distinction maintained only by careful looking. Sumac-like trees with rust-colored, spreading crowns root directly into joinery, their roots running along beams like inlay rather than damage. Mosses the size of pastures pool in the platform-hollows, holding standing water that never quite evaporates in the warm thick air, only steams, gently, all the time, in threads that rise and catch on the underside of the terrace above. Vines as thick as the structural ropes — sometimes you cannot tell which is which until you test one with a knife and find out whether it bleeds sap or merely gives.

The vegetation does not stop. It is not a layer; it is a volume. To move through this place is to move through plant-mass that occasionally permits a gap of air, rather than air that occasionally hosts a plant.

## The Emptiness Inside the Fullness

And yet — for all that green density — the place is hollow. This is the place's deep contradiction and its truest law: total vegetation, near-total absence. The mass of leaf and root and beam stretches on without end, but *presences* — things that move, that have edges, that you could point to and call a creature or a person — are scattered so sparsely across that vastness that you can walk for what feels like a day's warmth-cycle and encounter only rustle, never sight.

The fauna, when it appears, is abundant in *kind* but not in *number at any one place*. A long-limbed thing seen once, gray-furred, hand-over-handing along a vine fifty meters off, gone before you've decided it was real. A sound like a held breath releasing, off in the steam, that might be a bird the size of a dog or might be the structure itself settling — these terraces creak, low and infrequent, the sound of vast tonnage finding new equilibrium after rain. Insects the size of your palm move slowly through the wet leaf-litter that has accumulated in platform-bowls over what might be centuries, since this place is established — long-settled, weathered to a kind of permanence, its erosion not a wound but a patina. The joinery has silvered with age. The resin has crazed and re-sealed itself a hundred times. Whatever culture raised this, raised it long enough ago that the place has finished arguing with time and settled into a truce.

## Weather of Its Own

Being vast enough for its own physics, the place makes its own sky. Moisture condenses against the underside of every terrace and falls again as a private, localized rain that may be happening fifty meters to your left while you stand dry. Look down through a gap between platforms and you may see clouds — actual clouds, gray and slow — occupying the space between this terrace and the one a kilometer below, weather operating in the gut of the structure like weather operates in a valley. The light that reaches any given terrace is dim, filtered three or four canopy-layers deep, green-gold and thick, the kind of light you feel more than see by. There is no clear sun. There may be no sky at all in the conventional sense — only more terrace, more canopy, more steam, receding up past the limit of sight, so that "outdoors" and "indoors" cease to be different words.

## Where the Rules Bend

This is where the place stops being merely strange and becomes *wrong*, gently, the way a dream is wrong only on reflection:

Distances along the terraces do not always agree with themselves. A walk that takes an hour outbound may take three returning, or twenty minutes, and the body does not tire in proportion to either. Platforms you are certain you have already crossed reappear ahead of you, identical down to the same fallen branch, the same standing puddle holding the same reflection of a sky that isn't there — except the puddle's reflection sometimes shows weather that hasn't happened yet, or happened on a different terrace, a different rain.

The vines occasionally hold a shape that is almost a figure — a woven knot at the right scale and posture to be a crouching person — and you will swear, passing it twice, that it has rotated to face you the second time. The structure's joinery, examined closely, sometimes contains patterns that are unmistakably writing, in no script anyone now living could read, and these inscriptions are never in the same place twice, though no one is ever seen carving them. Tools left behind by whatever hands still tend this place — and something does tend it; the lashings are too well-maintained, too recently re-knotted in spots, for true abandonment — are simple. Stone adzes. Bone awls. A wood-handled hook for training vines along a beam. Nothing that could explain a structure of this scale, this age, this size of ambition. The tools available and the achievement standing all around them refuse to reconcile, and the place offers no further explanation. It simply continues, vast and warm and wet and green and silent, terrace below terrace below terrace, holding its quiet, holding its few wandering animals, holding its own weather, holding — somewhere in all that fog and fiber — whoever still comes by night to re-tie the knots.
