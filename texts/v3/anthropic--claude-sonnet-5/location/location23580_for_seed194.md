The Husked Town

There is no light here, and there has been none for longer than anything living can remember being told about. What there is instead is a kind of pressure-dark, a darkness with body to it, thick with the smell of dry green things and something papery, something like old bread or the inside of a seed pod left too long in a drawer.

The settlement — and it is a settlement, unmistakably, with streets that bend at intentional angles and thresholds set at the height of someone's hip — is built entirely from and into a single dead-standing thicket. Not a forest. A thicket, vast beyond what a thicket should be, vines the diameter of bridge cables grown rigid as oak and never decayed, stalks like townhouse walls, husks like fallen roofs. Nothing here is alive in the way you'd mean it. The vegetation reached some terminal state long ago — flowered once, maybe, or never flowered, just grew and grew low across the flat ground until it filled every cubic foot of the low basin it sits in, then stopped, then dried into permanence. It does not rot. It does not feed anything. There is no fauna because there is nothing here for fauna to want.

Into this standing mass, small hands did the rest. You find it by feel: doorframes notched into the thick stalks with hand axes, dead pith hollowed out and packed into floors, partitions woven from the brittle, never-rotting fiber and lashed with cord spun from the same plant that built the wall it hangs on. The architecture is the plant. The plant is the architecture. A street is a corridor worn smooth between two stalk-walls by feet that passed this way for centuries. A plaza is a clearing where some larger growth fell sideways long before anyone arrived and was simply built around like a fallen column nobody bothered to clear.

It is a town. It is sized like a town — districts, a center, outlying clusters connected by passages — but you would barely know it from any single vantage point because there is no single vantage point. The scale reveals itself only intimately, room by room, threshold by threshold, your hands finding the adze-marks on a wall, your foot finding the worn dip of a step. No skyline. No silhouette. Just an endless near-empty interior, chamber opening into chamber, half-felt walls receding into smell and dark.

It is loud in here, and that's the strange wrongness of the place, because there's nothing to be loud — no animals, no water, almost no wind that you'd call wind. But the dried structure ticks and groans and cracks constantly, a slow architecture settling at a register far below comfort, vast stalks contracting against the cool, fiber walls popping like knuckles somewhere two streets over, and the sound carries oddly through the dead hollow stems, so a creak from the far district arrives at your ear sounding close enough to touch. Sometimes there are long low moans, air finding a reed-throat in some hollowed trunk and singing through it for whole minutes before going silent again. The town breathes, in other words, without anything in it breathing.

Population: unclear. Doors are kept. Floors are swept — you can feel where the dust has been pushed into corners by something with intention. Tools rest in alcoves: stone-bladed, bone-hafted, the simplest fabrication, an axe here, a needle of hardened thorn there, set down as if to be picked up again. But nothing moves, nothing speaks, nothing answers a call into the dark. Whether the builders left, or whether they simply learned to keep so still and so silent that their presence reads as absence, is not a thing you can determine by feel alone.

Time passes here the way it passes in stone. Things shift, but at the pace of centuries: a wall slumping a finger's width per generation, a passage slowly choked as a stalk above it dries further and sags. The erosion isn't water-cut or wind-scoured — it's the erosion of dead plant matter settling infinitely slowly into its truest, most collapsed shape, a town quietly subsiding into the bones of the thing it was carved from. It will likely look almost exactly the same in another hundred years. It has likely looked almost exactly the same for several hundred already.

There is no horizon, no sky, no telling where the thicket-mass ends and open ground might resume, if it ever does. The cool dark just continues, room after husked room, the groaning going on overhead and underfoot, patient, total, settled into whatever final shape this was always going to take.
