# The Stack at Kelvinhead Terrace

It is a single structure, and it is the only structure for a hundred miles, and it goes up so far that the upper third of it disappears into a band of permanent cloud that it generates itself.

Call it a terrace because that's the word the people who still live near its base use, though there is no slope here, only the Stack — a fluted column of riveted iron and green-black bronze, no wider at its foot than a small village square, rising in narrowing tiers like a ziggurat that someone took and stretched into a needle. Each tier is a ring-shaped garden, cantilevered out from the central shaft on iron brackets the size of bridges, and each ring is thick with growth: ferns the size of trees, mosses that drip in continuous sheets, fruiting vines that have colonized the gaps between rivets so thoroughly that from a distance the whole Stack looks half-plant, half-machine, a green spine wrapped in iron ribs.

Nothing about it is natural. The soil in the rings was carried up in buckets, generations ago, by machinery that still runs. The water comes from condensers near the cloud-layer, vast cupped intakes that wring moisture from the Stack's own induced weather and send it down through a thousand miles of capillary piping, dripping out through valve-flowers at each tier — small brass nozzles shaped, ornamentally, like orchids, that hiss and weep at intervals nobody has ever fully mapped. The dampness here is not rain. It is plumbing, sweating, leaking, a slow intermittent weeping from joints that have wept in the same places for centuries and grown moss-collars around themselves as proof.

The scale is intimate despite the height. You are never in a vast room. You are in a terrace, a ring, a landing — each one no bigger than a chapel or a walled garden — and then a stair, narrow, spiraling, hot with engine-breath from the shaft's core, and then another terrace. The whole Stack is a sequence of small green rooms threaded on an iron spine, climbing into the sky one modest chamber at a time. Between terraces, the space is empty — stairwells, conduit shafts, vertical nothing, a held breath of air that smells of hot metal and chlorophyll.

The sound never stops and never resolves into anything you could call a rhythm. Pistons somewhere below the lowest visible ring drive the water up; gear-trains the size of houses turn inside the shaft's core, their meshing audible as a continuous grinding roar that the iron conducts upward and outward into every terrace, so that even the topmost garden, swaddled in cloud, thrums with it. Layered over the mechanical roar: birds. Dense, abundant, deafening — colonies of something like swifts that have nested in the cogwork housings for so many generations they're indistinguishable from native fauna, screaming and wheeling in updrafts that the Stack's heat generates, hunting insects that breed in the wet moss. Frogs occupy the lower rings in numbers that should be impossible given how little standing water exists; they've adapted to brass-nozzle drip-cycles instead of rain, calling in response to the hiss of valve-flowers rather than weather.

The light is ordinary daylight, filtered through the Stack's self-made cloud-band into something diffuse and gray-gold, equally distributed, shadowless. Warm air rises continuously off the central shaft's machine-heat, so each terrace sits in its own faint thermal updraft, keeping the gardens warm even where altitude should make them cold. This is the wrongness everyone who climbs the Stack eventually notices: it gets no colder as you ascend. The machine has overridden elevation. Ferns that should die in the thin air at the upper terraces instead thrive, fat and glossy, fed on engine-warmth and condenser-damp, three hundred floors above anything that should support them.

The erosion here is not decay — it's patina. Centuries of the same drip patterns have worn channels in brass. Centuries of the same root systems have cracked and re-set the same joints until crack and joint are the same thing, grown together, established, settled. Nothing is falling apart. Everything is exactly as worn as it should be after this long, no more, no less — a machine in equilibrium with its own slow erosion, repairing at the same rate it wears, tended by absolutely no one that anyone living has ever seen.

Because that's the other rule: it is empty. You can climb for days — past a hundred terraces, each one a small lush iron-walled garden loud with birds and dripping valve-flowers and the grinding bass roar of the core — and meet no one. There are signs of tending: cut vines stacked neatly, a swept landing, a valve recently re-packed with fresh gasket-leaf. But never a person at the work. The maintenance happens in the gaps between your visits, on its own slow schedule, by hands or mechanisms unseen. The few human residents cluster at the bottom, in Kelvinhead Terrace proper, and rarely climb past the twentieth ring — not from rule, but because the higher you go, the more total the absence becomes, the more the Stack feels like a thing sustaining itself for reasons that have stopped requiring witnesses.

At the very top, above the cloud — so the climbers' stories go — there's one terrace ringed not with garden but with bare iron, swept perfectly clean, facing a sky that's said to be a different color than the sky below. No one currently living has confirmed it. The stairs continue. The birds thin out past a certain height and stop. The grinding roar of the gear-core is still audible even there, they say — fainter, but never silent, climbing with you the whole way, the one sound the Stack will not let you leave behind.
