# The Hanging Meadows of Vond's Throat

The mountain does not have a summit. It has a series of agreements with gravity, each one a shelf of black rock cantilevered into fog, and on each shelf grows a meadow that should not exist at this height, in this cold, in this thin and grudging air.

You climb through darkness that never quite resolves into night or day. The light here is ambient, sourceless — a grey-violet bruise that seeps from the cloud-ceiling above and the cloud-floor below, so that the mountain seems to hang suspended between two skies. Mist threads through everything, not falling as rain so much as simply *condensing*, beading on every blade of grass, every lichen-crusted boulder, running in thin filaments down rock faces worn smooth by some erosion so patient it has stopped seeming like erosion and started seeming like the rock's natural shape, its settled truth.

## The Terrain

Extreme elevation here doesn't mean peaks — it means *tiers*. The mountain rises in a long ladder of plateaus, each one wide enough to lose sight of its edges in the mist, each one separated from the next by cliffs that vanish upward and downward into cloud. Scale is regional: you could walk for a day on a single shelf and not cross it. The rock is old basalt, established into rounded knuckles and long fissures, padded everywhere with moss so thick it swallows footsteps.

Vegetation is lush in a way that seems to argue with the cold — fernlike growths with leaves like wet leather, low groundcover that glows faintly blue-green when bruised, stands of pale trees with bark like peeling paper, their canopies always dripping condensation even when no rain has fallen in weeks. The water table here is strange: barely any standing pools, no rivers, just a constant low humidity that the plants seem to drink directly from the air. Damp, not wet. Everything glistens without ever being soaked.

## What Moves Here

The density is low — vast gaps between any two living things — but the fauna is abundant in aggregate, spread thin across enormous terrain. You hear them before you see them, and mostly you only ever hear them: huffing breath in the fog fifty meters off, the click and drag of something heavy-bodied moving across stone, wingbeats overhead that displace air without ever revealing their source. Pale, long-limbed grazers move in loose herds through the high meadows, cropping the glowing groundcover, their eyes reflecting nothing because there is no light to reflect. Things with too many joints in their legs pick their way along cliff-edges with unbothered confidence. Occasionally, far below in a lower tier, something enormous exhales — a sound like a held organ note — and every smaller creature on your shelf goes still for a full minute.

The ecology operates in slow, vast cycles. Predation here is unhurried, almost ceremonial — things stalk for hours, kilometers, across open meadow, in near silence, and the kill, when it comes, is a single muffled event swallowed instantly by the fog and the moss.

## The Machines

And then — the engines.

There is no built structure here. No walls, no roads, nothing assembled by hand. And yet, embedded in the rock itself, as if grown rather than installed, are mechanisms: turbine-shapes half-sunk into cliff faces, their blades long stilled or turning so slowly you doubt your own eyes; pipe-like formations of black metal-rock hybrid material threading through the basalt like veins, occasionally exhaling a slow plume of warm vapor that the cold immediately seizes into fog. Somewhere below, perpetually, there is a sound like a heartbeat made of pistons — felt more than heard, a vibration through the soles of your feet that never quite stops.

Nobody built this. Or: something built this so long ago, and so thoroughly, that it has stopped being artifact and become geology. The mountain runs on something. It has always run on something. The meadows grow in the heat-bled margins of machinery that may never have had an operator, may simply have been turning since before there were eyes to notice.

## The Quiet

Sound here is rare and therefore enormous. Quiet sounds — moss compressing, water beading and dropping, far-off animal breath — carry with unnatural clarity precisely because there is so little else. The silence is not empty; it is *occupied*, the way a held breath is occupied. When something does make noise — a distant turbine-groan, an animal's call, a rockfall swallowed by cloud below — it arrives like an event, a small puncture in the fabric of the place, and then the fabric closes again.

## The Rule of the Place

Nothing here is finished arriving at what it is, but nothing is in a hurry either. The mountain has been doing this — growing, breathing, turning its hidden machinery, raising its grazing animals through tier after foggy tier — for a span of time that doesn't compress into a number. Weather moves through in slow fronts: fog thickens to a near-blindness, thins to a translucent grey, never clears. Temperature drifts by hours, not by season; you feel cold settle into your tier and then, gradually, lift, replaced by a slightly less cold cold.

There is a sense, walking here, of being a very small and very late guest at something that has been quietly happening, unattended, for a length of time that makes the word "ruin" useless. Nothing has fallen here. It has only ever continued.
