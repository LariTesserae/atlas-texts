# The Plateau of Standing Engines

The elevation arrives first, before anything else can be understood. This is a highland so old it has forgotten the violence of its own making — a vast tableland worn down not into jaggedness but into a kind of geological exhaustion, every peak that once existed now sanded into long shallow swells of dark rock. There was water here once, the slight dampness in the air seems to admit, but that was unfathomably long ago, and what's left is a memory the stone keeps without telling anyone.

No plants. Nothing chlorophyll-bearing has ever taken root in this soil, or if it did, that chapter closed before the current rocks finished cooling. The ground is mineral through and through — basalt-black, iron-red in patches, a fine grit that doesn't blow because there's barely wind to lift it. The silence has weight here, not emptiness but density, like the quiet inside a held breath.

## The City That Does Not Sleep Because It Was Never Awake

Across the plateau, spaced with strange deliberateness, rise the built structures — and "urban" is the right word, though no word feels quite sufficient. These aren't ruins. They aren't new. They simply *are*, the way the rock is, fixed into a permanence that erosion gave up trying to argue with eons ago. Towers of riveted dark metal and fitted stone stand in loose clusters, connected by causeways of the same black material, their geometry industrial — smoke-stacks, gantries, the bones of machinery — but utterly without the bustle that word implies.

The factories run. That's the strange part. Low mechanical sounds — a distant hum, an occasional deep clank, the sigh of some pressure system releasing — drift across the open ground between settlements, mostly quiet but never fully silent, as though the machinery breathes once a minute instead of constantly. No one is seen tending it. The engines have either always been running or have been running so long that the distinction between automatic and abandoned stopped mattering. Pipes still carry something between buildings. Gauges, where visible by touch or the dim ambient glow of instrument-lights, still hold steady readings, unchanging, as if the whole apparatus reached its operating equilibrium millennia ago and has simply continued to hold that note.

## Darkness as a Permanent Condition

There is no day here, or if there is, it has been so thoroughly eclipsed that no one designs around it. The dark isn't absence so much as the native medium — the way water is the native medium of a sea. Light comes from small persistent sources: a banked furnace-glow leaking from grates, dull amber bulbs strung at long intervals along the causeways, the occasional cold blue flicker of an instrument panel. These don't illuminate so much as mark — way-points in the dark, confirming that the architecture continues beyond what you can see, that the plateau's built spaces extend onward, sparse but present, for a long way yet.

## The Scattering

The density is low — this is the key to the place's mood. Structures cluster, but the clusters are islands. Between them: long stretches of open black ground, silent, where the wind, what little there is, moves grit in slow drifts against foundations. Walking between settlements means walking through genuine emptiness, the nearest tower a shape on the horizon for a long while before it resolves into detail. The settlements don't reach toward each other. They were built complete, each one, and have stayed that way.

## What Lives Here

Almost nothing, and what does is rarely seen — small things, the suggestion of small things: a scrabbling sound from inside a wall conduit, never traced to its source; something with too many points of contact moving along a girder far overhead, gone before it can be confirmed. No birds, no insects in any recognizable sense. If anything evolved here it adapted to the machinery rather than to any biology, found niches in vents and crawlspaces and the residual heat of engine-housings, and has lived there, unbothered, for longer than memory holds.

## The Strangeness Underneath

It isn't merely that the place is industrial and ancient at once — though that contradiction alone unsettles, factories that should have rusted to nothing instead caught in a permanent working stasis, never decaying, never advancing. It's the suspicion, which grows the longer one stays, that the machinery isn't maintaining the plateau so much as the plateau is maintaining the machinery — that the high, ancient, settled landform and the iron towers are not separate things at all but one continuous structure that finished becoming itself an immense span of time ago and has simply continued, by some logic that doesn't require makers or maintainers, to *be*. The gauges hold their readings not because anyone reads them but because the system reached a final form and final forms, on this plateau, do not change.

There are doors. Some open. Behind them: rooms with the same dim amber light, the same patient hum, furniture or fittings arranged for purposes that can be guessed at but not confirmed, because there is no one to ask, and the rooms do not seem to mind the absence of an asker. Whatever built this place may still, technically, be present — diffused into the infrastructure itself, indistinguishable now from the engines it built to serve it.

The overall impression is of a vast, dark, warm, breathing machine-country, laid out like a city but inhabited like a mineral bed — patient, settled, finished, and listening, in its sparse and quiet way, to its own unaltering hum.
