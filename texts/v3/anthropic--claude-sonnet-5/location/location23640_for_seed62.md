# The Cooling Terraces

The plateau sits high enough that the air thins and sharpens, but the light here behaves wrong for the altitude — it comes down too bright, too even, as if the sky itself were a lid of brushed metal rather than open atmosphere. There is no haze. Every ridge edge cuts clean. This is young country: the rock has not yet learned to round itself off, and the escarpments still wear the fracture-patterns of whatever violence made them, fresh as a break in green wood.

Out of this raw stone grows an improbable density of plant life. Not jungle exactly — the cold keeps it from sprawling into true riot — but a tight-packed, vertical lushness: moss in thick plates over every horizontal surface, ferns unfurling from cracks with a kind of mechanical precision, broad waxy leaves stacked in shingled layers that catch the bright flat light and throw it back in dull green coins. The vegetation doesn't sprawl outward so much as accumulate, layer over layer, like sediment that happens to be alive. Walking through it means pushing past near-touching fronds at chest height while your boots sink into spongy ground that never quite turns to mud — there's just enough dampness, condensation beading on leaf-undersides, faint runnels finding their way between roots, occasional standing pools no wider than a hand — to keep everything green without ever pooling into a real river.

Threaded through this — and this is the part that takes longest to notice, because the plants have grown *into* it rather than around it — is machinery. Not ruins. Working machinery, or machinery that still moves as if working: terraced installations of riveted plate and pipe running along the contour lines of the slopes, the kind of architecture that exists to manage heat or pressure at scale. Cooling towers, squat and barrel-ribbed, stand at intervals across the open plateau like a dispersed herd, venting nothing visible, their flanks gone mossy-green halfway up. Pipelines run along the ground under the fern-cover, surfacing here and there in elbow-joints stained with verdigris and rust both, disappearing again into the undergrowth. Everything is scattered rather than clustered — no single facility, no town, just components of something larger spread across kilometers of terraced highland, the structures occasional enough that you can walk a long while between them, dense vegetation filling every gap.

It runs on no fuel anyone supplies anymore, or runs on a fuel the land itself provides — geothermal, hydraulic, something patient and slow drawn up from the young rock beneath. The dynamic here is glacial: a valve will shift position over the course of a season. Steam, if there is steam, rises in thin private threads from a few of the towers, dissipating into that bright sourceless light before it reaches head height. Otherwise the machines hold still, gauges fogged with condensation, dials reading numbers in no script you recognize, needles that haven't moved in a way perceptible to a human visit.

The silence is total in a way that feels engineered rather than empty. No birdsong — fauna is scarce to the point of rumor: a pale, long-legged something glimpsed once at the treeline and never confirmed, beetles in the moss too small to make a sound, no insects loud enough to fill the air. Even wind seems muffled, as if the dense canopy and the thin cold air conspire to swallow it before it carries. Your own footsteps in the spongy ground barely register. Occasionally — rarely — a structure exhales: a soft mechanical sigh, metal cooling or warming by some fractional degree, and the sound travels remarkably far across the open terraces precisely because there is nothing else to compete with it.

The strangeness accumulates slowly, the way the moss does. It's not any single wrong thing. It's that the towers are too evenly spaced to be accidental and too overgrown to be tended; that the pipes carry warmth from below that never fully explains the bright cold light above; that nothing here has rusted through in a way that matches how old the plants growing on it seem; that the ferns near the warmest joints have leaves shaped subtly differently from the ferns thirty meters off, as if responding to something other than light and water. There are no bodies of water large enough to reflect that huge flat sky, just damp leaf-litter and the held-breath quiet of pipes underfoot, and the terraces go on, ridge after ridge, into a bright haze-less distance where more towers stand, smaller with range, vanishing not into fog but into sheer optical limit — the air here too clean to blur anything, so the world simply ends at the edge of what the eye can resolve.

You could walk for a day and meet no one, hear no engine spin up, see no smoke — and still feel, the entire time, that the place is *working*, patiently, on a schedule that has nothing to do with you.
