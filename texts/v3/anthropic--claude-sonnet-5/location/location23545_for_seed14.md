The Trench of Standing Silt

Below the surface — far enough that the last grey ribbon of light gives out and the water turns the black of wet basalt — there is a basin so old that the sea has worn its own floor smooth. This was not always sea. The bones of the place are continental: a sunken valley, wide as a county, walled on both sides by long-eroded ridgelines that have been planed down by current after current until they hold no sharp edge anywhere. Three billion seconds of moving water, or near enough, have rubbed the rock to the texture of a worn doorsill. Nothing here breaks at an angle. Everything slumps, rounds, settles.

And yet it is not still. That is the first wrongness a visitor would feel, if a visitor could feel anything down here besides the cold pressing in through every seam of their suit. The terrain is ancient — flattened, finished, a landscape that has stopped becoming anything new — but the water over it is in constant violent motion. Cold currents fall through the basin like avalanches you can't see, only feel as a shudder against the ribs. Plumes of disturbed silt stand up off the floor in slow, leaning columns, caught in cross-currents that never resolve into a single direction. The whole basin seems to breathe wrong, in fits, the water thickening and thinning with suspended sediment that never finishes settling because something always stirs it again before it can rest. Ancient floor, restless water — the two halves of the world disagreeing about what century it is.

The packing is what disorients most. This is not a sparse deep — there is almost no open water here at all. Suspended matter chokes every cubic meter: silt, flocculent organic snow drifting down in endless grey flurries, mineral haze exhaled from cracks in the old rock, the fine comminuted dust of things that died long before anything alive now was born. Visibility is measured in arm-lengths. You do not see the basin so much as feel its density change against you, a held-breath thickness, a sense of being inside something rather than above it.

Vegetation is sparse to the point of rumor — pale stalked things, leafless, more mineral than plant, anchored in cracks where some faint warmth leaks up from below; they bend in the turbulence without resistance, like hair underwater, which is what they essentially are. Fauna rarer still: the occasional blind, segmented thing moving low along the silt with a patience that suggests it has nowhere to be for the next thousand years; once in a long while, a shape too large to look at directly passing through the murk overhead, gone before its outline finishes registering.

There is no light worth naming. What exists is the deep cold blue-black that isn't darkness exactly, more an absence so total it has its own texture. Sound likewise is mostly withheld — a heavy quiet, broken occasionally by low groans of moving silt, the click and tick of suspended grit against itself, a far-off boom that might be settling rock or might be nothing, the sea clearing its throat.

Nothing here is built. No frame, no wall, no tool left standing — the place predates the idea of leaving things behind. But it is not without hands. Rare, scattered, there are signs of primitive presence: a bundle of stripped stalks lashed crudely with fiber-cord, a worked stone with one deliberate edge, half-sunk in the silt and going under a little further with each passing current. Whoever or whatever comes here to work does so with the simplest tools imaginable, and leaves almost nothing, and the basin erases even that almost-nothing within a generation or two. The place has no memory for intention. It only has memory for erosion.

To stand at the centre of this basin — if "stand" means anything in water this thick, this cold, this blind — is to feel two timescales running through your body at once: the work of millions of years that flattened these ridgelines into silence, and the violent, ungovernable churn of right now, this current, this collapsing plume of silt, this water that refuses, even after all this time, to lie down and finish settling. The world has finished arguing about its shape. It has not finished moving.
