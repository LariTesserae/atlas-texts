# The Roost of Quiet Wings

The terrace cuts into the mountainside like something was peeled back rather than built — a single shelf of pale, sharp-edged stone no larger than a modest village green, ringed by a low wall of fitted rock that hasn't yet had time to soften. The masonry is recent. You can tell by the way the stones still bite at the hand, by the absence of the moss-rounding that comes with age. Whoever raised these walls did so within living memory, and they did it high — high enough that the air carries a thin coolness even at midday, high enough that clouds sometimes simply occupy the space where a roof might otherwise go, draping the place in damp grey wool for an hour before moving on.

There is little water here in any standing sense — no pond, no stream — but everything is faintly wet anyway. Condensation beads on the undersides of broad leaves. A fine mist exhales from cracks in the young rock each morning, gone by midmorning. Rain comes in brief, hard pulses, gone as fast as it arrives, leaving the sharp stone steps slick for a half hour and then dry again.

## Structure

The settlement, if it can be called that, is sparse: a scatter of perhaps a dozen structures across the terrace and its descending shelves, each one built from timber and unworked stone, lashed and stacked rather than mortared, roofed in layered bark and broad cut fronds. There is no center. Buildings sit at irregular distances, connected by packed-earth paths that bend around outcrops too new and too sharp to bother leveling. Between them: a startling amount of empty space, planted thick with growth, so that walking from one structure to the next means wading through chest-high fern and broad-leafed shrub still glossy with the morning damp.

The architecture is humble — hand tools, simple joinery, no metal beyond a few iron fittings on doors. But the *placement* is strange. Structures perch at the terrace's outer edge facing the drop, doorways oriented not toward each other but outward, toward open air. Some buildings have been raised on stilts for no reason the ground requires — the stone beneath them is solid. Tall poles stand at odd intervals across the open ground, bare of any banner or carving, their purpose unstated.

## What Lives Here

This is the part that overwhelms. The vegetation is lush, almost frantic in its density — broad ferns, low flowering shrub, vine threading the stone walls — but it is the fauna that floods every sense. Wings everywhere. Not loud wings; the place holds an eerie quiet despite the sheer mass of creatures occupying it. Birds in numbers that should be deafening move instead in near-silence, a susurrus of feather rather than song. They roost on the stilted houses, line the bare poles in rows, drift between terrace shelves in slow spirals.

It isn't only birds. Small mammals thread the fern-floor in numbers that suggest no predator culls them, or a predator too patient to be seen. Insects hang in the damp air in visible drifts, settling on leaf and shoulder alike without urgency. At dusk something larger moves along the terrace's outer wall — never fully seen, only felt as a displacement of air, a creak of wood that isn't wind.

The villagers — if they are villagers — seem to live alongside this density rather than against it. Doorframes are built oversized, perhaps to accommodate something that isn't human. Food stores sit uncovered and untouched. No nets, no traps, no fences. Whatever rule governs the relationship between the built and the wild here, it isn't predation in the way you'd expect, and it isn't quite domestication either.

## Light and Air

The light never commits. It's dim throughout the day, filtered through a permanent high haze that the altitude seems to generate rather than merely receive — not quite fog, not quite cloud, just a dimming of all edges. Shadows pool soft and directionless. At what should be dusk, the place doesn't darken so much as deepen, the same grey thickening until it becomes a kind of underwater night.

Sound carries strangely in this dimness — or rather, doesn't carry. The overwhelming animal population produces almost no noise: a population that should roar instead murmurs, rustles, occasionally clicks. The quiet is not empty, though. It has texture, the way a held breath has texture. Something about the place suggests the silence is a behavior, not an absence — that all this lush, swarming, multiplying life has collectively agreed not to announce itself.

## The Strangeness

Everything here is in process — the stone still sharp from its forming, the settlement clearly young, the ecology mid-explosion, populations clearly still finding their ceiling. Nothing has settled into its final shape. And yet the strangeness isn't only in the youth of the place. It's in the wrongness of proportion: too many creatures for too little visible food, too much silence for so much life, doorways too large, poles too bare, a settlement built facing outward into open sky as if waiting for something to arrive, or to be seen by something already circling above the haze.

Stand still long enough on the terrace at dusk and you'll feel watched not by one thing but by hundreds — every roosting bird, every burrowed mammal, every unseen mass shifting along the outer wall — all quiet, all attentive, all waiting on a signal that hasn't come yet, in a place still deciding what it's going to become.
