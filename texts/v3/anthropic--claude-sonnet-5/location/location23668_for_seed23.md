# The Wellings at Karst-Mourn

The light comes first — it always does here. A high-altitude brightness with no warmth in it, the sun thrown back off ten thousand wet surfaces until the air itself seems to glow from inside, like standing inside a struck bell of mica and rain. You squint even in shadow. There is no shadow that is truly dark; everything is rinsed, over-exposed, silvered.

This is a settlement built into and out of a stone amphitheater near the top of the world — not a peak exactly, but a held breath of elevation, a plateau-shelf where the land seems to have paused mid-thought. Bare rock dominates: pale grey-green stone, water-veined, sweating constantly from springs no one has ever located the source of. The vegetation is sparse but precise — black-stemmed sedges growing in clenched fists from cracks, a low lichen the color of old bruises crusting every horizontal surface, the occasional dwarf willow bent flat and silvered like everything else, holding on at the lip of a pool. The sparseness reads not as absence but as editing — as if something has been stripped away on purpose, leaving only what could survive the cold and the wet and the press of everything else.

And there is a lot of everything else. This is the strange part: the density is wrong for the apparent emptiness. Stone troughs, channels, weirs, and stair-cut terraces are crammed together at impossible proximity — houses built shoulder to shoulder into the rock-face, water-stairs descending three, four, five courses in the space you'd expect one, walkways crossing over walkways, a layered town stacked into a cold wet bowl of stone like a hive built by something that distrusted open space. The built environment is primitive in its means — no metal but for a few hammered iron fittings gone the color of dried blood, no wheels larger than a hand-cart, everything is hand-set stone, lashed timber, woven channel-liners of willow-bark — but urban, even imperial, in its ambition. Whoever raised this place did not lack for hands or time, only for ore and engines.

Water is the medium the whole settlement metabolizes. It rises through the rock as if the mountain were leaking from a wound that never closes — not rain, though there is some, but groundwater forced upward by pressures below, surfacing in hundreds of weeping faces, cold springs, basin-pools that brim and never overflow because the town has cut channels for every possible excess. The terracing isn't decorative. It's plumbing. Whole neighborhoods exist to move water from upper basins to lower ones, cistern to cistern, a hydraulic liturgy carved by hand into living stone over — how long? Long enough that every edge is rounded, every stair-tread is dished by feet and runoff both, every channel has the unhurried roundness of true age. This place is old. It has reached an erosional truce with itself: nothing here looks new, even the parts being rebuilt today.

Because something is always being rebuilt. This is the contradiction the place holds without apparent strain: ancient and settled in its forms, and yet constantly, violently in motion. Walls fall. Terraces slump and are immediately reset, stone by stone, by silent crews working with wet hands in the bright cold. A spring will close overnight and a new one erupt forty feet away, flooding three lower chambers, and by the time you've climbed down to look, people are already cutting a new channel, expressionless, soaked to the knee. The instability isn't decay — decay implies a single direction. This is more like boiling seen too slowly to register as boiling: the whole town as a held shape in a process that never resolves, creation and collapse running concurrently in every quarter at once, so that an observer staying a week would find half the place rearranged and would struggle to say whether it had been an improvement.

And it is quiet. This is the part visitors find hardest to credit, given everything moving — but the silence is real and total in texture, broken only by water-sounds: the percussive drip and trickle and the deep glassy gulp of a basin filling, the occasional grind of a dragged stone, never voices raised, rarely voices at all. The people — if they are people, and most travelers' accounts insist they are, just spare and economical with themselves — work without calling to one another. Instruction seems to pass by glance, by the position of a hand, by some shared and wordless choreography learned so early it no longer needs saying aloud. The quiet is not empty; it has the charged, attentive quality of a held breath in a large cold room.

What is not quiet — or rather, what fills the silence without breaking it — is the fauna. Improbably, gorgeously abundant for so sparse and cold a place: pale amphibious things that live half in the basins, surfacing to blink with two sets of lids; flocks of small white-grey birds that move in such dense, fast formations over the water-stairs that they read more like a single distributed organism than a multiplicity; eyeless fish, fat and unhurried, visible through the clearest pools, fed by hands as they pass. Insects too — clouds of them in the brightest hours, glittering, soundless, a kind of living dust that seems to thicken the very air into something you could almost cup. The ecology has filled every available niche with a thoroughness that seems to mock the bareness of the rock. The animals do not flee the inhabitants. They circulate among the stonework as if they too were employees of the place, with duties.

The strangeness sits exactly here, in this productive disproportion: a cold, bright, nearly silent, sparsely vegetated highland — the kind of place that by every reasonable expectation should feel austere, minimal, depopulated — instead crammed dense with structure, thick with creatures, perpetually rebuilding itself, ancient and unsettled at once, loud with implication while making almost no sound. Walking its stair-stacked terraces in the shadowless glare, you get the sense of a machine so old it has become a landscape, and so alive that "landscape" is already the wrong word — a held, breathing, water-fed instability, dressed in the settled patience of stone.

No one you can find will tell you who built the first basin, or why it was built up instead of out, into this dense vertical hive rather than spread across the gentler ground below. The most anyone offers, in the unhurried economy of the place, is a gesture at the springs — at the water finding its own way up out of the cold rock, unaskable, unrefusable — as if that were the whole and sufficient answer, and the town merely its long argument with the wound that feeds it.
