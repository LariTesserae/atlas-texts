# The Cistern of Aem-Vohl

There is a place high in the drowned range, where a mountain was hollowed out by hands instead of rivers, and the hollow filled with water that has not moved in a very long time.

You reach it by climbing through cold air for hours, no leaves anywhere, only switchbacks of fitted stone laid by people whose names didn't survive them, and then you crest a lip of worked rock and the path simply stops at black water. This is the cistern. It is not large — you could walk its rim in twenty minutes if the rim were walkable, which mostly it isn't — but it is *deep*, and what fills it presses against you the way only old water does, with weight rather than current.

## What Was Built

Everything here is made. The walls are coursed stone, fitted without mortar in a style so old the joints have fused — you cannot get a knife blade between them anymore, not because the masons were perfect but because two thousand years of mineral seep welded the seams shut. Stairs descend into the water on all sides, symmetrical, leading to a floor no one living has seen. Pillars rise from below the surface, just barely — the tops of seven or nine of them (the count is disputed, because they're never all visible at once) break the water at odd intervals, capped with carved basins now blind with sediment, like the architecture is still reaching for air it gave up needing.

The builders worked in a warm climate with simple tools — no metal scarring on the stone, only the patient bruise-marks of hammerstones and the long scrape of abrasive cord-and-sand sawing. Whoever they were, they had centuries, and they used every one. Nothing here was built quickly. The terracing alone, the courses that step down into the dark, suggest a labor measured in generations, the kind of project a culture starts knowing it will not finish, and finishes anyway because someone's grandchildren want to.

There is no soil. There is no plant. The stone goes straight to water with nothing softening the transition — no moss, no algae-green, nothing growing at the waterline. The water itself, against all expectation, sustains nothing photosynthetic; it is too deep, too dark, too old. Whatever lives here eats something other than light.

## What Lives Here

And yet it is full of bodies. This is the strangeness of the place — total construction, total absence of vegetation, and yet *abundant fauna*, dense with presences that have no business surviving on bare mineral. Pale crustacean things cling to the submerged steps in colonies thick enough to look like a second masonry, a fur of shell. Eyeless fish hang motionless in the basin-pillars' shadow, packed close, barely stirring, feeding on something that drifts down from above or seeps up from below — mineral-eaters, perhaps, or eaters of each other's molts and waste, a whole economy of nothing built on nothing, sustained the way deep vents sustain their tube-worms.

Things surface rarely. A ripple with no clear source. A long pale shape rolling once near a pillar-top and gone. The water is warm — not hot, but blood-near, holding heat the way old systems do, some slow chemistry or some sealed-in remnant of the mountain's interior warmth still leaking into it after all this time, keeping the cistern liquid and inhabited while the air above stays cold.

## Light and Sound

Light arrives reluctantly. What gets in comes down through the original sky-cuts the builders left — narrow chimneys angled to catch sun for an hour or two at certain times of year, and otherwise the place is dark, a wet dark that swallows torchlight a few feet from the flame and gives back only the wet gleam of fitted stone. You see the nearest stair. You see your own light doubled in the black surface. That is usually all.

It is quiet in the way deep enclosed water is always quiet — your own breathing, the small slap of water against the steps when something below shifts and sends a ring outward, the occasional click or knock of crustacean claw on stone, far off, like something keeping a slow, patient time signature for no one. No birds, because there's no surface ecology to draw them. No wind, because the chamber is sealed against it. The loudest sound most visitors ever hear is their own pulse.

## The Slowness

Nothing changes here quickly, and the place seems to insist nothing should. The waterline has not moved within memory. The pillars do not erode further — they reached their final, smoothed, ancient shape so long ago that they read now as fact rather than as *process*. Even the pale things in the water seem to live on extended time, moving as if they have agreed among themselves there is no reason to hurry.

There's a belief among the people who still climb to it — not to use it, since no one knows its original purpose, only to visit it — that the cistern is the mountain's *finished* state, the thing the mountain was always going to become once erosion and ambition and water had all had their say, and that disturbing it, swimming in it, taking anything from it, sets the slow clock back to zero. So they don't disturb it. They sit on the dry steps above the waterline, where their grandparents sat, and they look down into black water that holds something fed and old and patient, and they leave before the light through the ceiling-cuts fails entirely, and they tell their children it is rude to throw stones into water that has finally stopped wanting to be anything else.
