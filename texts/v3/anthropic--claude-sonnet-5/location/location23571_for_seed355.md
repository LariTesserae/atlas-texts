# The Sunk Town

There is a town here, low and wide across a basin that holds heat like a held breath, and the town is full of water that should not still be standing in this temperature, and there is no light by which to properly see it, and yet you see it anyway — not by sun, not by moon, not by lamp, but by some ambient permission the place grants the eye, a visibility that arrives without a source.

The terrain is flat to a fault, a shallow bowl of cracked pale ground that has settled into its shape across what feels like centuries — the erosion here is not violent, not recent; it is the erosion of long agreement, of a place that argued with wind and water a long time ago and has since gone quiet about it. Streets run between buildings of mud-brick and pale stone, structures two and three storeys tall, close-walled, flat-roofed, the kind of architecture that knows the sun is an enemy most of the year. Except there's no sun now. There may never be sun again, here. The town doesn't seem troubled by this.

Water sits everywhere and explains nothing. It pools in the lee of walls. It stands ankle-deep in doorways without flowing in or out. It sheets thinly across the cracked clay flats between buildings, catching the sourceless light in long silver tongues. The air itself is heavy and wet, condensing on every surface — door-beams weep, stone sills bead and drip, the undersides of roof-eaves grow dark damp beards though no rain has fallen and no rain is falling. The heat is real, thick, equatorial, but it never dries anything; the moisture and the warmth have struck some unnatural truce, coexisting where they should cancel each other out.

The vegetation, what little there is, grows wrong for the rules it's been given. A few isolated stalks — leafless, the colour of old bone — stand rigid in the standing water at long intervals, more punctuation than plant. They do not bend in any wind, because there is no wind. They do not seem to be reaching for anything. They are simply present, the way a comma is present in a sentence that doesn't need one.

The built scale is the strange center of this place: this is no ruin, not abandoned-looking, not collapsed — it is a town, organized, planned, streets meeting at clean intersections, walls intact, roof-lines unbroken, doors set evenly into walls at human-scaled intervals. Infrastructure exists: stepped wells, paved courses, what might be irrigation channels though there is nothing growing for them to feed. It has the unmistakable shape of a place built for life. And no life is in it. No animal sound, no insect hum, no skitter in the alleys. The silence is not the silence of emptiness exactly — it's the silence of a stage after the actors have gone, lights still up, water glasses still full, chairs still pushed back at the angle of someone who just stood.

Sound, where it exists, is minimal and strange in its specificity: the drip of condensation off an eave, landing in a puddle with a single clean note; somewhere distant, a long low creak like a door or a hull settling, though no door is seen to move; the faint suck and release of water shifting beneath the cracked ground, as though the whole basin breathes, slowly, through its skin.

This is where the place stops behaving. Walk one street and count nineteen doorways; walk back the way you came and count twenty-two, the same street, the same doors, but more of them, and you cannot say which new ones appeared or where. A standing pool throws back no reflection of the building beside it — instead it shows the same building from across town, the one with the blue-black water-stain shaped like a hand. Walk toward any structure that looks notably taller than its neighbors and the streets will curve you gently away from it, without a single turn that feels like a turn, until you are facing the opposite direction and the tall structure is now, somehow, behind you and closer.

The wet rooftops sometimes show, in their sheeting condensation, the suggestion of words — not letters from any alphabet you'd swear to, but clearly text-shaped, clearly meant, present for the four or five seconds it takes to notice them and gone by the time you look again, the stone dry as if nothing had been written.

The town is dynamic in a way that has nothing to do with weather or growth. It rearranges itself at the scale of attention rather than the scale of time. Things are not decaying and not being built; they are being *adjusted*, continuously, just outside the frame of direct observation, by no hand, toward no apparent design — except that the design, cumulatively, seems to be a town that remains a town no matter how it's reconfigured, the way a held note remains the note even as the bow keeps moving.

There is a rule here, evidently, though it was never posted: the town will always have just been lived in. Every room holds water in a basin as though set out an hour ago. Every step has a faint damp print upon it, single, unaccompanied by a second print, leading nowhere, originating nowhere. The whole place has the architecture of recent departure and the geology of having stood since before departure was a concept, and it holds both of these at once without strain, the way the water holds the heat without surrendering to it, the way the dark holds enough light to see by without admitting where the light comes from.

If you stay long enough — and the place does not discourage this, makes no move against you, offers no danger you can name — you start to suspect that the town is not waiting for anyone to return, and is not the site of some catastrophe to be deciphered, and is not a riddle with a key. It is simply a place where the ordinary agreement between cause and consequence was quietly suspended, the way a held breath is not refusing to exhale, only delaying it, indefinitely, in a room full of water that has nowhere left to go and isn't going anywhere regardless.
