The Wickwork

You enter it before you understand you've entered it — the ground underfoot already lattice, already joint and lashing, long before any wall declares itself. This is the first wrongness: there is no outside to this place that you can locate. The built has eaten the substrate entirely. Somewhere below, impossibly far below, there may be stone or soil that this was built upon, but no one living has touched it, and the structure does not seem to need it.

What stands here was raised by hand. Not by machine, not by any process larger than a person with a blade and a length of fiber and a great deal of time — and a great many such persons, working over a great many years, in the dark, near each other, without ever quite finishing. The material is wood-that-is-not-wood: dense fibrous strut-stuff, grey-brown, slightly damp to the touch, jointed and lashed and pegged into a structure that has no single plan. It reads like a settlement that grew the way coral grows — each generation building onto, around, and sometimes through what the last generation left, until the whole thing achieved a density that no individual intended. Walkways stack on walkways. Rooms nest inside larger rooms like cysts. Ladders of knotted cord disappear upward into joists so thick with crossbeam that you cannot see more than thirty feet in any direction before the architecture simply occludes the view with more of itself.

It is low here — you rarely climb or descend more than a few storeys at a time before the terrain levels back out into long horizontal sprawl, valley-sized, town-sized, a vast shallow tangle rather than a tower. The scale reveals itself slowly: corridors give onto plazas of crossed beams, which give onto more corridors, which open suddenly onto wide flat decks where the structure thins enough that you can feel — though not see — that there is a great deal more of this extending in every direction, regional in its sprawl, unmeasured.

The air is cool and faintly damp — not wet, never wet, but every surface holds a film of moisture, condensation beading on the underside of beams, occasional drips finding their way down through forty layers of joinery to patter on a deck far below. There is no rain here that anyone has seen, no river, no standing pool — but water moves through the structure anyway, sweating out of the material itself or seeping from some source no one has mapped, finding the lowest joints, darkening the grain, occasionally pooling ankle-deep in a low room before draining away through some gap days later. The dampness is structural, ambient, permanent, and never enough to rot the wood-that-is-not-wood faster than new wood-that-is-not-wood gets lashed atop it.

There is no vegetation, not even moss, not even the patient lichen-things that might be expected to colonize such damp dark material. Whatever this stuff is, nothing grows on it. This absence is itself unsettling — a built world this old, this saturated with moisture and shadow, should be furred with life, and it is bare, swept-looking, almost groomed, though no one seems to be doing the sweeping.

Darkness is the medium you move through more than air. Light exists in small committed doses — tallow lamps, resin-soaked bundles, the occasional shaft of something paler falling from a gap in the upper structure that might be sky, might be another layer of construction lit from elsewhere. You navigate as much by sound and touch as sight, learning the grain of handholds, learning which creaks mean weight-bearing and which mean rot.

And the sound — the sound is the place's pulse. It is loud here, always, a compound loudness made of many small loudnesses: the structure itself talking, creaking, settling, joints complaining under shifting weight; water finding its way down through forty levels in a percussion that never quite repeats; and voices — distant hammering, the rhythmic chock of blade against fiber-strut as someone somewhere is always building, always repairing, always extending the wickwork further into its own darkness. Tools are simple — stone-edged, bone-handled, the occasional worked sliver of true metal passed down like a relic and resharpened past recognition. The work never stops, which means the sound never stops. You sleep, if you sleep, inside it.

Fauna is rare but present — small pale things that move along the joints, neither quite insect nor quite mammal, seen mostly as a flicker at the edge of a lamp's reach, gone before identified. No one has cataloged them. There may be predators among them; there may be nothing larger than a hand. Their rarity makes every sighting an event, passed along corridors as gossip.

The whole place feels emerging rather than aged — raw joinery still pale at its newest edges, sections that are clearly unfinished, scaffolding-within-scaffolding that has been scaffolding so long it has become permanent. Nothing here looks done. Erosion is barely beginning; the moisture darkens but does not yet truly decay. You get the sense that the Wickwork is not a ruin and not a monument but a thing perpetually under construction, has always been under construction, and that the builders themselves no longer remember — if they ever knew — what final shape, if any, this was meant to take. New rooms get lashed onto old ones to solve problems (overcrowding, a leak, a collapse) rather than to fulfill any plan. The structure's logic is improvisational, accretive, almost biological in its growth pattern despite being entirely, totally, unrelentingly made.

The strangeness sits less in any single feature than in the accumulation: a fully built world with no machines, no metal to speak of, no plants, lit by hand-fire, damp without rain, dense without crowding (you rarely see more than a few people at once, despite the constant evidence — sound, recent tool-marks, warm lamp-oil — of many, many people very near you), old without erosion, finished nowhere. It feels like the inside of something. Like a nest built by builders who have forgotten they are also, themselves, the nested.

The rule, if there is one, seems to be: build toward the dark, lash what you find to what is already there, and do not stop, because stopping is the only form of collapse anyone here truly fears.
