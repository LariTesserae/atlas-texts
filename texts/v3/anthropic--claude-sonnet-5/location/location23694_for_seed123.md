# The Cistern Garden

It is not large. That is the first thing the body understands, stepping down through the broken culvert mouth into it — a single hollow, maybe forty paces across, walled by raw concrete and a collar of cracked basalt that still shows tool-marks from whatever cut it free of the larger world. The ceiling is sky, but a narrow ribbon of it, cut by the lip of the surrounding trench. Light comes down normal and clean, midday-colored, but it arrives like something filtered through a slot, and so the whole bowl reads as a room with the roof removed rather than as open land.

Everything in it is close.

## Ground and Material

The floor is young rock — not soil, not yet. Slabs of poured aggregate, fresh-fractured along hairline seams, sit beside genuine bedrock that still carries the chisel-bruises of its emergence, as though the hollow were pried open within living memory rather than worn down over ages. There is none of the rounded patience of old erosion here; every edge is a true edge, every shadow has a hard line at its root. Dampness gathers in the seams — not standing water, but a perpetual sweat, condensation beading on the undersides of pipe-stubs and rebar nubs that jut from the walls like the stumps of some dismantled architecture. Where two slabs meet, moisture pools to a coin-sized darkness and stays there, never enough to run, never gone.

Scattered through this: machinery. Not ruins, exactly — too clean, too recently silent. A turbine housing, waist-high, sheared at the collar, its blades visible inside like teeth in a closed jaw. Sections of conduit. A control box, glass face starred but intact, one small indicator light still pulsing amber on some current nobody can trace to a source. The built things are sparse but insistent — single objects rather than a structure, each one complete in itself, none of them adding up to a building. Whatever produced them is elsewhere, or gone, or was never more than this.

## The Crowding

And then the rest of the space — the parts not taken by rock or machine — is taken by life, completely, with no interval left over. This is the place's true character: it is full. Sparse vegetation, yes, by any wide-angle accounting — a few hardy fans of grey-green succulent wedged into the seams, some bracket-fungus shelving off the turbine housing, a single contorted shrub rising out of a crack with the dense, gnarled patience of something that has decided this is enough room and will not ask for more. But where there is anything at all, there is abundance stacked on abundance. The succulent leaves host clusters of pale snails, three and four deep. The fungus shelves are lined, gill to gill, with motionless beetles the color of wet rust. The shrub's few branches sag under nests — not birds, something smaller and many-bodied, woven structures of grey fiber packed tier on tier like apartment floors.

It is as if the hollow contains exactly as much life as a place ten times its size, compressed down by some pressure into this one warm bowl, and nature has obliged by interlocking every available surface rather than spreading thin.

## Ecology and Motion

Despite the crowding, despite the sheer occupied density of it, the place is mostly quiet. The sounds are small and specific: a continuous dry tick from the beetle-shelves, like a clock with too many escapements. The occasional wet click of a snail releasing its grip and resettling. A low electrical hum from the control box, so steady it becomes a kind of silence itself. Underneath, irregular: a tapping from somewhere in the conduit, water finding its slow way through a buried channel, arriving nowhere anyone can see.

It is active, though — everything here is mid-process, never resting in the way old places rest. The beetles rotate through the fungus shelves in a slow circulation, vacating one section as another fills. The snails migrate, almost imperceptibly, following the moisture line as it shifts a few inches with the day's heat. Something unseen — fast, low, glimpsed only as a disturbance among the succulent leaves — moves through every so often and the snails near it pull tight to their shells for an hour after. The amber light on the control box pulses on a cycle nobody set and nobody has come to silence.

## The Strangeness

What unsettles, if anything does, is the proportion of things. The beetles are too uniform, ranked in the fungus like components in a tray. The nests in the shrub show a geometry too regular for what ought to be improvised — hexagonal mouths, identical to the diameter of a fingertip, in tiers exactly as far apart as a hand's width. The indicator light keeps a rhythm that, listened to long enough, resolves into something almost like counting. None of this announces itself as wrong. It simply accumulates, fact by small fact, until the visitor realizes that this overfull, warm, dripping hollow behaves less like a leftover fragment of wilderness and more like a single sustained process — a vessel something is still quietly using, tended by no one, for a purpose that finished, or never started, depending on how you read the amber light.

## Rule of the Place

The cistern garden obeys one law above all: nothing here is alone. Every surface that can hold a second thing holds it, every niche has its occupant and the occupant's lodger and the lodger's parasite, in a packed, overlapping economy with no spare margin anywhere. The dampness is just enough to keep this density alive and never enough to let it spread beyond the bowl's lip. Step up out of the culvert mouth, back into the wide dry trench above, and within a few strides the abundance falls away to ordinary sparseness, as if the hollow were the one place that collected everything the rest of the land could not hold, and sealed it, gently, just below the surface of the visible world.
