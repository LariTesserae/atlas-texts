The Cistern Above the World

You reach it by going up, which is the first wrongness, because the air should thin and instead it thickens, and the path — there is no path, only a sense of having arrived — opens onto a shelf of dark, young stone, sharp at every edge as if the mountain had been poured and set yesterday. Cracks in it still look wet at the lip, the rock equivalent of a wound that hasn't decided whether to close. This is high ground. You can tell by the way the clouds sit *below* the tree line in places, snagged like wool on the upper branches of something that should not, at this elevation, be a tree at all — and yet here they are: a sparse stand of them, maybe nine, maybe eleven, broad-leaved, glossy-wet, spaced with the deliberate emptiness of a room arranged by someone who values the gaps between objects as much as the objects.

The ground breathes moisture without being a marsh. Water doesn't pool here so much as it *hovers a decision away from pooling* — condensation beads on every leaf-underside, runs along stone seams, and never quite reaches the floor before it's gone again. The temperature sits at the cool edge of comfortable, the kind of cool that makes your own breath visible only sometimes, inconsistently, as though the air's willingness to show you anything is a mood rather than a law.

And then the sound arrives, because this is the place's second wrongness: a low, continuous industrial drone, the bass register of engines, threaded with the higher intermittent clatter of mechanism — belts, gears, something like a distant stamping press — and there is no factory. There is no built thing anywhere in sight. No wall, no foundation, no rust, no pipe. The noise simply comes from the rock, or from the space between the trees, or from underneath the thin moss in the cracks, as though the mountain itself were the engine, idling, and had simply never grown the skin of a building around its working parts. You stand in a sparse, lush, cathedral-quiet grove and the bones of the place hum like a foundry at full shift.

The fauna does not seem bothered by this. In fact, the fauna is the loudest sane thing here — abundant in a way that contradicts the emptiness of the scene, as though density of *life* and density of *matter* were unrelated metrics in this place's accounting. Something with too many legs moves along a branch overhead, unhurried, glistening. Things that might be birds, if birds were translucent at the wingtip, circle the gap in the canopy where the dim light comes through — light the color of late afternoon that never advances toward evening, never retreats toward morning, simply *is*, perpetually, the held note of a single hour. Calls echo between the trees: clicks, a long warble that sounds mechanical until it resolves, halfway through, into something with breath in it. The animals do not flee you. They do not approach you either. They behave as though you are weather — present, irrelevant, ongoing.

This is the heart of the third wrongness, the one that makes the first two feel almost like decoration: nothing here is changing, has changed, or will change. The water that should be dripping into pools has been *about to drip* for what feels like a very long held instant. The light that should shift with cloud-cover does not shift. The animal call that began three of your breaths ago is still, somehow, the same call, not looped, not repeating — simply uninterrupted, as if time agreed to bend around this shelf of stone rather than pass through it. The drone of the unbuilt machinery does not rise or fall. You could stay an hour or a year and the bead of water on that leaf would still be a bead of water on that leaf, still deciding.

The rules, such as they can be inferred: this is a place where industry exists as a property of stone rather than a product of hands — the mountain manufactures *something*, ceaselessly, by means that have no visible gears, no visible heat, no exhaust, no waste, and whatever it manufactures is also never finished, because nothing here finishes. The lushness is real but unconsumed — leaves that should yellow do not, animals that should hunt seem to graze on standing air, mist that should fall stays suspended a half-inch above the ferns like a decision deferred. Erosion has barely begun, as though the place arrived complete a moment ago and has simply continued being that moment since.

You notice, eventually, that your own pulse is the only thing keeping time here, and that the place seems to be waiting — not for you to leave, not for you to stay, but for some third option it hasn't offered you the name of yet. The drone continues underfoot. The wet leaves continue glistening. Somewhere above the canopy a wing beats once and does not, as far as you can tell, ever complete the downstroke.
