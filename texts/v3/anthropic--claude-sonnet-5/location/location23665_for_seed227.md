The hollow takes you before you see it. You step off a moss-felted shelf of stone into a fold of low ground, and the world narrows—walls of rock and root closing in overhead until what's left is a sort of room, no bigger than a few generous strides across, roofed by a tangle of bent alder and old yew whose canopies have grown into each other so thoroughly that daylight only arrives as suggestion. It's cold here in the way that stone is cold, not the cold of wind but the cold of long shade, a temperature that has not changed in living memory and won't.

The first thing, before your eyes adjust to anything, is the sound. It arrives whole, already enormous, and it takes a while to understand it isn't one sound but the compression of many: water somewhere above the hollow's lip falling in a thread no wider than a wrist, but falling into a basin of rock that has spent millennia learning exactly how to amplify it, throwing the noise against the close walls until it returns on itself, layered, doubled, deafening in a space that holds almost nothing else. There is no shouting over it. There is no purpose in trying.

The dampness is sparing but constant — not flood, not soak, just enough: condensation beading on the undersides of leaves, a slow weep of moisture along certain veins in the rock that have been doing this so long they've grown a fur of pale moss and liverwort, fed by almost nothing and needing almost nothing. Pockets of standing water sit black and still in stone hollows worn smooth, untouched by the falling water's noise, holding it instead, the way a cupped hand holds wind.

Vegetation crowds every surface that isn't actively wet rock — fern under fern, the alder roots wearing aprons of moss, fungus in pale shelves at the joints of old wood, ivy threading the gaps between stones that were once, perhaps, stacked on purpose. The growth is old growth, slow growth, nothing rushing toward light because there is so little light to rush toward; the lushness here is patient, accumulated rather than vigorous.

And there, beneath the ivy, the marks of someone: a ring of flat stones set into the damp soil, half-sunk, edges rounded by the same erosion that rounds everything else, clearly placed and clearly very old — a hearth, maybe, or a boundary, or both. A single worked flint lies wedged into a root crease nearby, its knapped edge dulled by handling and weather both, a tool abandoned or left on purpose, nobody left to say which. No other structure remains. Whatever else stood here returned to the hollow's slow chemistry long ago, dissolved back into moss and silt the way everything here eventually is.

Almost nothing moves. The emptiness is not the emptiness of death but of scale — a hollow this small, this saturated in noise, doesn't suit much life. Once in a long while a wren threads through the alder roots, astonishingly unbothered by the roar, vanishing into a crevice; rarer still, the quick retreat of something larger glimpsed only as a shifting of fern. Mostly the place keeps itself, its only inhabitants the water, the stone, and the moss that has had centuries to decide exactly how it wants to grow.

Nothing here is sudden. The dripping vein in the rock will still be dripping in a hundred years, depositing its mineral patience one grain at a time. The fall will go on overwhelming the small room with its single, endless syllable of sound. The flint will weather another fraction smoother. To stand in the hollow is to feel time pass at the speed of stone — and to be, for as long as you stay, deaf to everything in the world except this one close, dark, roaring room that someone, once, thought worth pausing in.
