# The Niche at Cold Altitude

You find it by accident, or by something that wants to look like accident. A fold in a high ridge, no wider than a small room, no deeper than a held breath — a pocket of世界 tucked into the body of a mountain that has otherwise forgotten what mountains are for.

The walls are stone, technically. Grey-black, fine-grained, the kind of rock that has been exactly this shape for a very long time and intends to remain so. Frost rimes the upper edges in a way that never quite melts and never quite thickens — held, as if temperature here is a suggestion rather than a law. The air is thin and dry and absolutely still. No water has ever been here. There is no need for water.

At the center of the niche, occupying perhaps a third of its floor, sits the thing that makes this place a place rather than just a gap in rock.

It has no good name. It is roughly the size of a folded coat, matte black-grey, faceted in a way that doesn't quite resolve when you look directly at it — eleven sides, then thirteen, then a number your eye declines to finish counting. It does not appear to be a machine. It does not appear to be a stone. It rests on three contact points so thin they should not bear its weight, and where each point touches the ground there is a hairline groove worn into the rock — worn smooth, worn deep, worn over a span of time that does not match the object's apparent stillness.

It is doing something. You can't say what. There's no sound — none of the hum or tick you'd expect from a device, none of the wind-sound you'd expect from a mountain gap this high. Just a quality of quiet that is *attentive*, the silence of a held position rather than an absence of noise. Once, distantly, you think you hear a single low tone, gone before you can confirm you heard it at all — the way a sound exists if you weren't quite listening for it.

Occasionally — every few minutes, every few hours, it's hard to keep track of time here — one facet of the object catches what little light exists and throws it somewhere it shouldn't be able to reach: onto the underside of an overhang, into a crevice with no line of sight to the object at all. The light arrives anyway. It sits there a moment, pools faintly, and goes out.

There is no plant here, no animal, no insect, no lichen even, which is its own kind of strange at this elevation — usually something colonizes the cracks. Nothing has. It isn't that life failed to reach this niche. It's that the niche seems to have declined it, the way a held note declines a second note from joining it.

Scattered near the walls — not arranged, just present, the way debris settles — are a handful of small fragments. Pieces of something. A curved shard the size of a fingernail, faintly warm to the touch despite the cold. A flattened ring of material that isn't quite metal, half-sunk into a thin skin of frost. These don't look related to the central object and also don't look unrelated to it; they have the air of things that arrived before it, or because of it, or as a kind of sediment it sheds on a schedule too slow to witness.

The light in the niche is dim regardless of the hour outside — as though the opening to the sky is smaller than it looks, or as though the niche has its own dimmer sun. Shadows here don't fall the direction you'd expect from the gap above. They fall toward the object, faintly, all the time, from every angle at once, like the whole pocket of space leans a few degrees toward it without the rock itself tilting.

Nothing changes quickly. Come back in a year and the grooves under the three contact points will be a hair deeper. The frost line will have crept or retreated by some fraction no instrument you own would trust itself to measure. The object will be in the same place, doing the same unnamed thing, patient as the mountain that houses it, indifferent to whether anyone ever finds this fold in the rock again.

The rule of this place, if it has one, seems to be: *something is being maintained here, slowly, by something that does not need witnesses, in a shape too small for the universe to notice and too strange for it to fully ignore.*

You leave the way you came. The gap closes behind you no more than gaps usually do — and yet you find, descending, that you can't quite reconstruct which fold of the ridge it was, only that it is still up there, keeping its quiet, at its own enormous, unhurried scale of time.
