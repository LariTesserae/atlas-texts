# The Canebrake Works

There is no water here, and has not been for longer than the structures remember, yet everything is green-black and swollen with sap, as though the plateau drinks from some vein beneath the world that water itself abandoned. The land sits high, a tableland scoured flat by ages that ground down whatever mountains used to argue with the sky here. Now there is no argument. The horizon stays level for miles in every direction, broken only by the canopy-architecture — and that is the whole story of this place: that vegetation and construction stopped being separate categories so long ago that the distinction is now a kind of error.

Picture iron lattice-towers, riveted and black with age, but every strut wrapped thigh-thick in vine that has fused itself into the metal's tensile work, so that you cannot say anymore whether the towers hold up the plants or the plants hold up the towers. Picture brick chimneys, hundreds of them, still venting a slow heat though no one stokes them, grown through at odd angles by cane-stalks as wide as bridge pylons, woody and ringed like trees but hollow, and humming faintly with some internal industry of their own. The whole tableland is built — every surface engineered once, scaffolded, riveted, gear-driven — and every surface is also entirely consumed by growth, packed shoulder to shoulder, leaf to leaf, frond to flange, with no gap of bare ground or open air anywhere at eye level. You walk through corridors of stalk and strut both, dense as a jungle, dense as a foundry floor stacked with machine parts, because it is both at once, layered into the same square meter of space.

It is hot — the heat of old engines that never fully cooled, the heat of greenhouse air trapped under a million leaves, the heat of fermentation happening continuously in sealed vegetable matter somewhere below the catwalks. There's no wind to carry it off. The temperature just sits, thick, settled, the way the whole place is settled: ancient, worn into its final shape. This is not a landscape becoming something. This is a landscape that became, completely, eons ago, and has been quietly repeating its own ending ever since.

It is dark. Not lightless — there's a strange phosphor-glow in the deeper cane-thickets, bioluminescence or some leftover electrical filament still threading current through copper veins braided into root systems, casting a sick green-amber under-light upward onto the canopy-ceiling, so that you see the undersides of fifty layers of leaf and gear lit like a cathedral nobody finished. But no sun reaches here, no sky is visible, and your eyes never adjust past a kind of permanent dusk.

No animals. No birdcall, no insect-static, no rustle of anything moving with intention. The silence is not total — there is the mostly-quiet hum of distant turbine-things grinding somewhere under the root-mass, a slow tick of cooling metal, an occasional wet creak as some stalk shifts its enormous weight against its iron trellis — but it is a quiet empty of company. The ecology here is plant only, total and totalizing: vines that have learned to conduct electricity, fungal mats that phosphoresce along old wiring conduits, cane-forests that have grown gear-teeth into their bark from centuries of rubbing against flywheels, flowers the size of factory doors that bloom and rot and rebloom in cycles measured in hours, not seasons.

And that is the turbulence, the strangeness: nothing here is calm despite how ancient and settled the bones of the place are. Things are constantly collapsing and constantly regrowing. A grain-silo of fused bramble might groan and topple in the night, crushing some sub-corridor of pipe-vine, and by the time you pass again the wreckage will already be reclaimed, knit over, sprouting fresh cane through the broken brick within days. Steam vents open suddenly where a root has finally split an old boiler-seam, hissing into the dark, scalding nothing because nothing is there to scald. Whole sections seem to *breathe* — vegetative bellows-systems, ancient and self-operating, expanding walls of frond-and-bracket that compress old gas-pockets and release them in slow exhalations that move the air without ever cooling it.

There are rules, half-understood: the deeper galleries are warmer and older, pure cane grown so dense it reads as solid wall, threaded still with wire and rail from a transit system whose trains no longer run but whose rails the vines have incorporated like rebar, still conducting whatever current the place generates from its own decomposition. The upper levels are where machinery still nominally "shows" — gear-faces, valve-wheels, conveyor-skeletons — visible between fronds, all of it half-operating, turning slowly under vegetable torque, threshing nothing, producing nothing, an industry now run purely for the sake of its own continuation, a closed loop of growth feeding mechanism feeding growth.

No one comes here to mine it or harvest it. No one could move fast enough through the density, and there would be nothing to take to anywhere else, because the place doesn't believe in elsewhere. It is total, self-sufficient, finished and unfinished simultaneously: the oldest stable form of a system that never decided whether it was a forest or a factory, and so became, with magnificent patience, both — packed, hot, dark, endlessly working in the quiet, endlessly falling and regrowing, world without water, world without animal, world that built itself a body out of iron and cane and never stopped.
