# The High Plateau of the Standing Mosses

The road, such as it is, ends at a cairn of unmortared basalt before the ground begins to rise in long, stepped terraces — not staircases cut by hands but shelves of raw rock still settling into itself, edges sharp, faces unweathered, the whole formation looking as though it surfaced last season rather than last epoch. This is high ground, two and a half thousand meters by the feel of the thinning air and the way light comes without filter, but it is not bare. Nothing here is bare.

## Substrate

The rock is a pretext. What you actually walk on is moss — not a carpet but a *terrain*, meters deep in the low folds, building itself up over the young basalt in slow accumulating shelves of green-black, green-gold, a green so saturated in places it reads as wet shadow. Sphagnum heads swollen with held water. Cushion mosses domed like the backs of buried animals. Liverworts lacquering every vertical seep. Where the rock breaks open — and it breaks often, this is young stone, still cracking along its own cooling joints — the moss has already colonized the wound, packing the fissure, holding it together as much as anything geological does. There is essentially no soil here independent of the plant mass; the moss *is* the soil, layered over uncertain rock, and you sink to the ankle, sometimes the knee, into a mattress of living tissue that has never known a dry season.

Water moves through this mass constantly and visibly. It doesn't pool so much as *seep* — every slope weeps from a hundred unseen points, threading down through the moss in films you only notice when your boot displaces one and a small bright vein of water reroutes itself with a sound like a swallowed word. Where the seepage concentrates, it cuts through to the rock and runs as actual rivulets, dozens of them, threading the terraces, falling shelf to shelf in modest, repeating, see-through curtains no higher than a person, each one feeding a shallow basin lined in a startling chartreuse algae before spilling onward. The air itself is heavy with the exhalation of all this water and all this green — condensing on every cold surface by midmorning, beading on lichen-crusted outcrops, fogging up from the basins in thin columns that the wind shears sideways and erases.

## Light and the engines beneath it

The light is enormous. At this elevation, with cloud cover thin or absent, it arrives nearly shadowless and total, a bright cool wash that makes the wet moss-mass throw back color like stained glass lit from within — every basin a held coin of glare, every dripping ledge a string of small suns. The brightness doesn't warm the air much; it's a cool light, a working light, the kind that shows you everything without comforting you.

And under that light, the sound is constant and enormous: not wind, not birds — there are barely birds — but *engines*. Scattered through the terraces, with great gaps of empty wet stone and moss between them, stand pumping installations: squat iron housings bolted into the young rock, belted to waterwheels turning in the rivulet-falls, driving compressors that hum and clatter and occasionally shriek when a valve cycles. They are sparse — you might walk twenty minutes of dripping silence-that-isn't-silence between one and the next — but each one is loud enough that its sound fills the whole terrace system, bouncing off wet rock faces, layering with the next engine's rhythm half a kilometer off until the valley reads as one continuous mechanical roar laid under the trickling water-sound, a roar so constant it becomes a kind of silence itself, something the ear stops parsing as noise and starts treating as weather.

Nobody quite remembers, or it isn't your business to know, what these pump-stations move or why — water drawn from below to somewhere over the ridge, or gas vented from some deeper process, or simply machines maintaining a level in some reservoir out of sight. They're tended by no one visible. Catwalks of riveted iron, beaded with the same condensation as the rock, lead up to housings; ladders rust gently in a climate too wet for rust to ever fully take hold, the metal staying perpetually at the *beginning* of decay, orange bleeding into the moss below in long stains, never progressing to collapse. The built world here is not a town, not a settlement — just these iron organs, scattered, industrial, deafening, standing in for a population that has clearly otherwise vacated the plateau or never lived here in numbers at all.

## Ecology of near-absence

Fauna is rare to the point of rumor. Once in a long while a thing moves at the edge of vision in the deep moss — pale, low, gone before you classify it. Insects exist mostly as a presence felt rather than seen: midges hanging in static clouds over the warmest basins, a single deliberate beetle the size of a thumbnail working across a lichen face with the patience of something that has nowhere else to be. No grazing animals; nothing has eaten this moss down or even disturbed it into the kind of patchy, trampled mosaic you'd find where larger life moved. Lichens grow undisturbed into perfect, ancient-looking targets on stone never touched by a hoof. The world feels stood-up but not yet populated — a stage set after construction, before the cast arrives, machines already running to test the system.

## Time here

Despite the engines' frantic local noise, the place as a whole moves with great patience. The basalt is still emerging — you can see, in places, fresh fracture faces with no moss yet on them at all, oxidation barely begun, the rock's edges still geometric and clean in a way that erosion hasn't had time to round. But the moss colonizes faster than the rock can finish forming, so the two processes run in a strange race: stone arriving, moss claiming it almost as soon as it cools, water already finding its channels through both. Nothing here looks finished, and nothing looks like it's hurrying to finish. The waterfalls will cut their basins a little deeper this century. The moss will thicken by a few more centimeters of itself, dying into its own understructure, becoming the peat that someday — long outside the frame of this visit — will be soil proper, will hold trees, if trees are ever going to be allowed up here at all.

## What it feels like to stand in it

Cool, wet through your boots within minutes, brilliantly lit, and *loud* — a place that overwhelms two senses (sight, with its glare and green saturation; hearing, with its mechanical roar laid over water-sound) while leaving you strangely alone in it, scattered engines and scattered moss-shelves and great gulfs of empty bright air between, the whole valley-or-plateau extending past what you can take in at once, ridgeline behind ridgeline, each one wearing its own thin rivulet-veil, each one humming faintly with its own hidden pump. You don't feel watched. You don't feel unsafe. You feel like a very small, very wet, very deafened witness to something enormous quietly assembling itself, one cooling rock and one new moss-cell and one tireless piston-stroke at a time.
