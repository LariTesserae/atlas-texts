# The Tablelands of Var Ossuin

## Approach

You come to it across a long climb of switchbacked stone, the air thinning and warming at once — a contradiction the body accepts before the mind does. By the time the grade levels, you are standing on a plateau that seems to have always intended to exist this way: a high, sun-struck shelf of rust-and-bone-colored rock, stretching toward several horizons at once, broken by shallow draws and the occasional standing outcrop like a held breath. This is established country. Nothing here looks recent. The wind has been at it a long time, and the wind has won every argument it cared to have, leaving behind only the shapes that agreed with it.

## Ground and Sky

The rock is sedimentary in feeling if not origin — layered, the color of dried clay and old rust, banded sometimes in paler mineral streaks that catch the light like veins of something once molten. Underfoot it is mostly hardpan, compacted dust over stone, with patches of fine gravel that shift and resettle underfoot with a sound almost too quiet to call a sound. Where water has passed — and it does pass, in brief violent intervals — there are dry channels, smooth-bottomed, carved over what must be centuries into sinuous arroyos that thread the plateau like dried river-ghosts. Most days these are empty. Some mornings, condensation beads on the shaded undersides of rock shelves and trickles down into shallow pockets where it sits, dark and still, until the sun finds it.

The sky is enormous and mostly cloudless, a pale hammered blue at midday going toward something molten and layered at dusk. Light is the dominant fact of this place. It does not flicker or filter — it simply arrives, full-strength, and the plateau receives it the way old stone receives anything: without complaint, with a kind of practiced indifference. Shadows here are sharp-edged and have weight. Things hide in them on purpose.

## What Grows

Vegetation is sparse but not absent — scattered drought-hardened shrubs with small, waxy, silver-backed leaves, growing in the lee of boulders or along the seams where moisture lingers longest. Some have grown for what looks like a very long time without growing very large at all, gnarled and low, woody trunks twisted into the shapes the wind suggested. There are lichens in patches on the north faces of rocks, rust-orange and pale green, and in the draws, after the rare rains, a flush of small flowering things appears with startling suddenness and is gone again within days, seed-cycling against a clock only they understand.

## What Moves

The fauna is the loud part of this quiet place, though "loud" undersells how it actually announces itself — mostly through absence-of-stillness rather than noise. Things move at the edges of vision. Large soaring forms ride thermals overhead for hours without a single wingbeat, riding the plateau's rising warm air in lazy gyres, patient in a way that suggests very good eyesight and an evolutionary relationship with whatever scurries below. The scurrying things are abundant too — small mammals, reptiles sunning openly on warm rock then vanishing into seams of shadow at a single misjudged footstep, larger ground-dwelling creatures that move in loose family groups across the open ground at dawn and dusk, grazing on what little there is with patient, economical movements.

There is more life here than the sparseness of the land would predict — an abundance that seems to depend on the very emptiness, as if a thin ecology spread very wide is more stable than a dense one folded in on itself. The animals do not call much. Mostly there's a click, a rustle, the dry scrape of claw on stone, the soft concussive thump of something large landing somewhere out of sight. Quiet is the baseline state. Sound, when it comes, is information, not atmosphere.

## What Was Built, and What Builds Still

Scattered across the tableland — never clustered, always solitary or in pairs, as if placement itself were a kind of grammar — are structures that do not belong to any obvious lineage of construction. Some are clearly old: low circular foundations of fitted stone, waist-high, their original purpose long since worn into ambiguity, filled now with windblown dust and the husks of small lives. You could mistake these for ruins of a simple pastoral people, if not for what stands among them.

Because scattered at the same low density, indifferent to the old stone rings as though built by people who never noticed them, are the other things. A slender tower of material that has no visible seam, surface faintly iridescent, photophoric, casting back the sunlight in colors slightly wrong for any spectrum you remember — not damaged, not decaying, simply present, the way a boulder is present, except boulders do not hum almost below hearing, do not warm the air for several meters around them regardless of the sun's angle, do not occasionally — rarely, maybe once a day, at no fixed hour — emit a vertical lance of light that goes up farther than the eye can hold and then is simply gone, leaving an afterimage and a smell like ozone and crushed mineral.

These structures don't cluster into anything like a settlement. They are scattered the way the shrubs are scattered, the way the fauna is scattered — as though governed by the same underlying law of spacing, an ecology that happens to include both lizards and impossible towers, both wind-carved stone and a thing that, if you put your palm against it, you would swear was listening.

No one tends them. No paths lead to them, though the animals seem to give certain ones a wide and habitual berth, and others they sun themselves against, as if the heat-bleed off the strange material were simply one more resource the plateau offers, like shade, like the rare standing pool of water in a shadowed crevice.

## The Quiet Itself

What you notice eventually, having been on the plateau an hour, half a day, is the particular character of its silence — not an absence of sound but a kind of held space around what little sound exists. Wind moves across the open ground with a low continuous hush, more felt than heard, a pressure rather than a noise. Within it: the click of a beetle's wings, the far cry of a soaring hunter answered minutes later by another a kilometer off, the occasional groan of stone expanding in heat, the not-quite-sound the towers make, which some travelers swear they only feel in the fillings of their teeth or the small bones of the inner ear.

At night the temperature does not drop as much as the elevation would suggest it should — another small wrongness, attributable maybe to the towers' patient warmth bleeding outward into the dark, maybe to something deeper in the rock. The stars come out with unusual clarity, and the iridescent surfaces of the structures pick up faint light and give it back, dimmer, slower, like the afterglow of something remembering daylight.

## The Rule of the Place

If this land has a law, it might be this: nothing here is dense, and nothing here is recent, and yet nothing here is finished. The plateau holds nomadic abundance over sparse ground, ancient erosion alongside technology that argues with physics, voicelessness threaded through with vast slow activity — predator and thermal and migrating cloud-shadow and the rare blinding vertical light. It is a held breath that has been held so long it has become a kind of breathing. Things are still happening here. They are simply happening slowly, spread wide, and mostly without sound — the way a very old, very patient mind might think a very long thought, one quiet click and shimmer and footstep at a time, across a great deal of warm, bright, weathered space.
