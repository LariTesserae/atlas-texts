# The Standing Works of Anhalt's Reach

The plateau announces itself first as a smell — mineral steam, hot iron, something like wet slate left too long in a kiln. You climb a switchback road of fused gravel to reach it, and the air thickens with each hundred meters, not with vegetation-breath but with something else: water vapor rising off ground that should be dry at this elevation and isn't.

## Terrain

A high tableland, vast and nearly level, broken by escarpments of dark basaltic rock that bleed moisture from invisible seams. The stone is wet without rain — sweats it, the way a glass sweats in summer — because somewhere below, an aquifer presses against geothermal heat and the whole plateau exhales. Shallow pools collect in stone basins, never deep, always warm, rimmed with mineral crusts the color of rust and bone. No soil to speak of. No green anything. The ground is pale gray to black, polished in places by centuries of feet and wheels, pitted elsewhere by weather that has had a very long time to work on it and has settled into a kind of truce with the rock — nothing dramatic happening now, just the long arithmetic of erosion completing itself slowly, evenly, everywhere at once.

The scale is regional — this isn't one structure but a reach, a whole elevated district's worth of ground, with the works strung across it like beads with enormous gaps between them.

## The Built Works

And yet — across this naked, steaming highland — a city. Or what was a city, or is one in some attenuated, late-hour form.

Towers of riveted iron and dark fired brick rise at intervals, connected by causeways of the same fused gravel as the road, by pipe-bridges that hiss faintly and weep condensation along their undersides, by rail lines whose ties have rusted into the stone itself. The architecture is industrial-era heavy: smokestacks (cold now, or nearly — a few still issue thin grey threads), gantries, cooling towers shaped like wide hourglasses that catch the plateau's ambient steam and recycle it into their own private weather, drip-drip-dripping in courtyards no one stands in.

This is built at urban density of *construction* — layered, organized, infrastructural — but almost void of presence. Density of life: nearly nothing. You can walk a kilometer between one structure and the next and meet no one. Windows are intact, mostly, lightless or lit by something dim and steady that isn't quite fire and isn't quite electric — a low ochre glow behind glass, unattended.

Engines work somewhere. You hear them more than see them: a deep subsonic chuff, regular as a heartbeat, traveling up through the ground and into your teeth. Industrial-tier tech — boilers, pistons, belts — but tended by no visible hand. The machines have the look of things still doing their job long after the point of the job was forgotten or transferred to no one.

## Light and Air

Dim throughout — not darkness, but a perpetual late-afternoon-before-storm quality, sourceless, possibly the steam itself diffusing what sun there is into a flat pewter wash. Heat presses close to the top of the scale: this is a hot place, almost oppressively so, the warmth and the wetness combining into something like the inside of an animal. Sweat doesn't evaporate so much as join the general moisture of the world.

## Sound

Mostly quiet, but textured: that deep mechanical pulse underneath everything; the occasional valve-shriek, short and far off; condensation ticking onto hot metal; the basins bubbling faintly, almost like breath. No wind to speak of — the air sits, heavy and still, trapped against the plateau's heat.

## Ecology

Almost none. No plants — not even lichen, not even the simplest crust-life that usually colonizes wet stone — as if something here actively discourages it, or the chemistry is wrong, or it's simply too young or too strange a substrate. Fauna is rare to the point of myth: a pale, eyeless thing was once reportedly seen moving between the cooling towers, low to the ground, more rumor than record. Steam-moths, maybe — small filamentous shapes that gather where the vents breathe — but you may walk the whole reach and see nothing living at all except, possibly, yourself.

## The Strangeness

What unsettles is the *maintenance*. Things should have failed by now. Established, weathered, long-existed — the erosion here speaks of centuries — and yet the machines run, the lights hold their dim glow, the pipe-bridges weep their orderly condensation, all without crew, without visible repair, without decay matching the scale of time clearly written into the stone. It is a city that has outlived every reason for itself except continuation. Doors open without resistance into rooms with warm floors and humming consoles and no dust, in a place where dust should be the only inhabitant.

There's a theory, traded by the rare travelers who pass through and don't stay: that the plateau's heat and water aren't geological at all, but exhaust — that somewhere beneath the visible works, something larger and original is still running, and the city above is only its furthest, coolest extremity, kept alive the way a fingernail stays warm from a body it barely feels anymore.

No one has found the center. The rail lines all seem to lead inward, toward a darker quarter of taller stacks where the steam is thickest and the chuffing underfoot grows loud enough to feel like a second pulse alongside your own — but the roads, oddly, curve before they arrive, every time, as though the place prefers to be circled rather than entered.
