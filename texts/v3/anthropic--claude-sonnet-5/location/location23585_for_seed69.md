# The Smoking Highlands

There is no sun here, and there never was one, but the place is not blind. It has learned to see by other means, and so will you, given time.

## Terrain

Picture a plateau system raised so high that weather forms beneath it as well as above — a continental shelf of stone lifted into permanent dark, its surface folded into ridges and basins the way skin folds at a knuckle. The erosion here is old and finished in the way a riverbed is finished: not static, but settled into its grooves, running the same paths for ten thousand years. Long scarps run for hundreas of kilometers, their faces slick with seep-water, glistening when anything throws light on them at all. Between the ridges lie basins of black soft ground, saturated, holding water just under the surface so that every footstep finds it within a few centimeters of digging. Walk anywhere here and the ground answers wetly.

The vastness is not exaggeration — this is a place with its own jet streams, its own seasons measured in differentials of pressure across thousands of kilometers of dark stone and darker canopy. Storms are born in one valley and die three provinces away. You could walk for a season and not cross it.

## Materials

Wet basalt, wet limestone, slicked with algae-films that never see light but live anyway, drawing on warmth and dissolved minerals instead. Iron-stained seep-rock weeping rust-colored water from fissures, the water warm to the touch, faintly metallic in smell. Standing pools black as the air above them, skinned with mineral films that flex like membrane when touched. Everywhere, condensation: on stone, on leaf, on the cooling jackets of machines, on your own skin within minutes of standing still.

## Vegetation & Fauna

Lush in this dark is a strange lushness — not green (there is no light to make green mean anything) but textured, layered, dense with broad waxy leaves that have given up photosynthesis for filter-feeding on mineral-rich mist and the chemical exhaust of the machinery below. Trunks grow thick and soft, more fungal than woody in places, glowing faintly bioluminescent at branch-joints — not for show, but as a byproduct of metabolic processes that the ecosystem has bent toward this depthless dark. This soft cold-fire light is the only light there is: a sourceless, scattered, blue-green pricking of glow across kilometers of canopy, like a second, slower set of stars.

The fauna is abundant in the way a coral reef is abundant — many small things, several large things, all of it close together, all of it audible. Herds of heavy-bodied grazers move through the lowland fog in groups numbering hundreds, their calls long and low, more felt through the ground than heard. Above, things glide on membranous wings between canopy-gaps, screeching in registers that hurt human ears. Things move under the water-skinned pools, surfacing only as ripple and retreating sound. Everything here has adapted to live without seeing very far, and so everything is loud — loudness is sight, here. Echo-calls roll across the basins continuously, a layered conversation of creatures locating each other, locating food, locating threat, in a dark that asks for sound the way the surface world asks for color.

## Built & Tech

And under all of it, threaded through the ridgelines like circuitry through a board: the machines.

Not cities — nothing so concentrated, so deliberate. Scattered installations instead: a refinery sunk into a basin, its stacks breathing slow orange flame upward into low cloud, lighting the cloud-belly from below in pulsing amber that never quite reaches the ground. A pumping station half-swallowed by climbing vine-fungus, still working, its pistons audible for kilometers, thudding like a vast slow heart. Pipeline gantries strung across gorges, dripping condensate, their metal warm with old heat and live current, hosting nests of something that has learned to love the warmth of machinery more than the warmth of biology. Single structures, here and there — a foreman's tower, abandoned or not abandoned, nobody quite sure; a generator shed swallowed in soft trunk-growth, still humming.

Nobody built a civilization here. Something built *infrastructure* — sparse, scattered, industrial in nature but never urban in form — and then the green and the dark and the animals grew up and around and through it, until machine-sound and animal-sound and weather-sound became indistinguishable in the mix. The factories never stopped. Nobody is sure who or what still tends them. Lights that aren't bioluminescent flicker sometimes from deep vents — sodium-orange, mechanical, unmistakably electric — and then vanish for years.

## Atmosphere & Sound

The air is warm, wet, heavy — the kind of warmth that sits on the skin like a second humidity, not heat exactly but a held-breath closeness. Condensation drips constantly: off leaf, off pipe, off stone overhang, a continuous percussive background, like rain that fell once and never finished falling. Mist banks roll through the lower basins in slow tidal pulses, driven by heat differentials off the refinery stacks, mixing industrial exhaust with organic mist until you cannot smell, by scent alone, which is which.

And over all of it: noise. The engines never stop. The grazer-herds never stop. The echo-calling things in canopy and pool never stop. It layers — mechanical thud under biological scream under dripping water under distant thunder — into something that isn't chaos, exactly, because it has been going on so long that it has found its own rhythm, the way two clocks running long enough in the same room eventually drift into sync. There are moments — rare, valued by whatever lives here long enough to value anything — when everything aligns for a few seconds into something almost like silence. It never lasts.

## Rules of the Place

Nothing here was designed as one thing. The machines were not built to live alongside the ecology, and the ecology did not evolve to live alongside the machines — but both have been here long enough, in the established, weathered, settled-into-itself way of this place, that they no longer compete. They've reached an equilibrium of mutual indifference: the refinery breathes its heat and the canopy breathes its filtered mist and the grazers walk migratory paths that loop politely around the gantries, generation after generation, because the paths were always like that and no animal here questions a path that works.

There is no day, so there is no day-rule. Instead there's a slower pulse: a pressure-cycle, hours or days long, when the storms gather over the high ridges and break downward into the basins, and everything — animal, plant, and machine alike — seems to brace, vents hissing steam to match the rain, herds bunching tighter, canopy-glow dimming as if in anticipation. Then it passes, and the long settled noise resumes, and the dark — vast, warm, wet, and entirely unconcerned with whether anyone is there to call it beautiful — goes on being exactly what it has always been.
