# The Reef of Coals

There is a basin in the high country, perhaps a hundred paces across, that the maps refuse to show—not from secrecy, but because no one who returns from it agrees on its borders. It sits in a fold of dark rock, walled by slopes of glassy scree that climb toward a sky you cannot see from inside, the basin's own heat-shimmer erasing the stars. The ground is mineral and dead—no lichen, no rot, no green anywhere—just cracked plates of something like obsidian and something like bone, fused at their seams, radiating a heat that comes up through your boots before you've taken three steps.

And it is full. Packed. There is no patch of that cracked ground, no ledge of the scree walls, that does not hold a body.

## The Fauna

They are small, mostly—fist-sized to dog-sized, though larger shapes move at the basin's far edge, glimpsed and then not. Their skins (if skin is the word) carry a faint inner glow, banked-ember orange and deep blood-red, the only light in the place. This is why the basin seems to breathe with color even in total darkness: thousands of low coals shifting against each other, dimming when undisturbed, flaring when something moves too close.

They do not fly. They do not run far. They climb over one another in slow tides, displacing and resettling, finding gaps that close within seconds. The density is the first law here: there is no ground to walk on that is not also a back, a flank, a folded limb. To enter the basin is to walk on the animals themselves, and they seem to expect this, parting just enough and no more.

You hear them less than you'd think. A heat-environment sound—not chirps or calls, but a low continuous friction, like coals settling in a grate, like a held breath let out slowly through gritted teeth, multiplied by ten thousand. Occasionally a single sharper note cuts through—a click, a hiss—and a ripple of brightening passes outward from it, the way a stone's drop ripples a pond, before the glow subsides back to its ember hush.

## The Rules

No water has ever been here, and none is needed—whatever moves in these bodies runs on heat directly, drawn up from the rock below, which is itself warmed by something older and unseen. The animals do not drink. They do not seem to eat each other, exactly, though periodically a body goes dark and still and is absorbed somehow into the press, gone by the next stretch of looking. New shapes appear at the walls with no clear birth. The basin's population holds steady, as if it were not a population at all but a fixed quantity of something, redistributing itself eternally among available bodies.

The strangeness deepens at the edges. The scree slopes are not stone all the way through—broken plates show hollow interiors, chambers, things like the inside of a wasp nest crossed with a furnace flue. Old built things, half-swallowed: a ring of stacked black stone, mortarless, forming a doorway to nothing; a flat platform too regular to be natural, now buried under three bodies deep of the glowing fauna; a single post of fused glass-rock standing at the basin's exact center, scorched, ancient, its purpose lost to whoever piled the coal-things around its base like an offering or a barrier.

No tools are used here, by anything. No tracks but the bodies' own. No tending. If the platform and the ring and the post were built, their builders left nothing else—no tools, no remains, no second structure to suggest return visits. One ruin, one purpose maybe, then the long centuries of the basin filling with its current occupants until the old stone became furniture for something else's accumulation.

## The Weather of the Place

Heat moves through the basin in slow tides independent of any visible cause—areas brighten and dim across many minutes, the whole reef of bodies shifting attention or temperature together, like a single organism remembering different parts of itself. Sometimes a section near the wall goes entirely dark and cold for a stretch, and the bodies there go rigid, glassy, indistinguishable from the rock, only to wake again later with no apparent trigger. Visitors who camp at the rim report the basin "breathing" in long irregular cycles—four minutes of brightening glow and louder friction-sound, then six or seven of near-total quiet and dark, as though the whole packed mass were inhaling.

There is no day here, not really, and no night beyond what is already total. The light is entirely the animals' own, dim and red and constant in aggregate, shifting in particulars. To stand at the rim and look down is to see a held coal-bed the size of a hamlet, breathing, clicking, dense beyond walking, ancient beyond memory, hot enough that the air above it bends the basin's far wall into a wavering lie.

No one has counted the bodies. No one has named the species. It is simply the place where the heat-things are, in numbers that fill every available space, around two unexplained constructions a few centuries or millennia old, in a high dry basin that holds no water, no plant, no other light, and—based on the wear of the stone—has held this exact arrangement, this same dense quiet seething, for far longer than anyone has been alive to ask why.
