# The Sounding Flats

The land has been worn down to its last patience. Once there may have been mountains here — the bones of something taller persist as low, rounded swells breaking the flatness, their surfaces polished to a sheen by water and time working together for longer than seems reasonable. This is erosion at its terminus: not a process still happening but a process *finished*, the rock having given up everything it was ever going to give. What remains is smooth, low, and absolute. There is nowhere left for this land to go.

It is wet without being green. No plant has ever taken hold here, or none has survived to show it — the water moves through a world that never made the bargain with biology that most wet places make. Instead the wetness sits as a kind of permanent exhalation: shallow sheets of standing water lie across cracked mineral pans, fed by springs that seep up through fissures in the old rock as if the ground itself were sweating. The air carries a perpetual dampness, thick and cool, condensing on every surface by what passes for night. Stone here weeps — runnels of mineral-stained water bleed down the flanks of the low swells, leaving pale tide-lines of dissolved salts, rings within rings, a geology of patient leakage.

The light is dim, diffused through some high, unbroken haze — not dark, but shadowless, a grey-gold ambient glow with no clear source, no sun you could point to. Everything is lit evenly and from nowhere, which makes distances hard to judge across the open flats. The horizon dissolves rather than ends.

And it is *loud*. Deafeningly so, which is the place's great contradiction — a terrain this old, this barren, this still in its bones, generating a sound-field of staggering volume. The noise comes from the fauna, which is abundant in a way nothing else here is. Enormous colonies of some unrecognized creature — not quite insect, not quite crustacean, something that split off from any familiar lineage long ago — live in the cracks and flooded hollows of the stone. They are small individually, pale, many-legged, faintly luminous along their seams, and they exist in numbers that defy the surrounding emptiness. Their collective noise — a continuous overlapping drone of clicks, percussive taps against shell-plates, and a deep subsonic throb from larger, unseen things moving in the flooded crevices below — fills the whole basin like weather. It rises and falls in tidal pulses, swelling toward what might be dusk into something almost unbearable, then receding to a duller roar before building again. There is no silence here, only quieter loudness.

The ecology seems to run on the water and on each other — smaller swarms feeding the chittering mid-tier predators that stalk the shallows on jointed, stilting legs, which in turn are stalked by larger things glimpsed only as ripples and a vast moving shadow beneath the standing water, never fully surfacing. Bones — if that's the word for their chitin-like remains — accumulate in drifts at the edges of pools, bleached pale, adding their own faint percussion as the wind, when it comes, knocks them together.

Scattered through this active, ringing, ancient flatness are the marks of someone who came before, or who comes still. Small structures: rough cairns of stacked flat stones, just tall enough to stand on, placed at intervals across the swells as if marking paths through the standing water, or marking the deafening's safe thresholds. A few low frames of lashed bone and dark fibrous cord, half-collapsed, suggesting shelters built and abandoned, or built seasonally, by hands using only what the place offers — no metal, no engineered material, just leverage, knot, and patience. Nothing suggests a settlement. Everything suggests passage — a primitive people moving through this drone-filled basin the way one moves through weather, briefly, with respect, leaving cairns like prayers and shelters like punctuation.

There's a wrongness underneath the recognizable wetness-and-erosion logic, a low-grade unusualness that never resolves into anything nameable. The rings of mineral residue around the pools are too regular, concentric in a way that suggests pulsation rather than simple evaporation, as if the pools themselves breathe on some vast slow cycle synced to nothing visible. The luminous seams on the creatures sometimes flare all at once, across the whole basin, in a single shared flicker, then go dark again — a coordination with no apparent trigger and no apparent purpose, like a thought passing through a creature too large and dispersed to see all at once.

Nothing here moves quickly except the creatures. The water seeps at its own glacial rate. The stone holds its shape because there is no shape left to lose. But the sound never stops, and the swarms never rest, and the basin pulses with a kind of frantic stillness — an ancient, settled landscape vibrating, every dim hour, with more life and more noise than its emptied, patient geology seems built to hold.
