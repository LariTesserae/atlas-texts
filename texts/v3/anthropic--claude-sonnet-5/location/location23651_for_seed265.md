# The Steam Basin

There is no light here, and yet you can see — or rather, you perceive, which is not the same thing and you will spend a long time figuring out the difference.

## Ground

The basin spreads outward past any edge you could call a boundary — a low wet country, young in the way of land that arrived recently rather than eroded into being. The rock has the wrong kind of freshness: black basalt-like sheets, still faintly warm from below, cracked into polygons too regular to be geological, as if something pressed a mold into the earth before it cooled. Between the plates, water sits in shallow standing sheets — not pooling from rain, since nothing falls here, but seeping up through hairline fractures from some pressurized reservoir underneath. The ground exhales. You feel it more than hear it: a slow, patient out-breath every few minutes, fogging warm and mineral-smelling against your shins.

The vegetation is sparse but not absent — scattered stands of cartilage-pale reeds, jointed like fingers, growing in loose colonies wherever the water collects deep enough. They don't sway. There's no wind to move them, but they shift anyway, slowly, on a rhythm independent of anything you'd call environment. Some of them, you'll notice eventually, are facing you.

## Architecture of Industry, Largely Unmanned

Scattered across the open basin — never close enough to form a skyline, never far enough to forget — stand isolated industrial works: a stack here venting a slow curl of vapor that glows faint amber from some internal source though there should be no light to make it glow; a squat brick housing there, riveted, with a single great flywheel turning at a speed too slow to produce any sound, its motion almost imperceptible except that each time you look back it's in a different position. These structures don't connect. No rail line, no pipe, no road links one to the next, yet the steam from all of them drifts toward a common, unseen point, as though the whole basin were exhaling toward a single lung somewhere past the horizon you cannot reach.

Tech without operators. Engines running on nothing you can identify, for purposes that resolved themselves before there was anyone left to ask. Occasionally a valve releases with a great pressurized shriek — the loudest thing in the basin — and then silence reasserts itself so totally that the shriek seems retroactively cancelled, like it happened to a different basin a long time ago.

## Population

This is where it stops being explicable.

The fauna here is abundant — overwhelmingly, uncomfortably so, given how little plant life supports it. You perceive presences everywhere, dense, layered, near-touching in the gloom: shapes wading through the shallow steam-water, shapes perched motionless atop the cooling vents, shapes that might be herds moving in loose unison across the basalt flats. But the darkness only gives you edges, and the edges don't agree with each other. A silhouette that reads as a long-necked wading bird at fifty meters reads, at five, as something with too many joints in its neck, bending in places necks don't bend, watching you with an eye that wasn't on that side a moment ago.

They are mostly quiet. That's the unsettling part — a basin this full of life should churn with calls, splashes, the percussion of bodies in shallow water, and instead you get hush, broken by occasional sounds with no clear source: a low fluting note that seems to come from inside the reeds; a wet rhythmic clicking, like a great clock built from teeth; once, distantly, something that might be laughter played backward.

They do not flee from you. They do not approach. They reorganize — herds you watched walk west are, an unmeasured time later, standing east, having apparently never crossed the distance between.

## The Weirdness, Specifically

Reality here is not broken in a dramatic, announce-itself way. It's broken in the way a single frame is missing from a long reel of film — you don't always notice the cut, you just feel, retroactively, that something didn't happen that should have.

The valves vent steam upward and the steam sometimes falls back down as something other than condensation — fine ash, or a soft chiming dust, or once, reportedly, a slow rain of small dry insect husks that dissolve before reaching the ground. The flywheels turn without a power source and the polygonal ground-plates occasionally swap positions overnight, the basin's geography reshuffling itself like a hand of cards while everything continues operating exactly as it did before.

Distance is unreliable. The scale of this place is enormous — regional, valley-spanning — but "far" and "near" are organized more by attention than by geometry. Walk toward a distant stack for an hour and it's no closer; stop thinking about it, glance back, and it's behind you.

Nothing here moves quickly. The dynamic is slow, glacial, patient — the basin's changes happen across cycles too long to witness directly, except for the small disqualifying violations: a flywheel's position, a herd's geography, the level of standing water that should evaporate in this warmth and somehow never does.

## Atmosphere, In Total

It is warm — warm like a held breath, like proximity to something large and sleeping. The air sits thick with condensation that never quite becomes fog, never quite clears. It smells of hot mineral water, machine oil, and very faintly, beneath both, something organic and herd-warm, the smell of a great many bodies breathing slowly in the dark nearby.

You are never alone here, and you are never with anyone either.

The rule of this place, if you press for one, seems to be: *everything continues*. The engines keep working though no one built them recently and no one tends them now. The animals keep their numbers though nothing here should feed them. The basin keeps exhaling its warm wet breath into a darkness that swallows the steam before it can become weather.

It is not hostile. It has simply stopped requiring your understanding as a condition of its functioning, and has not yet decided whether to require your departure.
