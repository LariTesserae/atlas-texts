# The Scaffold Heights

The mountain has not finished becoming a mountain.

You can see this in the rock itself — raw seams of stone still soft at the edges, ridgelines that look freshly extruded rather than worn, cliff faces with the faint striation of something cooling rather than something aged. Erosion has barely begun its work here. The peaks rise to elevations that should be impossible for stone this young, jutting through a permanent ceiling of low cloud that never quite resolves into sky. The air at these heights is cool, thin, and damp in patches — not raining, exactly, but sweating. Condensation beads on iron railings and dribbles down the undersides of catwalks in slow, fat drops that never seem to dry.

And built into this raw, unfinished altitude: a city.

Not at the base — *up*, scaffolded directly into the geology itself. Industrial towns clamp onto outcroppings like barnacles that grew their own architecture, connected by elevated rail lines and pipe-bridges that span the gaps between peaks. Furnace-stacks rise from plateaus, exhaling steam that joins the permanent cloud cover and thickens it. The settlements are sparse across the range — you might walk for an hour along a ridge-road and pass only one smelting tower, half-lit, before reaching the next cluster of dwellings — but each cluster, when you reach it, is dense: tiered streets, conveyor gantries, housing stacked twelve stories into a cliff-face like a hive carved by something patient and mechanical.

The light here is always dim. Not nighttime-dark — more like the sun has been filtered through three layers of gauze and industrial smoke, leaving everything in a perpetual late-dusk grey-amber. Sodium lamps along the rail lines burn weakly, more habit than illumination. Furnace-glow from the stacks provides the only real warmth of color, orange smears against the fog.

## The Green That Shouldn't Hold

Vegetation clings here with unreasonable confidence. Moss has colonized the undersides of pipe-bridges. Ferns — broad, dark-fronded, almost black in this light — grow directly from cracks in the half-formed rock, rooted in stone that geologically should still be inhospitable. Vine-growth threads up through abandoned scaffolding and through the latticework of cooling towers, indifferent to the machinery, indifferent to the engines. The vegetation is lush in pockets — gathered densely around water-seep points, around condensation-runoff, around anywhere the damp accumulates — while the spaces between are bare iron-grey stone and rust.

It feels less like nature reclaiming industry and more like the two grew up together, neither one older.

## What Moves

The fauna is abundant and loud, which is the unsettling part, because the terrain looks like it should support little. Enormous flocks of pale, long-necked birds nest in the gantry-works, their calls echoing off cliff and metal alike in overlapping, layered cries that never fully stop — a constant ambient roar underneath the mechanical noise, the two sounds so intertwined you stop trying to separate them. Something larger moves between the lower ridges at long intervals — you hear it more than see it: a vast slow displacement of gravel, a groan that might be geological or might be breathing.

The smaller creatures are the strange ones. Insects with too many wing-pairs drift near the lamp-light, unbothered by altitude. Things that look like rats but move in straight architectural lines, turning at exact ninety-degree angles as if obeying a blueprint rather than instinct.

## Where the Rules Thin

This is a place where reality has started to fray at the same rate the mountain is still forming — as though the geological incompleteness and the ontological incompleteness are the same unfinished process.

Stairwells in the settlements sometimes lead to a landing that is also, simultaneously, the landing eleven floors below — not a loop, but an overlap, two places occupying the same coordinate without acknowledging each other. Workers walk through it without comment; this is simply understood, the way you'd duck under a low pipe.

Furnace-light occasionally throws shadows that don't match their source — a worker silhouetted by orange glow casts a shadow with too many limbs, or one that moves a half-second ahead of him, anticipatory.

There are streets that exist only when approached from one direction; turn around partway down and the street behind you has become unbroken rock, no doorway, no rail line, as if it had never been laid. The townspeople have a name for these — they call them "unfinished thoughts" — and avoid walking them after the furnace-shifts end, when the light dims further and the population thins toward the residential tiers.

## The Pace of It

Despite all this, nothing here happens quickly. The strangeness is not an event but a *condition*, weathered into daily routine the way the moss has weathered into the pipe-joints. The furnace-shifts run on long cycles, hours of slow-building heat and slow-fading light. The clouds reposition themselves over the peaks across days, not minutes. Even the spatial anomalies seem to operate on a kind of geological patience — a street might destabilize only once a season, a stairwell-overlap might shift its target floor gradually, by inches, year over year, the way a glacier moves.

The overall sense is of a civilization that built itself onto a mountain still being assembled by forces it doesn't fully understand, in a place where matter and space haven't entirely committed to their final shapes — and the people living there have simply adjusted their lives to the slowness of that becoming, the way you'd adjust to weather. They've industrialized the in-between. They've made factories out of incompletion. The birds nest in it. The ferns grow in it. And somewhere above the cloud ceiling, the peaks are still — slowly, patiently — finishing themselves.
