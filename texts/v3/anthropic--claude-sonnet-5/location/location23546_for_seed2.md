# The Drowned Works

They call it the Cistern Continent, though no one who lives there bothers with names anymore — it is simply *Below*, or *the Works*, or, among the engineers born three generations deep in it, just *home*.

Picture a structure built for a sky that has long since been replaced by water. A lattice of concrete and rusted alloy, spanning farther than weather systems, holding back — or perhaps holding *in* — an ocean that should not exist at this depth, at this scale, in this much volume. The flood came young, geologically speaking: the erosion is fresh, the metal still sharp at its broken edges, the concrete still bears form-marks from the molds that poured it. Nothing here has had time to soften.

## Terrain

There is no ground, not in the old sense. Instead: tiers. Vast shelves of poured stone and girder-work descend in irregular terraces, each one a half-kilometer thick, separated by hundred-meter gaps of open water. Walking is something you do on a catwalk, a platform, a spillway roof — always something built, never anything grown. The builders clearly intended floors, walls, ceilings, in the manner of any structure, but the flood arrived mid-construction or shortly after, and now those floors are submerged corridors you swim through, those ceilings are the undersides of the tier above, dripping with condensation and strung with cable-roots like a parody of vines.

The scale defeats the eye. A single tier can host its own weather — localized fronts of fog where warm runoff from a still-running turbine meets the cool ambient water, miniature storms that rain *sideways* through gaps in the structure, driven by ventilation systems nobody has serviced in decades but which still, somehow, run.

## Water

This is the dominant fact of the place: water at 2.8, water as medium rather than feature. It fills the lower two-thirds of every visible volume, sheets down through stress-cracks in the upper works as endless waterfalls, hangs in the air itself as a perpetual cool mist where it hasn't fully pooled. You are never dry here. You are never far from the sound of it — a continuous percussion of drip, gush, slosh, distant roar, layered so densely that silence is the unfamiliar sensation, the thing that makes you look up.

The water is clear near the active filtration plants, cloudy and green-black in the dead zones where the pumps have failed and algae has had its way undisturbed for years.

## Materials and Built Environment

Everything — and the parameters insist on *everything* — is made. Concrete pylons. Riveted steel bulkheads gone to soft orange lace at their edges. Pipe-works as thick as canyons, some still pressurized and groaning, others burst and serving now as roosting-tubes for the fauna. There is no soil. There is no stone that wasn't poured. Where vegetation manages to take hold — and it does, sparsely, defiantly — it roots in joint-seams, in rust, in the silt that accumulates on the leeward side of standing water, never in earth, because there is no earth.

This produces a strange, melancholy aesthetic: a continent-scale machine, slowly being reclaimed not by a forest but by mineral deposits, biofilm, and a scattering of hardy water-grasses and bracket-fungus blooming on wet steel like a disease the structure cannot shake.

The industrial tech level means much of this is still *active*. Turbines somewhere far below turn, audibly, a bass thrum felt in the chest more than heard. Conveyor systems run empty routes through flooded galleries, hauling nothing, forever, because the shutdown protocol was never triggered, or because someone, somewhere, still depends on the cargo they used to carry. Electric light fixtures — caged bulbs, sodium-orange, strung on cable through the wet dark — provide the "normal" light level: not bright, not dim, just sufficient, pooling in functional cones that leave the vast spaces between them in blue shadow.

## Density and Space

Despite all this machinery, the place feels *empty*. The scale is so enormous and the built density of presence so low that you can travel for what feels like an hour, gliding tier to tier, hearing the omnipresent roar of water and engine, and see no other living thing, no other person, nothing but the architecture receding into mist in every direction. The structure was built for a population, a purpose, a throughput of materials and bodies that no longer exists at anything like the original scale. What's left rattles around inside it like a handful of dice in a cathedral.

## Ecology

Fauna, though, is abundant where it does occur — clustered, not distributed. Colonies of pale, blind eel-things nest in the dead pipe-sections, drawn out by vibration. Flocks of something between bat and fish — true creatures of this hybrid medium, moving through air and water with equal ease — wheel through the larger atria in spiraling, deafening clouds at certain hours, hunting the insect-analogues that breed in the standing pools. Larger things live in the deep flooded levels, glimpsed as displacement more than shape: a wake, a pressure-change, a tier-wide shudder of disturbed water that sends the smaller fauna scattering upward in unison.

These ecologies cluster around active machinery — heat, vibration, nutrient runoff — making the structure's functioning sections oases of riotous noise and movement, while the dead sections, structurally identical, lie in near-total stillness. You can cross from a screaming, fluttering, splashing aviary-flood into a silent black gallery within fifty meters, just by passing a bulkhead where the power finally, finally gave out.

## Sound

It is loud almost everywhere, but the *character* of loud shifts constantly: the white-noise roar of a waterfall through a stairwell shaft; the metallic clang of something settling, structure-wide, like the whole continent shifting its weight; the chitter-shriek of the bat-fish flocks; the deep subharmonic of turbines; the percussive drip-chorus of a thousand small leaks in a dead gallery, each drop landing on a different pitch of standing water, an accidental, endless music nobody composed and nobody, anymore, is left to hear except whoever is walking through it.

## Rules of the Place

A few things hold true, learned the hard way by whoever still lives and works here:

The tiers flood and drain on cycles tied to machinery no one fully understands anymore — pumps elsewhere in the structure pulling water through for purposes lost to record, so a corridor that's a swim at dawn may be a dry, dripping walk by the time the light-cycle (such as it is, run off the same failing grid) shifts to its dimmer setting.

Sound travels strangely through the water-and-steel medium — you can hear something a kilometer off as if it's beside you, and miss something close behind a single bulkhead entirely. Trust your eyes, not your ears, for danger.

Never follow a conveyor line to its end expecting cargo. Follow it expecting only more structure, more water, more distance, because the things it once carried, and the people who once received them, are a continent and a few generations gone.

And: the fauna cluster where the machines still run, which means the machines still running is the only reliable sign that a place is, in any meaningful sense, *alive*.
