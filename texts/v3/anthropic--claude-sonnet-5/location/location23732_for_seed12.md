# The Drowned Highlands of Mirenastal

The peaks should not be underwater. That is the first wrongness, and the mind keeps returning to it the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

Picture a highland plateau — ridgelines, saddle-backs, the bones of a mountain country — submerged not in the way a valley floods, but in the way the sky might flood, if the sky were thick and warm and the color of old jade. The water here is almost still, holding its two-point-nine-out-of-three fullness like a held breath. Visibility runs forty, fifty meters before the warm murk closes the view into soft-edged suggestion. Light comes down from somewhere far above — dim, filtered through whatever ceiling sits over this drowned world, a perpetual late-afternoon-through-smoked-glass glow that never brightens and never quite fails.

## Terrain

The elevation registers in the body before the mind explains it: pressure that should crush, doesn't; depth that should darken, only dims. The land beneath the water still rises in stone shoulders and switchback ridges — raw, new-cut rock, sharp-edged where erosion (only 0.4) hasn't yet softened a single arête. Scarps drop away into blue-green nothing. Talus slopes look freshly calved, angular, untumbled — as if the mountains were assembled last week and the water poured in after, before anything had time to round off.

Terraces have been cut into the slopes — by hand, you can tell, because the cuts are uneven, slightly off-level, the marks of adzes and fire-cracked stone still visible on the riser-faces where algae hasn't yet claimed them. These terraces step down into the murk in long flights, vanishing past the limit of sight, suggesting a vastness of cultivated drowned land that no single vantage can hold.

## Water and Light

This is not a flooded place in the aftermath sense — there's no wreckage, no drowned-roof melancholy. The water feels *original*, structural, the medium the world was built to hold. It moves in slow, body-temperature currents, warm as bathwater, carrying threads of silt-light that drift and turn like smoke under glass. Where the dim sun-disc (or its substitute) penetrates, it breaks into shafts that lean at odd angles and crawl, almost imperceptibly, across the terraces over the course of what might be a day.

Sound travels strangely thick and close here — the mostly-quiet hush of deep water, punctuated by structural groans (settling stone, the slow flex of new ground), the chime of loose scree sliding somewhere out of sight, and very occasionally something that might be a voice, might be a horn of shell or hollow bone, carried in muffled bloom from a terrace two ridges over.

## Settlement

Density and built-ness collide here into something particular: a town-scale settlement, layered up the drowned slopes, built from unworked or barely-worked stone, packed lashed reed, fired clay weighted with rock ballast. Structures cluster close — near-touching, stacked along the terrace-edges in clots of six, ten, twenty — beehive domes and low platform-houses anchored against the slow currents with root-cables of some fibrous lush plant that grows in dense dark stands between the buildings.

The vegetation (lush, though not overwhelming) takes the form of broad-leafed amphibious growths, anchored kelp-analogs with leaves like wet leather, and strange fern-trees that have adapted — or were always suited — to growing fully submerged, their fronds moving in slow unison with the current like a held congregation. They grow thickest in the terrace-beds, clearly tended, in rows too regular to be wild.

The tech is primitive — hand-shaped tools, no metal beyond the rare worked nugget hung as ornament, fire conspicuously absent (how do you forge underwater, what does a kiln mean here — this is one of the world's first private mysteries), construction by lashing, weighting, packing, patient accretion. People — if they are people — work the terraces in slow procession, moving with the unhurried economy that water enforces on everyone, gathering leaf-growth into woven creels, replacing slipped facing-stones, walking rather than swimming, weighted at the ankle by stone-soled sandals so their steps stay rooted to the ground.

## Ecology

Fauna are rare, and their rarity is itself a feature: an occasional pale fish thick-bodied and slow, grazing the kelp-stands; something larger and seldom seen that leaves a vee-wake of disturbed silt across a terrace and is gone before the shape resolves; small bottom-walking things with too many legs that the terrace-workers seem to ignore entirely, as if they're vermin, or pets, or punctuation.

## The Strangeness

The weirdness sits exactly where it should — not loud, but present in every structural fact of the place. Fire-cracked stone tools, used underwater. Settlements built from materials that shouldn't pack and hold in submersion, yet do. Terraces cut for crops that grow without sun-cycles anyone from outside would recognize. The dim light source overhead that never moves enough to mark true day or night, only a perpetual long dusk — the dynamic of this place crawls, the *slow* setting made literal: erosion measured in centuries, terrace expansion measured in generations, the whole settlement seemingly mid-construction in a project that may never finish and may not be intended to.

There is a rule here, unstated but felt: nothing in Mirenastal was *flooded*. It was *built this way*, by hands that have never known dry air, in a world where the sky itself is the sea, and the old highland bones simply happened to be high enough, and dim enough, and warm enough, to hold a civilization's patient, terraced, half-finished climb toward a surface that may not exist.
