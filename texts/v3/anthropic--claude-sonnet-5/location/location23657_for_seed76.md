# The Highreach Steppes

The plateau begins where the clouds used to end. You climb through a band of mist so old it has worn a permanent groove into the mountainside, a kind of ceiling-scar, and then you are above it, and the sky does something it cannot do anywhere else: it surrounds you on three sides and beneath you on a fourth, where the cloud-floor stretches out like a frozen sea, slowly boiling at the edges where lower air pushes up against the escarpment and fails to climb further.

This is a vast tableland, continental in reach, the kind of high country that generates its own weather out of pure altitude and stubbornness. The light here is bright in a way that has nothing to do with heat — thin air, sharp shadows, a sun that feels closer though it isn't. Everything is lit from slightly more directions than it should be, as if the place is somehow being shown to you rather than merely existing.

Vegetation covers the steppe in lush, low waves — but it is a strange lushness, adapted to cold and thin air and long light. Grasses the color of unripe wheat grow in vast combed sweeps, parted by wind into patterns that hold for days before the wind redraws them. Between the grasses: cushion-plants, dense as packed moss, dark green going to maroon at their edges, growing in slow-spreading mats that may be centuries old, each one a single organism the size of a cottage. Stands of a stunted, silver-barked tree-analog cluster in the lee of rock outcrops — not trees exactly, more like vertical lichen colonies that have learned to hold a shape against the wind, none taller than a person, all of them leaning the same direction, frozen mid-genuflect.

The ground beneath is old basalt and pale sedimentary rock, weathered into long ribs and shallow basins by erosion that has had unimaginable time to work and nothing left to prove. No sharp edges remain anywhere. Boulders sit smoothed and settled into the turf like they grew there. Wind has carved fluting into any rock that stands proud of the grass, work as patient and complete as water but without water — because there is no water here, no rivers, no standing pools. Whatever moisture the plants need they pull from fog that rolls up the escarpment most mornings and burns off by midday, leaving the soil dry and friable, the grasses faintly dusty to the touch.

The scale dwarfs you immediately and permanently. Horizons sit absurdly far away, broken only by the long slow swells of the land itself rising toward distant ranges blue with distance, their tops lost in their own private weather — you can watch storms happen to mountains forty miles off and never feel a drop. Above you, vast cloud-formations build through the day with theatrical, silent slowness: cumulus towers rising like the plateau is exhaling them, casting cathedral-shadows that slide across miles of grassland in stately procession, each shadow taking long minutes to cross from one side of your vision to the other.

It is quiet, but not still. The quiet is full of process. Wind moves constantly through the grass with a sound like dry surf, rising and falling, occasionally gusting hard enough to flatten whole acres at once in a visible wave you can watch travel across the steppe. Somewhere distant, almost below hearing, a rock face sheds a thin slide of scree — the steppe settling further into its already-settled shape. Insects too small to see make a fine static in the cushion-plants at midday, then go silent as the cold returns each evening, fast, the temperature falling with the sun like something let go.

Fauna is rare, present at the edge of expectation rather than the center of it. Herds — you assume they are herds, though you may see only three or four animals at a time, moving with the patient, ground-covering gait of creatures who have a lot of land and no urgency. Long-necked, deep-chested, pale-coated for the high light, they graze the cushion-plants in passing bites and move on before doing real damage, a relationship of grass and grazer worn smooth by the same vast time that smoothed the rocks. Smaller things keep to the lee of stone: something like a hare, something like nothing at all, glimpsed once at dusk and not again. A bird-shape — maybe a true bird, maybe not — rides the updraft along the escarpment edge for what can be hours, barely moving its wings, a black mark of patience against the bright sky.

There is nothing built here. No path, no cairn, no broken tool half-buried in moss. The absence is not eerie so much as clean — a place that has simply never required marking. If there is a weirdness to the Highreach Steppes it's not in any single feature but in the accumulation: the too-bright light, the too-old smoothness of every rock, the sense that the cloud-floor below is a sea you could in theory walk down into, that the cushion-plants are old enough to remember things, that the wind-combed grass is responding to a rhythm slightly longer than any rhythm you know. Nothing is wrong. Everything simply continues, at a scale built for itself, indifferent to the smallness of anyone who climbs up to see it.

By night the cloud-sea below glows faintly with reflected starlight, and the cold comes down hard and clean, and the silver tree-things creak very slightly as they contract, and that is the only sound for miles — that, and the wind, finishing the same work it has been doing since before there was anyone here to call it weathering.
