The place is called, by those who still bother to name it, the Lowsteam — though no one agrees on whether that's a description of the air or an instruction.

It occupies a basin no larger than a few city blocks turned in on themselves, hemmed by a ridge of mangrove-dark hills that never rise high enough to be called mountains. The elevation never asks much of you. Everything pools here instead of draining, which is the first rule worth knowing: water comes down off the ridges as a constant gray sweat and never finds its way back out. It just accumulates, sinks, resurfaces somewhere else, runs along the seams of brick and pipe like it owns the deed.

The settlement itself is old in the way that a tooth is old — worn into a shape it will now keep forever. Squat brick chimneys, iron catwalks gone soft-cornered with rust, a scatter of low workshops with corrugated roofs holding puddles that never quite empty. The architecture is industrial but not new-industrial; the machines here were built to last centuries and are several decades into proving it. You hear them before you see them — a steady, multi-throated chugging that fills the basin wall to wall, engines turning over in buildings whose function is no longer legible from outside. Belts slap. Somewhere a valve releases pressure on an irregular schedule, a long wet shriek that the locals (if there are locals; this is unclear) seem to have stopped noticing.

The vegetation has come up through everything — not jungle-thick, but insistent, opportunistic: broad waxy leaves erupting from gutter-seams, root systems threaded into brickwork, a kind of low fern colonizing every windowsill and catwalk joint. It's lush without being crowded. Plenty of space between one green eruption and the next, plenty of bare wet stone and tarnished metal in between. The heat keeps it growing; the wet keeps it growing; nothing seems to be eating it, because nothing here moves on four legs or two that isn't already machine or person. No birds. No insects you can find, though you'd swear, in the heavier middle of the day, that you hear something like wingbeats in the rafters of the largest structure — always just around a corner, always resolving into a loose belt or shutter when you check.

The light stays low no matter the hour — not because of cloud cover exactly, but because the basin holds its own perpetual condensation-haze, lit from within by sodium-colored lamps strung along the catwalks and from without by a sun that never quite punches through. Everything has the underwater quality of dusk at 2pm. Surfaces sweat. Stone facades weep dark trails downward, mineral-stained, that have been weeping long enough to leave permanent marks, like the buildings are visibly, slowly crying out their own age.

What's strange — and it is strange, in a way that accumulates rather than announces itself — is the sense that the machinery is doing something for no audience. The engines run, the belts turn, steam vents on schedule, but there's no throughput visible: no goods arriving, no goods leaving, no workers crossing the catwalks with purpose. The industry has the loudness and structure of production without its evident object. It's active in every sense — water cycling, steam venting, vegetation pushing centimeter by centimeter into mortar, metal corroding by degrees — but nothing in it seems aimed at a destination. It is a settlement keeping itself going, like a body breathing in a deep sleep.

The sparseness matters here too: you can walk a long way between one inhabited-looking structure and the next, your footsteps loud on wet metal grating, the ambient roar of the place wrapping around you from every direction at once without ever resolving into a single source. Sound here doesn't travel in lines, it pools the way the water does — collects in the basin's bowl shape and sloshes back at you off the ridgeline, so a generator three buildings away sounds like it's directly behind your shoulder.

There are rules, half-felt: the water always returns to where it started. The plants will eventually take a structure, but slowly, on a timescale that makes it feel less like decay and more like negotiation. The machines do not need fuel delivered, or if they do, it arrives by means no one has seen. Nothing here is hunting and nothing is being hunted, and the absence of that whole circuitry of predation makes the air feel held-breath, like the ecosystem is missing a verb.

If you stay long enough — and the basin doesn't discourage staying, doesn't discourage anything, really, just continues — you start to register the steam venting and the leaf-drip and the belt-slap as a kind of unintentional music, loud and arrhythmic and strangely companionable, the only sign of anything like company in a hot wet green iron room with no edges you can see and no one, as far as you can tell, left to run it.
