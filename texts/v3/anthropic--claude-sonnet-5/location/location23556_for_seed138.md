# The Cistern Works

The basin sits low between young hills, hardly more than mounded earth, their slopes still raw-cut where construction trenches haven't grassed over. The whole settlement could be crossed in fifteen minutes, end to end. A hamlet, but a loud and humid and overbuilt one — as if a much larger industrial mind had been compressed into a kettle of land barely big enough to hold it.

It is always damp here. Steam rises in shafts of dim light wherever the boiler-pipes run close to the surface, and the ground itself seems to sweat — condensation beads on iron railings, on stone footings, on the undersides of the corrugated roofs. The air doesn't move much; heat sits on the skin like a second humidity. There is standing water everywhere in shallow forms: runoff channels cut crisp and fresh into the clay, puddles that never fully evaporate between rains, a low marsh fringe at the basin's southern edge where the drainage from the works pools and goes brackish. The water table feels close, like the whole place is built on a held breath.

## Materials

Everything here is young — the stone unweathered, edges still sharp where it's been quarried or cast; the brickwork hasn't yet softened into a settled colour, still showing kiln-red and mortar-white in patches. Iron predominates: riveted plate, pipe-runs slung overhead between buildings on simple trestles, tanks squatting on stub legs, all of it dark with damp and faint rust-bloom that hasn't had time to spread far. Concrete poured recently enough that form-lines are still visible in its face. Nothing has had the centuries needed to wear into anything else. The sharpness of the place is almost startling against the heat-shimmer and the wet sheen on every surface.

## The Works Itself

A cluster of low industrial structures — three, four boiler-houses, a cooperage, a pump-station, a scatter of storage tanks — packed close despite the open land available, as if huddling for some functional reason rather than out of necessity. Smoke and steam rise together from short stacks, barely climbing before the heavy air flattens them into a haze that sits over the whole basin like a second, lower sky. Conduits and flumes run between buildings at ankle and waist height, carrying water drawn up from below — this whole settlement exists, you sense, because something here keeps water close to the surface, and the engineers built around that fact rather than against it.

The dim light is partly the hour, partly the haze: a sun somewhere up there, diffused to a flat bronze glow, no real shadows, no real glare. Visibility is good but colorless, everything rendered in damp greys, rust, and the dull green-black of standing water.

## Sparse Green, Abundant Movement

Vegetation barely persists — reed-clumps in the marsh fringe, a few tough grasses colonizing the spoil-mounds, lichen on the cooler undersides of pipe-runs. Nothing grows tall enough to compete with the structures. But fauna fills the gaps the plants leave: this is a wet, hot, loud place, and it teems. Insects in dense, audible clouds near the marsh — a constant high frequency hum underlying everything else. Amphibians unseen but vocal, throats going in the reed-beds, especially when the machine-noise dips. Birds — scavenger types, drawn to the warmth of the boiler-houses and whatever the works discards — perched along every available pipe and gutter-edge, more of them than there are trees to hold them, so they crowd the iron instead.

## Sound

The place is loud in two registers that never quite synchronize: the mechanical — pumps thudding in steady cycles, steam venting in hard exhalations, the clank of iron on iron from some unseen yard, a low engine-drone that never stops — and the biological, the insect-hum and frog-throb and bird-clatter that fills every gap the machines leave open. Voices, when there are any, get folded into this without rising above it. There is no quiet here, day or night, only a shifting balance between the two kinds of noise.

## Equilibrium

Nothing changes here, or nothing visibly does. The pumps cycle, the steam vents, the frogs call, the birds shift position on the pipes — all of it looping at scales too small to register as change. The young-cut edges of the basin don't erode because the rain that falls is steady and unspectacular, not violent. The works don't expand because they were apparently built to a finished size and have simply continued, unaltered, since. It feels less like a place arrested mid-development and more like one that arrived at its particular balance quickly and then stopped — a small, hot, wet machine-hamlet running forever at the same idle hum, sharp-edged and damp and crowded with small lives, under a sky the colour of an old coin.
