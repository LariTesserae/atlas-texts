# The Root-Hollow

You enter through a fissure no wider than a shoulder, where the limestone has cracked along some old fault line, and the air changes before the light does — it goes warm and thick and green-smelling, like biting into a stem. The crack is young; its edges are still sharp enough to draw blood if you're careless, white mineral scars where the rock tore rather than wore.

Then the ceiling opens up into a chamber that should not contain what it contains.

## What Fills It

There is almost no stone visible anymore. The hollow — perhaps the size of a generous room, perhaps a cathedral nave, the scale plays tricks because nothing inside agrees on how large it is — has been entirely annexed by a single root-system descending from some unimaginable canopy above, or below, or sideways. Direction has stopped being reliable here. The roots don't simply hang or dangle; they *architecture*. They braid into vaulting, knuckle into stairs, flatten into shelf-fungus-soft floors you could sleep on. Where one root crosses another they have fused, grown into each other like clasped fingers, and at the fusion points small white flowers push out sideways, blooming out of solid wood-flesh with no soil at all.

The vegetation doesn't grow *in* the hollow. The vegetation *is* the hollow now. Stone is a rumor underneath it, felt occasionally through gaps as a coldness against the back of your hand.

Moisture comes in licks, not floods: condensation beading along root-hairs thin as eyelashes, releasing in fat single drops that fall absurd distances and land with a sound too loud for their size — a *clap*, not a drip. Somewhere unseen, water moves through xylem with an audible tick, almost a heartbeat, almost a clock with no numbers on its face.

## The Wrongness

Here is where the place stops behaving:

The flowers track you. Not toward light — toward *you*, specifically, swiveling on stems too rigid to bend that way through any mechanism you'd call botanical. A bloom near your ear will open and close like a slow eye and you will feel watched by something that has no brain, no plan, possibly no awareness, and yet.

Bark patterns, looked at long enough, resolve into faces — not metaphorically, not "if you squint." Actual symmetrical features, eyes-nose-mouth, pressed into the wood-grain like a death mask taken from someone who was never born. They do not move. They are simply *there*, dozens of them, none repeating, staring from different angles out of root-flesh that has never seen a sculptor's tool.

Sound arrives from nowhere consistent. A chamber this enclosed should swallow noise, but instead it amplifies and relocates it — you hear chittering laughter (no fauna visible to make it), the dragging creak of something enormous shifting its weight three rooms away that don't exist, root-groans that resolve, if you listen past denial, into syllables. Not language you know. Language nonetheless, in cadence, in the way it pauses for breath that has no lungs behind it.

## Ecology of Near-Absence

Despite all this mass of living material, the place feels *empty* — vast internal distances between presence and presence, as if the architecture-of-roots dwarfs whatever inhabits it. You might walk for what feels like an hour along a single root-bridge spanning a gap into blackness and encounter nothing. Then, abruptly, the rarest of fauna: a single insect, pale, wingless, the size of your palm, walking with unhurried purpose along a vein-track only it can see, and it will not acknowledge you, and somehow this indifference is worse than aggression would be.

Things grow here at a pace too fast for the world outside. A shoot you stepped over on the way in has become a knee-high stalk by the time you double back, already budding, already opening a flower that wasn't a bud five minutes prior. The dynamism isn't seasonal. It's *immediate* — the whole hollow breathing through accelerated tides of growth, bloom, and slow long death, all three states visible simultaneously in different corners, so that you can watch the entire lifecycle of a plant in the time it takes to cross the room.

## Marks of Hands

Someone has been here, or something with hands. Not built — nothing so grand as construction — but *used*: a root worn smooth on its underside where bare feet or palms have gripped it for purchase, a notch cut with a stone-edge tool into a thick trunk-root, crude, recent-looking, the cuts still pale and uncallused by time. No one is here now. There's no fire-ring, no shelter, no sign of permanent habitation — just the passing evidence of someone who came this way once, touched what they touched, and left, possibly years ago, possibly an hour before you arrived, the distinction unclear in a place where growth-time runs faster than human time.

## Light Without Source

The light is plain, ordinary, the kind you'd expect at midday through cloud-cover — but there is no opening big enough to explain it. It simply exists, diffuse and shadowless in some pockets, then knife-sharp and directional in others ten feet away, as if the hollow has stitched together several different times of day into one stretched skin. You'll round a root-buttress from soft gray afternoon-light into a shaft of something like noon, gold and hard-edged, falling from a ceiling-crack too narrow to admit it.

## The Rule, If There Is One

The hollow seems to operate on a logic of *correspondence* rather than cause: things resemble other things and that resemblance has consequence. A root shaped like a screaming mouth will, eventually, make a sound. A flower shaped like an eye will, eventually, see you back. Nothing here is merely decorative. The place has not yet decided what it's becoming — it's young, the erosion clock barely started, the fault-line still sharp — and you get the persistent, skin-crawling sense that you have wandered into something still drafting its own rules, and that your presence, witnessed by all those wooden symmetrical faces, might be getting incorporated into the draft.

You should decide, fairly soon, whether you want to be a footnote in that draft or get out before the ink — sap, water, whatever is moving through all that wood with such audible purpose — dries.
