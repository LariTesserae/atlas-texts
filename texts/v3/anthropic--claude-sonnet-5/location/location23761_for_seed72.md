# The Cistern Works

You enter through a fissure that wasn't always there—or rather, was always becoming there, the rock around its mouth still raw and unweathered, sharp-edged in a way that suggests the opening is younger than the structures it leads to. This is the contradiction you live with here: the stone is new, the iron is old.

Below the surface, the air sits thick and condensing. Heat doesn't radiate down here so much as it pools, accumulated from sources you can't immediately locate—geothermal seams, certainly, but also the breath-heat of ten thousand bodies and the exhaust-heat of machinery that has no business running this deep. Moisture beads on every cold surface and there are almost no cold surfaces, so it beads on warm ones too, runs in slow rivulets down pipe-casings, drips from joints in the ceiling that were sealed only recently and imperfectly. The walls weep. Not dramatically—just a constant, patient seep, water finding its way down from some water table you'll never see, collecting in runnels cut into the stone floor, channels that look hand-tooled rather than natural, directing the wet toward grates and cisterns and the dark.

The space itself is modest—not a cavern-cathedral, nothing vaulted or grand. Call it hamlet-scale: a single irregular chamber, maybe a hundred meters across at its widest, with three or four lower side-galleries branching off into shadow. The ceiling is low enough in places that the taller structures—gantries, a water-tower built of riveted iron, a furnace-stack venting upward through a shaft drilled crude and recent—nearly brush it. The rock itself still feels embryonic. Cut marks are fresh. In places the walls haven't finished settling; you can find chips and rubble that fell only days ago, fine dust still hanging in patches of light. The masons-or-miners-or-whatever-they-are seem to be excavating outward from a core, the chamber widening at its edges into ragged unfinished hollows where the bones of the room are still being decided.

Onto this raw, unset geology, someone has built a town.

Not a large one. But dense, layered, urgent—small low buildings of fitted stone and salvaged iron sheet, stacked sometimes two levels where the ceiling allows, connected by catwalks and ladders bolted directly into the living rock. Pipes run everywhere, overhead and underfoot, hissing faintly, sweating constantly, carrying water or steam or something darker between a cluster of structures that read as *industrial*—a forge-shed with its bellows-roar, a pump-house with a great iron wheel turning with aching slowness, belt-driven machinery whose purpose isn't obvious but whose rhythm is: a slow chuff-chuff-chuff that seems to be the heartbeat the whole settlement is built around. Tech here is mechanized but not refined—exposed gears, leather belting, oil-black and improvised, the kind of industry that arrived faster than the infrastructure to support it cleanly.

And over and through all of it: the fauna.

This is the place's dominant fact. Despite the sparse vegetation—a few lichen-crusts near the damp walls, some pale fungal shelf-growths feeding on timber struts, nothing more—the chamber teems. Not with variety necessarily, but with sheer overwhelming presence: things that have adapted to heat and dark and human industry rather than around them. Insects in dense, low-humming clouds near every lit lantern. Pale, fast-moving creatures—something like rats, something like longer things—coursing along the pipe-runs and rafters in numbers that make the shadows seem to move on their own. Roosting colonies of something winged crammed into the unworked hollows at the chamber's margins, stirring in slow tidal surges, their collective sound a constant undertone beneath the machine-noise. The settlers seem to have made an uneasy peace with this: nets over food-stores, smoke-smudges to keep the worst of the swarms back, a general acceptance that you share this space, that you are not the most numerous species here even if you are the one with the forge.

The sound, accordingly, is loud and layered and never stops: the mechanical chuff of the pump, hiss of escaping steam, drip and trickle of water finding its channels, the furnace's low roar, voices raised to be heard over all of it, and beneath everything the animal hum—wingbeats, skitter, the occasional shrieking surge when something in the colony takes flight en masse and the whole chamber seems to shiver with displaced air. Light is dim and warm-colored, lanterns and firelight and the orange glow leaking from furnace-vents, throwing huge moving shadows from the swarms and roosts above.

Nothing here moves fast except the animals and the machinery's small fast parts. The town itself changes at geological pace—new galleries opened maybe once a season, the rock-cutting slow and deliberate, the pump-wheel's great turn the visible metronome for a settlement that otherwise just endures, sweats, works, and waits for the water and the heat and the swarming dark to be, eventually, fully tamed. No one really believes that day is close.
