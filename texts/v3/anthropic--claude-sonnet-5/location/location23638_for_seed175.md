The plateau holds itself at a height where the air thins just enough to taste of mineral and rain that has not yet decided to fall. You reach it by a long climb that the body forgets quickly, and then the ground levels into a shallow bowl, no bigger than a few unhurried fields stitched together, ringed by a lip of weathered stone that has the look of something once larger, sanded down across ages into a soft, rounded ridge no higher than a seated person. The bowl is not empty, but it is unhurried about being full.

Water is everywhere here without being a feature exactly. It surfaces in the gaps between flat stones as a kind of perpetual sweat — not pooling so much as glistening, a wetness that seems to rise rather than fall, as though the rock itself were a held breath finally let go in small, continuous exhalations. Where it does gather, it forms shallow basins fringed with a soft green sediment, water clear enough to show the pitted stone beneath, ringed with thin mineral rings like the marks left by many slow-evaporating years. No streams rush; nothing here is in a hurry. You hear, instead, an intermittent drip-tap from somewhere in the stone's interior, irregular, patient, like a clock built by something that no longer cares about exact intervals.

The vegetation grows in loose stands rather than a continuous carpet — broad-leafed plants with a faint waxy sheen, clustered around the wet hollows, their leaves cupped to catch and funnel condensation down toward their own roots. Between these stands the ground is bare stone or a thin crust of dark soil, cracked into polygonal plates that have settled into their shapes long ago and show no sign of shifting again soon. The spacing between plant and plant, basin and basin, is wide enough that you walk through open warm air for stretches before reaching the next green cluster, and this spacing itself feels deliberate, as if the place were arranged with room to breathe rather than grown wild and crowding.

Nothing moves that isn't moved by water or warmth. There is no animal sound — no birdcall, no insect hum, no rustle of anything fleeing your approach. The silence isn't dead, though; it has texture, filled with the small percussion of water finding its way through stone, the occasional soft creak of a leaf unfurling another increment in the heat, the faint hiss of moisture lifting off sun-warmed rock into the thick, warm air. The light overhead is plain and even, neither harsh nor dim, falling through a sky hazed faintly with the same moisture that beads on every surface, so that the whole bowl seems lit from slightly everywhere, shadows soft-edged and shallow.

Scattered through the bowl are the strange things — not built, never built, but shaped: stones split with too much symmetry, their inner faces smooth and faintly warm to the touch as though something had pressed through them once and left a memory of pressure; small depressions in the rock that hold water in perfect circles no matter how the rest of the basin tilts; places where the cracked soil forms patterns too regular to be entirely accidental, spirals and radiating lines that the plants seem to respect, growing along their borders rather than across them. There are no tools, no walls, no paths cut into the stone — only the suggestion that hands, or something like hands, passed through at some point long enough ago that the marks have weathered into near-naturalness, and have not returned since.

Whatever shaped those few strange stones did so with simple means — a sharp edge dragged rather than a precise blade, a deliberate but unrefined pressure — and then left the place to its own devices. Since then it has settled, the way old wounds settle into scars indistinguishable from skin. The erosion here isn't a force tearing things down; it's the slow agreement between rock, water, and time that this is the shape things will hold for a long while yet.

Time moves thick and slow across the bowl. The drip continues. The basins neither fill nor empty perceptibly within a single visit. The leaves widen by degrees too small to watch. Whatever process holds this place together — geological, organic, or something stranger that uses both as its grammar — works on a clock too long for footsteps to measure, and the only honest way to know the place has changed at all is to leave it for a long while and return, expecting to find it almost, but not quite, the same.
