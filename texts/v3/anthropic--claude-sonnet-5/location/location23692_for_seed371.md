# The Cleft

The basin is small enough to walk across in twenty minutes if the walking were possible, which it mostly is not. It is a wound in a young mountain — a vertical fracture maybe four hundred meters deep, knife-sharp at its rim where the rock has not yet had time to round itself into anything gentler. The stone here is dark and raw, basalt-black and still showing the conchoidal scars of whatever violence split it open. No lichen softens the edges. No moss has had the patience to spread. This is geology in its adolescence, all cheekbones and elbows, nothing worn smooth.

Into this cleft, against all sense, has come the green.

It arrived the way water finds cracks — opportunistically, totally. Ferns the size of doorways grow in stacked terraces down both walls, their fronds so dense in places that you cannot see the rock behind them, only a wall of black-green going down into blacker green going down into nothing. Vines as thick as a man's arm rope between outcrops, not climbing so much as colonizing, indifferent to the eighty-degree pitch of the walls. Things that might be related to horsetail rise in stands so packed that walking through them is like wading through a crowd. The vegetation does not thin with the meager water available — water here is a rumor, a condensation-sweat on the cold stone at dawn, a mist that beads and falls rather than rains — but the plants seem to have made peace with thirst, storing what little comes in fat stems and waxed leaves, and where one species fails to find purchase another simply takes the space instead. There is no give in this ecology. Every cubic meter of air at any height in the cleft has something growing through it.

There are no animals. This is the place's quietest fact and its loudest — because despite the absence of birdsong, despite no rustle of anything moving through the undergrowth on legs or wings, the cleft is not silent. It roars.

Somewhere below, unseen in the dark — because no light reaches the bottom, and precious little reaches anywhere — there is machinery. Not relics: active, working, industrial machinery, the source of a sound that fills the gorge wall to wall, a deep mechanical pulse layered with grinding, with hiss, with the occasional shuddering clang of something enormous shifting its weight. Steam or vapor rises in columns from below and condenses against the cold rock higher up, which may be the only meaningful source of moisture in this whole place — an engine sweating the cleft into bloom. No one built a path to it. No one built anything visible at all; there is no platform, no rail, no structure that announces itself as made. The technology is present but unhoused, as if the mountain itself contains an engine the way a body contains a heart, working without architecture, without a builder's hand evident anywhere in the tangle.

And it is not steady. The pulse changes tempo without warning — slows to something like a held breath, then redoubles, then throws a clattering arrhythmia up through the rock that you feel in your molars before you hear it. Once, every so often, there is a detonation-deep WHUMP and a gout of hot vapor blasts up through the green, flattening fronds in a radius, and then the plants — impossibly, immediately — begin to stand back up, unbothered, already recovering before the echo has finished bouncing off the far wall. The whole cleft seems to breathe in fits, a system in some kind of permanent crisis-equilibrium, building toward something and then venting it and starting again.

No light gets down here, not really — the rim is a thin pale slot far above, open to a sky you cannot see, and what daylight does survive the fall arrives diffused and gray, drowned further by the steam-mist. Most of the cleft exists in true dark, navigable only by sound and the rare orange flare of something igniting or overheating far below, a brief instant where the layered fern-walls flash into silhouette, jagged and crowded and gone again.

It is cool here, not cold — the rock's youth keeps it from the deep chill of older stone, and the machine-heat below pushes a constant low warmth up through the damp air, so the temperature settles into something like a greenhouse held just short of comfortable. The smell is green and mineral and faintly hot — crushed plant matter, wet basalt, and under it something metallic, oiled, working.

There are no paths because nothing here was built for feet. To move through the Cleft is to climb among the ferns, trusting roots wedged into cracks too fresh to have settled, while below you in the dark something enormous turns over, and over, and over, venting its heat into a garden that has no name for what feeds it, and does not seem to need one.
