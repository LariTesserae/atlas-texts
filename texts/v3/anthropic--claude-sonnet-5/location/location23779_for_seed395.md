# The Standing Thickets of Murn Hollow

The land is flat for as far as the eye can carry, a low basin under a wide warm sky, and onto this flatness has been pressed something that should not coexist with the word "flat": a city built entirely of growth, without a single cut stone or fired brick, stretching to every horizon in a packed, breathing mass.

Understand first that nothing here was built by hands or tools. There are no tools. There is no metal, no wheel, no fire-hardened anything. And yet the place reads, unmistakably, as *urban* — because the vegetation itself has organized into settlement-scale structure, the way termite mounds or coral reefs organize, except at a scale that makes those comparisons embarrassing. Towers of fused banyan-root rise eighty, ninety meters, their trunks braided by centuries of mutual pressure into wall-like ramparts. Between them run avenues — not paths cut by anyone, but corridors left open by the simple fact that two great root-towers grew apart rather than together, leaving a gap wide enough to walk, and walked-in enough by generations of moving fauna that the ground has worn to bare warm clay.

The terrain underneath is young. This is the part that unsettles, once you notice it: the basin floor is raw, low, barely creased by drainage that hasn't had time to cut real channels. No water moves here now — what shaped this ground was something else, some earlier wet violence whose evidence is fading, leaving behind smooth mineral flats the color of old bone, cracked into plates that the roots have only begun to colonize. Erosion is just starting its work. You can find, in the gaps between thickets, stretches of unweathered rock so fresh it still shows the texture of whatever process laid it down — no soil skin yet, no moss rind, just raw planar stone, like a wound the forest hasn't finished closing over.

The vegetation is the geology now, or close to it. Imagine fig-strangler logic run continental: trunks that started as separate seedlings centuries ago have fused at the base into single root-masses the size of city blocks, then sent up multiple crowns, then those crowns' aerial roots have come down to thicken adjacent trunks, until the whole basin reads as one decision made by plant tissue, repeated at scale, for scale's own sake. The canopy above doesn't form a flat ceiling — it forms districts. Some quarters of canopy sit low and dense, forty meters, dark beneath; others punch up into towers of crown that catch the warm light directly and host their own weather of mist and condensation at the top, a tiny private climate sitting on stilts of wood.

Density here means there is no negative space left unclaimed. Every surface — trunk, branch, the underside of leaves, the cracked stone flats — carries something living on it: bromeliad-analogues packed shoulder to shoulder along every horizontal branch, fungal shelves stacked in overlapping fans up every standing dead trunk, vines occupying the air itself in such number that some avenues are floored not with clay but with a springing mat of vine-fall a meter thick, soft to walk on, slowly composting from below while still green on top.

Fauna moves through all of this at the volume the place demands. Abundant, but not loud — the dynamic here runs on a quiet register. Grazer-herds the size of small buildings pick paths down the wide thicket-avenues at dawn, moving with the patient gravity of things too big to hurry, browsing the lower bromeliad fringe as they pass and leaving it stripped to stubble that will regrow within the week — this place's pace is fast, biologically, even though nothing about it looks rushed. Above, canopy-dwellers — gliding, climbing, never quite seen whole — keep to the high architecture, crossing the avenue-gaps in long silent arcs from one root-tower's crown to the next, a transit system with no schedule and no sound but the brief hiss of air past membrane.

Predator-prey plays out at the threshold between districts, in the dim mid-canopy zones where light drops by half and grazers slow down to feed more carefully. You don't see the kills. You see, sometimes, the aftermath: a sudden burst of canopy-dwellers scattering upward in silent waves, a grazer-herd changing direction as one body without any audible cause, the temporary stillness that follows wherever something serious has just happened thirty meters off the path.

The sound, overall, is the sound of enormous quiet machinery — except there is no machinery, only biology operating at a scale that produces the same acoustic effect. A continuous, low textured hush: leaf-friction in slow wind, the occasional structural groan of root-towers settling fractionally as they grow, the soft percussive drop of overripe fruiting bodies releasing from high shelf-fungus and falling through several layers of canopy before landing — a sound like something far away knocking twice. Nothing here is silent in the absolute sense, but nothing here is loud either; the place keeps its own volume down out of what feels almost like architectural habit, the hush of a cathedral that grew itself.

Light comes down warm and ordinary, filtered into the lower avenues as moving coin-shapes, fuller and more direct in the canopy-top districts where the aerial-root towers punch through into open sky. There's no strangeness to the light — it behaves the way light behaves — which only makes the scale around it stranger: ordinary daylight, falling across something so vast and so continuously inhabited that no single vantage point, however high you climbed, would show you its edge.

And the edge, as far as anyone walking it could tell you, may not exist. The basin is geometrically vast, plausibly continental, and the thicket-city shows no sign — in any direction yet explored along its clay avenues — of thinning back into open land. It simply continues: tower after fused tower, avenue after vine-floored avenue, herd after silent-moving herd, the raw young stone underneath patiently being claimed inch by inch by roots that have nowhere else to be and all the time in the world to get there.

There are no rules here beyond growth, pressure, and patience. Nothing was decided. Everything arrived at this density the only way density like this can arrive — by each part pressing against its neighbor for centuries, with no tool, no fire, no plan, until pressing was indistinguishable from building, and growth produced, by sheer persistence, something that looks — from any high enough crown, in any warm enough light — exactly like a city.
