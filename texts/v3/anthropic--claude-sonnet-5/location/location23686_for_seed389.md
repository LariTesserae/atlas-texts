# The Spire-Works of Anhedral Reach

The mountain is still being born. That is the first thing the eye refuses and the mind eventually accepts: this is not erosion's aftermath but its precondition, a vertical country of raw basalt and rust-colored ignimbrite thrust up so recently that the rock retains the angularity of fracture rather than the roundness of age. Cliffs go up in clean shears for hundreds of meters, their faces showing the conchoidal scarring of stone that has barely cooled, barely settled, barely agreed to stay where gravity puts it. There is no soil. There is no green. There has never been a seed here, and the rock seems to know it — exposed, raw, embarrassed almost, like geology caught mid-sentence.

Into this unfinished verticality, something has built a city.

It does not perch on the mountain so much as *graft* onto it — terraces and gantries and riveted towers driven into the cliff faces at every elevation, connected by a webwork of pipe-bridges and funicular tracks that climb at angles no level ground would permit. The built structure is dense beyond what the rock seems to invite: factories stacked on factories, smelting halls cut into the stone itself, chimneys rising from ledges so narrow that the buildings cantilever out over thousand-meter drops on iron legs the thickness of a man. Steam and heat-shimmer rise constantly from vents low on the slopes, where something — magma-proximate, or engineered, no one says which anymore — keeps the deep levels furnace-hot even where the open air runs merely warm. The heat is the valley's pulse: not violent, but ever-present, radiating up through boot-soles, warping the horizon into wavering bands above every rooftop.

Water exists here only as exception, never as feature. It comes as condensation sheeting the undersides of pipe-joints in the cool pre-dawn hours, as brief mineral-laced rains that fall in narrow bands and are gone within the hour, as the hiss of runoff hitting a hot vent and vanishing to vapor before it travels a meter. Cisterns are sacred architecture here — squat riveted drums standing sentinel at every terrace, fed by gutter-systems that comb every rooftop for whatever the sky deigns to release. A culture has organized itself entirely around capturing what evaporates almost as fast as it falls.

The air is thick with the city's own breath: particulate haze, oil-smoke, the dry tang of hot metal, layered close in the windless folds between towers, so that density here is less about crowding bodies than about *crowding presence* — machinery stacked tight against machinery, structure against structure, the whole assembly packed into the available rock like organs into a ribcage too small for them. And yet, against all this saturation of *stuff*, the place is quiet. Industrial quiet — the low continuous hum of turning engines, the occasional clang of metal settling against metal, the long sighing exhale of a pressure vent releasing on some internal schedule nobody outside the engineering guilds understands. No birdsong, because there are no birds. No wind-through-leaves, because there are no leaves. Rare is right: a single line of lichen-analog crusting some shaded pipe joint counts as wildlife here; the occasional scuttling thing seen at the edge of lamplight, pale and unbothered by the heat, is rumor as much as record.

Light falls normal and indifferent across all of it, neither harsh nor dim, picking out the rust-reds and iron-blacks and the dull copper sheen of well-used machinery, the haze softening every middle distance into smoked glass.

The strangeness isn't loud. It's structural. The factories produce — something. Cargo moves on the funiculars in sealed containers nobody discusses, downward more often than up, as though the mountain itself were the resource and the city merely its tap. The rock under the lowest levels is reportedly warm to the touch even where no vent runs near, and certain chambers near the working face are kept lightless and silent and entered only by crews who do not speak of what they find there in terms anyone else can use. There is a sense — never stated, always felt — that the engineering here keeps pace with something it does not fully control: that the mountain is still rising, fractions of a degree, fractions of a meter, year over year, and that the city was not built *on* the geology so much as built to *outrun* it, terrace by terrace, always one construction-phase ahead of whatever process keeps thrusting the rock skyward beneath their feet.

Change, when it comes, comes slow — a vent shifting its angle of exhaust over a decade, a terrace re-anchored after the rock beneath it groans a half-degree off true, a chimney's smoke shifting color as some deep ore-vein is finally exhausted. The patience of it is its own kind of menace: nothing here moves fast except the heat-shimmer and the rare flash-rain, but everything is moving, and has been since before anyone currently walking these gantries was born, and will be long after.
