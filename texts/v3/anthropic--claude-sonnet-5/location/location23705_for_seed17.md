# The Standing Sea of Kharras Vel

There is no horizon here, not in the way horizons are usually understood. There is only the place where the grey water meets the greyer sky, somewhere distant enough that the eye gives up trying to find the seam.

This is a high plateau drowned on purpose, or by accident so old it has calcified into purpose. The land beneath was raised—you can feel it in the pressure of the place, the way the cold air sits thin and sharp in the lungs—and then the water came and never left, finding every basin and crevasse of the new-made stone and filling it to a depth no sounding line has found bottom in. The rock here is young. You can tell by its edges. Black basalt columns break the surface in clusters, hexagonal, sharp enough to open skin, not yet worn round by time or weather. They look freshly cooled, freshly cracked, as though the planet finished making this place last week and has been too busy since to soften it.

Nothing grows. Not on the rock, not in the water that anyone has found. No kelp-analogue, no scum, no patient lichen creeping across the dark stone. The world here skipped the green chapter, or finished it somewhere else and never sent the news this far up. What you get instead is mineral cleanliness: stone, water, cold air, and an enormous, echoing absence where vegetation should be.

But it is not empty of life. Far from it.

## The Machines and the Few

Scattered across the standing sea, kilometers apart, sometimes farther, stand the rigs. Iron-boned, black with rust and oil, they rise out of the water on legs sunk into the unseen floor below—drilling platforms, pumping stations, refineries built for some resource no one visiting would recognize, though the smell of it is sulfurous and metallic and stays in the throat for days. These are not ruins. They are running. Pistons fire in deep, slow rhythms. Chains the thickness of a torso haul something up from below at intervals measured in hours, and dump it, glistening, into holds that groan under the weight. Smoke—or steam, or something between the two—rises in columns that don't disperse so much as simply stop, hanging at a fixed altitude as though the sky itself has a ceiling here and the exhaust has found it.

No one has ever counted how many rigs there are. They are too far apart, the water too vast, and travel between them—by the heavy, churning ships that ply fixed routes, engines roaring underwater in long thrumming pulses—takes long enough that most who work one rig never see another in their lifetime. The structures are not connected by anything except the water and the noise.

And the noise is the first thing you notice, and the last thing you stop noticing only because your ears eventually surrender.

## The Sound That Never Stops

This is a deafening place. The rigs hammer and clank, their engines turning over in massive subsonic concussions that you feel in your sternum before you hear them in your ears. Steam vents in shrieking exhalations on schedules that seem almost biological. Metal stresses against metal in long groaning chords. And beneath all of it, constant, oceanic: the sound of impossibly large fauna moving through impossibly large water.

Because the water is full of things. Abundant, even, in a place so otherwise barren. Vast pale shapes cruise beneath the surface, visible only as moving shadows or as the sudden displacement that rocks a rig on its legs for no visible reason. Some surface—armored, finned, ancient-looking despite the youth of the world they live in, evolved or arrived from somewhere else entirely, no one is certain which. Their calls are enormous, low-frequency moans that travel for miles through the water and up through the iron legs of the platforms, so that the workers feel the whale-songs (if that is what they are) in their boots before they hear them in the air. Smaller things swarm in the wakes of the harvesting ships—pack hunters, fast, silver, hundreds at a time, drawn by whatever the rigs disturb in the deep. Their feeding frenzies sound like the ocean boiling.

Between the animal noise and the machine noise, there is no silence here. Ever. People who work the rigs speak in a kind of permanent shout that becomes their normal voice, even on the rare visits home to quieter places, where they're told they sound like they're always angry. They aren't. They've just forgotten the volume of normal speech.

## Scale Without Reference

The scale here defeats memory. Stand on a rig's platform and look out: water in every direction, broken only by distant black columns of stone and the occasional, smaller-than-it-should-look silhouette of another rig, hazed by distance and the spray that never quite settles. The plateau this sea fills is continental—storms form within it that have no relationship to any weather beyond its rim, vast cold fronts that spin up out of nothing, dump freezing rain in walls so thick they look architectural, then dissipate, having affected no one and nothing because there's no one and nothing out here to affect except the rigs, which are built to take it.

The water itself seems to have its own gravity of attention—you find yourself watching it for hours, waiting for the next shadow, the next rolling back, the next exhalation of mist from something surfacing to breathe.

## What Doesn't Change

This place does not progress. The rigs have always been pumping, for as long as anyone keeps records, and they will be pumping for as long as anyone can imagine forward. The resource they draw never runs short and never quite explains itself. The fauna do not seem to grow scarcer or more numerous season to season—if there are seasons; the cold here is constant, the light "normal" in a flat, shadowless, slightly overcast way that doesn't seem to track any sun closely enough to call it day-and-night so much as light-and-slightly-less-light. The storms come, the storms go, the rock is exactly as sharp as it was the last time anyone looked closely at it.

There is something almost reassuring in this stasis, and something quietly horrifying in it too—a sense that this place found its equilibrium very early in its life and has simply elected to stay there, industrious and loud and utterly without development, forever. The workers who run the rigs are themselves the only built thing besides the rigs—small huts bolted to platform corners, a few rusted skiffs, a chapel-sized structure on one of the larger rigs that nobody quite admits is a chapel, faint chalk marks on interior walls that might be tallies, might be prayers, might be nothing.

Out past the rigs, far past where any vessel goes, the stone columns rise in greater clusters, and it is said—by the kind of people who say such things, half-shouting over the engine noise—that something built those clusters, shaped them, before the water came up to claim the plateau. No one has gone to check. The water is too deep, the cold too patient, and the things that surface to breathe are reportedly larger out there, where the rigs don't disturb them, and considerably less curious about the noise of engines than the noise of engines might prefer.
