# The Rising

There is no horizon here, only the place where dark stone stops being visible and dark sky begins. You learn the difference by the stars disappearing in patches, swallowed by silhouettes that should not be moving — but are.

This is a mountain range still being born. Not metaphorically: the peaks are *gaining elevation as you watch*. Somewhere below, in the lightless mantle of this place, something pushes — and the pushing has no schedule, no fault-line logic, no patience for geological time. A spire that was four hundred meters at dusk may be six hundred by what passes for morning. You hear it more than see it: deep concussive groans, the bass note of rock tearing from rock, settling into new angles with sounds like continents clearing their throats.

The materials are raw and unsoftened — no wind has had time to round them, no water has ever touched them to carve a valley or polish a face. Everything is edge. Obsidian-black basalt in sheets that fractured an hour ago and haven't finished falling. Pale mineral veins, exposed and still warm, running through cliff faces like nerves through dark meat. Here and there, glassy slag pools — not lava exactly, but something that was liquid recently and is now a frozen lake of black mirror, cracking slowly under its own residual heat with sounds like dry ice.

The air is warm, thick with mineral dust that never settles because the ground beneath it keeps moving enough to stir it back up. It smells of hot stone and something faintly sulfurous, though no obvious vents are visible — just a pervasive heat that seems to come from everywhere at once, radiating up through your feet.

## Scattered Marks

Across this turbulent immensity — and it is vast, ridgelines receding into the dark for what feels like a hundred kilometers — there are things that don't belong to geology. Rare, isolated, never explained: a low wall of fitted stone, three courses high, standing alone on a plateau that is itself only weeks old by the look of its raw edges. A single archway, freestanding, no structure attached, facing nothing in particular. These remnants predate the violence around them, somehow, or were placed *during* it by hands or processes unknown. They show no tool marks consistent with metal, no char, no decoration. They simply are — older than the upheaval, untouched by it, as though something built briefly here long before the mountains decided to rise, and then left, and now the land is trying to catch up to monuments that already existed.

No vegetation grows on this stone — not yet, not ever, perhaps. No lichen, no fungal bloom, nothing. The surfaces are too new, too hot, too unstable for anything to take hold. If this place has biology at all, it is geological biology: stone behaving like something almost alive, growing, fracturing, healing along new seams, exhaling heat like breath.

## The Quiet Between

For all its violence, this is a mostly quiet place, because the violence is intermittent and *enormous* — long stretches of near-silence (the faint tick of cooling slag, the dry whisper of dust sliding off a ledge) broken by sudden seismic events that can be heard or felt from kilometers away: a ridge collapsing into itself, a new spire shouldering up through an older one, the bell-tone ring of stone splitting clean.

There is no wildlife. There never will be, not for a long time — nothing could survive the instability, the heat, the way the ground itself cannot be trusted to remain ground. No tech, no devices, no light but what little starlight or ember-glow leaks through the dust. Darkness is the dominant condition; you navigate by sound, by heat-shimmer, by the felt vibration of the next upheaval gathering somewhere beneath your feet.

## What This Place Is

It is a range in the process of arriving — extreme elevation achieved not through age but through violence and immediacy, a turbulence so constant it amounts to a kind of weather, except the weather is the bedrock. The scattered ruins suggest this is not the first geological era to occupy this space, that something stood here in a calmer age, was perhaps buried, and the present convulsion is less creation than *exhumation* — the ground remembering an older shape and breaking everything trying to reach it.

The rule of this place, if it has one: nothing here is finished, and nothing here is safe to call permanent — not the peaks, not the silence, not even the dark, which occasionally cracks open with a seam of dull orange light from somewhere far below, gone again before you can be sure you saw it.
