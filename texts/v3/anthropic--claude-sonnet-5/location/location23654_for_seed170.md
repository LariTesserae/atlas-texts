# The Plateau of Standing Combustion

The land sits high—a great mineral table flung up into thin hot air, its edges lost in haze that might be horizon or might be the place where the table simply stops being calculable. The stone underfoot is old in the way that doesn't suggest age so much as *settlement*, a basalt-and-rust skin gone glassy in patches, cracked into plates the size of village squares, each plate slightly canted from its neighbors as though the ground had been laid down by hand and never quite finished agreeing with itself.

Nothing grows here. There is no green, no fiber, no spore-dust drifting—the world skipped that chapter, or finished it so long ago that even the memory weathered off. What's left is mineral honesty: oxide reds, the bruised purple of cooled slag, veins of pale crystal that catch the normal, unremarkable daylight and throw it back unremarkably, as if the light itself were embarrassed to be ordinary in a place like this.

And yet the place is not empty so much as *hollowed*—density is low, the air between things vast, distances overstated. You can stand on one plate and see for kilometers before your eye catches the next feature: a ridge, a slumped tower of stone, a column of heat-shimmer rising motionless. The openness amplifies everything else. Sound, especially.

---

Because this is the heart of it: the plateau roars. Not from wind—there is no wind to speak of, the air sits thick and heavy and heated, 2.7 toward scorching, pressing down like a held breath—but from *engines*. Enormous, unbuilt engines.

There are no walls here, no built structures by any human measure. No foundations, no rivets, no architecture. And yet tech sits high, industrial-grade, because the *land itself* runs like machinery. Geysers of compressed gas vent on schedules too regular to be geological—every forty minutes, exactly, a fissure a kilometer long exhales a column of orange vapor with the bass shudder of a furnace door. Stone gears, if you can call them that, turn without turning: slow rotational fractures spiral through certain plates, the cracks themselves advancing in oiled, ratcheted increments, as if something underneath the crust were being wound. Combustion happens in open bowls of rock—shallow craters where a blue-white fire burns low and continuous, fed by nothing visible, never spreading, never dying, just *running*, like a pilot light the size of a quarry.

None of it was built. All of it behaves as though it were.

---

The fauna is the part that breaks the register entirely.

For a world this dead, this scoured of biology, the *creatures*—and the word strains to hold them—are overwhelming in number and impossible in kind. They do not eat, evidently; there's nothing to eat. They do not seem to need water, of which there is nearly none. They are seen everywhere in this open scale, multiplying past any census: heat-colored things that resemble, at a glance, the shimmer-distortion above the fire-bowls, except the shimmer has a gait, has dozens of legs articulating out of thin air, has eyes that are just temperature gradients given the dignity of sockets. They pour across the plateau in slow herds that take hours to pass a single vantage point—slow, because the dynamic of this whole place is patient, unhurried, a world content to change over centuries rather than seconds. But while individually slow, collectively they are *uncountable*, a teeming that violates the emptiness around them, fauna-density and place-density disagreeing with each other in a way that should not resolve and does not try to.

Some of them are clearly mechanical, jointed in articulated chrome that shouldn't have survived the heat, walking with the unbothered rhythm of factory pistons, leaving no tracks. Some of them are clearly *not* mechanical, fleshless but somehow muscled, made of the same red stone as the ground, peeling up out of the plates and reforming a body before your eyes, indifferent to the distinction between geology and organism. Some of them are both, seamlessly, a sheet-metal flank fused without seam into a ribcage of obsidian.

None of them acknowledge each other. None of them acknowledge you, particularly, though sometimes one will pause near you for an uncomfortably long while, vapor breathing in slow rasps, gear-clicks issuing from somewhere behind a face that has no mouth.

---

This is a place where the rules have started disagreeing with each other and decided not to resolve it. Shadows fall toward more than one sun's worth of source, though there is, observably, one star in the sky—small, white, indifferent. Distances measured by eye and distances measured by walking do not match; a ridge that looks an hour off takes four, or fifteen minutes, with no pattern discernible across repeated tries. The fire-bowls burn the same flame whether you stand near for a second or a year, but the *gear-cracks* in the stone—the slow ratcheting fractures—seem to remember you, taking subtly different routes around a place you've stood before, as if avoiding rather than ignoring.

There is industry without industrialists. Machinery without makers. A factory's worth of process scaled across a whole geological province, running on schedules and feeding outputs nobody collects, watched over by a fauna that outnumbers any conceivable food chain, on a high dry plateau that has clearly stood like this—weathered, established, *settled into its wrongness*—for a very, very long time, slow and unbothered, as if reality broke here gradually, the way stone cracks: not all at once, but plate by plate, over centuries, until cracking simply became how the place breathed.

The heat never breaks. The roar never quite resolves into a single source. The herds keep crossing, unhurried, toward a far edge of the table that may or may not exist when they get there.
