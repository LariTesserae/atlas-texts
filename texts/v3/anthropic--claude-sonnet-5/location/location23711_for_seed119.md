The place is called, by the few who have a word for it at all, the Cistern-Hold, though no one living built it and no one living understands its plumbing.

It is small — the size of a hamlet, no more, bounded on every side by walls that curve away before you can be sure they're walls. The boundary is absolute. Nothing here is stone-that-was-always-stone or earth-that-was-always-earth. Every surface has been shaped: floors of fitted brick laid in spiral courses, channels cut to gradients too precise for the crude tools that seem to have made them, basins sunk into the ground like sockets for eyes that were never installed. The construction is primitive in its means — you can see the adze-marks, the chisel-stutter, the places where a tool slipped — but total in its extent. There is no dirt here that isn't actually crushed and leveled fill. No rock that isn't a cut block. The builders, whoever or whatever they were, finished the job and then either died or left, long enough ago that erosion has had its say: corners rounded, steps dished in their centers, mortar lines softened to seams of moss-dark shadow. The place has settled. It is not ruined so much as worn into agreement with time.

Water moves through everything. Not flooding, not drowning — a managed wetness, as if the whole structure were designed as an organ for water rather than a shelter from it. It seeps up through floor-joints and stands in the dished basins. It runs in the carved channels with a sound like conversation held just out of comprehension. The walls weep where mineral-stained water finds old fracture-lines, leaving tracks the color of weak tea. The air condenses on every cool metal fitting (and there are fittings — strange small bronze or copper findings set into the brick at regular intervals, function unknown, too deliberate to be ornament, too sparse to be structural). Underfoot is often a thin gloss of standing water reflecting the dim light back up at you, doubling the few shapes that exist into uncertain pairs.

Vegetation is sparse to the point of punctuation rather than presence — a fringe of reed-like growth at channel edges, pale lichen colonizing the worn high points of walls, one or two stubborn shrubs rooted in soil-filled cracks where the brickwork has given up a few inches of itself. Nothing crowds. Nothing canopies. The green that exists reads as commentary on the built surface rather than a competing ecology.

The light is dim, indirect — there is no clear sense of an open sky, though it isn't fully enclosed either; perhaps a high lattice of corbelled stone filters daylight into something perpetually like late afternoon under cloud, gray-gold and shadowless. It might be underground. It might simply be a place light has trouble finding, due to the geometry of its own walls bending away from any direct path.

And into this small, empty, ancient, wet, dim architecture comes an enormous amount of life and noise.

The fauna is abundant — startlingly so, given how little vegetation could support it. Things move through the channels: pale eel-bodied creatures with too many fins, navigating by some sense other than sight, slapping audibly at the water's surface. Things nest in the high lattice-gaps: colonies of small flighted creatures, leather-winged, whose calls ricochet down through the structure as a continuous percussive clatter, layered over the lower croak and boom of unseen amphibian things hidden in the basins. Things scuttle along the dished steps — chitinous, fast, numerous, indifferent to the human-scaled paths because the paths were not built with anything currently living in mind. The density of objects is near-zero; the density of presences, audible and glimpsed, is high. You are nearly always alone in a room full of sound.

That's the strangeness of it, really — not any single uncanny object but the mismatch: a structure this total, this deliberate, this finished, standing nearly empty of stuff and yet roaring with creatures that clearly belong to it now more than any builder ever did. The ecology has moved in entire, complete, self-sustaining, indifferent to the architecture's original purpose, using the channels as rivers, the basins as ponds, the wall-niches as roosts. The Cistern-Hold functions perfectly as a water-driven habitat. It may never have been intended as one.

The rules of the place, such as they can be inferred:

Water always finds the lowest channel and that channel is always clear, even when everything else is overgrown with lichen or littered with shed exoskeletons — something maintains the water's path, though nothing maintains anything else.

The bronze fittings hum, faintly, on no schedule anyone has caught — a low resonance felt in the teeth before it's heard, and in the seconds after, the eel-bodied things in the nearest channel go still.

Sound carries unnaturally far and unnaturally clearly along the curved walls, so that a footstep two rooms away arrives as if from beside you, while a shout from arm's length can vanish into the lattice-noise entirely. Direction is unreliable. Distance is unreliable. Only the water's path is reliable.

Nothing here decays the way it should. The wood-handled tools left wedged in old brick-gaps (evidence of repairs attempted generations ago, themselves now ancient) have softened and grayed but not rotted away. The place is established — long past its making, fully reconciled to age — but it has stopped, somehow, at a particular stage of wear and gone no further, like erosion itself grew tired partway through the job.

To walk through it is to be steadily, continuously outnumbered: by sound, by unseen life, by the accumulated evidence of intention you cannot reconstruct. The hamlet-sized emptiness of the place is not peace. It is the kind of emptiness that has simply been thoroughly inherited by something other than whoever meant to live there.
