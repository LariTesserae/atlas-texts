# The Cistern at Mörnsedde Pocket

You enter through a throat of wet basalt, recently torn—the rock still has the unhealed look of stone that hasn't finished deciding what shape it wants. The opening is barely a meter across. What lies beyond it is not.

The pocket is a single chamber, no larger than a cathedral nave, but it holds an ocean. Not metaphorically. Water fills nine-tenths of the volume, and you are inside it, breathing it, the way you might breathe air, because something here has made that arrangement of facts negotiable. The water is the temperature of a held breath, cool but not cold, and it is *full*—threaded through with cabling-thick strands of pale infrastructure that rise from the floor like reeds grown out of pipework, packed so densely that there is no sightline longer than four meters in any direction.

These stalks are the only vegetation, if that's the word: sparse in species, but each individual is wrapped, root to crown, in a kind of conduit-sheath, translucent, pulsing with traveling beads of light that are probably information and possibly blood. They don't sway. The water moves in slow gyres around them, but they hold their geometry like they've been told to.

## What Was Built Here

Someone settled this submerged room. Not colonized it—*settled* it, the way you settle a town, with the layered, accreted, half-organized look of infrastructure built in shifts by people who didn't all agree on the plan. Platforms hang at odd depths, connected by stairs that climb at angles water shouldn't permit stairs to hold. There are doorframes with no walls. There are walls with no rooms. Lanterns—if that's what they are—burn underwater without producing bubbles, throwing a clean, even, almost clinical light that makes the whole packed chamber visible at once in long diffuse shafts, like light through cathedral glass that has forgotten what color is.

The construction is young. Edges are sharp, unworn, the basalt still showing tool-marks and the metal still showing weld-seams. Nothing here has had time to erode. And yet it is *full*—every cubic meter claimed by something: pipe, platform, stalk, strand, scaffold, machine. There is no negative space. The density is the first thing your body understands, before your mind catches up: you are never more than an arm's length from a presence.

## The Engineering

The tech is not modern. It is the kind of built thing that makes you suspect the builders solved problems you didn't know were open. The water itself behaves with a logic that isn't quite fluid dynamics—it holds shapes for a half-second longer than it should before collapsing, like it's rendering at a slightly delayed frame rate. Threads of light run through it independent of the conduit-stalks, threading between platforms in straight lines that ignore the water's currents entirely, as if a second, drier geometry were laid invisibly over the wet one.

Machines breathe in the walls. You can feel them more than see them: a slow systolic compression of the chamber's outer boundary, maybe every forty seconds, that pushes a pressure-wave through the packed water and makes every conduit-stalk shiver in unison, a held chord struck once and let ring.

## Sound

It is loud—not chaotic, but *full*, the way the space is full. A bass hum sits underneath everything, the chamber's own resonant frequency, maybe the breathing of those wall-machines. Over it: clicks, arriving in clusters, like distant valves. A high continuous tone, almost a singing, that seems to come from the lanterns themselves. And periodically, every few minutes, a deep structural groan that travels through the basalt and into your sternum—the room settling, or growing, or both. Nothing here is silent. The water carries sound with an eerie fidelity, so a click two platforms over arrives as crisp as one beside your ear, robbing you of any reliable sense of distance.

## What Lives, What Doesn't

No animal life. None. Not fish, not the smallest drifting thing. The water is too purposeful for that—every motion in it traceable to a mechanism, a current with a job. This absence is conspicuous in a way that makes the place feel less like an ecosystem and more like a sealed instrument, built and inhabited, perhaps, but not by anything that needs to eat.

And yet it does not feel dead. The dynamism is constant: the systolic pulse of the walls, the gyring water, the traveling light-beads in the stalks, platforms occasionally re-angling themselves by degrees over the course of an hour, lanterns dimming and flaring in patterns that might be a language or might be a tide. Things are *happening*, continuously, urgently, without any organism to explain why.

## The Strangeness

It accumulates the longer you stay. The stairs that hold their angle against the water. The dry geometry threaded through the wet one. The way sound arrives without distance. The unworn sharpness of everything, as if the whole chamber surfaced—or was built—only days ago, in a place with no sky to surface into.

There are no windows, but there are places where the basalt thins to a dark, glassy membrane, and through it, you can see more light. Not above. *Beside*. As if this packed, submerged, settled, instrumented pocket is only one room in a structure with many, each one its own contained ocean, hidden at this scale, multiplying outward into a darkness that has no use for elevation, because down here, *down* and *outward* might be the same direction.

The rules, as far as you can infer them: water is the medium, not the obstacle. Density is not crowding but architecture. Nothing ages quickly here, and nothing arrived by growing—it arrived by being placed, sharp-edged and recent, into a fullness that was prepared in advance to receive it. You are, you begin to suspect, inside a single chamber of something built at a scale that has no name yet, and the smallness of this room is the smallness of a single cell in a body too large to see from anywhere you'll ever stand.
