# The Canopy Terraces of Vael Mourn

The road that brought you here stopped being a road forty switchbacks ago. What carried you up the last thousand meters was wood — not planks but living wood, growth-trained over centuries into ramps and causeways, the bark worn to a polish by feet and cart-wheels and something else that left no footprints but a faint mineral smear.

You are high here. High enough that the air thins and carries a coolness that never quite becomes cold, high enough that clouds pass *through* the place rather than over it, snagging on the canopy-towers and dissolving into a mist that beads on every leaf-surface and then is gone before it can be called rain. This is the dampness of the place — not weather, but breath. The vegetation exhales water it never asked for, and that water survives for minutes at a time before the dry high wind reclaims it.

## The Substrate

There is no ground. Or rather: the ground gave up being soil an unknowable age ago and became wood, vine, root, and trunk instead, layered so thick and for so long that it achieved its own geology. Erosion here did not wear stone down — it wore *growth* down, smoothing the great trunk-ridges into something like ancient hills, rounding the cathedral-root buttresses into slopes you can walk without climbing. This is the oldest stable form of a forest: not a young tangle but a settled empire, every conflict between species resolved generations ago into a kind of treaty written in cambium and shade-tolerance.

The town — because there is a town, sprawling and open across maybe four kilometers of canopy-terrain — was not built *in* the trees. It was built *of* them, and *into* them, by people who at some point stopped distinguishing between carpentry and horticulture. Buildings are grown-and-grafted: living trunks serve as load-bearing columns still slowly thickening, vined facades are pruned into windows, roofs are broad fused leaves the size of sailcloth, kept alive and replaced leaf-by-leaf rather than re-shingled. Streets are root-causeways, wide and flat, smoothed by ten thousand years of use into something almost like cobblestone, except it's all one continuous fused organism underfoot.

## The Machines Among the Leaves

This is where the place stops being merely beautiful and starts being wrong, productively wrong.

Because grown alongside all this — threaded through it, married to it — is industry. Iron flues rise out of hollowed trunks like trees growing their own chimneys. Belt-driven mills are housed in vine-wrapped barns, their gears turning on bearings of fossilized resin instead of grease. Pistons fire somewhere unseen, and you feel the thrum through the root-road under your feet before you hear it — a deep mechanical pulse competing with the slower pulse of sap and structure. Electric light strung on living cable-vines flickers at dusk, the bulbs themselves grown as bulbous fruit-pods that some lost art taught these people to harvest and wire.

The noise is constant and layered: engines somewhere churning out a low industrial growl, the high creak and groan of trunks shifting in wind, the chatter and clang of unseen workshops, distant bell-tones that might be a foundry or might be something calling across the canopy in a language with a rhythm but no words. It is loud here in the way a city is loud — many processes overlapping, none of them quiet, none of them ceasing.

## The Wrongness

The weirdness sits in the *scale relationships*. A door is sometimes ordinary-sized and sometimes three times too tall, grown that way deliberately or accidentally, no one quite remembers which, and no one fixes it. Staircases spiral up through the inside of a trunk and emerge somewhere that, by any reasonable accounting of the trunk's height, should not exist — extra floors implied by the canopy's outside silhouette that the inside cannot account for. Some say the place was grown around an argument between two kinds of time.

Maps of the town disagree with each other in ways too consistent to be cartographic error — like the settlement itself is mid-revision, still finalizing where its districts actually are. Twice a day, usually around the cooling of dusk, certain causeways seem to lead to a square that wasn't on your earlier walk, and back again by morning. Locals don't remark on this. There are protocols: chalk marks renewed nightly on doorframes, certain bells rung at certain hours not for time-keeping but for *correction*, like the maintenance of a machine whose drift must be constantly compensated for. You get the sense the town's engineers consider this an infrastructure problem, filed somewhere between drainage and load-bearing capacity.

## Ecology, Thinned

Despite all this living mass, animal life is scarce. The vegetation has won so totally and so long ago that it doesn't need fauna for pollination or distribution anymore — it has found other means, slower, internal, vine-to-vine, spore-on-wind. What animals remain are small and infrequent: a stilt-legged grey bird that walks the causeway-rails at dawn and is gone by mid-morning; something pale that lives entirely inside hollow trunk-walls and is heard more than seen, a soft thumping like a second heartbeat in the wood; insects so well camouflaged as bark-flecks that you only notice them when they move, and then only at the edge of vision. The scarcity makes their appearances feel like punctuation in a long, plant-thick sentence — a comma of motion in all that green stillness.

## Open and Sparse, Despite Everything

For all the density of growth and the busyness of the machinery, the *town itself* is spread thin. Districts are separated by long stretches of unworked canopy-forest, root-causeways running for a kilometer between settlement-clusters with nothing but moss-dripping trunks and the cool mist on either side. You can stand in the market square — itself only moderately populated, voices carrying oddly far in the damp air — and within a ten-minute walk find yourself entirely alone among groaning trunks, the industrial clamor reduced to a distant rumor, the only company that strange two-toned thumping from inside the wood.

This sparseness is part of the order of things, not an absence of plan. The original builders — if "original" means anything in a place that revises its own floor plans — seem to have valued the silence between settlements as much as the settlements themselves. Space is held in reserve. Whether it's held in reserve for growth, for ritual, or for something the town's chalk-and-bell maintenance crews are quietly managing, no one currently living seems entirely sure.

## At Dusk

The light is ordinary, even gentle, filtering down gold-grey through layers of canopy that never quite let it through clean — normal daylight, but always a few degrees diffused, like the whole town lives under a very high, very thin lampshade. At dusk this softens further, the industrial lamps take over, fruit-bulbs glowing amber along the root-streets, and the temperature drops the last cool degree into something that asks for a coat.

This is when the bells start. This is when the chalk gets renewed. This is when, if you're new here, someone will take your arm gently and walk you back toward the district you started in — not because you're in danger, exactly, but because the town, at dusk, is doing maintenance on itself, and it would rather you not be standing in a hallway when it decides where that hallway goes.
