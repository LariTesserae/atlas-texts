# The Underroot Sky

There is no ceiling, though you are below the surface of the world. That is the first thing the mind refuses to accept, and the second thing it accepts completely, because the light makes the refusal pointless.

Call it a cavern if you need a word, but the word fails almost immediately. The space goes down — or in, or sideways, depending on which gravity-feeling your inner ear has settled on this hour, because gravity here is not quite a fixed argument — for distances that produce weather. Real weather: pressure fronts, cold fog banks that form in the deep distances and roll for kilometers before dissolving, faint thermal columns that you can watch as visible shimmer rising past plant-mass so dense it reads less as forest than as continent. The sky-that-is-not-sky glows with a bright, shadowless, sourceless light — not sun, not flame, something more like the inside of a pearl scaled up past sanity, a luminance that seems to live *in* the chlorophyll itself, or in spore-haze suspended permanently in the upper air, so that there's no true horizon, only a soft white blaze that the highest canopy-spires fade into.

## Substrate

Vegetation is not a feature of this place. Vegetation *is* the place. The ground, where there is ground, is root — root grown thick and tiered and old-young, fresh ruptures of pale new growth shouldering through root-mats that are themselves only decades deep, because everything here is recently arrived. The erosion is young: cliffs of packed root-soil show sharp shear-lines where whole shelves have calved away within living memory, raw amber wood-flesh exposed and already being recolonized by hair-fine white tendrils. Nothing has had time to round its edges. Boulders, if you find stone at all, sit cracked and angular, half-swallowed by bark that grew around them in restless decades.

Trunks the width of districts rise and vanish into the glow, not as individual trees but as a kind of skeletal civilization — their bark fissured, sap-bled, hung with vine-causeways that themselves have grown other vines, a recursion of plant-on-plant that produces architecture without architects: natural balconies, accidental amphitheaters, hollowed boles big enough to lose a settlement in. The understory, where there is room to call it that, is thin and clear in patches — long, eerie clearings of bare root-floor stretching far enough that you feel the emptiness more than the abundance, an unsettling void at ground level under a sky-ceiling of total green-gold mass.

## Damp, Not Wet

Water exists here as rumor and incident rather than rule. No rivers, no standing lakes — but the canopy condenses its own atmosphere, and so moisture happens in pockets: a hollow stump that's filled by years of drip, a fold in a root-shelf glazed with permanent dew, fog that gathers in the deep cold troughs near the floor and burns off — or rather, *brightens off* — as the high light strengthens through the day-cycle. The cold keeps everything faintly damp rather than wet; condensation beads on root-bark and never quite dries, never quite drips. You can survive on what you lick off your own gathered leaves if you're patient. The plants do most of the same, drinking the air.

## What Moves

Fauna is rare enough that an encounter feels like an event worth marking on a wall. Something pale and many-legged that lives entirely inside one trunk-system, never surfacing. Birds — if they are birds — that are heard far more than seen, single calls crossing kilometers of canopy and arriving thinned to almost nothing, a single struck-glass note swallowed by scale. Occasionally something larger disturbs a whole hanging vine-causeway and you watch the tremor travel a quarter mile without ever seeing the cause.

The quiet is not silence. It is the *mostly*-quiet of a vast pressurized space: the constant ultra-low groan of trunks settling against each other, sap moving in volumes that produce sound, distant percussive cracks as new growth splits old wood, the white-noise hiss of air moving through countless billion leaves that never quite stops, never quite rises to the level of attention either.

## Turbulence

Despite the quiet, despite the emptiness between things, this is not a stable place. The dynamism here is structural and total: growth is visibly, audibly fast in geological terms, meaning that in *human* terms whole sections of canopy can fail in an afternoon. A trunk-system that has stood through a dozen generations of whatever lives here may simply give way — root-locks releasing, sap pressure undoing itself catastrophically — and bring down a hectare of green-gold mass in a roar that takes minutes to finish falling and weeks to be reclaimed by new shoots erupting through the wreckage. The plant-mass is at war with its own weight, perpetually overbuilding, perpetually collapsing, perpetually regrowing through the wound. It is bazaar-energy translated into botany: nothing here is finished, nothing is at rest, and the apparent eternal-forest stillness is a trick of scale — you are simply too small and too brief to watch the whole thing churn.

## Scattered Hands

People — or something like people — have been here, lightly. Not built so much as *placed*: a ladder of lashed vine-rungs ascending one trunk into the haze, decades old, half-grown-over. A ring of fire-blackened stones in one of the bare clearings, cold for longer than seems possible given how little time anything here has had. A single woven shelter wedged into a fork eighty meters up, abandoned, its lashings simple enough to have been tied with hands and teeth and nothing sharper than stone or bone. The tech ceiling is low — knots, fire, simple cutting edges, woven fiber — but the marks are *everywhere and nowhere*, scattered thin across distances that make each one feel like a flag planted on a continent. Whoever or whatever made them did not stay. Maybe could not. The place doesn't punish presence, but it doesn't accommodate it either; it simply keeps growing, keeps falling, keeps filling its own enormous bright hollow with more of itself, faster than anything could ever build against it.

## The Unusual Part

It is the light that unsettles longest. Below the world, there should be black. Instead there is this shadowless bright nothing-source pearl-glow, strong enough to cast weak diffuse shadows beneath the heaviest canopy-shelves but never strong enough to locate — no glare, no squint, no single direction to shield your eyes from. Some who've spent long enough here start to believe the plants themselves are the source: that enough chlorophyll, grown thick enough, over enough centuries-that-are-actually-decades, begins to give back more light than it takes in. No one has found the actual sun substitute, if one exists. Maybe there isn't one. Maybe the glow is just what becomes of green, multiplied past every reasonable limit, with nowhere left to put all that borrowed brightness but back out into the damp, cold, empty, roaring, still-growing dark.
