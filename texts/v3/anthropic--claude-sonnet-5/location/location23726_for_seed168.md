# The Standing Plateau

The land rises before it has finished deciding what it will be. This is the first thing you notice — not the height itself, though the height is considerable, a tableland thrust up into thin bright air, but the unfinishedness of the rising. The rock here has not yet learned the patience of erosion. There are no rounded shoulders, no wind-combed dunes of scree, no millennia of water teaching stone where to be soft. Instead: raw basalt shelves stacked at the angles they cooled in, fissures still sharp-lipped as if cut yesterday, mineral veins of green and rust-orange running through grey stone like something still settling into its final arrangement. The plateau looks less like a place that was made and more like a place still in the middle of happening, except that it has been this way, unchanging, for longer than memory.

And yet — green. Improbable quantities of it. Vegetation spills across the broken shelves in defiance of the youngness of the ground beneath it: long-bladed grasses the color of unripe wheat, low shrubs with waxy dark leaves that hold a slight oily sheen, things that might be ferns unrolling from cracks where no soil should logically exist. The plants do not seem to need the slow patient soil that erosion usually grinds out of mountains. They have simply arrived, fully committed, into a landscape too new for them, and persisted. There is no animal sound moving through any of it. No bird crosses the pale warm sky. No insect-hum rises from the grass-shelves. The lushness is total and total silent — a green that has never been eaten, never been walked through, never been disturbed by anything with a pulse.

The scale opens outward and outward: this is not a single outcrop but a whole high country, shelf after shelf of plateau receding toward a horizon where more rises, blue-grey, into haze. The air up here is warm in the unbothered way of altitude that has not yet learned to be cold — a temperate plate suspended above whatever lies below, which you cannot see, because the plateau's edges fall away into a haze that swallows detail. Light falls evenly, neither harsh nor dim, a clean and ordinary daylight that seems almost wasted on a place this strange.

Scattered across the open shelves, with great distances between them — sometimes a kilometer, sometimes more — stand the machines.

They are not ruins. They are not occupied. They simply *are*, placed at intervals too regular to be accidental and too sparse to suggest any settlement. Towers of riveted iron, vented at intervals, some trailing pipe-work that runs along the ground for a hundred meters before terminating in nothing — no tank, no other structure, just a capped end sitting in the grass. Squat cylindrical drums with the look of boilers, cold to the touch, their gauge-faces frosted with a fine patina that is not quite rust, not quite anything terrestrial chemistry would produce so fast on metal this seemingly recent. Long horizontal frames holding rows of toothed wheels, motionless, interlocked, as though waiting for a single hand to turn the first one — and never receiving it.

There is no path between them. No rail, no road, no wire. Whatever logic connects one machine to the next is not present at ground level; perhaps it was never built, or perhaps it operates on a principle that doesn't require physical connection at all. They do not hum. They do not vent steam or smoke. They are switched off, or perhaps switched *on* in some permanent, currentless way — industrial in form and silent in function, like engines built for a labor that has not yet been assigned to them, or was finished so long ago that the work itself has been forgotten while the tools remain, patient, unweathered, waiting in the grass.

The emptiness between these objects is the loudest feature of the place. Density here is almost nothing — you can stand on one shelf, see a tower a kilometer off, a boiler-drum further still, and between you and them nothing but grass moving faintly though you feel no wind. The air seems to swallow sound preemptively; even your own footsteps on stone arrive at your ears strangely muffled, as if the plateau prefers its quiet kept.

Nothing here decays. The machines do not rust further than whatever fixed degree they arrived at. The rock does not erode past its raw, new-cut angles. The grass does not grow taller or die back into autumn — there is no autumn, no observable season at all, only this one warm suspended state, held perfectly level, as though the entire plateau were a single frame extracted from a much longer motion and left running on a loop too subtle to notice.

There is a feeling, walking here, that you have arrived either before the story started or long after it concluded, and that the geology has not been informed which.

There are no rules posted, no warnings, no paths worn by other visitors — there have been no other visitors, evidently, ever — but the place enforces a few unspoken laws regardless: nothing you find will be warm to the touch except the rock itself, baked faintly by the sun. Nothing mechanical will respond to your hands. The green will not catch fire, should you try, and you will find — though you cannot say how you know it — that you should not try. The horizon will always show one more shelf, rising slightly higher, holding one more distant tower, for as far as you are willing to walk. The plateau does not end. It only continues to almost-finish, forever, gently, in the warm and ordinary light.
