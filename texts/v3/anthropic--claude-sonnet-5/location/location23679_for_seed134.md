A Drowned Cistern at High Altitude

You reach it by going up, which is the first wrongness, because everything about the place insists you should be descending into it. The land climbs in raw, unweathered shelves of dark stone — basalt-black, glassy in fracture, still showing the combed ridges where it cooled — until the slope simply stops being slope and becomes lake. There is no shore in the ordinary sense. The water has flooded a high, young hollow scraped into existence so recently that it hasn't decided yet whether to be a crater, a cirque, or a wound. Vegetation never arrived. Nothing green has had time, or permission, or inclination. The rock is bare the way a fresh cut is bare.

The basin is small — you could walk its rim, such as it is, in under an hour — but it is so densely inhabited that the smallness becomes irrelevant. Density here doesn't spread; it stacks. The water itself is nearly full, 2.9 of however-much-water-can-be, a held-breath quantity, brimming past where a lake should logically crest, lapping a few centimeters over old high-water terraces as if the basin is still deciding how much of itself to keep.

Cool air sits over cool water — not cold, not warm, a held middle temperature, like the place is being kept at a deliberate setting. Light is ordinary, full daylight, unfiltered, which makes everything that follows worse, because there's nowhere for the strangeness to hide in shadow. It's all right there, lit plainly.

The Engines

There is no built structure here — nothing was constructed, no wall or platform or foundation — and yet the place runs like a factory floor. Tech without architecture. Sound without source. From beneath the water comes the unmistakable thrum of machinery: deep pistoning vibrations, rhythmic clanks, the whine of something turning at sustained RPM, the occasional pressurized hiss breaking the surface in a domed boil of foam. None of it has a visible engine. None of it has a housing. The mechanism, whatever it is, has no casing — as though industry itself condensed directly into this water the way frost condenses onto cold glass, skipping the step where someone builds the thing that does the work.

The sound is loud, genuinely loud, loud enough that conversation at the rim requires raising your voice, an unbroken industrial chorus layered over the slosh and suck of the lake against black rock. It doesn't vary much. It is a slow place at heart — the changes here happen over months, years, geologic patience — but the sound fools you into thinking something is urgently, continuously happening, when really the same four-stroke rhythm has been repeating since before you arrived and will repeat long after.

The Population

This is where density earns itself. The water is thick with bodies — not schools, not herds, but an abundance that has no clean taxonomy. Things that pulse with the cadence of the hidden machinery, as if their physiology runs on the same hidden cycle. Long segmented forms that surface in arcs and vanish without splash, leaving rings that don't match their size. Clusters near the rim that might be one clustered organism or many separate ones holding position against a current that shouldn't exist in a still-looking lake. Pale shapes deeper down, lit by nothing, visible anyway — a glow that the eye supplies more than the water does.

None of them touch vegetation because there is none to touch. None of them seem to eat anything visible. The ecology, such as it is, seems to metabolize the sound itself, or the heat bleeding up from the engine-thrum, the way certain deep vents feed things that have never seen the sun. Weirdness here isn't decorative — it's structural. The fauna behave like components. They surface on schedule, submerge on schedule, and the schedule matches the machine-rhythm a half-beat off, like a second hand chasing a minute hand it can never quite catch.

The Rule

If this place has a law, it's this: industry preceded life, and life arrived to inhabit the industry rather than the other way around. Nothing was built, in the sense of intention and hands and design — but something is undeniably running, has been running since the rock itself was still cooling, and the abundant strange fauna are not intruders on a machine. They are its current output, its product, continuously extruded from process into population. The lake doesn't sit in the crater. The lake is the byproduct chamber, and it has been brimming, slow and patient, for exactly as long as the engine beneath it has been turning over.

You don't get used to the sound. You get used to standing in it.
