# The Drowned Lattice

There is no surface here, no horizon where water meets air, because the air gave up long ago. What remains is a single substance — cold, dark, and total — through which the old structures rise like the ribs of something that died standing up.

Call it a city, though that word implies streets and the comings of people, and there have been no people here for longer than memory holds shape. What it is, is a vast and ancient lattice of worked stone, fitted timber gone to black sponge, hand-cut channels, and joints lashed once with cord that rotted into ghost-fibers still tracing the angles where it used to bind. Everything here was made. There is no original ground beneath it — only more building, descending past where any light could ever follow, terrace under terrace, foundation under foundation, until the structure simply becomes the bedrock it forgot it wasn't.

## Terrain

The elevation is low and the land — if it can be called land — runs nearly flat, but flatness here means something particular: it is the flatness of a thing erased by time rather than built level. Walls have slumped into ramps. Stairs have softened into shelves. What were once distinct structures — granaries, terraces, retaining walls, the low stone houses of people who shaped rock and rope and little else — have settled into one another over uncountable slow seasons of seep and silt, until the whole territory reads as a single continuous fabric of worked material, folded and refolded by its own weight and the patient grinding of water that never stopped moving, even when it seemed to.

This erosion is not violent. Nothing tore this place apart. It simply persisted, cycle after cycle, until every sharp thing surrendered its edges, every corner rounded into a kind of soft inevitability, every gap silted closed. The world reached what it was always reaching for. This is its final shape, or as final as shapes get.

## Water and Light

The water is everything — not a feature of the landscape but its medium, the substance the entire place is suspended in and built for. It moves with a slowness that has its own kind of weather: vast cold currents that arrive over the span of what would be, in air, a season, pressing gently against the lattice, sliding around stone teeth and through doorways that have long since lost their doors. There are tides here too, if tides is the word — pressure-breaths that swell and ease across cycles too long for any single vantage to perceive as motion at all. You only know they happened by how the silt has settled afterward, in new soft drifts against the windward faces of walls.

Darkness is total, or nearly — light arrives only as suggestion, a deep bruise-blue filtering down from somewhere so far above it might as well be theoretical, just enough to separate one shade of black from another, just enough that the eye, given time, can resolve the line where a wall stops being wall and starts being silhouette. There is no daylight cycle to speak of, only a glow that brightens by some imperceptible degree and dims again, slower than breath, slower than thought.

## Vegetation, dense and lightless

And yet it is lush — astonishingly so, given the dark. The vegetation here is not the vegetation of sun: it is pale, frond-soft, anemic in color but not in vigor, growing in such density that it has become a second architecture layered atop the first. Long ribbon-growths drift from every available ledge, their slow sway the only consistent motion in this place, stirred not by wind but by the water's own creeping circulation. Thick mats of something like moss-coral cushion the corners of every room, softening the worked angles further, blurring stone into garden into stone again. In places the growth is so dense it has begun to take on structural duties of its own — bridging gaps where timber has dissolved, webbing doorways shut, holding up a lintel that the original makers never intended to need help.

This vegetation needs no animal life to thrive, and finds none — there is nothing here that moves with intention, no fish, no crawling thing, no glint of an eye catching what little light exists. The absence is not eerie so much as settled, as if the ecology decided long ago that motion was unnecessary, that growing slowly toward nothing in particular was enough.

## Made, not grown — and yet

Despite the lushness, nothing here is wild. The built parameter governs absolutely: every wall, every channel, every terrace-step was placed by hands using simple tools — stone adzes, fire-hardened stakes, the kind of patient pre-metal craft that takes generations to finish a single retaining wall and considers that a reasonable use of generations. You can still find, if you drift close enough and let your eyes adjust, the toolmarks: parallel scrape-lines on a sill, the slight asymmetry of a doorway cut by eye rather than instrument, a drainage channel angled with a cleverness that required no calculation, only an old and exact intimacy with how water behaves when it's allowed to choose its own way down.

There is no metal to speak of, or what little there might have been has long since given itself back to the water as rust-stain and ghost-shape. This was a hand-tool civilization, and it built at a scale that belongs to civilizations of much greater means — vast terracing, kilometers of channel-work, structures whose true extent the dark refuses to reveal all at once. It seems to have taken them — whoever they were — a very long time, working very slowly, content with that slowness, and then it seems they simply stopped, or were stopped, and the water did the rest.

## Sound, and its absence

It is mostly quiet, but not silent — quiet has a texture down here, made of small constant events: the faint percussive tick of silt settling somewhere far off, a structural groan too low to really call a sound, registering more as pressure against the chest than vibration in the ear. Occasionally a held breath of current will stir the ribbon-growths into a long, soft hiss against stone, like a vast and very slow exhale, and then it passes, and the quiet folds back in.

## What it asks of you

There is a rule, unstated but absolute, that governs any presence moving through this place: patience. Nothing here rewards speed. The currents move on their own schedule and will not be hurried. The dark will not yield more than it's ready to yield no matter how close you press toward any wall. The growth, if disturbed, takes its own slow time deciding whether to resettle or to drift, indefinitely, in some new configuration.

This is a finished world — not dead, exactly, since the vegetation still grows and the water still moves and the silt still settles in its long unhurried arithmetic, but finished in the way that a sentence is finished: nothing more is going to be added to it. It found its shape. It is cold, and dark, and slow, and vast beyond any one vantage's capacity to hold, and it was built, every last terrace and channel of it, by hands that are gone now, with nothing left to mark their going except the exactness of the angles they left behind, slowly losing their exactness, slowly becoming something softer, something closer to the water that swallowed them, something that, given enough more time — and time is the one thing this place still has in abundance — will eventually be only water, and even that will take a very, very long while to finish becoming.
