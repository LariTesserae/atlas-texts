# The Cistern at Anvete's Spine

It is not large. You could walk its full circumference in perhaps four minutes, if the footing allowed for walking rather than the careful, weight-shifting crouch the place demands. But scale here is a lie the architecture tells about itself — a single chamber, sunk into the high ridge-rock at the place the old surveys called Anvete's Spine, that contains more event per cubic meter than most cities manage across their whole sprawl.

You reach it by climbing. The ridge itself sits high and bare, a knuckle of warm stone with almost nothing growing on it — a few wind-flattened mats of grey-green lichen-analog clinging in the lee of larger structures, the occasional spike of some bristled, leathery plant that has rooted itself in a crack and decided that's enough world for one lifetime. The air at this elevation has the thinness and clarity of altitude but none of the cold you'd expect — it sits warm against the skin, humid, almost feverish, like breath held too long in a cupped hand.

Everything here is made. There is no bedrock that isn't also infrastructure; no soil that isn't substrate laid down on purpose centuries or millennia ago and since weathered into the appearance of geology. The cistern's walls are a fused ceramic-metal composite, the color of old amber going dark, and they have been here long enough to have settled into their final form — chipped at the rim, rounded at the edges, mineral-stained in long vertical streaks where condensation has run the same paths for so long it has carved shallow channels into material that was supposed to be inert. This is erosion as a finished story, not an ongoing argument. The place has found its balance and intends to keep it.

## Inside the Bowl

Descending into the cistern proper, the light drops fast — what gets through is filtered, blue-grey, dim enough that your eyes take a full minute to adjust and never quite stop adjusting, because the source isn't the sky. It's the walls themselves, veined with something that pulses faint luminance on a cycle that doesn't match your pulse or your breath or anything else you can measure yourself against. Tech beyond explanation, here: the light doesn't behave like light from a fixture. It seems to be a property of the water.

And there is water everywhere, though never as a body you could swim in. It seeps from the walls in slow continuous sheets, beads on every overhang, drips in countless unsynchronized rhythms into shallow channels carved at the cistern's base that were clearly built to receive it — a recovery system, ancient and still functioning, still doing its one job with the patience of something that has never once been thanked. The rock — if it is still right to call it rock — weeps. It has always wept. It will likely weep after whoever built it is a rumor.

## The Density of Things Living

This is where the chamber stops being merely strange and becomes overwhelming. The cistern, small as it is, is packed with fauna at a density that defies the sparseness of everything growing — because almost nothing grows here, and yet everything *moves* here. Things that don't need plants to survive on. Filter-grazers the size of your fist cling to the wet walls in slow-shifting colonies, dozens to a square meter, each one a translucent dome through which you can watch some internal mechanism — not quite organ, not quite machine — pulse in time with the wall-light. Above them, in the dim shaft of open air at the cistern's center, something winged circles in tight repeating loops, too many of them, a column of bodies stacked and turning like a slow drill bit, each pass scraping moisture off the air with membranous wings built for exactly this humidity.

Smaller things move underfoot in the runnels — segmented, quick, faintly luminous at the joints, traveling in trains of a dozen or more, nose to tail, never breaking formation. You learn quickly not to put your hand where the water pools, because something in there resents company.

None of it is hostile, exactly. None of it pays you much mind. You are simply one more large slow-moving object in a system that has long since stopped being surprised by large slow-moving objects.

## The Sound

This is the thing that will stay with you longest. The cistern is *loud* — deafeningly, continuously, structurally loud — in a way that seems impossible given its size. The dripping alone, multiplied across thousands of points and bounced off curved amber walls built (you begin to suspect) specifically to gather and amplify sound rather than dampen it, becomes a roar, a standing wall of percussion with no rhythm and no end. Into that the wing-things add a papery, layered thrum. The grazer-colonies emit, you eventually realize, a low chorus of clicks — feeding sounds, thousands of small mouths working in overlapping time — that sits underneath everything else like a pedal tone. The segmented trains hiss along the channels. Somewhere below, unseen, something larger occasionally lets out a single low concussive note that you feel in your sternum before you hear it, and everything else goes very slightly quiet for exactly four seconds afterward, then resumes as if nothing happened.

You begin to suspect the sound itself is part of the engineering — that the cistern was built to be loud, that the acoustics serve some function in whatever process moves the water through this structure and into the larger system the Spine is presumably part of. Noise as plumbing. Noise as a sorting mechanism, perhaps, for which the lifeforms here are mere incidental beneficiaries, opportunists who moved into the wet thrumming dark because wet thrumming darks are where the food collects.

## What the Place Believes About Itself

Nothing here was natural, but everything here behaves as though it has always existed. The rules are old rules, fully embedded, not argued over: water moves downward and is caught; light comes from below and beside, never above; things that can cling, cling, and things that can't, circle. The cistern does not perform itself for visitors. It has been running this same warm wet deafening process for so long that "running" is the wrong verb — it simply *is*, the way a held note is.

If you stay long enough, past the point your ears adjust to the roar and start parsing it as texture rather than noise, you notice the cycles: the wall-light dimming and rising on its slow unmatched pulse; the wing-column thinning at certain points and thickening at others; the single low note from below, every few minutes, patient as a held breath. Active, yes — alive with process — but never frantic. It has had a very long time to learn its own rhythm and feels no need to perform it faster for an audience of one, briefly crouched at the rim, getting wet, getting deaf, and trying to remember which direction leads back up to the thin warm sparse air of the ridge above.
