The place is small. This is the first thing the water tells you, pressing close on every side like a held breath: this is a pocket, a single chamber of drowned world no larger than a held room, and yet inside it everything that exists is trying to occupy the same volume at once.

Call it a flooded hollow — the inside of something that might once have been a hill, or a fallen tree's root-cavity, or the cupped space beneath an overturned boat nobody remembers sinking. Cold water fills it almost entirely, dark, near-black, lit by nothing except the faint phosphor-memory of things that grew here before they drowned. There is barely a margin of air left at the very top, a thin lung-shaped pocket where the water hasn't quite finished its claim. You float in that margin, or you don't — the rules here are unconfident about what floats.

The walls, what can be felt of them, are young. Sharp-edged stone, raw root-mass, mineral crusts still soft enough to dent under a fingernail — this place was made recently, geologically speaking, within some event's immediate aftermath, and it has not yet been worn smooth by time. Everything has edges. Everything still remembers its own making.

And it is full. That's the second thing, after the smallness: density past any reasonable limit. Stems, fronds, root-hairs, drifting leaf-matter, pale fungal threads, translucent egg-sacs the size of fists — all of it laced together so tightly there is no clear water anywhere, only water *occupied*, water as medium for growth rather than space around it. You don't swim through this place so much as part it, the way you'd part a curtain of wet hair. Plant matter brushes every surface of you at once. There is a permanent, gentle, complete contact, like the hollow itself is a closed hand and you are something it's holding.

The vegetation is lush but strange in its lushness — broad pale leaves that have never seen direct light, growing toward some other gradient instead, maybe warmth, maybe the faint phosphorescence that drips off decaying stalks like slow-burning fuse. Spores hang motionless, neither rising nor sinking, caught in water that has its own ideas about gravity. Some of the leaf-shapes repeat with a frequency that feels less like ecology and more like echo — the same frond, again and again, slightly different, as though the space is unsure how many times it already showed you this one.

Fauna is rare, but not absent — and what's here is wrong in small, specific ways. A single pale shape drifts at the edge of vision, finned or maybe just frilled, and it takes a long time to register that it has not moved in the whole time you've watched it; only the water around it moves, sliding past a body that holds its position like a fixed point the current can't dislodge. Somewhere below, something exhales — a slow bubble-column rising through black water, perfectly spaced, a metronome with no visible source. It might be breath. It might be the hollow itself, breathing on a cycle too slow to call a rhythm, more like a tide with a memory of having once been a heartbeat.

There are marks of building here, faint and few — a scatter of fitted stones half-sunk in silt, arranged with just enough intention to suggest hands once worked them, though no hands could fit in this space now. A single post, vertical, furred with pale growth, holding nothing up, supporting nothing, just standing where someone once needed it to stand. No tools. No metal. No device has ever been near this place, and the absence feels less like deprivation than like a held door — nothing manufactured has been allowed in, on purpose, by an authority nobody can name.

It is dark — properly dark, the kind that makes the eye invent shapes out of loyalty to its own function, and most of what you'd call seeing here is closer to sensing pressure, temperature-shift, the brush of fronds against skin standing in for sight. The cool water carries sound badly and well at once: mostly quiet, a near-total hush broken occasionally by a *tok* of something striking stone far off, a long fizz of released gas, a creak that might be the hollow's young walls settling further into a shape they haven't finished finding.

Change happens, but slowly — the slow drip of growth, the unhurried sink of dead leaf-matter toward a floor it will take a year to reach, the gradual thickening of root-mass at the walls. Nothing here is in any hurry, except possibly the wrongness, which seems to arrive all at once and then sit, patient, daring you to have noticed it.

Because that's the real weather of this place — not water, not cold, but the sense, growing the longer you stay, that the hollow is not behaving like a space should. Distances inside it don't agree with each other; the margin of air at the top is sometimes nearer, sometimes farther, without your having moved. The fixed pale fin-shape is, on a second look, facing a different direction, though you'd swear on everything you have that it never turned. The repeating frond-shapes, when counted, come up with a different number each time, never wildly different, just off by one, by two, a rounding error in the world's accounting of itself.

It is small, and full, and quiet, and cold, and so packed with green growing life that there is no possible room for anything more — and yet the place keeps somehow finding room anyway, keeps fitting one more impossible thing into a space that was already, by every measure, completely occupied. That is the rule here, the only rule, the one the water and the dark and the lush packed dripping walls all seem to agree on without saying it aloud:

there is always room for one more wrongness, no matter how full the room already is.
