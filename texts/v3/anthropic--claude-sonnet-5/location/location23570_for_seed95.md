# The Shoulderlands

The plateau does not end so much as exhaust itself, mile after mile, into a horizon that has long since stopped pretending to be anything but flat. This is what remains after everything sharp has been worn away — not a young world's drama of jagged peaks but the smoothed remainder of one, sanded down across spans of time that make the word "ancient" feel almost too recent, too eventful. The rock here is the color of old bone and rust-going-to-grey, striated in shallow bands where some long-gone water once moved with more confidence than the present moisture ever will again.

You are high here — high enough that the warm air carries a thinness to it, a quality less of cold than of absence, as though the atmosphere itself has been worn thin alongside the stone. The sun sits at a normal, unremarkable brightness, neither glaring nor dim, which somehow makes the place stranger: this is not a landscape that needs special lighting to feel wrong. It is wrong in plain daylight, wrong in a way that announces itself slowly.

## Ground and Damp

The plateau's surface is a dense mosaic of low outcrops, cracked pavement-stone, and shallow depressions — basins no deeper than a footprint, scattered without evident pattern, each holding a held breath of water. Not pools, exactly. Damps. Some are dew-fed, some seep up from below through capillary fractures in the old rock, and a few — the ones ringed with a faint mineral crust — seem to never fully empty or fully fill, just shift level on a cycle nobody is present long enough to track. Rain comes here rarely and without announcement: a brief textured patter on stone that doesn't smell quite like other rain, sharper, almost metallic, gone in under an hour, leaving the depressions slightly fuller for a day or two before the warmth pulls them back down.

The vegetation that survives this is determinedly sparse and strangely deliberate-looking: lichen in dry oranges and a green so dark it reads as black at a distance, growing in concentric rings around the damp-pockets like the watermark of some vanished tide. Wiry low shrubs, leafless or near enough, claw sideways out of cracks rather than upward — as if standing upright here was tried once, long ago, and abandoned as an unprofitable strategy. Nothing grows tall. The horizon is unbroken by any green thing.

## What Moves

And yet — despite all this austerity — the place teems. This is the plateau's central contradiction, the one that makes visitors stop and recalibrate their sense of what "barren" should mean. The fauna here is overwhelming, dense beyond what the sparse vegetation should be able to support, as if the ecology runs on some other currency than the one the dry stone advertises.

Things move across the rock in numbers — small many-legged creatures the color of the ground itself, gathering in the shade-pockets of outcrops, dispersing into the open in patterns that look almost like weather. Larger forms slip between distant rock-shoulders, glimpsed only as movement, never as silhouette held still long enough to be classified. Burrows pock the softer-eroded slopes by the hundreds, openings no wider than two cupped hands, and at the wrong angle of light you can see motion within nearly every one of them — a population pressing against an environment too thin, by any normal accounting, to hold it.

The damp-basins are the real centers of gravity. Around each one, ecology compresses: insectile rafts skimming the still water-skin, small reptilian things sunning at the rim in stacks of three or four, a kind of pale crustacean-like scavenger working the mineral crust at the basin edges. Nothing here is large. Nothing here is loud. But the density is uncanny — too many creatures occupying too little resource, coexisting at a closeness that should produce conflict and instead produces only this strange, silent, layered crowding, as if scarcity here has bred not competition but compression, everyone learning long ago to take up less room.

## Quiet, and What Breaks It

Sound is sparse, almost rationed. Wind across open stone, a continuous dry exhale rather than a gust. The occasional click or scrape of carapace against rock. No birdsong in any conventional sense — instead, at long intervals, a single high call, source unidentifiable, that carries an improbable distance across the flat ground and is never answered. The quiet is not empty, though; it has the textured quiet of a room full of held breaths, the silence of an overwhelming population that has, collectively, learned not to announce itself.

Change here moves on long gradients. The damp-basins fill and drain across days, the lichen rings widen by some near-imperceptible annual increment, the burrow networks shift their densest concentration from one outcrop-field to the next across what must be years, though nothing marks the years for you. This is a slow place even by the standards of its own enormous population — fast lives, perhaps, lived out within a glacially patient stage.

## The Scattered Remains

There are marks of something before. Not ruins, not really — the word implies too much structure. Just scattering: a low wall-stub of fitted stone, eroded to knee height, running for eleven paces before vanishing back into undifferentiated rock. A circle of post-holes, empty, rimmed with the same lichen as everything else, holding no memory of what once stood in them. A flat worked stone, face-down, half-swallowed by sediment, its underside (if anyone ever turned it) bearing the suggestion of shallow carved lines too regular to be erosion and too worn to be read.

No tools remain. No metal, no fired clay, nothing that argues for any technology more advanced than hands and patience. Whatever built the wall-stub and dug the post-holes did so a very long time ago and left almost nothing else — not because they vanished suddenly, but, the place seems to suggest, because they simply stopped, the way the lichen stops climbing the rock, the way the water stops trying to be a river. The plateau absorbed them the way it absorbs everything: slowly, completely, without disturbing the silence.

## The Wrongness, Unspecified

The strangeness of the place resists being pinned to any one feature. It's cumulative — the too-many creatures in too-thin a landscape, the basins that hold water by no logic the dryness should permit, the lichen rings that look too deliberately concentric to be accident, the single far call that nothing answers, the worked stone with its almost-pattern face-down in the dirt. None of it is supernatural exactly. All of it suggests an order operating just beneath the visible one — old rules, patient rules, that the plateau still quietly obeys long after whoever set them is gone.

Stand here long enough, in the warm thin light, amid the click and scuttle and stacked-up density of small lives, and you start to feel it: not danger, not quite, but attention. As if the high worn ground, having shed every excess feature down to bone and basin, retains just enough structure to notice you noticing it back.
