The basin announces itself before it's seen: a pressure in the ears, then a wall of sound that doesn't crest or recede but simply *is*, the way the inside of a shell is always roaring. You arrive at the lip of the lowlands and the noise rises to meet you — not one sound but thousands braided into a single rope, taut from horizon to horizon.

Here is what the land is made of: raw stone, freshly arrived. Not weathered, not yet rounded — knuckled outcrops and sheet-rock still wearing the sharp edges of their own making, as though the ground stopped mid-thought. No soil to speak of, just mineral skin, gray-ochre and rust-veined, broken everywhere by seep-pools where the water table sits just under the surface and presses up through every crack. The whole basin glistens without being wet — a sheen, a held breath of moisture, condensing on stone in the warm dim air and beading along ledges before finding its way back down into the rock. It never quite rains and never quite dries.

Almost nothing grows. No grass, no scrub, just here and there a crust of something between lichen and mineral stain, more chemistry than life, spreading in slow continents across damp boulders. The absence of plant cover makes the place feel new — a world still in its packaging — and yet it is anything but empty.

Because the basin is full of building, and the building is full of bodies.

Low settlements terrace the shallow rises in unbroken sprawl: stacked stone, mud-mortar, timber lashed with hide-rope, walls leaning into walls in a dense weave of dwellings, pens, causeways, and stairs cut directly into bedrock. No structure stands far from another — the architecture touches itself constantly, alleys narrow as elbows, rooftops doubling as paths, water channels cut shallow and straight between foundations to drain the seep before it pools. It is a town-sized organism of stone and lashed wood, built by hand, by people who have not yet found metal or have only just begun to — every edge of every tool-mark in the rock is a chisel-strike, a grind, a patient repetition. Nothing here is forged. Everything is worked.

And around and through and over all of it: animals, in numbers that outscale the people. Herds pressed into the lower flats, their bodies a second topography, shoulder to shoulder, raising a heat-haze of breath in the warm air. Flocks roosting on every available ledge, wingbeats overlapping until the air itself seems to flutter. Things burrow in the wet ground and surface in agitated clusters; things larger move along the causeways with the unhurried right-of-way of population, not predation. The ecology isn't balanced so much as *saturated* — every niche filled to the edge of its container, the basin a held breath of biomass that has found its equilibrium and has no apparent intention of leaving it.

This is the source of the sound: not weather, not machinery, but sheer aggregate creature-noise — bellow and shriek and chitter and footfall and the percussion of ten thousand small actions happening at once, compounding in the basin's bowl shape until distance offers no relief, only a different timbre of the same roar. People here have learned to speak in gesture and touch as much as voice; voices are for emergencies. You see it in how they move — close-packed, attentive to hands and shoulders, the kind of communication built by generations who never expected silence to do any work for them.

Light comes low and filtered, as if the brightness above never fully resolves into a definite sun — a soft warm gray-gold wash, shadowless, even, leaning more dusk than day regardless of hour. Under this even light the wet stone shines uniformly, the herds steam gently, the rooftops gleam with the same damp sheen as the bedrock, and nothing in the scene seems to be in the process of becoming anything else. The rock is raw because it is *young*, not because it is being worn — there is no erosion here yet, no slow grinding toward smoothness. Edges stay sharp. Channels stay where they were cut. The settlements have clearly stood, unchanged in their essential form, for as long as anyone living can attest; additions get made at the margins, but the basin's basic arrangement — stone, water, animal, dwelling, noise — feels less like a moment in a history and more like a fixed condition, a sentence the world decided to keep saying the same way forever.

Walk far enough and the basin doesn't end so much as repeat — another terrace, another press of dwellings, another herd-mass, the sound never breaking stride. The valley extends past any one vantage, a regional fact rather than a single scene, every part of it dense with the same ingredients recombined: wet stone, warm haze, raw unweathered ground, hand-built shelter, and the ceaseless layered roar of too much life occupying too little silence, forever, by design or by accident, settled into a stillness made entirely of motion.
