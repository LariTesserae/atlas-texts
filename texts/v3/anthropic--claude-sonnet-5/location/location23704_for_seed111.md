# The Stack

It rises before the high places have learned to wear down — a single tooth of dark rock punched up through the earth's skin so recently that its edges still cut. You would not call it a mountain. A mountain implies range, shoulders, a family of slopes leveling out into foothills and rivers. This is a single needle, a cathedral-scale fang of stone, maybe four hundred meters of vertical reach packed into a footprint you could walk around in twenty minutes. Its flanks are nearly sheer. Where they are not sheer, they are worse — overhangs, shelves, fractured terraces that look like they were broken off something larger by a hand that has since walked away.

It is always dark here, or close enough to dark that the difference barely matters — a perpetual dusk-gray, the kind of light that seems to come from no particular direction, soaked into the cloud ceiling that never quite breaks. The cold is the cold of altitude rather than season: dry-edged but not biting, the kind that gets into rock and stays there.

## What the rock is made of

Young basalt and a paler, almost bone-colored stone banded through it in vertical streaks, like the mountain was poured rather than uplifted. The fracture patterns are sharp — fresh conchoidal breaks, hexagonal columns still showing their crystal logic, scree fields where the scree hasn't had time to round into pebbles. Everything here still has corners. You could cut yourself on the landscape, and people have.

Water doesn't run on the Stack so much as *happen* to it. Condensation beads on the cold stone in the pre-dawn hours and drips down in threads that vanish before they pool into anything you could call a stream. Higher up, near the broken terraces, there are basin-pockets — fist-sized to bath-sized hollows in the rock — that fill from mist and rare rain and hold their water like secrets, dark mirrors with nothing in them, or nothing you can see in this light.

Vegetation is sparse in the way of something recently arrived rather than something dying out: lichen in patches of acid yellow and bruise purple, low cushion-mosses wedged into cracks, a few wind-flattened shrubs with leaves like leather, clinging to the terraces where soil has had time — barely — to gather. Nothing here is old. Nothing here is tall.

## What lives there

This is the strange part, the part that doesn't fit the rock's youth: the Stack is *thick* with animal life, far more than its sparse vegetation should support. Fauna density that should belong to a rainforest instead colonizes a young volcanic spire with almost no plant matter to sustain it.

They are everywhere, in every crevice, on every shelf, hanging from overhangs in clustered colonies — pale, large-eyed things, some winged, some many-legged, some that move like they're not sure gravity applies to them consistently. They do not seem to eat the lichen. They do not seem to eat much of anything visible. There's a theory, among the few who've climbed partway up and come back to tell of it, that something deeper in the rock feeds them — mineral seeps, geothermal residue, some chemistry the mountain itself provides, the basin-pools doing more than holding water.

The sound is the first thing that hits you from a distance, before you can even see the Stack clearly in the gray light: a continuous loud chorus, clicking and keening and a low collective thrum like the mountain itself is humming, layered calls overlapping calls overlapping calls until individual voices are unrecoverable. It does not stop at night because there isn't really a night here distinct enough to notice. It is simply always *full*, always *occupied*, a wall of animal noise wrapped around a tower of nearly bare stone.

## The wrongness of it

It shouldn't work. Sparse plant life, young cold rock, minimal water — and yet density of fauna that overwhelms every other measure. People who live in the lowlands around the Stack's base (and a few do, in scattered huts, hand-tool cultures who have learned to take what the mountain offers without asking it questions) have a saying: *the Stack feeds itself out of nothing, so don't ask it to feed you too.*

Things are sometimes seen on the upper terraces that don't match any of the smaller colony-creatures — larger silhouettes, slower, watching rather than clicking. They appear at the edge of the noise rather than inside it, like predators who've learned the supporting cast covers their footsteps. No one has mapped how far up the Stack goes in any reliable sense; the gray ceiling that caps the sky seems to come down to meet the peak rather than the peak reaching the sky, and climbers report the topmost terraces as somehow *colder going up but warmer going further*, a contradiction nobody has resolved because nobody has gone far enough to resolve it and come back to say so clearly.

## The pace of the place

Despite all this saturation — the noise, the crowding, the wrongness — the Stack changes slowly. The rock is young by geological measure, but geological measure is glacial by any other. The lichen colonies expand by handspans across years. The basin-pools fill and evaporate on cycles measured in seasons. The creature colonies shift position on the terraces gradually, the way a held breath shifts. It is loud and dense and strange and crowded, but none of it hurries. The Stack exists in the manner of something that has just woken up and intends to take its time figuring out what it's for.

The hand-tool people at its base have given it a hundred names over generations, and none have stuck longer than a lifetime, because the Stack — sharp-edged, dark, perpetually droning with unseen life — resists being fixed into any single story. It simply continues: a fang of new stone, wearing its scree like fresh wounds, hosting a chorus too large for the meal it's been given, in a light that never quite commits to day.
