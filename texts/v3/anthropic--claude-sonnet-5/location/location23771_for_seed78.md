# The Canopy-Steppe of Vael Sorrow

## What it is, before what it looks like

You must first understand a contradiction the world solved before you got there: this is a forest with the bones of a tundra. It rose recently — geologically speaking, recently — when something tectonic underneath pushed a plateau toward the sky, and the forest did not retreat from the new cold and thin air. It conquered it instead. What grows here grew angry about the altitude, dense and low and endless, a vegetative total occupation of a place that should by all rights belong to bare rock and wind.

There is almost no bare rock. There is almost no wind that you feel, though you hear it constantly, screaming somewhere above the canopy-roof like a separate weather system that the forest itself filters out. This is the strangeness at the center of the place: a vast highland, exposed and raw and freshly thrust upward, completely sealed under an unbroken lid of cold-adapted growth.

## Terrain and substrate

The ground, where you can find it, is young rock — sharp-edged scree, fractured basalt-like sheets, the kind of stone that hasn't yet been smoothed by enough centuries of weather. But you rarely touch this ground. You walk instead on a mattress of root-mass and matted needle-leaf, sometimes a meter thick, springy and cold and slightly damp from condensation that never quite becomes rain. The terrain heaves and dips beneath this mattress — you're walking over the same young, jagged elevation-changes the rock itself makes, just with all the violence padded out.

The plateau extends far enough that it generates its own cloud-systems at its own edges; you'll see, on the rare ridge-break, banks of fog forming below you, rivers of mist running through valleys you're standing above. The scale here is continental in feel — you could walk for a season inside this single canopy-system and not reach its edge. It is one biome behaving like one organism.

Water is scarce in volume but everywhere in trace — condensation beads on every needle and frond at dawn, fat drops that fall erratically through the layers above and strike the leaf-mattress with a sound like distant footsteps. There are no rivers that you'd recognize; instead there are damp seams, places where the rock beneath gathers what little moisture exists into thin, cold trickles that vanish into moss before they can become anything as organized as a stream.

## The vegetation-architecture

This is the place's true structure. Three, maybe four canopy tiers, each one a near-total ceiling:

The lower tier is shoulder-height scrub, woody and twisted, growing in directions that suggest it remembers wind even though the upper canopy has long since stopped most of it from reaching down here. Its bark is pale, almost bone-colored, fissured from rapid growth rather than age.

Above that, a mid-canopy of thicker trunks, widely spaced but interlinked by vine-growth and structural root-bridges that have fused over what must be centuries of this tier's existence — these are the closest things to true "old growth," gnarled and load-bearing, holding up everything above.

The uppermost tier is the loud one — needle-clusters and broad, leathery wind-leaves that take the brunt of the screaming high-altitude air and turn it into sound: a constant roar, a hiss-and-thrum that never stops, modulating with gusts into something almost vocal. This is where most of the "weather" of the place actually happens — visible only as motion and shadow-shift far overhead, felt as pressure-change, heard as that endless rushing voice.

Between all this: almost nothing. The density is the strangest sensation of the place — for all this total vegetative coverage, the actual interior volume, where you move, is empty. Cavernous. The plant-mass forms walls and ceiling and floor, but the space they enclose is hollow, echoing, vast pockets of cold dark air between root-wall and canopy-roof. You're not walking through a thicket. You're walking through architecture made of living material, rooms and corridors the forest has grown around itself.

## Light and dark

Almost none reaches the ground-layer. What little does comes through as scattered grey-blue shafts, shifting and rare, more like memory of light than light itself. Most of your time here is spent in a kind of perpetual dusk-that-never-fully-arrives, navigable only because your eyes adapt to work with almost nothing. At true night, it is total — the kind of darkness that has texture and weight, where sound becomes your only reliable sense.

## Sound

This is a loud place, which surprises anyone who expects forests to hush. The canopy-roar above is constant, like surf or distant engines. Below, in the hollow dark, sound carries strangely — cracks and calls bounce off root-walls, arrive from impossible directions, double back on themselves. Animal calls, when they come, are sudden and enormous in this acoustic chamber. Dripping water sounds louder than it has any right to. You will frequently believe something large is closer to you than it is.

## Fauna

Abundant, but rarely seen — heard, smelled, felt as displaced air. Things that live in cold, in dark, in vertical wooden architecture: long-limbed climbers that move through the mid-canopy root-bridges with unsettling silence given their size; ground-dwelling burrowers that have made the leaf-mattress into a sponge of tunnels, surfacing only to feed; pale flightless birds, if they are birds, that scream territorial calls into the dark and go quiet for hours after. Occasionally, eyes catch ambient light at a distance and hold there, considering you, before vanishing without sound. The ecology operates almost entirely by ear and scent; the dark and density have made vision a secondary sense for nearly everything that lives here, predators included.

## People, and what they left

Built presence is sparse and old-feeling despite the youth of the land itself — which means whoever or whatever made these marks arrived early and didn't stay, or stayed thin. You find: a circle of standing root-stakes, deliberately placed, lashed together with cord gone to fiber. A lean-to of woven branch, collapsed, reclaimed halfway into the mattress-floor. Notches cut into mid-canopy trunks at regular intervals, like a ladder-code, leading up toward the roaring tier and stopping abruptly at a platform now half-rotted.

The tech-signature is hand-tool, cord-and-blade, fire-scarring on a few trunks where someone burned back growth to make a clearing that the forest has since mostly forgiven and absorbed. No metal you can find, though one rust-streak on a stone suggests someone, once, had a little.

Nothing suggests these people are gone with certainty. Just that they are quiet, and the forest is enormous, and you have seen perhaps four structures in days of walking.

## The unusual edge

The weirdness here doesn't announce itself, it accretes: the way condensation drops fall in patterns that almost seem rhythmic, on still nights, like something tapping. The standing root-stake circles that recur — you'll find another, days later, with the same diameter, the same number of stakes. The fact that sound seems to organize itself near these circles, quieting in a perimeter around them in a forest that is otherwise loud everywhere. Nothing confirms anything. The cold, the dark, and the scale make it very easy to assign pattern to coincidence — but the patterns keep recurring.

## Living in it

You move slowly here, between root-wall and canopy-roof, in grey-dark cold, with the roar overhead and the abundant unseen life around and below. The young sharpness of the land underneath has been entirely buried by total, ancient-acting growth — a fresh wound wearing the costume of deep time. Rain rarely falls as rain; it falls as drip, as held breath releasing late. The forest is one vast organism's architecture, mostly hollow, mostly listening, and you are a small loud thing moving through its low and empty rooms.
