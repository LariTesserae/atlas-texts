The Canopy-Continent of Ar-Vethna

You come upon it from above first, because there is no other way to come upon it: a ceiling of green-gold canopy stretched across an entire highland mass, unbroken for what your instruments insist are hundreds of kilometers in every direction, sitting at an elevation that leaves the air thin and the light merciless. There is no water here. There has never needed to be. Whatever moves through these plants moves through something else — a clear, faintly oily sap-system, vast subterranean reservoirs of dissolved mineral salts, root-networks that seem to manufacture their own internal weather of fluid without ever touching rain. The world has simply declined the ocean and built something else instead.

The vegetation is not landscape feature here — it is the landscape. There is no ground in the way you mean ground. Beneath the upper canopy, descending for what might be a kilometer of vertical depth, is a continuous architecture of trunks, the diameter of towers, braided together in load-bearing lattices that have clearly been growing into and against each other for geological time. Some trunks have fused at the base into single fluted columns wide enough to lose a vehicle inside. Between them: not undergrowth but *understructure* — fibrous mezzanines of woven smaller growth, platforms of dead-flat leaf-mat hardened by mineral deposit into something like decking, hollow boles big enough to hold a settlement that nobody built. The erosion here isn't wind on stone. It's centuries of growth and collapse and regrowth, slow vegetable tectonics, trunks the size of cliffs leaning, dying, rotting into soft red country that new trunks immediately colonize. Everything is established, ancient, in long balance — and yet none of it is still.

Because that's the contradiction the place wants you to sit inside: it is old and it is turbulent simultaneously. The canopy is in constant convulsion. At this elevation the storms have nothing to interrupt them, and they come through as walls of pressure-change you feel in your teeth before you hear them — and you will hear them, because sound here is total. The wind doesn't whistle through this canopy, it plays it, kilometers of hollow cane and fibrous resonating trunk turning every gust into chord-structures so loud they register as physical impact. Add to that the fauna — abundant, large-bodied, mostly airborne or canopy-dwelling, things with chitin-and-feather hybrid wings the span of small aircraft, colonies of clicking, screaming, droning life that nest in the hollow boles and erupt in waves numbering in the tens of thousands when the pressure-storms roll through. The combined noise-floor of wind-through-wood and animal-alarm and structural creak is close to deafening at all times, and during storm-passage it becomes a single undifferentiated roar that swallows speech entirely.

And then there's the industry, which shouldn't be here and is the unusual thing that tips this place from merely vast-and-wild into something with an edge of wrongness. Scattered through the high canopy — sparse, no two installations close enough to see each other, low-saturation against all that endless green — are things that are unmistakably engines. Not built structures in the conventional sense; no scaffolding, no foundations, nothing that reads as architecture. Instead: trunks and boles that have grown around machinery, or that *are* machinery in some way the eye resists, cylindrical forms venting heat-shimmer and a thin chemical exhaust that the wind tears away instantly, drivetrain-sounds of immense unseen mechanisms turning somewhere down in root-depth, audible even over the animal roar as a bass throb felt through whatever you're standing on. No one knows who runs them, or if anyone does. They have the worn, integrated look of things that have been operating for a very long time, weathered into the same established equilibrium as everything organic around them — rust-stains the color of dried sap, growth fused seamlessly to housing, vines grown directly through turbine-grilles that still, impossibly, turn. The place has absorbed its own machinery the way it absorbs everything else: given enough centuries, even an engine becomes terrain.

Light comes down in shafts and sheets, bright and relentless at this altitude, broken into moving dapple by the upper canopy's constant motion, so that the understructure beneath is never lit the same way twice — a slow strobing flicker across the red-gold rot-and-growth surfaces, gold one moment, deep green-black shadow the next, as the canopy churns overhead in wind that never fully stops. Temperature sits cool and thin, a highland cool that has nothing to do with shade and everything to do with elevation, and the air itself carries a sharp mineral-green smell, sap and ozone and the machine-exhaust threading through it in places, occasionally an animal-musk wave rolling through ahead of a flock.

There is no water, so nothing pools, nothing reflects, nothing softens an edge by erosion-of-the-usual-kind. Instead the world holds itself up by tension and fiber and growth, sparse in its punctuation — long stretches of unbroken canopy-and-trunk between any "event," whether that event is an engine, a rookery, a storm-front, a fall of some great dead trunk taking a slow century to come down — and then, when something does happen, it happens at the full volume the place seems to insist on: total, deafening, continental, and somehow, despite all of it, still exactly itself, the way it's apparently been for longer than anyone arrived to measure.
