# The Stacked City of Verengal Spire

The mountain does not end. This is the first thing you understand about it, and the understanding arrives not through measurement but through the slow failure of measurement itself — you look up, and the summit is there, close enough to map with your eye, a clean ridge of grey stone against a sky the color of old pewter. Then you look again, perhaps an hour later, perhaps a breath later, and the summit has receded, or multiplied, or revealed itself to be a foothill of something else stacked above it, slightly transparent, slightly *insistent*, as though the mountain were still deciding how tall it wants to have been.

The city is built into this uncertainty rather than against it.

It rises in tiers — terraces of dark, fine-grained stone the color of wet slate, though no water has touched this place in longer than its builders could remember, longer perhaps than memory was a technology available to them. The stone holds a faint sheen anyway, an optical memory of wetness, light pooling in its facets like something that forgot to evaporate. Streets switchback along contour lines that don't quite agree with each other — a road that climbs gently for a hundred paces will suddenly require that you have already arrived at a height you have not yet walked to. People — if they are people; shapes wrapped in long dust-toned garments, moving with unhurried purpose — seem to navigate this without confusion, as if the discontinuity is simply another kind of weather, expected, dressed-for.

The buildings are old in the way that erosion is old — not ruined, not crumbling, but *settled*, the way a tooth is settled in a jaw. Windowless lower floors give way to clusters of small dark openings higher up, and higher still the architecture stops pretending to be for bodies at all: spires within spires, stairways that climb the outside of towers and then simply stop, three hundred feet up, facing open air, as though awaiting a destination that hasn't been built yet, or was built and then quietly removed.

There is no green here. No moss, no lichen crusting the stone, no rootlets finding purchose in the cracks. The mineral surfaces are bare and exact, swept by wind that makes almost no sound — a thin cool air, dry as old paper, moving among the terraces with a sound like breath held just shy of a whisper. Occasionally something that might be a bird — long-necked, slate-colored, wings folded close — stands motionless on a parapet for what feels like hours, then is simply gone without having flown anywhere visible.

Nothing here was made with tools you would recognize. No scaffolding-marks, no tool-scars, no seams where stone meets mortared stone. The buildings seem to have been *arrived at* — grown into their final shape the way a held breath arrives at stillness — and yet they are unmistakably a city: streets, plazas, stairwells, the unmistakable grammar of habitation, just authored by a hand, or a process, that left no fingerprints.

The emptiness is the strangest part. For a city this size — and it is enormous, terraces receding across the mountain's flank for what might be miles, what might be more than miles, since distance here behaves like the summit does — there is almost no one in it. A figure crosses a distant plaza. A door, three tiers up, stands open onto darkness. The density of presence is so low that the architecture begins to feel less like a city and more like a diagram of one, a city's idea of itself, populated just enough to remain a place rather than becoming a fact.

And the mountain keeps not-finishing. Terraces you are certain you climbed reappear below you. A stairwell ascends into a tower that, from the next terrace over, isn't there. Twice, three times, the angle of the light shifts — not with any sun you can locate, since the sky offers only a diffuse dim pewter glow with no single source — and in that shift, whole districts seem to fold quietly into ones adjacent to them, the way a reflection folds into the glass holding it. No one reacts to this. It may be the most ordinary thing about the place.

Sound carries strangely and rarely: a footstep two terraces below arrives sharp and close, as if at your ear; your own breathing vanishes entirely, swallowed by the cool dry air. Once, a low tone — not a bell, not a voice, something with the patience of both — moves through the stone underfoot and is gone before you can decide it happened.

The rule of this place, if it has one, seems to be this: that height and time are loosely associated variables here, not fixed quantities, and the city was built — or grew, or was always — to hold a structure too large for any single consistent geometry, so it simply uses several, switching between them with the unconcern of a sleeper changing position. The established, weathered calm of the stone, the settlement-logic of streets and doors and stairs, persists underneath all of it, untroubled — as if the city has had a very long time to get used to not quite agreeing with itself, and has found, in that disagreement, something stable enough to live in.
