# The Greenhouse That Grew Itself

It is small—you could walk its full circumference in the time it takes water to boil, if there were water, which there is not. But the smallness is deceptive, the way a single room can hold a forest if the forest doesn't believe in distance.

## The Built Floor

Nothing here is original ground. Beneath the green is a lattice of fired clay tiles, each one hand-pressed and thumb-marked, fitted together by people or hands or *something with hands* so long ago that the joining-mortar has gone the color of old teeth. The tiles are not flat. They swell and dip like the world is breathing very slowly beneath them, and in places they have cracked open to let woody roots through—roots that, if you trace them, do not lead down into soil, because there is no soil, only more tiles, stacked twelve, twenty, forty deep, a foundation that goes further than anyone has dug.

The walls, where they exist, are wattle-and-daub gone ancient: woven branches plastered with a clay-dung mixture that has hardened into something like horn. They lean inward at the top, almost meeting, but never quite—a structure built by someone who understood enclosure but not ceiling, or who deliberately left the sky an option.

Everything here was *made*. This is the rule you learn first and trust least: even the boulders are masonry, fitted stone-on-stone with the seams hidden by moss; even the low hills are heaped clay armatures, hollow inside, that hum faintly if you press an ear to them.

## The Green

The vegetation is lush in the way of a held breath—dense, close, leaning into every gap. Broad fan-leaves the size of dinner plates grow directly from the daub walls, rootless, fed by something other than dirt. Vines braid themselves through the clay-tile cracks in patterns too symmetrical to be growth alone—spirals, lattices, the suggestion of woven baskets that decided to keep living after they were made. The air smells of crushed mint and clay-dust and the particular sweetness of fruit that is always one day from rot, never further.

Flowers open on hinges. This is not metaphor. Small jointed blossoms, three or four petals on tiny wooden axles, swing wide in the warmth and click shut at intervals that have nothing to do with any sun, because there may not be a sun—the light here is warm and even and shadowless in a way that suggests it comes from everywhere and nowhere, diffused through the high leaning walls like something filtered through skin.

## What Lives Here

Fauna is abundant, dense, layered—small bodies everywhere you look once you start looking. Beetles the size of thumbnails with shells fired and glazed like ceramic, genuinely glazed, geometric patterns baked into their backs. They click across the tile-floor in unhurried processions. Birds, if they are birds, perch in the vine-lattices with feathers that look woven rather than grown, each one a tiny thatched roof of plumage, and they do not sing so much as *tick*, soft wooden sounds, like clocks made of twigs.

Something larger moves in the deep leaf-shadow near the leaning walls—glimpsed only as a shift in the green, never as a shape. It is patient. It has been patient for a very long time. The small creatures are not afraid of it, which tells you something about how long the patience has lasted, or how well it has kept its bargains.

## The Quiet

Mostly silence, but never true silence: the tick of beetle-feet, the click of hinge-flowers, the dry rustle of woven-feather birds settling, and underneath it all, almost below hearing, a low resonant hum from the hollow clay hills—as if the whole place were a held note, sung once a very long time ago, still not finished decaying.

## Where the World Breaks

Here is the thing you must accept, the thing that makes this place a place and not simply a garden: the geometry does not agree with itself.

Walk along the inner wall, following its lean, and you will arrive back where you started having only gone three-quarters of the way around. Walk the other direction and you complete the circle in five steps. The vines on the eastern face are reaching toward a light source that does not exist on that side. A fruit picked from a branch will, if uneaten, still be hanging on that branch the next time you look, unpicked, plump, waiting—your having taken it was simply one of the things that happened to that fruit, not a thing that removed it from happening again.

The hollow hum from the buried tiles is, some visitors swear, the *sound* of all this being decided. Not a glitch, not a malfunction—a deliberation. The place is still partly being made, even now, by makers who are either extremely slow or simply unconcerned with the difference between finished and finishing. The young-erosion sharpness of every edge—the crisp unworn lip of each tile, the fresh-cut look of branch-joints that should be centuries soft—suggests the whole garden is recent. Recent, and dense, and still arriving.

Nothing decays past a certain point here. Nothing completes past a certain point either. It is a room built to hold abundance in permanent arrival—lush, warm, crowded, quiet, made entirely by hands that understood clay and wood and weaving but apparently not, or not yet, or not ever intending to teach, the difference between *grown* and *built*.

You may stay as long as you like. The garden will not notice the difference between your visit and your absence. It has already made room for both.
