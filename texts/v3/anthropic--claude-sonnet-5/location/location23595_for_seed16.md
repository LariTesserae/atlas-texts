The place is called, by those few who know of it at all, the Cistern Town — though it has no real name, only the name people give to the shape of a thing once they've walked through it.

You find it by dropping down. There's no announcement above ground — no rise, no spire, nothing on the flat hot scrubland to suggest what waits beneath. Just a fissure in the rock, wide enough for two people abreast, descending at a manageable grade through dry red stone gone smooth with ten thousand years of feet. The air changes before the light does. It thickens, reddens, takes on a mineral sweetness, and somewhere in that first hundred meters of descent you start to hear it — not water, never water, but a low continuous roar that sounds like water and isn't: wind, forced through the throat of the world, organ-piping across edges that long-dead hands once cut on purpose to make it sing.

And then the shaft opens, and you are standing at the lip of a sunken bowl of a town, built into and out of a vast dry hollow in the rock — a town with no water table beneath it, no spring, no well, sustained entirely on stored cleverness and the strange dense green that chokes every terrace and balcony and crevice of the place. The vegetation here is lush in defiance of everything else about the world above: thick fleshy leaves, vines like rope, fruiting bodies that seem to bulge wetly though no water has touched them. They don't drink rain. They drink heat. There are whole guilds, whole lineages of families, whose only craft is the cultivation of these heat-succulents, plants that pull whatever scant moisture exists from the superheated air itself, exhaling it back out at night as a kind of dew that the town has learned, over centuries, to lick from leaves and stone.

The town itself is layered like a cut geode — terraces of mudbrick and fired clay stacked one above the other in a bowl maybe four hundred meters across, low ceiling of open sky far above like the mouth of a jar. Walkways switchback across the terrace faces, supported on wooden joists pegged rather than nailed, the whole structure looking improvised and ancient at once, the kind of architecture that has been rebuilt in the same pattern so many times it might as well be geological. Doorways are low. Walls are thick — thick enough to hold the cool of the deep night against the brutal furnace of the day. Everything is built from what's at hand: clay, dried fiber from the heat-plants, a little worked stone, no metal beyond a few hoarded blades and tools passed down like relics.

It should be empty. It feels empty — that's the strange tension of the place. The terraces stretch on and on, the bowl is vast for what it holds, and yet there are so few people per square meter that you can walk for what feels like a long while along a switchback path before passing a door with a person behind it. And yet the sound is enormous. That's the unusual thing, the thing that makes new arrivals stop on the rim and just listen before they descend: the noise of the place is completely disproportionate to its population. The wind-organ stone formations, carved deliberately into the upper rock by founders long forgotten, turn every shift of the surface heat-draft into a chord — and the town has learned to live inside that chord, has built its daily life around it, ringing iron triangles and dragging clay jars and clapping wooden clappers in counter-rhythm to the wind's drone, so that the whole bowl resonates almost constantly with a kind of layered, hollow, half-musical roar. You can be entirely alone on a terrace and still feel like you're standing inside a crowd.

It's hot — savagely, continuously hot, heat that doesn't break even when the light goes dim, because the light here was never strong to begin with. The sun only reaches the bowl's floor directly for an hour or two near midday; the rest of the time everything exists in a deep ember-colored dimness, walls glowing faintly red-orange with reflected light bounced down off the bowl's upper rim, the heat-succulents looking almost black-green in the gloom, like something better suited to a deep sea than a desert. There is no animal life at all — no insects, no birds, no scuttling thing in the dark corners — and this absence is felt rather than noticed, a faint wrongness that visitors describe as a town that hums but doesn't breathe.

The processes here are unending: the wind-organs cycle through their tones with the day's heating and cooling, the heat-plants swell and weep their nightly dew on a schedule as reliable as tide, the terraces are perpetually being patched and re-mudded against the slow erosion of the dry air, irrigation troughs (carrying dew, not rain) trickle audibly downhill from terrace to terrace in a centuries-old gravity-fed network of clay channels. Children — there are children, somewhere, their voices occasionally cutting through the wind-drone — learn early which leaves to press in the dark hours for the most water. Old craftsmen test the pitch of a newly carved wind-stone before setting it into the rock face, tuning the town like an instrument that's never finished being built.

The rule of this place, if it has one, is that everything must be coaxed rather than taken. There is no water to dig for, no rain to wait on, no animal to hunt, no metal to mine in quantity — only heat, abundant and oppressive, and the patient, ancient art of turning that heat into food, drink, and sound. The town survives by treating its harshest fact as its only resource. It is a place that has made peace with deprivation by inventing an economy out of the one thing it has too much of, and the result is a settlement that feels, despite its scale and its history and its undeniable organization, like a secret — tucked below the visible world, humming to itself in the dark heat, heard before it is ever seen.
