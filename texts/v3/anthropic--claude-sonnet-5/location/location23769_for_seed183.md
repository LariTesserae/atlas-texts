# The Weeping Heights of Sallow Karst

The mountains here do not so much rise as *insist*. Extreme elevation pulled taut over an established, weathered bedrock — peaks and ridgelines that have clearly stood for a very long time, their edges rounded by something other than wind, their faces streaked dark with permanent damp. No soil holds them. No root touches them. This is a mineral world, scoured clean of the green and the crawling, and yet it is not dead — it is *busy* in a way that has nothing to do with biology.

## Terrain

Picture a high plateau shattered into hundreds of vertical fins and table-topped towers, packed close — dense, near-touching, the gaps between them narrow enough that you could often reach across and not quite touch the opposite wall. The rock is pale grey-gold, banded with darker mineral seams that look almost like sediment but resolve, on closer inspection, into something closer to *frozen circuitry* — repeating fractal traces too regular to be geology, too eroded and ancient to be anything built.

Water is everywhere and nowhere: not rivers, not lakes, but a constant seep. The rock weeps. Every vertical face has its thin sheet of moisture working down it, beading, breaking into rivulets that vanish into cracks before they ever pool. The air sits at maybe ninety percent of saturation — cool, heavy, and clinging. Mist gathers in the throats of the canyons each morning and burns off by midday under a light so bright it seems to come from more than one direction at once.

## Light

This is the place's first wrongness. The light is bright — startlingly so for a world with no visible sun-disk, only a high white glare that seems to emanate from the wet stone itself as much as fall onto it. Shadows fall at inconsistent angles between adjacent towers, as though each fin of rock is lit by a slightly different sky. Stand in a slot canyon at "noon" and you may see your own shadow split into two, pointing in directions sixty degrees apart, neither fully dark.

## Sound

Loud, but not with voices or wind alone. The water seeping through the rock produces a constant low resonance — the whole massif seems to hum, a structural tone somewhere below conscious hearing that you feel in your sternum before you notice it in your ears. Layered over this: cracks and reports, sudden and sharp, as some unseen process inside the stone releases tension — not rockfall, exactly, since nothing falls. More like the sound of a held breath escaping. Occasionally a tone rings out clean and bell-like from deep within a tower and is answered, seconds later, by another tower a kilometer off, as if the whole range is in slow conversation.

## Ecology (or its absence and replacement)

No fauna. No flora. No fungus, even, in the recognizable sense. And yet density is high — the place feels *inhabited*, crowded, saturated with presence. What fills the niche where life would be is something else: thin filaments of pale mineral deposit that grow along the wet seams of rock, branching in shapes that mimic root systems, then dissolving back into the stone over the course of days, only to regrow elsewhere along a different seam. They are not alive by any definition that holds elsewhere. They do not metabolize, reproduce, or respond to stimulus in any consistent way. They simply appear, branch, thicken, and recede — a circulatory pattern in rock that has all the *form* of biology and none of its substance. Travelers have taken to calling them weep-vines, though everyone agrees this is a misnomer they keep anyway.

## The Built World

Scattered, partial, old beyond easy reckoning: single dwellings carved into the leeward faces of the larger towers, doorframes cut at angles that don't match any plumb line, sills polished by hands using only stone and bone tools. The tech level is primitive — no metal in evidence, no wheel-ruts, nothing more sophisticated than a chisel-mark and a fitted lintel — and yet some of these structures incorporate the fractal mineral banding of the natural rock into their architecture so seamlessly that it is often impossible to say where the carved ends and the grown begins. Cairns stand at irregular intervals along the ridgelines, built from stones that do not match the local geology, as if carried up from somewhere that no longer exists or never existed nearby.

No bodies. No tools left mid-use. No fire pits with ash still soft. Whoever built here did not leave in crisis; they simply are not here, and the structures have weathered exactly as long as the rock around them, as if built at the same geological moment the mountains were.

## The Breakdown

Here is where this place stops being merely strange and becomes *wrong* in the productive sense. Distances between towers do not stay constant — a gap crossed in ten paces going one direction takes forty returning, or three, never the same value twice, though always positive, always finite. Echoes return before the sound that caused them. Looking down a long defile, you can sometimes see your own back, very far away, walking the opposite direction, not in mirror but in *delayed continuity*, as though the canyon is a fold in something rather than a cut.

The weeping water, collected, never tastes of stone — it tastes of whatever you most recently thought about. The bright sourceless light occasionally dims for a single heartbeat in a wave that crosses the whole visible range in one synchronized blink, and in that instant every weep-vine in sight flares with a brief, cold phosphorescence, then resumes as before. No one has explained this. No one who stays long enough seems bothered by it anymore.

## Dynamic Character

The place is active rather than static: condensation cycles drawing mist up each dawn and burning it back by midday; the slow pulse of mineral growth and dissolution in the weep-vines; the periodic structural groans and bell-tones of the rock settling into states it does not stay settled in; the irregular pulse-dimming of the light. Nothing here is in true equilibrium — it is *in-balance* the way a held chord is in balance, a tension maintained rather than resolved, established not because the processes have stopped but because they have found a stable loop to run in, over and over, for as long as anyone can tell.

## Rule of the Place

If there is a single law that holds the Sallow Karst together, it might be this: *form persists longer than substance*. The shapes — of mountains, of dwellings, of branching mineral growth, of distance itself — are stable. What fills those shapes is negotiable, temporary, recursive. The architecture remains; the architects do not. The vine-shapes remain; their substance dissolves and reforms nightly. The canyon remains; its length is a suggestion. Anyone who stays here long enough is advised to fix their attention on shapes, not contents — and to remember that the bright wet hum filling the air is the sound of a vast, old, patient *form*, still holding itself together long after whatever first poured into it has gone.
