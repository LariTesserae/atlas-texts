# The Combing Heights

The plateau begins where the lower clouds end, a shelf of high ground that runs for kilometers under a sky the color of skim milk. Cold up here — not bitter, but the kind of cold that sits in wet wool and never quite leaves it. Cold that keeps the mist from ever becoming rain, and the rain, on the rare occasions it commits, from ever quite becoming a storm.

Everything is plant. This is the first and last fact of the place. There is no soil visible anywhere — what was once ground is now eight, ten, fifteen meters of accumulated growth: root-mat over root-mat over the fossilized root-mats of generations past, compressed into something between peat and architecture. You don't walk *on* this land so much as walk *on top of* the lives of everything that grew here before. It gives slightly underfoot, like a mattress that has forgotten how to fully recover.

The dominant growth is a tall, fibrous reed-cane, blue-green going to ash-grey at the tips, growing in stands so dense that individual stalks lose meaning — they become wall, become corridor, become a kind of fibrous fog that you move through rather than past. Between and beneath these stand columnar fungal towers, pale and ribbed like the inside of a ribcage, some leaning, some split, all of them load-bearing in some sense the canopy seems to understand even if no one built it that way. Lower down, in the crawl-spaces between root buttresses, mosses run in unbroken sheets, holding the only standing water in the place — not pools, just damp held in green cupped hands, here and there, never enough to call this a wetland, never little enough to call it dry.

## What moves

This is where the place stops being still.

Despite the cold, despite the quiet, despite no creature larger than a hand's span ever showing itself — the vegetation itself is in a state of open war. Stands collapse overnight under their own mass and are overgrown again within days. You can hear, if you stand long enough in the silence, the occasional low groan-and-crack of a fungal column failing somewhere out of sight, followed — always — by the soft percussive settling of cane stalks falling into the gap, already competing for the new patch of grey light. The place rewrites its own geography on a schedule too fast for erosion and too slow to ever actually witness the motion. You leave a stand of reed at dusk and return at dawn to find the path gone, replaced, rerouted by collapse three meters to the left.

This turbulence is strange given how settled the underlying land is. The plateau itself, the long bones of it, has clearly stood here unchanged for a very long time — the erosion is old, gentle, complete, valleys worn into soft rounded combs by ice ages the cane has long since buried. The instability is all in the green. It's as if the land achieved peace eons ago and the plants never got the message.

## The machine smell

And then, underneath the wet-rot, mushroom, and chlorophyll smell of all this living mass, there's something else: a tang of hot metal and ozone, faint, like a factory floor three valleys over carried up on a thermal. There is no built structure here — not one wall, not one path, nothing made by hand or hand's tool. But the *tech* is in the air anyway. Sometimes a sound arrives that the silence can't absorb: a long mechanical exhale, distant, rhythmic, gone before you can orient to it. Sometimes the cane-stands vibrate in a register too low to be wind. No source is ever found. The cold air seems to carry it from somewhere beyond the visible plateau — a valley industry, a hidden works, something vast and engined operating just past every ridge you climb, never arriving, never confirmed, always one fold of land farther on.

Whether the vegetation grows toward that sound or away from it, no one walking the Heights has stayed long enough to determine.

## Ecology of absence

Fauna is almost a rumor. Once, twice a day, something disturbs a cane-stand at the edge of vision and is gone — a pelt-color, a rustle, a weight briefly transferred to a root-mat that springs back too quickly to hold a print. No birds call. No insects hum. The silence here isn't empty so much as withheld, as if everything capable of sound has agreed, by some old treaty, to let the plants do all the talking — and the plants speak only in slow collapses and the wet creak of new growth taking up the falling weight.

## To walk it

You move through corridors that close behind you. The light stays level and grey-gold all day, filtered twice over — once by the cloud ceiling, once by the cane canopy — so that the hours blur into a single extended afternoon with no real morning or evening to anchor it. The cold keeps your breath visible. The damp keeps your hands cool and the cane-fiber slightly tacky to the touch. And every so often, far off, that engine-sound exhales again, and a thousand stalks lean very slightly toward it, or away, before the silence reclaims the heights and the long slow architecture of growth and collapse resumes its turbulent, ancient, soundless work.
