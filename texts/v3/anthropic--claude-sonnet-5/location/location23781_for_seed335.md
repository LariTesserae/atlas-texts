The place is small enough to miss. That's the first thing to understand about it — you could walk past the lip of it in fog and never know it was there, because it doesn't announce itself with height or breadth. It announces itself with sound.

You find it by following a vibration up through the soles of your boots, a low continuous shudder that doesn't match anything in the visible world — no wind strong enough, no river wide enough — until you crest a shelf of new black rock, still sharp-edged, still unsoftened by time, and the ground simply opens. A hollow, maybe eighty meters across, sunk into a basin of young stone that looks like it cooled last season. The walls are wet everywhere — not streaming, but weeping, a constant sweat that beads and runs and beads again, fed by something below rather than above. There's no sky-fed runoff here; this water comes up from underneath, from a deep press of something hot and mechanical, surfacing cold by the time it reaches air.

Moss has taken the wet rock fast and total — improbably fast, the way things grow when given heat, water, and time measured in single years rather than centuries. Ferns crowd the shadowed pockets, fat-leaved and dripping. Between the moss-shawled boulders are pools, black and still, skinned with a faint iridescent sheen — not oil exactly, something thinner, an industrial residue carried up in solution and left behind when the water gives up its heat to the cold air.

Because that's the thing underneath all of it: the place runs on a machine nobody built and nobody runs. Not literally built — there's no scaffolding, no rivet, no human mark anywhere in the basin — but somewhere below the rock floor there's an engine of geology doing factory work. A heat-body, a pressure-body, churning at a scale and tempo that reads as industrial even though no hand made it: rhythmic discharges of warm vapor through cracks in the stone, hissing on a cycle you can almost set a watch to, a deep thrum that comes up through your feet every forty seconds like a turbine three floors down. The sound dominates everything. It's not ambient — it's structural, a wall of noise that the whole hollow lives inside, punctuated by sharper percussive vents, knocks, a metallic clang-without-metal as superheated water meets cold stone and the rock itself contracts and complains.

The light is low even at midday, filtered through a permanent skin of mist that the warm vents throw up into the cold air, condensing instantly into a ceiling of low fog that never quite clears. It diffuses what daylight gets in, turns it the color of dishwater, soft-edged, shadowless. Visibility maybe thirty meters before everything dissolves into grey.

And into this — heat, wet, noise, dim light — life has poured in disproportionate, almost violent abundance. This is the strange heart of the place: density of matter is low, the basin is mostly empty space, fog, water, rock — but everywhere there is life, it is thick with it. Insects first, clouds of them around the warm vents, drawn to the heat the way moths go to flame, and from the insects everything else follows. Amphibians, fat and slow, sit at the pool edges with skin like wet leather, gulping at the cycle of mist. Small mammals, something rodent-shaped but oddly bare-skinned, adapted maybe to the heat, run trails through the fern-floor that show as darker green tunnels in the moss. Birds — or something bird-adjacent — nest in the high crevices where the warm air rises and keeps eggs from the cold, and their calls layer on top of the engine-thrum, sharp and frequent, arguing with the vents for dominance of the air.

Nothing here is large. Nothing here is built. There are no nests of sticks, no dens dug with intention beyond animal instinct, no path worn by feet that weren't four and unshod. And yet everything moves with the busy, overlapping purpose of a factory floor — predator threading through fern after something smaller, something smaller fleeing into a crack, the vent firing its forty-second cycle regardless of what's near it, scalding nothing because everything has already learned the rhythm and stays clear.

The rules of this place, as far as they can be called rules: heat comes from below, cold presses from above, and the line between them — right at the surface, right at skin-level — is where everything happens. Stray too high out of the basin and the cold flattens the lushness into bare young rock and lichen. Stray too low, too near the actual vents, and the heat empties life out into bare wet stone, scoured and lifeless and steaming. The whole ecology is a ring, a lucky band of habitable temperature circling a process it has no say in, abundant precisely because the conditions are precise, narrow, and temporary — because nothing here is old. The rock says months. The moss says a season or two, at most. This hollow might silt in, might freeze over, might be swallowed by its own vent collapsing inward, and none of the creatures living loud and busy in it now would have any way of knowing, or any reason to stop.
