# The Cistern Heights

The plateau sits at altitude where the air should thin and doesn't — held thick and wet by something the engineers built into the bedrock generations ago, or grew, or summoned, the histories disagree. Cold settles into it anyway, a cold that doesn't bite so much as *soak*, because everything here is wet. Water doesn't fall as rain so much as condense directly out of the dark, beading on every cold surface, running in sheets down towers that haven't finished deciding what shape they're going to be.

The rock underneath is young. Not young like spring — young like still-forming, like the plateau is an argument the planet is still having with itself, basalt and something paler than basalt rising in slow lobes that haven't yet eroded into anything you'd call a hill or a cliff. Raw nubs. Half-shapes. Stone that looks poured rather than uplifted, with skins of mineral glaze where water has run over it ten thousand times and left translucent terraces, ledges like cooled wax, steps that lead nowhere because the next step hasn't precipitated out yet.

No plants. None. Whatever green this world might have had, it skipped it, or it's still further down the queue. Instead: the city.

It rises out of the wet rock in dense ranks — not a skyline so much as a thicket, towers and lattices and structures whose function you couldn't guess crowded close enough that their outlines merge into single black masses against a sky with no light to speak of. The built material doesn't look poured or stacked; it looks *grown* by methods that have opinions about thermodynamics you weren't taught. Surfaces that shouldn't hold weight, hold weight. Bridges between towers with no visible support, vibrating very slightly, always, with a hum below hearing that you feel in your teeth. This is tech that has stopped explaining itself to anyone, including, maybe, its makers.

There is no light. Not absence-of-light like a closed room — absence-of-light like the dark is a medium the city moves through deliberately, broken only by faint internal glows deep inside structural joints, a few hundred sourceless gleams scattered up the towers like something bioluminescent decided to live in the walls. You navigate by sound and by the wet shine on stone, by silhouette against the marginally less-black middle distance.

And the sound is enormous. This is the loudest kind of place — not chaotic-loud but *populated*-loud, the loudness of overwhelming numbers of bodies. Because the fauna here defies the missing vegetation entirely: things live on this plateau in unaccountable density. Not herds — *masses*. Calls that ricochet between towers and come back changed, wingbeats in numbers that displace the wet air in gusts, clicking and chittering and long low moans pitched to travel through stone, the slap and scrabble of bodies moving across wet rock in coordinated surges. Whatever they eat, it isn't plants, and whatever ecology supports this density without a single visible producer at the base of the chain is one of the plateau's open arguments — maybe they eat the condensation-borne minerals, maybe they eat each other in cycles too slow to watch, maybe the city itself feeds them, vents of something nutritive bleeding from the infrastructure into troughs and pools where things gather to feed in the dark, audible only as a frenzy of wet sound.

Everything moves slowly at the scale that matters. The towers don't change shape day to day — they change over years, decades, the rock beneath still extruding its half-finished forms at a geological murmur. The animal masses surge and recede in patterns that take seasons to complete a cycle. Only the small-scale motion is fast: a wing, a slap, a chittering swarm crossing a bridge in seconds. The big rhythms are patient, almost sleeping, while the small ones scream over top of them constantly.

Walking it: you'd feel the cold first, the wet second, soaking through within minutes. Underfoot, stone slick with mineral sheen, never quite solid-feeling, like the ground hasn't finished agreeing to hold still. Towers lean in close overhead, dripping, glowing faintly at their seams, humming with that buried frequency. The dark presses close, but it's a *crowded* dark — you can feel, more than see, the press of moving bodies somewhere near, the city's masses of fauna surging through unlit channels between buildings, their sound arriving in waves that overlap into a continuous roar threaded with discrete shrieks. Every so often a strand of light spills from a structural seam and catches, for a half-second, the wet gleam of too many eyes, too many wings, too many backs, moving in a current that has nowhere obvious to go and goes there anyway, patiently, forever, while the rock keeps deciding what it wants to become.
