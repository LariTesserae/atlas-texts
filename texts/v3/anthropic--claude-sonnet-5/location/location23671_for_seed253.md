# The Drowned Spires of Velken Tor

## Approach

You come upon it from above, always from above, because there is no other way to come upon it. Cloud-cover or true darkness — the distinction has stopped mattering by the time you arrive — and below you, no, around you, an architecture of impossible height stands chest-deep, throat-deep, crown-deep in black water that does not move with tide because there is no moon left to pull it, or there never was.

The mountains here did not erode into this shape. They were carved, once, by hands or by something with hands' intentions, into towers and terraces and stairs that wound for what must have been religious or administrative or unknowable purposes, and then the water came — 2.9 of it, nearly total, a flood that arrived so long ago it has become a kind of bedrock itself — and never left. Not a flood in the sense of event. A flood in the sense of *permanent condition*. The water is not rising. The water is not falling. The water is simply how high the water is, the way gravity is simply how hard things fall.

## Vertical Geography

Elevation maxed, water nearly maxed: this is the central fact. Towers — call them towers, though they might have been temples, granaries, the vertebrae of some buried god — rise out of the flood at intervals that feel deliberate but are too far apart to be sure. Between any two visible structures: open black water, sometimes a kilometer of it, sometimes more, scaled to a valley's worth of emptiness. You travel between them and the travel takes a long time and nothing changes while you do it.

The towers themselves are drowned from some indeterminate point downward — twenty meters, two hundred, no way to know, the water keeps its own counsel — and rise from that drowned base into open, dark air for anywhere from ten to four hundred meters above the surface. Their upper reaches are not towers anymore by the time vegetation gets hold of them. They are armatures. Scaffolding with a green and growing skin.

## The Vegetation, Which Is Not Decoration

This is the second fact, and it is total, and it is the only thing that has happened to this place in longer than memory.

Every exposed stone surface, every ledge, every ruined window-arch and stair-landing and parapet, is given over entirely to plant matter that has had so much time it has stopped behaving like plants behave anywhere else. It does not climb the towers. It *is* the towers, now, in any meaningful structural sense — vine-trunks as thick as the original masonry, grown so old and so woven that in places you cannot tell where worked stone ends and living wood begins, because the wood has been load-bearing for longer than the stone has been standing alone. Drape-mosses the size of collapsed roofs. Canopy-ferns the size of cathedral transepts, holding pools of standing rainwater in their crowns that have their own ecosystems, their own scaled-down version of this same drowned-stillness, repeating.

Nothing here is dense in the way a jungle is dense. The growth is total but the *space* is sparse — this is important, this is the unusual thing — because everything that grows here has grown to enormous, ancient, singular scale, and singular enormous things leave space between them. A single fern-crown forty meters across. Then nothing — open dark water-air — for two hundred meters. Then another. The vegetation has filled every available foothold completely and has nothing left to do but get larger and older in the spaces it already claimed. It is a forest with the density of a graveyard. Each growth is a monument to itself.

## Stillness as Law

Nothing changes here, and this is not poetic — it is closer to a physical law of the place. The water level: fixed. The growth: so slow it might as well be fixed, each plant having reached whatever final, load-bearing, water-stabilized form it was always going to reach, centuries or millennia ago, and simply persisting there now in equilibrium with its own weight. No seasons reach this depth of antiquity. No storm has rearranged a single drape-moss in longer than any visiting consciousness could verify. The place is not dying and is not really alive in the growing-changing sense either. It has achieved a final form and is now simply *occupying* that form, the way a held breath occupies a chest.

## What Lives Here

And yet — fauna, abundant, loud. This is the place's contradiction, or its mercy, depending on temperament.

Despite the stillness of the structure, the place is full of voices. Things that nest in the fern-crowns and hunt the standing pools. Things that have given up flight for swimming and given up swimming for something in between, moving through the black water with a heaviness that suggests they've made peace with never breaking its surface again. Colonies of pale, fat-bodied roosting things clustered along the underside of every overhang, audible before visible — a constant low conversational roar, clicks and long groaning calls that travel oddly across the water, arriving from directions that don't match their sources. Something large surfaces, very rarely, with a sound like a door closing underwater, and is gone before the ripples reach you, and the ripples take a very long time to reach you because the water is so still that ripples are a kind of news, here, an event of some local importance.

The loudness of the place sits strangely against its silence of *change*. Everything sounds; nothing moves the architecture. It is a held, occupied stillness, filled wall-to-wall with the noise of creatures who have always lived exactly this way and will always live exactly this way, their calling no more a sign of progress or development than the dripping of the moss-crowns is a sign of rain.

## Light, or Its Absence

Darkness near-total. What little light exists comes secondhand — a bioluminescent fringe along certain water-pooled fern-crowns, pale blue-green, attracting and revealing small swarms of something insect-adjacent that never quite resolve into shape before they're gone. Occasionally, far off, between two distant towers, a paler patch of sky-water-nothing that might be where a moon would be if this place still kept track of one.

Mostly you navigate by sound and by the cold, mineral smell of very old wet stone underneath very old wet growth — a smell with two thousand years of moss-rot folded into it and no sharpness left, just a kind of permanent green dampness that has stopped being a smell of anything in particular and become simply the smell of the air itself.

## Temperature and Texture

Cool, not cold — 0.8, a held-breath chill, water-cooled air that never warms because there is no sun reaching it directly and never quite freezes because there is too much mass of black water beneath holding a deep, even, ancient temperature. Stone under hand is slick, soft at the edges where centuries of moss have eaten the worked corners into rounded organic shapes. You can no longer find a true right angle anywhere below the waterline-equivalent height; above it, in the dry air, the carved geometry survives a little longer before vanishing under growth, like a voice trailing off mid-sentence.

## The Rule of the Place

If this landscape has a single law, it is this: everything that was going to happen here has already happened, long enough ago that even the memory of change has worn smooth. The flood arrived. The towers drowned to whatever point they drowned to. The plants climbed as high as plants can climb and then simply continued existing at that height, forever, getting old instead of getting different. The animals settled into niches exactly fitted to a world that doesn't change, and so they don't change either, only call to each other, endlessly, abundantly, in the dark, across water that holds their voices and returns nothing back but echo and time.

You are not visiting a place in the middle of its story. You are visiting the place a story arrived at, long ago, and was satisfied to stop.
