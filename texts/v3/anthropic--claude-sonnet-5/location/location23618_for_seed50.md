# The Cistern at the World's Top

You reach it by climbing, which is the wrong direction for water to be going. The trail switchbacks up raw, unweathered rock — stone so newly exposed it still has the brittle look of something cracked open rather than worn smooth, edges sharp enough to open a knuckle, no moss, no soil skin, no patience yet laid over it by time. And then the trail simply stops at a lip, and below that lip is not a valley.

It is a basin of sky-high water that should not hold together at this elevation, and yet it roars.

## The Pour

Picture a lake standing nearly vertical, or perhaps it's better to say: picture the idea of a lake that hasn't yet decided to obey gravity in any single direction. Water arrives from above in a curtain too wide to see the edges of, falls partway, then seems to *catch* — eddying upward in places, sheeting sideways in others, as if the basin itself is still negotiating where down is. The sound is total. Not a roar so much as a pressure, a wall of white noise that removes the possibility of your own voice, that you feel in your sternum and teeth before you register it in your ears. There's no speaking here. There's only gesture, and even gesture gets washed thin in the spray.

The light is ordinary, almost insultingly so — a clean, neutral daylight that falls evenly across all this violence, making the chaos legible rather than ominous. You can see everything. That's almost worse.

## What the Water Hasn't Finished

Because erosion here is barely begun, the rock under the water-violence is still raw — you can see fresh fracture planes, conchoidal scars, stone that looks bitten rather than carved. The cistern is *new*. Something — a quake, a collapse, a birth — opened this high basin recently, geologically speaking, and the water rushed to fill a shape that hasn't settled into its final form. Ledges crumble as you watch, not dramatically, but with small, frequent concessions: a fist of stone calving every few minutes into the spray, swallowed instantly, becoming nothing, becoming part of the basin's slow self-revision. This is a place still being decided.

## Sparse Green, Rare Life

Vegetation clings in pockets so isolated they read as accidents — a stand of wiry, cold-adapted reed-grass wedged into a crevice that the spray hasn't yet scoured clean, a single contorted dwarf-shrub rooted in a crack, leaves rattling uselessly against a sound that drowns even the wind. You will walk for what feels like a long time between these pockets. The space between them is hollow, near-void — bare wet stone, mist, and the white roar.

Animal life is rarer still. A pale, long-legged thing — bird or not-bird, hard to say at this elevation in this light — stalks the margins where spray and stone meet, impossibly steady on legs that seem to read the chaos and step accordingly, never wrong-footed. You see it twice. You're not sure if it's the same one.

## The Hands That Were Here

Scattered, not settled: a low ring of stacked stones, unmortared, half-collapsed, sited at a place where the spray seems to part very slightly — whether by chance or by some calculation made by whoever stacked these stones, you can't tell. A few worked flints near the ring, chipped not cast, the only tech this place admits. Something like a basin carved by hand into a flat rock, sized for collecting a cupped handful of water, now permanently overflowing. No roof, no walls, nothing that suggests anyone meant to *stay*. These feel like the marks of people who came up to be near this, did something small and deliberate, and went back down.

You start to suspect the ring of stones isn't shelter. It's an instrument — a place to stand where the sound organizes itself into something almost rhythmic, almost intelligible, if you stand exactly there and let your skull resonate with the basin instead of fighting it.

## The Wrongness

Here is the part that doesn't resolve: water shouldn't gather like this at such a height, shouldn't fall *upward* in places, shouldn't hold a near-vertical sheet across open air without some structure you can't see holding it. There's no rim downstream big enough to explain where all this water *goes* — it seems to simply have always been falling, always been arriving, with no visible source above the basin's lip and no visible drain below it. A closed loop, or a place where water is being generated rather than merely passing through. Stand long enough, watching the light move evenly across all of it, and you start to feel that this isn't a flood pooling in a high place by accident. It's being *made* here, freshly, continuously — this whole roaring vertical lake is closer to a wound that hasn't stopped bleeding than a lake that has finished arriving.

The rock keeps cracking. The water keeps falling sideways and up and through itself. The stones someone stacked still stand, mostly, marking a spot where a person once decided this deafening, raw, half-finished place was worth climbing all the way up to, and worth touching with hands, and worth leaving something small and careful behind.
