# The Greenhouse Wards

There is a district, low and small, built into the crook of a dead river-channel, where a town once put up walls of brick and iron and then, for reasons no one living quite remembers, let the plants take the insides. Not abandoned — the place is industrial still, the engines run, the lights burn at the normal hour — but somewhere a century back a decision was made, or a negligence allowed, and now the vegetation is the architecture as much as the brick is. You walk through it less like a town and more like the inside of something that breathes slowly and on its own schedule.

**Terrain and structure.** The ground is low, barely above where the river used to bend, and it has been established this way long enough that nothing about it feels new. Cobbled lanes run between brick-and-iron facades, but the cobbles have settled into shallow basins, mossed at the seams, and the buildings themselves — three and four storeys, soot-darkened, riveted at the corners with old plate — have long since stopped being separate from what grows on them. Walls are sheathed in thick climbing growth, decades-thick, woody at the base like trunks, the vines themselves load-bearing in places where the original mortar gave out and nobody minded. Roofs have gone to canopy: broad-leafed things, fern-crowned chimneys, a few date-pale flowering vines that only open for an hour near dusk. The town's old engineering — gantries, pipe-runs, a skeletal gasworks tower at the ward's eastern edge — stands inside the green rather than beside it, rust-streaked iron wrapped root-tight by something that has decided the metal is just another kind of trellis.

The scale is intimate: this is not a city, it's a ward, a hamlet's worth of streets, maybe a dozen structures and the spaces between them, bounded by the old channel-bank on one side and a brick perimeter wall, half-collapsed and wholly overgrown, on the others. You could walk its full length in twenty minutes if the canopy let you walk in straight lines, which it does not.

**Vegetation as substrate.** This is the dominant fact of the place. Plant matter doesn't merely occupy space here, it *is* the space — the total mass of green is so thick that air itself takes on a chlorophyll tint in the slanting light, and walking down what was once a street means walking through alternating bands of clear cobble and root-buckled understory, never quite sure which is original ground. Sparseness shows itself only in pockets: a cleared market square, kept open by some unspoken civic habit, where a single fountain (dry, fern-choked at its basin) sits surrounded by hard-packed dirt and nothing else; an old loading yard where iron rail-tracks remain bare and silver because trains, or what serve as trains, still run there twice a day. Outside these scraped clearings, growth closes back in immediately and totally — vine-throttled lampposts, fig roots cracking foundation stones into slow geological drift, moss laid over machinery like a sheet pulled over sleeping equipment.

**Dampness, not wet.** Water here is a rumor more than a presence. The dead river left only intermittent dampness behind: condensation beads on cool iron in the mornings, runs in thin tracks down brick where pipes sweat in the cool air. There are no standing pools, no streams — just a held humidity in the deep green interior, a damp hush among the leaves that never resolves into actual rain except rarely, and when it does come, it comes as a soft, short fall that the canopy absorbs before it reaches the ground, so you hear it more than feel it.

**Industry, embedded.** The tech level is real and unhidden: this was and is a working ward. A small power-station hums somewhere under its growth, vented through a chimney now wrapped to its crown in a woody vine that seems unbothered by the heat. Conveyor rails run between two structures, slatted with rust, half-swallowed by groundcover but still moving, twice daily, hauling something — crated goods, perhaps cuttings, perhaps fuel — between buildings whose function nobody passing through could now guess. Streetlamps are gas-fed, lit by a lamplighter mechanism that still fires at dusk regardless of how thoroughly the lamp's glass is shrouded in leaf. The infrastructure persists, stubbornly, underneath and through the green, neither winning nor losing the argument with the plant life, the two simply having reached an old accommodation.

**Atmosphere and light.** Light here is ordinary, not dim, not dramatic — daylight filtered to a soft green-gold by canopy, occasionally breaking clean through where a building's collapse opened a gap straight up to sky. Cool air, always; this isn't a hothouse-warm jungle but something more temperate, almost autumnal in feel even when the vegetation is at its most lush, a coolness that keeps the moss soft instead of slick and keeps the smell green rather than rotten.

**Sound and ecology.** Mostly quiet. The dominant sound, if you stop to notice it, is leaf-friction — a low constant whisper as canopy moves in whatever air finds its way down between buildings — undercut by the occasional industrial sound: a distant compressor cycling, the conveyor's rail-clatter, a steam-valve venting somewhere with a long exhale. Fauna is rare almost to the point of folklore — a one-in-a-visit sighting of something moving in the high leaf-cover, more rustle than shape, and birds heard far more than seen, their calls oddly delayed-sounding as if filtered through all that intervening green. Insects are more honest about their numbers but still hushed, a low ambient tick and whir rather than a roar.

**Dynamism.** Nothing here is static, despite how settled and established it all looks. This is the place's great quiet trick: erosion has done its slow work for so long that every form feels finished, permanent, weathered into its rightful shape — and yet the system is in continuous motion underneath that calm. Vines add a visible hand's-width of new growth between one week and the next. The flowering night-vines open and close on their hour with mechanical regularity. The conveyor runs its schedule. Condensation beads, drips, dries, beads again. Roots work at foundations with geological patience that nonetheless never stops. It is a place that has arrived at a settled *form* while remaining, underneath that form, a live and working system — biology and industry each metabolizing at their own pace, indifferent to which one the ward's original builders meant to be in charge.

**The unusual note.** What unsettles slightly, on a second or third pass through, is the sense that the vegetation isn't simply *colonizing* the architecture but has been, in some unspoken way, *incorporated* into its maintenance — that the vine load-bearing the cracked lintel isn't an accident of neglect but is, in some sense, doing a job; that the moss over the machinery isn't fouling it but insulating it; that someone, or something, made a decision long ago to let this happen on purpose, and the ward has simply been running smoothly ever since on that arrangement, town and growth as joint tenants, neither one a guest in the other's house.
