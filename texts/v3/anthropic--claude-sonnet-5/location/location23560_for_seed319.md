# The Cistern Terraces

The town sits on a plateau that was once the floor of an inland sea, and the salt of that absence is everywhere — in the white crust along the gutters, in the taste of the wind, in the way the stone has settled into long ledges and risers like the rings of a drained bathtub a thousand years deep. Elevation here means distance, not peaks: you climb gradually for hours and arrive somewhere that simply feels closer to the sky, the air thinner and warmer, the light unfiltered and slightly too even.

Water does not fall here so much as it *arrives*, seeping upward through fissures in the old seabed, collecting in stone basins that the original builders carved directly into the terrace risers centuries ago. Every street has a cistern mouth, round and weathered smooth, breathing a faint coolness into the heat. The masonry above is darker where capillary damp has crept up through it for generations, a tide line frozen mid-rise, never reaching the windowsills, never quite drying either. Nothing is lush. Vegetation comes in stubborn, isolated outbreaks — a fig root splitting a foundation block, scrub-grass colonizing a drainage crack, lichen the color of rust spreading across anything left undisturbed for more than a decade. The sparseness of green only makes the architecture more total: this is a built landscape first, a natural one only at its edges and in its plumbing.

The town is large — spilling across several terraced shelves of the old seabed, joined by ramps, funiculars, and iron stairways that groan with disuse and overuse in equal measure depending on the hour — and yet it is not dense. Buildings stand with real gaps between them: yards of bare salt-pale ground, old foundations left as plinths, wide unpaved intervals where something was clearly meant to be built and never was. The skyline reads as industrial rather than residential: chimneys, gantries, the cross-braced silhouettes of pumping derricks drawing brine and water up from below in long unhurried strokes. Pipes run aboveground in iron trunks the width of a person, painted and repainted, dripping faintly at every union joint, feeding the cisterns, feeding the foundries, feeding something beneath the lower terraces that nobody quite discusses by name.

And it is loud — extraordinarily, structurally loud, a sound that doesn't spike but simply *is*, all day, every day: pump-engines thudding in their housings, belt-driven flywheels singing at slightly different pitches across six terraces so the dissonance never resolves, steam venting in long exhalations from relief valves bolted at odd intervals to the cistern walls, the iron stairways ringing under boots, hammering from a foundry shed two terraces down arriving slightly delayed and slightly distorted by the open air between. It is the sound of a machine landscape too large to ever go fully silent, even at 3 a.m., even when nobody is visibly awake. People here speak by leaning close. Children learn to read mouths before they're four.

The change is slow despite all this noise — the engines are old, the schedules generational, the maintenance crews patch rather than replace, and the town's rhythm is geological more than mechanical: pumps that have run continuously for ninety years, salt deposits that thicken by a finger's width each decade, terrace walls that have shifted maybe four inches since anyone started measuring. Erosion has had its say and mostly finished saying it — the risers are weathered into soft, definite shapes, corners rounded, stair-treads dished from feet, and the whole town has the settled, unkillable look of a place that argued with time long ago and reached a truce.

Fauna is scarce and specific: pale moths drawn to the relief-valve steam at dusk, a population of small lean dogs that live around the foundry sheds and seem to tolerate the noise as weather, the odd hawk riding thermals off the warm terrace stone, uninterested in anything below. No birdsong competes with the engines; it simply isn't present, as if the soundscape selected against it.

The strangeness is not in any single object but in the cumulative logic of the place — the cisterns that seem to breathe in time with the pumps, slow exhalations of cooler air timed almost too precisely to the engine cycles; the fact that water rises from below in a landscape with no visible source, no river, no rain to speak of, just a patient upward seep that the original engineers apparently caught onto centuries ago and built an entire civic body around without ever explaining why the water comes or where; the abandoned foundation-plinths scattered through the gaps between buildings, never reclaimed, never built on, as though something about them is understood, without being spoken, to be off-limits.

At evening the light goes long and brass-colored across the terraces, the steam plumes turn gold before they thin into the sky, and the noise — if anything — settles into something almost rhythmic, a town-sized machine breathing out its working day, slow and certain, exactly as it has for longer than anyone living can well account for.
