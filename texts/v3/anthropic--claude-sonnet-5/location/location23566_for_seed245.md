# The Wet Forge

There is no record of who began it, and no one left who could finish it. What exists is a basin without edges, low and flat, vast beyond the report of any single eye — and across its whole vast flatness, something is building itself, badly, out of nothing.

Call it built, because nothing here is original ground. Whatever substrate this place once had has been replaced, structure laid on structure, by hands or by something that worked like hands. But call it also unfinished, because the building has no plan and no rest. Walls rise out of the steaming murk at strange angles, mortared in courses of dark brick that sweat as they cure, and before they reach a height that could be called a height, they slump, or split, or are absorbed back into the ground they grew from. The basin is a worksite with no foreman, no blueprint, and no end of materials — and the materials are, themselves, not quite finished being materials. Bricks behave like bricks until you set weight on them, then remember they are something else.

**Ground and water.** The floor of the basin is not soil. It is fired clay, kiln-pale in patches, black-glazed in others, laid in tiles the size of doors that don't quite meet at their joins. Through every seam, water rises — not rain, not river, but a slow upwelling from beneath the tilework, as if the whole plain were built over a body that sweats. It pools in shallow sheets that mirror a sky the color of a struck bruise, then it's gone, drawn back down through the same seams an hour later, leaving salt-rings and a smell like hot iron left out in mist. The air sits at the edge of condensation always; nothing dries, nothing floods. Heat presses down and the wet rises up to meet it, and between the two, everything is left perpetually clammy, perpetually warm, like the inside of something alive that has no skin.

**What is being built.** Structures occur with the spacing of outposts in an empty country — sparse, deliberate-looking, though no two share a style. A kiln the size of a cathedral, still warm, its draft-holes breathing a heat-shimmer that bends the horizon. A half-raised colonnade of unfired brick columns, eleven of them standing, three lying in rubble, one in the act of falling, frozen mid-collapse for longer than collapse should take — held there, leaning, dust suspended off its crumbling base in a slow grey comma that never finishes falling and never resets. Nearby, low kilns of cruder make, hand-shaped, fired with what must be dung or scrub-fuel, though there is no scrub anywhere to be found — only the dead architecture of fuel-use, evidence without source.

Tools exist. Simple ones: stone-headed mauls left leaning on wet brick, wooden frames for forming clay blocks, twine of unknown fiber binding bundles of fired tile for transport that never comes. No hands hold them. They wait the way tools wait at the end of a workday, except the workday never ends and the workers never arrive, and some of the tools are older than the bricks beside them and some are newer than the rust that should not yet have formed on their fittings.

**The breakdown.** This is the place's true nature: it builds and unbuilds out of order. A wall completes itself in the time it takes to look away and look back — courses of brick stacking with no mason, mortar smoothing with no trowel, finished in eyeblink-time, while a hundred yards off another wall of identical make is still being laid one brick an hour by nothing, by no one, with the patience of geology. Time pools here unevenly, the way the water does. You may watch a kiln-door swing shut and rust shut and rot off its hinge within the held duration of a single breath. You may stand before an unfinished arch for what feels an hour and find, on looking down, that your shadow has not moved at all.

Sometimes a structure is built and unbuilt in the same gesture — rises and crumbles as one continuous motion, like a wave that is simultaneously cresting and having already broken. The eye can't hold it as one event. It registers as two memories with no moment between them.

**Light.** Dim, always, though never night. The bruise-grey sky has no sun that can be located, only a directionless glow, hot to stand under, as though the heat-source and the light-source have been separated and only the light bothered to show up. Shadows fall the wrong way from the architecture — sometimes toward the glow rather than away — and occasionally a shadow will belong to a structure that is no longer there, a dark column lying on the wet clay with nothing above it to cast it.

**Sound and silence.** Mostly quiet, but the quiet has texture: the suck and settle of water moving through clay-seams, the long groan of brick finding or losing its shape, a sound like breathing from inside the great kiln that might be thermal expansion and might be something using the kiln as a lung. No voices. No footsteps but possibly your own. Once in a long while, very far off, a sound like a wall the size of a hill falling all at once, a single percussive event with no aftermath — no dust cloud reaches you, no tremor follows. Sound has arrived alone, unaccompanied by its cause, the way a smell from a kitchen tells you the meal is already started without anyone telling you when.

**Ecology.** None, in the animal or vegetable sense. No green thing has ever needed to grow here, and nothing has ever needed to eat. But the place is not lifeless in the way of a true desert: it has the busy stillness of an anthill mid-collapse, that hyperactive sourceless industry, as though life's work — building, gathering, shaping — has been extracted whole from the living things that used to do it and left running on its own, unsupervised, in the dark.

**Rules, such as they're inferable.** Nothing decays in straightforward sequence; entropy is suggestion here, not law. Distance is generous but not honest — the basin is vast enough to host its own weather (you may watch a localized squall walk across the middle distance, dragging a curtain of warm rain that never reaches you, dissolving before it crosses whatever line marks "your" weather from "theirs") yet two kilns that appear a day's walk apart may, on the third hour of walking, turn out to have been the same kiln, approached from its other face. Tools and bricks are the only stable nouns; everything built from them is a verb pretending to be a noun, an action caught and labeled as a thing, and likely to remember, without warning, that it is actually still happening.

To walk here is to walk through an act of construction that has lost the thread of its own intention — vast, hot, wet-aired, scattered with the evidence of patient hands that are not present and never were quite present, building something that will, by the time you've named it, already be three other things, and rubble, and beginning again.
