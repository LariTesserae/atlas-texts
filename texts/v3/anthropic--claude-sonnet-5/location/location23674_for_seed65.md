# The Standing Heights

The plateau does not end so much as it surrenders to sky. You reach it after the long climb through switchbacks worn into bare rock — not by tools, but by the passage of hooves and feet across some span of time too large to hold in the mind — and then the ground simply stops rising and stretches instead, flat and vast and silver-grey under a light so bright it has weight.

This is the upper world. Below, perhaps, there is weather, river-cut valleys, green things crowding each other for soil. None of that survives the climb. Here the air is cool and thin and scrubbed clean, and the sun does not warm so much as illuminate — a hard, declarative light with no haze to soften it, throwing every crack in the stone into black relief. Shadows here are sharp-edged things, drawn with a straightedge.

The rock is old in the way that doesn't perform its age — no dramatic spires, no tortured hoodoos, just enormous tilted slabs and rounded shoulders of stone, smoothed by wind and old ice into a kind of settled dignity. Erosion has done its work and stopped; nothing here is still being carved. It sits in whatever balance it found, ages ago, and will sit there ages more. Lichen colonizes the flatter surfaces in patches of rust and grey-green, the only vegetation with the patience for this altitude — that and the occasional cushion plant, low and tight and grey-leaved, wedged into a wind-shadow crack, flowering for perhaps two weeks a year in some color no one is present to name.

Water exists here as rumor and exception. A seep darkens a rockface for an hour after the rare rain and is gone by evening. Snowmelt pools in stone basins worn smooth and round, standing dark and cold and utterly still, reflecting sky with such fidelity that from a distance they look like punched-through holes into some clearer world underneath this one. You can walk for an hour and not see water, then find six basins in a single hollow, none touching, none flowing into the next.

The space is open in the way that erases scale. A ridge that looks ten minutes away is two hours away. There is so little in the field of view to argue with the eye that distance becomes a kind of guesswork, a story you tell yourself walking.

And yet — for all this emptiness, all this thin cold silver air — the place roars.

It is the fauna. Enormous flocks of high-country birds — something between gull and raptor in build, pale-bellied, dark-winged, riding thermals that spiral up off the sun-baked stone — wheel overhead in numbers that should not be sustainable on lichen and whatever small things live beneath the rock, and yet are. Their calls layer on top of each other into something less like birdsong and more like a standing wall of sound, a roar made of ten thousand individual cries, rising and falling but never actually stopping. Beneath them, herds move across the flats — heavy-shouldered, sure-footed animals with horns swept back, that graze on the cushion-plants and lichen with apparent contentment and then, at some signal no human eye can catch, thunder across the stone in a mass that makes the ground itself seem to hum. Their hooves on bare rock are a sound like collapsing architecture, like something enormous breaking, over and over, for the ninety seconds it takes the herd to cross from one horizon-feature to the next.

Smaller things rustle and chitter in the rock-cracks — colonies of something burrow-dwelling, never quite seen, only heard, a constant undertone of chirp and scrabble beneath the bird-roar and the hoof-thunder. The whole plateau is loud in a way that contradicts its emptiness — there is almost nothing to see, structurally, and yet the ear is assaulted, overwhelmed, given no rest. Silence here would be the unnatural thing. Silence here would mean something had gone wrong.

There is no settlement. No cairn, no wall, no fire-ring. If people come here — and the worn switchback trail suggests they sometimes do — they come as a passage, not a habitation, carrying simple things: a walking staff cut from some lower-elevation wood, a skin for water, a knife of hammered or cold-worked metal, nothing more sophisticated than what hands and patience and a low fire can make. There is no metallurgy up here, no forge — only what is carried up and carried down again. Whatever culture knows this place treats it as a threshold, not a home. The cold and thinness make it clear that lingering is not what this place is for.

What strangeness there is comes sideways, not from monsters or magic but from proportion gone slightly wrong: the eerie perfection of the meltwater basins, so round and still they look made; the way the great herds turn in unison without any visible cue, as if the whole animal mass shares one nervous system; the way sound carries across the open plateau with no decay, so that a hoof-strike a mile off arrives at full, undiminished volume, just delayed, a thunder with no corresponding lightning anyone can find. None of it is wrong, exactly. It simply doesn't behave the way distance usually demands.

And underneath all of it, the place does not change. The herds will cross again tomorrow along the same line. The birds will rise on the same thermals off the same sun-warmed slabs. The basins will hold the same water at the same stillness until the next rare rain disturbs them and they settle back to glass within the hour. There is no spring coming, no winter retreating — only the held, bright, roaring permanence of a place that reached its form long ago and felt no need to keep becoming anything else.

You do not stay. You climbed up out of some lower, softer world, and you will climb back down into it, and the plateau will not notice either direction. It has the birds, and the herds, and the light, and the enormous, indifferent noise of being exactly, eternally, itself.
