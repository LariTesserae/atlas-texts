# The Standing Districts

The plateau begins where the clouds usually end, a tableland so wide that its far edges curve out of sight beneath their own haze. This is a high country, lifted long ago and worn since into a kind of permanent middle-age — not raw, not ancient-smooth, but settled into the particular shapes that centuries of wind and water carve into stone that has stopped arguing.

What stands here was built, and built at the scale of cities, but without a single tool's fingerprint anywhere in it. Towers of layered rock rise in dense clusters across the tableland — not natural formations exactly, though no hand shaped them, and not ruins, though they have the gravity of architecture. Call them what the few who've crossed this place call them: the Standing Districts. Mineral towers, grown rather than carved, accreted over geologic patience by water moving through stone the way blood moves through tissue — sheet after sheet of dissolved mineral, laid down, lifted up, laid down again, until what stands now are spires and partition-walls and roofed hollows that mimic, with eerie fidelity, the logic of streets and plazas and processional avenues. Districts. Quarters. A city plan with no citizens, written by the patient hydraulics of a wet plateau over a timescale longer than the word "history" can comfortably hold.

The water that built this place hasn't left. It's 1.9 out of 3 — not flooding, not drowning, but everywhere just beneath notice. The rock faces weep. Thin black seep-lines run down every vertical surface, drying and re-wetting on a cycle tied to a weather system this place is large enough to generate on its own — clouds form against the towers' upper reaches, condense, fall as a fine cold rain that doesn't so much arrive as *coalesce*, and drain into the thousand vertical channels the mineral has carved for it. Shallow pools gather in the lee of every district, brown-clear, fed from underneath as much as above, never quite evaporating before the next cycle replenishes them. Walk anywhere here and your boots will find a film of water sitting on stone, cold enough to ache through leather, present even when the sky above is, for the moment, dry.

The vegetation is sparse and knows its place. Lichen — vast sheets of it, grey-green and black, the only true color in a landscape otherwise built from stone-tones and water-shine — colonizes the lower walls of every tower in patches that look almost cartographic, like continents on a map nobody drew. Cushion mosses pack themselves into the wet seams between standing slabs. Here and there, in a pocket of accumulated grit at a tower's base, something has managed a low woody scrub, bent permanently sideways by wind, holding maybe a dozen square meters of actual rooted plant life — an event significant enough that the rare animals here treat each one like a landmark.

And the animals are rare, true to form, but not absent. A high-country bird with a long curved bill works the seep-lines for whatever small life clings there — you hear it before you see it, and even that is infrequent, a single dry call crossing a gap between towers and taking a long moment to die out in the open air. Some kind of pale, soft-footed mammal — fox-sized, fox-shaped, never quite seen clearly — leaves prints in the mineral grit and is gone. Mostly, though, this is a place without witnesses. The districts stand empty of anything that watches back.

Quiet governs everything. The scale of the place is vast — towers receding in ranked silhouette for what might be a hundred kilometers, the plateau itself wide enough to hold three separate weather fronts at once, visible simultaneously as distinct grey columns of rain working different districts at different times — and yet the sound is almost nothing. Wind moves through the gaps between towers and comes out as a low constant exhale, more felt than heard. Water drips with a particular flat acoustic, swallowed quickly by stone that doesn't ring. Footsteps, if there were more feet to make them, would carry a long way in this hush; as it is, there's almost nothing to carry.

The light sits at an honest normal — no drama, no golden saturation, no oppressive grey. It's the light of a high plateau under variable cloud: bright when the sun finds a gap, flat and pearl-grey when it doesn't, the towers changing color by the hour from bone-white to wet charcoal to a faint mineral blue in the gaps where iron has stained the rock. There's enough light, always, to see the next three districts ahead and the haze that swallows everything past them.

This is a place still actively becoming itself — the dynamic rating earns its keep. The Districts aren't a finished monument; they're mid-process, the same hydraulic engine that built them over millennia still running, still adding sheet by sheet to towers that will, ten thousand years from now, look subtly different than they do today. Seasonal cycles of freeze and thaw split thin slabs free with a sound like a held breath finally let go — rare, but when it happens, it's the loudest event in days, a long grinding crack followed by the slower settling rattle of debris finding new angles of rest. New pools open. Old channels silt closed. A district that was passable last wet season might require a different route this one, the water having quietly rewritten the geometry of its own architecture again.

There are no rules here beyond the water's. No paths but the ones water has already chosen and a traveler can choose to follow. No structure has a door, though many have openings that function as one, worn through by drainage rather than design. To move through the Standing Districts is to move through the inside of an enormous, slow, mineral idea — a city assembled by chemistry, inhabited by lichen and silence, perpetually rained on by weather it makes for itself, vast enough that no single vantage will ever show you all of it, and old enough, established enough, that it has stopped being surprised by anything that happens to it. It simply continues. The water comes down. The stone takes its time. Somewhere out past the visible ranks of towers, another front is gathering, and in a few hours it will be here, and after it leaves, one more sheet of mineral, microns thick, will have set into the long architecture of the place — anonymous, permanent, unremarked.
