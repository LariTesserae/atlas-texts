# The Terrace That Has Not Finished Becoming

You enter through a crack no wider than a shoulder, and the crack is warm, and the crack is breathing.

What lies beyond is a single folded room of mountain — though "mountain" is the wrong word, because mountains are old and this is not old, this is happening. The walls rise at an angle the eye refuses to commit to, sometimes sheer, sometimes leaning in close enough to touch both sides with outstretched hands, and they are wet everywhere, not with rain but with something the rock itself is sweating out, bead by bead, in the heat. The stone here is the temperature of a held hand. It steams where the water meets the air. The steam does not rise so much as *hang*, layered, like the room has its own slow weather independent of whatever sky might exist above the unseen ceiling.

There is no erosion here because nothing has had time. Every surface looks freshly extruded — ledges with sharp uncrumbled edges, terraces stacked one on another like wet clay still slumping into its final shape, mineral curtains mid-drip, frozen-but-not-frozen, still glistening as if the last second of their formation hasn't quite let go. You get the sense that if you came back in an hour the room would be subtly different. Not ruined — *advanced*. Further along whatever process is making it.

And it is made. All of it. This is the wrongness that sits under the skin: nothing here is raw geology, not really. Look closely at the terrace lips and you find tool-marks, chisel-scars, the rhythm of hands — but hands that worked alongside the stone's own growing, shaping wet rock the way a potter shapes wet clay, except the clay kept secreting more of itself even as it was carved, so the carving and the growing are the same gesture, ongoing, neither finished nor abandoned. Ladders of lashed green wood climb between terrace-levels, rungs slick with condensation, bound with cord that's somehow not yet rotted despite the wet — or it rots and is replaced so continuously that "rot" and "replace" have become one verb. Hand-tools lie where they were left: stone adzes, fire-hardened wooden wedges, a bone awl gone soft and pale with damp. No metal. No wheel. Just patient, repetitive, primitive industry pressed up against a geological process that should take ten thousand years and is instead taking an afternoon.

The vegetation does not grow from soil because there is no soil — there is barely room. Ferns and broad fleshy leaves erupt directly from seams in the stone, from the joints between terrace and ladder, from the gaps behind hanging cords, packed in so thick that you brush through curtains of leaf simply moving from one ledge to the next. Some of the plants are clearly cultivated — root vegetables coaxed from pockets of accumulated wet humus no bigger than a cupped palm, vines trained along the lashed ladders, fruiting in the dim light with a fruit nobody outside has a name for. Everything overlaps everything. There is no negative space. Where there might be a gap, a frond fills it; where a frond might end, a root continues it underground, if "ground" means anything when the whole environment is six meters across and folded sixty different ways.

Despite the density of growth, animal life is sparse, furtive, half-glimpsed: a many-legged thing the color of wet slate that lives in the steam-shadow under the lowest terrace and is gone before you can describe it properly; small pale moths that seem to be drawn to the warmth of the sweating walls rather than any light; once, maybe, eyes at the top of a ladder, gone when you look directly.

The light is the dimness of a room lit by nothing you can locate — not sun, not fire, though somewhere below the lowest terrace there's a reddish glow diffusing up through cracks, less illumination than suggestion. Everything is rendered in steam-softened silhouette, color guessed at more than seen, the deep wet green of the leaves, the ochre-red of the working stone, the black-wet of the ladder cords, all of it blurring at the edges where the humid air thickens.

And it is loud — that's the part visitors never expect from somewhere this small, this hidden, this single-pocket and contained. The sound comes from everywhere because there's nowhere for it to escape to: the constant percussion of water finding new channels down the rock, a deep subsonic groan that might be the stone *growing*, the creak of green wood ladders taking weight, the wet slap of leaves shifting against each other in air-currents that shouldn't exist in an enclosed space but do, rhythmic and tidal, like the room itself inhales. Under all of it, faint and arrhythmic and unmistakably tool-on-stone: tap, tap, pause, tap — someone, somewhere in the folds above or below, still shaping the place that is also shaping itself.

There are no rules here that hold for more than a single terrace-level. Up two rungs, water runs the direction you expect. Up two more, it runs sideways, beading along a horizontal seam and dripping off at an angle that argues with gravity as casually as a shrug. A leaf picked at one height wilts in your hand instantly, as though it belonged only to its specific patch of air; carry it three rungs down and it revives, plumps, drips. The carved ledges sometimes continue around a corner you didn't see, doubling back on themselves so that climbing "up" for long enough returns you, breathless and sweat-soaked, to a terrace indistinguishable from one you already passed, except the tool-marks are fresher and the steam smells faintly different — mineral, then floral, then something like burnt sugar, with no transition you can locate between them.

No one who lives here — and someone does, you're sure of it, by the ladders, the adzes, the worked terrace-lips — seems bothered by any of this. Their hands have made their peace with a mountain that won't stop arriving. Maybe that's the only stance available, this deep inside a single point of earth doing in an afternoon what earth elsewhere takes an age to do: not to understand it, not to predict it, just to keep carving, keep climbing, keep tending the thick wet green that fills every gap before it's finished being a gap — to work alongside the becoming, rung by rung, tap by tap, until either it stops, or you do.
