# The Root Galleries of Undersill

There is no sky here, only ceiling — and the ceiling is alive.

You are below the surface, in a cavern system that should be dark and is not, should be empty and is the opposite of empty: a vast underground hollow, scaled like a valley, filled root to root with vegetation that has forgotten it is supposed to need the sun. The light comes from somewhere — perhaps the plants themselves, a low bioluminescent wash the color of old brass, perhaps veins of mineral phosphor threaded through the stone, perhaps both, arguing with each other in patches of warm and cool glow. Normal light, the parameter insists, and it is, in its way — enough to cast shadows, enough to read distances by, though the source is diffuse and shadowless in the way that fog is shadowless. You stop asking where it comes from after the first hour.

## Terrain & Substrate

The cavern floor is barely a floor at all — it's compost going somewhere, a slow accumulating mat of root, husk, fiber, and fallen frond built up over what must be centuries of unhurried decay, dry decay, the kind that happens without water, just slow oxidation and fungal patience. Walking on it is like walking on a mattress that remembers being a forest. In places the ground hasn't finished becoming ground: raw stone shoulders through, pale and new-edged, like the cave itself is still being born around the plant mass that's colonizing it. This is the *emerging* quality — nothing here is finished. Stalactite-stubs that haven't grown points yet. Wall-seams where rock has only just begun to separate, packed with root-hair instead of water-mineral. The geology is young, or paused, or being actively unmade by the vegetable mass that's eating it from the inside.

There is almost no water. No streams, no condensation-glisten, no pools. The plants here have solved this problem by not needing it — succulent-bodied things, fiber-dense, storing what little moisture exists in swollen trunk-columns the width of factory pipe. Their bark is matte, chalky, faintly dusty to the touch, like dried clay rather than living tissue. This is dry vegetation, vegetation-as-mineral, plant matter behaving like geology because geology is what surrounds it and it has had to learn the local customs.

## Vegetation as Architecture

This is the place's organizing fact: plant matter doesn't just grow here, it *builds*. Towering trunk-columns rise from floor to unseen ceiling, branching at intervals into platform-like canopies that hold up other canopies, a stacked, multi-story arrangement that resembles a city of scaffolding grown rather than erected. Whole sections of wall are root-lattice, woven so densely they form usable surfaces — walls you could lean a ladder against, floors you could walk a second story on. Vines as thick as cable run in bundled conduits along what were probably once natural fracture-lines in the rock, now reinforced, almost *engineered* by growth pattern alone, as if the plants have independently arrived at structural logic.

And this is where the built environment enters, indistinguishable at the edges from the vegetable one. There is a town here — or the bones of one, settlement-scale, spread out across the open cavern floor with real gaps between its parts, sparse, breathing room everywhere. But the buildings are grown-into rather than built-upon: structures of timber and woven fiber-rope and hammered metal sheeting, slotted into the gaps between trunk-columns, using the living growth as load-bearing members. A foundry occupies a hollow between four colossal stalk-trunks, its chimney-flue routed up through a gap in the canopy-lattice, venting smoke that the plants seem to filter rather than choke on — leaves near the vent are darker, sootier, but alive. Gantries of iron run between platforms at different heights, bolted directly into bark gone hard as oak. Gear-driven elevators, chain-and-counterweight, climb the trunk-columns between levels, their engines a patient mechanical chuff in the quiet.

## Tech & Industry

The tech is industrial, properly so — not delicate, not digital, but iron and combustion and gear-ratio. Somewhere deep in the lower galleries a turbine works day and night, run not on water but on compressed gas vented from fissures, or perhaps on the slow combustion of harvested plant-pitch, fed into furnaces that glow dull orange behind hatch-doors. The whole settlement runs on this: smelters processing ore brought up (or down) through shaft systems nobody has fully mapped, looms weaving plant-fiber into rope and cloth, a refinery distilling resin out of the trunk-sap into something flammable, lubricant, medicinal — three products from one tap-line. Electric light exists in patches: arc-lamps strung along the main gantry-route, hissing faintly, casting a harder white against the organic gold of the bioluminescence. The two lighting systems don't compete so much as overlap, creating zones — amber-lit growth-zones, white-lit work-zones, and the long dim stretches between where neither reaches and you go by lanternlight or memory.

The density stays low throughout. This is not a crowded place. Between the foundry-hollow and the next cluster of dwellings might be three hundred meters of root-lattice and quiet dark, navigable by rope-bridge or catwalk, sparsely punctuated by single watch-lanterns. The town doesn't fill the cavern; it occupies it the way a few lit windows occupy a hillside at night — present, organized, but mostly absorbed into the larger emptiness-that-isn't-empty, because the vegetation fills what the people don't.

## Ecology & Fauna

Life here is abundant but mostly unseen — abundant in the sense of present-everywhere, sparse in the sense of any one place. Insects move through the trunk-columns in slow drifting populations: pale wood-lice the size of a fist, grazing on bark-fiber, tolerated by the settlers as a kind of background labor since they break down deadfall faster than time alone would. Something like moths, but slower, larger, wingspan a forearm's length, drawn to both the bioluminescent zones and the arc-lamps with equal indifference, circling in lazy gyres at the upper canopy levels. You hear them more than see them — a papery flutter overhead that never quite resolves into shape.

Larger fauna exists at the edges, rarely seen, often heard: something that moves through the deep root-tangle below the settlement's foundation-level, a low rhythmic creak of fiber under weight, gone before anyone gets a lantern to it. The settlers have a name for it, half-joking, half-not. Livestock — if you can call them that — graze on the cavern's outer fringe-growth: stub-legged, slow, vaguely ungulate creatures bred or adapted to eat the chalky dry-bark and convert it to something edible. Their lowing carries strangely through the trunk-columns, distorted, directionless.

## Sound & Atmosphere

Mostly quiet, but never silent — that's the texture of it. The ambient layer is mechanical-organic: a far-off turbine's chuff, the creak of canopy-platforms settling under their own slow growth-pressure, the dry whisper of frond against frond in air that has nowhere to be moving but does anyway, drawn by the convection of furnace-heat rising through the levels. Footsteps on the root-mat floor are absorbed, soft. Voices carry oddly far in some directions and not at all in others, depending on the density of growth between speaker and listener — a strange acoustic geography that the settlers have learned to navigate, knowing which gantry to call across and which to walk to.

The temperature is cool, almost cellar-cool, but the furnace-zones break this with pockets of dry heat that you walk into and out of like rooms. The air smells of dust, of sap-resin (sweetish, a little like pine but heavier, waxier), of hot metal, of the particular mineral smell of stone that's young and hasn't weathered into neutrality yet.

## The Strangeness

What unsettles, slowly, is the scale-mismatch and the quiet wrongness of growth. The trunk-columns are too uniform in their branching-intervals to be wholly natural — evenly spaced platforms, like the plant is unconsciously obeying some architectural logic, growing storeys the way a tree grows rings. Settlers talk about this without alarm, the way people talk about weather; they've built their lives around it, but late at night (there is no night, but there's a shift-change, a quieting) you catch them glancing up at the canopy with an expression that isn't quite trust.

And there's the matter of the cavern's edges, which nobody has reached. The settlement occupies the known middle of an open scale that keeps implying more — more galleries, more trunk-columns, more dark, going on past where the arc-lamp light gives out, past where anyone has strung rope. Expeditions go out along the root-lattice and come back with reports of identical architecture, repeating, as if the cavern is still deciding how big to be — which, given how young and unweathered the stone is, given how recently this all seems to have emerged, might be exactly true. The place isn't finished. It may not know yet where it ends.
