# The Anvil Highlands

The plateau does not end. You climb for days through switchback canyons of black-green stone, sharp-edged as if quarried yesterday, and you arrive somewhere that calls itself a summit only to discover it is a threshold to more highland, more of the same impossible elevation stretching toward a horizon that curves with the visible curvature of a very large world. This is a tableland built at the scale of weather systems. Clouds form *beneath* certain ridgelines and never reach the upper terraces at all. The sun here — and there is a great deal of sun, an unbroken brightness that seems to come from everywhere at once, bouncing off wet stone and wetter leaf — never quite warms anything. The cold is structural, load-bearing, a permanent fact like gravity.

## Ground

The rock is young. Fractured basalt and a paler, almost bone-colored mineral thrust up in plates and blades, faces still sharp enough to open skin, scree fields that haven't yet rounded into anything gentle. Nothing here has had time to erode into softness — this is geology still arguing with itself, ridges thrown up like breaking waves and frozen mid-crest. Between the stone, moisture collects in shallow basins, not rivers so much as a held breath of water: condensation pooling in rock cups, fog that beads on every surface each dawn and is gone by midday, intermittent seeps that vanish into the porous ground a few meters from where they emerge. Water is rare enough here to be a local event rather than a constant — you find it, you don't expect it.

## Green

And yet everything is full. Vegetation crowds every available surface — not jungle-dense, but insistent, opportunistic, a low cold-climate lushness of cushion plants, fat succulent rosettes, lichen in sheets of orange and acid green spreading across the young rock like a slow chemical fire, mosses that hold the fog-water in dense sponge-mats. Where the basalt plates tilt to catch runoff, thickets of a waxy-leaved scrub grow in dense collars, gray-green and glistening, packed shoulder to shoulder because there is no room left to grow, only room to grow *into* one another. The whole plateau reads as saturated — not with water, but with biomass. Every crevice has tenants.

## What Lives Here

The fauna is the first thing you understand, viscerally, on arrival: this place is *full of animals*, in numbers that shouldn't be sustainable and apparently are. Herds of something hoofed and shaggy-coated move across the high terraces in masses that take an hour to pass, their collective breath rising in visible columns. Above them, things wheel in numbers that blacken patches of sky — not one flock but flocks stacked on flocks, different species using different altitudes of the same air column like tenants on different floors. Burrowing things have turned whole hillsides into perforated honeycomb; you don't walk on ground here so much as on a thin roof over a teeming underground. Everything calls. Everything postures, forages, fights, mates, screams territorial claims into air that's already saturated with the calls of a thousand other things doing the same. There is no silence at any hour. The sound is not ambient — it's a wall, a structure of its own, layered: low percussive bellows from the herds, an upper register of shrieking from the wheeling flocks, a constant insectile fizz underneath that never resolves into individual notes.

## The Engines

And threaded through all of this — inexplicably, permanently — is machinery. Not buildings; there is nothing built here, no walls, no roofs, no roads. But scattered across the highland at intervals that feel almost geological are things that can only be called engines: vast partly-buried mechanisms of dark alloy, seamless, without visible maker's marks, venting slow plumes of heated vapor that the herds gather near for warmth. Massive flywheels turn in stone-lined pits at a pace too slow to call urgent — one rotation every few minutes — driving nothing visible, connected to nothing traceable, except that the ground around them hums and the lichen near their housings grows in unnatural, electric colors. Whether these are the relics of something gone, or organs of the plateau itself, machinery the world grew rather than built, no one here is positioned to say. The animals have adapted to them as if they were outcrops: nesting in their louvers, denning beneath their chassis, drinking the warm condensate that drips from their seams. The engines do not seem to require tending. They have perhaps never required tending. They will likely still be turning, at the same patient one-rotation-per-some-minutes pace, after everything currently alive on the plateau has died and been replaced several times over.

## The Pace of It

Despite the noise, despite the saturation of life, the plateau changes with great patience. The herds shift grazing grounds over a generation. The lichen advances across a rock face by inches per year. The engines' slow flywheels mark a time signature that has nothing to do with the frantic, loud, packed life churning around their bases. It is a place of vast slow structure wearing a coat of fast, dense, overwhelming detail — like watching a glacier from inside a beehive built on its surface.

## Rules, As Far As Anyone Can Tell

Nothing is built here, but things are *found* — the engines, certain regular hexagonal pits, mineral veins that run too straight to be entirely natural. The young, sharp rock suggests this plateau rose recently and fast, perhaps within memory of the oldest things living on it, and is still rising. Water is a gift, never a given; you drink from fog-collection leaves or you go thirsty until the next basin fills. The cold is constant enough that you stop fighting it and start understanding it as the price of the altitude, the brightness, the bigness of the sky. And the noise never stops, because nothing here is alone — every surface is tenanted, every niche occupied, and the engines turn beneath it all on their own indifferent, geological schedule, asking nothing, offering warmth to whoever presses close enough to take it.
