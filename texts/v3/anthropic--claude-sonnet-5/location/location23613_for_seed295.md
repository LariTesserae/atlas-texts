The place is called, by those who have ever needed to call it anything, the Roostings — though no one alive built that name new; it arrived already worn smooth, the way everything here arrives already old.

You reach it by climbing. The ground rises for a long while before you understand it has stopped being ground at all and become structure — terraces of fitted stone, walls grown into walls, arches that don't span gaps so much as commemorate gaps that closed centuries ago. There is no bedrock here that isn't also masonry. Even the soil, where soil exists in shallow pans on the wide ledges, sits in stone basins someone shaped and then died, and then whose descendants died, and so on, until the shaping itself became a kind of geology. Built, all the way down. Not a building in a landscape — a landscape that is the residue of building, weathered past the point where the difference matters.

It is high here, high enough that the air thins to something cool-edged even though the stone itself holds warmth, banked in its mass from a sun you do never quite see, because the light is low — not absent, but perpetually late-dusk, perpetually the hour just before a lamp would be lit if anyone here used lamps. They don't. There is no tech, no device, not even the simplest lever or hung rope — only the architecture, ancient and finished, and the bodies that have moved into it the way water moves into the cracks of a thing and stays.

And the bodies are everywhere. This is the fact that announces itself before any other: fauna overwhelming, layered, near-touching, filling every terrace and ledge and archway and the undersides of arches and the tops of walls and the black mouths of windows that go back into stone so far you can't see where they end. Wings, mostly — uncountable wings, in registers from the size of a moth to the span of a sail, roosting in tiers the way the architecture itself tiers, so that the built form and the living form have become almost the same gesture repeated at two scales. Strange the way you stop being able to tell, in this light, where stone ends and feather begins, where a carved finial is actually a sleeping shape with its head tucked down. Things move along the lower ledges too — many-legged, low-slung, threading between standing pools of damp that collect in every worn basin, every sagged lintel, drinking there, leaving wet tracks across dry stone that dry again before you've finished looking.

The sound is the second fact, and it does not let up. It is not a single voice but a permanent traffic of voices — calls, screeches, the percussive clatter of claws on stone, vast slow exhalations of wind funneled through throat-shaped passages in the architecture, the wet flap and snap of wings in close quarters, echo upon echo upon echo because every surface here is hard and curved and built precisely to carry sound further than it has any right to go. It is deafening in the manner of a forest canopy or a packed terminal — not violent, just total, a wall of noise you eventually stop hearing as noise and start hearing as weather, as the climate the place runs on instead of wind or rain.

Rain does come, but lightly, irregularly — water here is a damp that gathers rather than falls in any decisive way: condensation beading on the cool undersides of stone vaults, runnels finding the worn grooves long ago carved or worn into the floors, pooling in basins that were maybe cisterns once, maybe just shaped that way by a million years of drips finding the same path. Vegetation has taken every advantage of this dampness without ever overrunning the stone — vines thick and old as rope spilling down outer walls, moss furring deep into the shaded inner sides of arches, hanging gardens of broad fleshy leaves rooted in cracked mortar on the high terraces where the rare actual light gets through. Lush, but contained — green wherever water pools, stone wherever it doesn't, the two trading off in long bands across every visible surface.

Nothing here moves fast. The erosion is old and settled, every edge already rounded into its permanent shape, every crack already widened to the width it will keep. Whatever change happens — and it happens, the moss creeps, the stone gives by grains, the roosts shift populations season on season — happens at a pace too slow to watch, only to infer, the way you infer a a great tide has turned by finding the waterline somewhere new.

The scale opens past anything you can hold in one look. Climb to a high ledge and the terraces go on — tier below tier, archway behind archway, ledge upon roosting ledge, receding into the low brown dark until you simply lose the ability to resolve more detail, not because the place ends but because your eyes give up before it does. It feels less like a structure than a range — a built mountain range, valley-sized, where every formation, every peak and saddle and gorge, was at some point laid by hand, and has since been left so long that hand and weather are no longer different verbs.

There is nothing strange about it. That's worth saying, because everything about it sounds strange in the telling — the wings, the dark, the noise, the stone going on forever — but inside it, none of it reads as wrong. It reads as established, as inevitable, as the kind of place that was always going to end up exactly this full of exactly this much life, exactly this loud, exactly this old. You move through it carefully, not from fear but from the practical sense that everything here got where it is by knowing the terrain far longer than you have, and the only rule the place seems to enforce is patience: walk slow, let your eyes adjust past the point where you think they're done adjusting, let the noise become air, let the wings overhead resolve from threat into simply weather, into the ordinary respiration of an enormous, settled, breathing made-thing that has no plans to be otherwise.
