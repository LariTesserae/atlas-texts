# The Sweating Hollow

It occupies less ground than a village green, though no one has ever measured it properly, because measuring requires standing still in it longer than is comfortable. Call it a basin, though that implies a rim — better to say it is a *low place that water has agreed to occupy*, an oval depression maybe two hundred paces across, walled on all sides by mineral terraces that rise just high enough to keep the sky narrow.

## The Ground

There is no soil, not in the sense of anything that has ever been alive and died into the earth. The floor of the hollow is a packed mineral substrate — pale grey-white in places, rust-orange in seams, the color of things that precipitate out of water rather than grow. It has been forming for a very long time, in microscopic increments, sheet over sheet, like a held breath made solid. Walk across it and it doesn't crunch — it gives slightly, the way old wax gives, because beneath the dry-seeming crust is always a half-inch of standing damp.

Everywhere — and this is the first thing anyone notices — there is *stuff*. Not vegetation, not really anything you'd call alive, but the terraces and the basin floor are crowded edge to edge with mineral accretion: nodules, ridges, draped curtains of stone that formed where water ran and left its dissolved cargo behind. Cauliflower-shaped lumps the size of fists, packed shoulder to shoulder with their neighbors, climbing the low walls like a frozen crowd. There is no negative space. Every surface holds a texture, a deposit, a cooled drip, a terrace-lip, another terrace-lip behind it. The density is the haunting part — not a wilderness of plants but a wilderness of *precipitate*, every inch claimed by some slow chemical decision made centuries ago.

## The Water

It is everywhere and nowhere obvious. No single pool dominates — instead there are dozens of shallow basins, ankle-deep, stepped one below the next, connected by thin sheets of overflow you can hear before you see: a continuous, unhurried trickling, soft enough to seem like it's coming from inside your own skull rather than outside it. The water is warm — not hot, but blood-near, faintly mineral on the tongue if you were foolish enough to drink it (you'd be fine; you'd just taste iron and old stone).

The rock itself sweats. Even the parts that look dry have a sheen if you touch them, and in the dim light the whole basin seems to hold a faint, even glaze, as though recently rained on and never quite dried — because it never has, not in any season anyone could name. There is no season here, really. No dynamic. No gradient pulling the place toward some other state. It simply continues, the way a held note continues.

## Light and Air

The light is low — not nighttime, but perpetually late-afternoon-under-cloud, a flat pewter glow that seems to come from everywhere at once and casts no strong shadow. This may be the basin's own atmosphere: the constant evaporation off all that warm water keeps a thin haze suspended at about head height, softening every edge, making the far terrace walls look slightly unfinished, slightly like a sketch. The air is thick, warm, mildly mineral, easy to breathe but heavy to breathe a lot of — the kind of air that makes you want to talk less.

Sound is rare and small. The trickling water. Occasionally a single drop falling from an overhang into a lower pool — a sound that seems enormous in the quiet, then is gone, then is missed. No wind reaches the floor of the hollow; the terrace walls keep it out, so even on days when something is moving in the wider world, here the haze hangs motionless.

## What Lives Here

Almost nothing, and what does is easy to miss. No plants — the water is mineral-rich in a way that seems to discourage rather than feed any green thing. But it is not perfectly dead. In the warmest, most sheltered pools there are pale mats of something between a stain and a skin — not quite recognizable as moss or algae or anything with a name, just a faint biofilm, a slickness with a will of its own, clinging to submerged stone. It doesn't spread quickly. It doesn't seem to spread at all, on any timescale a person could watch.

Fauna is rarer still — the odd pale insect-analogue skating the meniscus of a pool, never seen twice in the same place, never explained. No birds nest in the terrace walls. There is a sense that nothing has decided to live here permanently, only to pass through, drink, and leave.

## Marks of People

Someone has been here, but lightly, and long ago, and not since in any numbers. A few flat stones laid as stepping points across the widest overflow channel, worn smooth, clearly placed rather than natural — their undersides, where visible, are squared off in a way water doesn't do. In one corner, half-submerged in a shallow terrace pool, a scatter of fired clay shards, too few and too old to reconstruct what they held. No structures stand. There might once have been a windbreak of piled stone against the eastern wall — now it's just a slightly more deliberate-looking heap among ten thousand accidental ones, and you'd need to look twice to tell.

No metal. No wheel-marks, no quarried blocks, nothing that suggests anyone stayed. It feels like a place people visited for the water — perhaps believing it had some virtue, the warmth, the mineral taste — and then continued on, leaving only the stepping stones as evidence that anyone needed to cross rather than simply admire.

## The Rule of the Place

The hollow does not change. This is not a poetic exaggeration so much as a fact you absorb the longer you stay: the drip rate is the same drip rate it was an hour ago. The haze sits at the same height. The mineral crowd of nodules and ridges has the look of something that finished happening a long time ago and is now simply *persisting*, the way a scar persists. Whatever forces built this place — and they were patient, additive, mineral forces, water leaving behind what it carried, over and over, for centuries — those forces have either stopped or slowed to a pace indistinguishable from stillness within any human visit.

It is not hostile. It is not even particularly strange, by its own terms — there's a calm logic to a low warm wet place collecting itself into stone over geologic time. But standing in it, surrounded on every side by that packed, claimed, finished-looking density, with the soft trickle as the only proof that time is passing at all, you may find yourself lowering your voice without deciding to, the way you would in a room where something is sleeping and you are not entirely sure it wants to be woken.
