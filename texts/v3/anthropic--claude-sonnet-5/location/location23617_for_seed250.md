# The Highwater Plateau

You come upon it from below, through layers of cloud that part like something is dragging them apart on purpose — and they are, though you won't understand why for a while yet. What you find at altitude is not a mountain but the *suggestion* of one: a vast tableland of raw, unweathered stone, still sharp at its edges, as if it finished forming sometime last week. The rock is dark and faintly vitrified in places, glassy where something — pressure, heat, a process with no name — has not yet had the centuries required to wear it down into anything gentle. This is a place erosion has not gotten to. Geology caught mid-sentence.

And over all of it: water. Not as a lake, not as a sea sitting politely in a basin, but as a *condition*. The plateau exists inside a column of falling, rising, suspended water that has no clear floor and no clear sky. Sheets of it descend in walls — visible as distinct vertical planes, like curtains hung at intervals across kilometers of empty air — while elsewhere whole volumes of water hang motionless, held aloft by something, vast lenses and droplet-galaxies suspended at different altitudes, refracting the pale even light into bands that drift slowly across the stone like the shadows of clouds that aren't there. Some of it moves upward. You'll see that and not quite believe it: rain falling sideways, then up, rejoining whatever reservoir above is breathing it back in.

The scale erases your instincts about distance. A waterfall on the far rim takes four full minutes to make a sound after you see it begin. The horizon, where there is one, bends — not from curvature of the world but from the water itself acting as a lens, so that far-off shapes loom larger or smaller depending on what's hanging in the air between you and them. Weather happens *here*, locally, made by the plateau's own logic: small intense squalls spin up over nothing, spend themselves in ninety seconds, and vanish, leaving the stone steaming faintly in their wake even at this cool, high temperature.

There is almost nothing else. Vegetation is a rumor — the odd low mat of some grey-green crust clinging in wind-shadow behind rock outcrops, a few wiry stalks that have rooted themselves into seams where dust has had time to collect, none of it more than ankle height, none of it dense enough to call a community. No animal stirs anything. No bird crosses the water-curtains. The silence where life should be is total and strange, made stranger by how *loud* everything else is — the place roars. Falling water against stone, the boom of air pressure changing as a suspended lens shifts and partially collapses, the long subsonic groan of rock somewhere adjusting to a hidden process beneath it. Loud, but empty of voices. A factory floor with no workers.

And there is no factory — no structure anyone built. No wall, no path, no cut stone. And yet you would swear something is *running*. Look long enough at one of the hanging water-masses and you'll see it: a structure to the suspension, an intelligence to the way droplets organize themselves into lattices too regular for chance, momentarily forming shapes — lenses, rings, what might be a hexagonal cell wall scaled up to the size of a stadium — before dissolving back into formlessness. The water is being *held*, *shaped*, *computed* by something with no visible apparatus. Maybe the rock itself does it; you notice now that some of those black glassy seams are not random — they run in long unbroken lines across the plateau, branching at angles that feel deliberate, like circuitry traced into the stone by whatever poured it there in the first place.

The light has no clear source either. It is even, shadowless in the way overcast is shadowless, but too bright for true overcast — as though it's coming from inside the water itself, every suspended droplet a tiny lit cell, the whole atmosphere mildly self-illuminating. At certain altitudes within the column you'll pass through bands where the light shifts color very slightly, violet then back to white, on a cycle that has nothing to do with any sun.

Nothing here is finished. That's the feeling that won't leave you: this plateau, this water-architecture, this whole vertical machine of falling and rising liquid, is being built in real time, and you are walking through the scaffolding while the structure decides what it's going to be. Rock shears off somewhere unseen every few minutes with a sound like a door slamming in another house. A new water-curtain unspools from above, finds its working height, and locks there with an audible *settling* — a bass thrum you feel in your sternum before you hear it. Stand still for an hour and the topography around you will have changed in some small but unmistakable way: a stone spur that wasn't there is there now, dark and steaming and not yet cooled to the ambient temperature of everything around it.

There is no danger you can name and no safety either — only the sense that you are inside a process much larger than any single intention, watching matter and water and light negotiate, provisionally, what kind of place this is going to be. It hasn't decided yet. You got here too early, or exactly on time, depending on what you came to see.
