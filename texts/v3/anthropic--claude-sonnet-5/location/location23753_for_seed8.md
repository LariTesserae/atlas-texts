The land lies low and has lain low for longer than memory holds shape. This is a peneplain — the word geologists use for a country worn down past the point of pretension, where mountains once stood and have been answered, over hundreds of millions of years, with patience. What remains is a vast shallow country, barely undulating, the bones of old ranges reduced to soft brown rises that the eye almost forgets are rises at all. There is nothing here that erosion has not already finished arguing with.

The rock that breaks the surface is rounded everywhere — no edge survives this kind of age. Domes of pale gneiss and laterite crust the color of dried blood-orange sit half-submerged in the soil like the backs of enormous animals sleeping under the ground, glistening faintly because water moves through them constantly, seeping up along old fracture lines, beading on the undersides of overhangs, weeping in thin dark trails down stone that has nowhere steep left to drain toward. The water doesn't rush here. It has no slope to rush down. It pools, it seeps, it stands in flat sheets after rain and takes days to remember to leave, gathering instead into shallow marshes and chains of warm, soup-colored pans that never quite dry, never quite fill — a permanent, ambiguous wetness, ground that squelches in some places and bakes pale and cracked an arm's length away.

Vegetation is sparse, but not absent — it's deliberate-looking, spaced as if something has been thinning it on purpose for an age. Tussocks of stiff blue-green grass stand in loose archipelagos across the mud-flats. Low, flat-crowned trees — broad, sun-beaten, the kind that have given up reaching upward and instead spread sideways to hoard whatever light and water come without competition — punctuate the middle distance every hundred meters or so, each one its own small kingdom of shade. Between them: bare laterite, standing water mirrors, reed-clumps at the marsh edges, lichen crusting the wetter stones in rust and ash-green patches. The spacing itself feels like a rule of this place — nothing crowds, nothing climbs over anything else, every plant has negotiated its real estate over geological time and the negotiations are long settled.

The air is warm and thick with the held moisture of standing water under open sun — not jungle-dense, but a slow simmer, a heat that has nowhere to escape to either, given how flat and open everything is. Light falls clean and full across the whole shallow basin, normal daylight, but it gets refracted everywhere off wet stone and wet mud and the standing mirror-pans, so the whole landscape seems to shine dully, low-angle glints scattered to the horizon. Heat-shimmer stands over the drier laterite flats at midday.

And it is loud — startlingly loud for a place with so little cover. Frogs in the marsh edges keep up a continuous percussive layer, swelling and dropping in waves. Birds are abundant and vocal — waders picking through the shallow pans on stilt-legs, raptors riding the thermals overhead, smaller flocking species exploding up off the grass tussocks in loud chattering sheets when something moves through. Insects saw away in the warm air, a dense static under everything else. Something larger — heavy, slow, ungulate-shaped at this distance — moves through the open ground in small loose groups, cropping at the sparse grass, occasionally calling to one another across distances of a kilometer or more, their voices carrying easily over land this flat and this empty of obstruction. Fauna is abundant precisely because the openness rewards visibility, and the wetness rewards persistence — this is old, productive, slow-cycling ground, the kind of ecology that settled into its rhythms a long time ago and has not needed to change them since.

Nothing has been built here. No path, no cairn, no cut stone — only the worn original rock and the standing water and the spaced trees, exactly as if the entire territory had decided, eons back, that this state — flat, wet, warm, half-bare, full of unhurried life — was the resolved one, the answer it had been eroding toward all along, and simply stopped changing. The change that does happen now happens on the scale of seasons and centuries, not days: the pans slowly shifting their boundaries, the laterite domes losing another imperceptible skin of grit to the rain, the loose groups of grazing animals tracing the same circuits their ancestors traced, year on year, across a country that offers no reason to ever do anything differently.

There's a particular quality to standing in the middle of it — the horizon is very far away and almost the same height as you in every direction, so the whole region feels visible at once, decipherable, an open page of shallow water and rounded stone and scattered low trees, scored continuously by frog-song and bird-cry and the occasional far-off bellow, under a warm and ordinary sun, in air that never quite dries.
