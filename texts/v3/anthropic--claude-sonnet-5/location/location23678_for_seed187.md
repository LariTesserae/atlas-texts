# The Hollow of the Standing Mass

The ground here gave up being ground a long time ago. What erosion left behind isn't soil but a memory of soil — flattened so completely that there are no slopes, no drainage lines, no high or low points, just a vast worn plate of old stone hidden somewhere beneath a single continuous skin of vegetation that has never, in any living record, been otherwise. The plant-mass doesn't grow *on* the land. It long ago became the land's only legible surface. Walk on it and your feet sink through layers of leaf and root and old leaf and older root, a compacted archive that has had so much time to settle it no longer remembers being separate things.

The space itself is small — a single bounded clearing-that-isn't-a-clearing, room-sized, grove-sized, ringed on all sides by the same mass that fills its interior, so that there's no clear edge between "in" and "out," only a gradual thickening. Within this boundary the density is wrong for how much plant there is: the vegetation is total, total ground-to-canopy, and yet the volume inside feels empty. Hollow. The mass arches up and over in old fused limbs — trunks indistinguishable from roots indistinguishable from the floor — leaving a high, vaulted interior emptiness, like the inside of a lung. You stand in a green nave with almost nothing else in it. No undergrowth clutter, no fallen debris drifted into piles. The leaves overhead are dense enough to hold a cathedral's worth of normal daylight, filtered to a steady, shadowless even green-grey, the same at every hour, because nothing here seems to track hours particularly closely.

It is cool, and just barely damp — not wet, never wet, but every surface holds a faint film, condensation gathered in the folds of bark-skin, in the undersides of leaves, beading and never quite falling. There's no rain happening, but the place remembers rain recently enough, or perpetually enough, that everything stays faintly slick to the touch, faintly cold.

And it is loud. Shockingly loud for how empty it feels. The fauna are abundant but unseen — clicks, drones, layered insect frequencies stacked on top of each other so densely they form something closer to industrial machinery than to birdsong. A steady churning hum, almost mechanical, comes from everywhere and nowhere: the texture of a factory floor laid overtop a forest interior, engines made of wings and mandibles. Underneath it, lower, slower: deep resonant creaks from the standing mass itself, the settling of impossibly old wood-that-is-rock-that-is-wood, a groan with the cadence of distant turbines. There is no built structure anywhere — no wall, no path, no tool — and yet the *sound* of the place insists on industry: scaled, mechanized, electric in its texture, though every source is biological. It's as if some factory-equivalent process has been running, unattended, inside this organism for longer than anything has been counting, and it will keep running at exactly this volume, in exactly this register, forever. Nothing here is changing. The hum today is the hum from ten thousand years ago. The leaves that fall are already accounted for by leaves growing to replace them in the same instant, an equilibrium so total it reads as stillness even while everything in it vibrates.

There are rules, and they are simple: nothing dies and leaves a gap. Nothing arrives and changes the proportion. The light is even. The temperature is cool and stays cool. The dampness is a constant film, not a cycle. Whatever creatures make the noise are never seen directly — motion at the very corner of vision, never centered — and whatever industrial hush their massed activity produces is the one sound that has been playing, unbroken, since before there was anyone to hear it as loud.
