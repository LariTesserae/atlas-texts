# The Cold Marsh of Annvethar Reach

The dark comes first—not absence of light but a thick, breathing medium you move through. What little luminescence exists comes in wrong colors: a sourceless blue-green that pools in the lowest hollows of the marsh like spilled chemical light, and the occasional hard white flare from somewhere overhead, gone before your eyes adjust.

The ground here is young. You can feel it in how the peat gives differently than peat should—too elastic, like something is still deciding what density it wants to be. Cracks run through black stone in fresh, unweathered lines, edges still sharp enough to cut, as if the bedrock surfaced last season rather than last epoch. Water sits everywhere without committing to a single body: not quite bog, not quite lake, not quite river, but a regional smear of wet that condenses out of the cold air directly onto every surface. Your skin, if you have skin, would be perpetually damp. Stone weeps. Moss doesn't grow so much as *arrive*, fully saturated, glistening.

Vegetation crowds in at a density that makes the eye search for gaps and find none. Reeds the height of a person grow in stands so thick they form walls, their stalks a deep bruised purple-green that the dark renders nearly black. Between them, broad-leafed things—not quite ferns, not quite cabbage, ribbed and waxy—catch the blue-green glow and throw it back distorted. Everything overlaps everything else. There is no negative space, only layers: canopy of reed-tops, mid-stratum of broad leaves, understory of low fungal mats that pulse faintly, root-tangle below that you occasionally step through rather than over.

## What moves through it

The fauna is abundant and largely heard rather than seen. Wings—too many wings, too close together—beat against the heavy air in overlapping rhythms that never quite synchronize. Something large wades at a distance, its footfalls landing as deep compression-thuds you feel in your sternum before you hear them. Smaller things chitter, click, and emit long descending whistles that get answered from three directions at once. The marsh holds a permanent, layered chorus, the individual voices indistinguishable from each other, building toward something that crosses from "loud" into "structural"—a sound you stop hearing as noise and start feeling as pressure.

This is the deafening part: not one din but many dins stacked, arriving from every elevation, none of them ever resolving into silence. Even the water contributes—not lapping but *humming*, a low resonance like the marsh itself is a struck instrument that never stops ringing.

## The machinery beneath

Here is where the place stops being merely a wet, dark, loud lowland and becomes something else: scattered through the reeds and half-sunk in the black water are structures that don't belong to any technology you'd recognize as built by hand. Not ruins, not quite functioning machines either—something in between, or something that exists in both states simultaneously depending on when you look at it.

A frame of material that isn't metal but holds metal's certainty rises out of one bog-pool, its geometry folding back on itself in ways that hurt to track with the eyes—angles that should require more space than the object occupies. It hums in a register beneath the animal chorus, a bass note that seems to be doing something to the water around it: the surface near its base doesn't ripple outward from disturbances but inward, toward the structure, as though gravity there has a slight preference.

Elsewhere, threads of light—the hard white flares you catch overhead—are not lightning but something deliberate, tracing paths between three or four similar structures scattered across the marsh at distances that shouldn't allow visible connection through this much vegetation and dark. When the threads fire, you feel it as a pressure change in your inner ear half a second before the light arrives. The fauna reacts every time: a ripple of shrieks and wingbeats that spreads outward and takes long minutes to settle.

These structures are not many—built density here is low, only scattered nodes across an open, valley-sized expanse of marsh—but their effect is total. They are doing something to the place. The young, sharp-edged geology might not be erosion-young so much as *event*-young: this terrain may have been remade, recently and repeatedly, by whatever these things do when they cycle.

## The instability

Nothing here holds steady. The marsh seems to be mid-process, caught in continuous, unpredictable transformation:

A stand of reeds you passed minutes ago is gone, the ground beneath it occupied now by a shallow new pool, dark water already finding its first ripples, steam—or something like steam, faintly luminous—rising off it into the black air. Somewhere distant, a structural hum changes pitch, and the change moves outward as a pressure wave that makes every winged thing in earshot scatter at once, a thunderclap of displaced air and shrieking.

The cold doesn't help orient you—it's a flat, penetrating chill, the temperature of a place where heat hasn't finished arriving or has already finished leaving, and it sits the same everywhere, giving no thermal gradient to navigate by. The blue-green glow shifts intensity according to no schedule you can determine, brightening in the hollows when the structures' hum deepens, fading toward true darkness when distant white threads fire and momentarily steal the marsh's attention elsewhere.

You learn quickly that this place does not have weather in the conventional sense; it has *episodes*. A given patch of marsh might be drowned, drained, regrown, and overgrown again within the time it takes to cross it. The fauna seems adapted to this, or perhaps generated by it—creatures that nest in growth they know will be gone by the structures' next cycle, that lay eggs timed to hatch in the brief windows between events.

## What it would feel like to stand there

Wet cold against the skin, sound pressing from every direction at a volume that makes thought effortful, the smell of peat and ozone and something faintly sweet from the broad-leafed plants when they're crushed underfoot. Darkness that is not empty but *occupied*—full of motion you can hear and barely see, full of structures that hum with purposes beyond explanation, full of a marsh-floor that might not be there in the same configuration ten minutes from now.

There would be no comfortable place to stop. The ground might decide otherwise. The light might decide otherwise. The only constant is the deep, layered roar of life and machine-hum and shifting water, and the sense—not weird, not uncanny, just simply *true* here—that you are standing in the middle of something being made and unmade, over and over, faster than a place this size should allow, while abundant unseen things go on living inside the churn as if it were simply weather.
