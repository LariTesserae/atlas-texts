# The Cistern-Garden of Höllenrach

There is a place, high up where the air should be thin but isn't, where someone built a mountain and then flooded it on purpose.

Call it the Cistern-Garden, though no one who built it left a name behind — only the structure itself, which speaks in rivets and pressure-seals.

## What It Is

Picture terraces, but each terrace is a tank. Picture a tank the size of a chapel, walled in riveted iron gone the green-black of old pennies, and inside it, water — warm water, the temperature of a held breath — rising to brimful, and through that water, growing upward toward a ceiling of glass and girder, a jungle that has nowhere left to put itself.

The vegetation is packed past any reasonable density: broad fern-fronds the size of doors pressed flat against tank walls because there is no room to spread them; cycad-trunks growing not straight but corkscrewed, having negotiated around pipe-clusters decades ago; a canopy, if you can call it that, formed three meters under what should be open sky but is instead a riveted dome, so the topmost leaves grow flush against iron, fanned out like hands pressed to a window from the inside.

Everything is built. Not "built around" — built *instead of*. There is no original ground here, no bedrock, no soil that wasn't poured. The terraces stack upward in a stepped ziggurat of tanks, each one ringed by a catwalk of perforated steel, connected by ladders still bright at the rungs from infrequent use. The whole structure reads young — sharp edges, unworn bolt-heads, paint that hasn't had time to fully give up. This was finished recently, as these things go. A generation, maybe two.

## How It Breathes

Pumps, somewhere below, the size of cathedral organs, move the water on a cycle so slow you'd need a season to notice it: tanks fill, crest, weep slowly downward through filter-galleries packed with root mass, and refill. The engines that drive this are coal-warm and iron-bodied, industrial in the old sense — flywheels, pressure gauges with hand-painted needles, brass fittings sweating condensation in the warm air. They run at a murmur. This whole vast machine-garden produces almost no sound: a low compressor hum, the occasional knock of a valve finding its seat, water moving against water with a sound like someone turning over in sleep one floor down. Quiet enough that your own footsteps on the catwalk grates feel like an intrusion.

## What Grows

The plants are lush beyond reason, growing in nutrient-water rather than soil, root-balls visible through chicken-wire baskets bolted to the tank floors. Nothing here evolved for this; everything here was *placed* for this, and it complied with unsettling enthusiasm. Vines the thickness of mooring rope spiral the catwalk railings, sprouting waxy trumpet-flowers that never seem to wilt, only replace themselves overnight, dropped petals dissolving in the warm water below like sugar.

There is no animal life. Not one insect drone, not a birdcall, not the flicker of anything moving that isn't water or leaf-sway from the pump-cycle's slow current. The absence has its own texture — you keep waiting for the rustle that would mean something alive is nearby, and it never comes, so the lushness reads less like a forest and more like a diagram of a forest, accurate in every leaf, missing the one ingredient that would make it convincing.

## Light and Air

The light comes down even and warm through layers of glass roofing grimed with mineral bloom, diffusing into something shadowless and a little sourceless, like being lit from inside a pearl. Warm, humid, water-heavy air — every breath here tastes faintly of hot metal and green sap.

## The Strangeness of It

The wrongness isn't loud. It's structural. Why build a mountain only to flood every level of it? Why grow a jungle that touches its own ceiling, packed root to root to root with no paths through it except the iron catwalks threading between tanks like capillaries? Why is everything *exactly* full — every tank brimming at precisely the same height, as though the whole ziggurat were a single instrument tuned to one note?

There are gauges everywhere, brass-faced, all reading numbers that mean nothing without a key nobody left behind. Somewhere below the lowest terrace, the engines that drive this cycle have been running long enough to need no attention, and may run long after there's any reason to. The place wasn't abandoned — it's tended, in the sense that it tends itself, water rising and falling on its slow tide, plants growing into the only shapes the iron permits them, the whole cathedral-stack of tanks breathing its one quiet breath, over and over, into air that nothing alive is left to use.
