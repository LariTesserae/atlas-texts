# The Condensation Deck

There's a platform up here, bolted into the shoulder of something larger, and it exists at an altitude that makes your ears ring even before the sound starts working on them. Call it three hundred meters above whatever ground used to be — though "ground" stopped being relevant the day this was built. Steel grating underfoot, fresh-cut, the edges of the welds still bright and unoxidized, sharp enough to catch a sleeve. Nothing here is old. The whole structure has the over-precise geometry of something assembled within the last few seasons — angles still true, no slump, no settling, no soft edges worn by time. It will erode eventually. It hasn't started yet.

**Water** moves through everything, but not as rain — as breath. Pipes the width of a torso run along every surface, lagged in cracked insulation, and where the lagging fails, they sweat. Condensation beads on every cold rail, every valve housing, every strut, and runs down in threads that pool in the grating's diamond gaps before dripping into the dark below. The air itself is heavy and grey-wet, a permanent fog generated by some unseen process upstream — cooling towers, maybe, or a reactor breathing off excess heat into the cool air. You can taste the metal in it. Everything that isn't actively heated wears a film of moisture; touch a railing and your palm comes away slick and faintly oily.

**Sparse vegetation** survives here only as accident: a film of black-green algae in the seams where water collects and never quite drains, a few resilient lichen-crusts clinging to bolt heads, nothing rooted, nothing that asked permission. It's not a place that supports life so much as one life-form has found a crack to persist in.

**No animals.** Whatever this place was built among, it left them behind. The absence is conspicuous if you think about it — no birds nesting in the joists, no insects in the condensation pools, nothing. Just machinery and the things machinery sheds.

**Built, total, industrial** — there is no substrate here that isn't manufactured. The "floor" is grating. The "walls," where they exist, are louvered vent housing and cable conduit bundled in steel-mesh sheathing. Ladders rise into fog in three directions and a fourth ladder, identical, descends into a different fog, and there's no way from this platform to know which one matters. Pipes the size of room-diameters cross overhead, lagged and dripping, hauling something — steam, coolant, slurry — toward or away from a process you'll never see directly. Everything is the color of wet iron and old paint: oxide red gone brown, safety yellow gone the grey of cigarette ash, the black of soot-stained steel.

**Light** comes from caged bulbs spaced too far apart, each one a small sodium-orange island in the grey, throwing a cone of visibility maybe four meters wide before the fog eats it. Between the cones: dim, almost dark, a kind of working twilight that never resolves into either day or night because there is no sky here, only ceiling, only more structure rising and falling away into the wet murk above and below.

**The sound is the thing.** It is deafening, constant, total — not one noise but a stack of them occupying every register at once. A bass thrum from somewhere structural, felt in the grating through your boots before it's heard. A mid-range shriek of something rotating at a speed it wasn't quite designed for. A higher hiss-and-knock of steam finding a valve seat imperfectly. Underneath all of it, arrhythmic clangs — far off, then close, then far off again, like the building is full of footsteps that never resolve into a walker. You'd shout to be heard by someone standing at arm's length and they still might not catch it. There is no quiet anywhere in this place. Silence is not a condition the architecture permits.

**Scale is hidden** — this platform is a single contained pocket, a room without walls, intimate in its dimensions (you could cross it in twenty steps) even as it's suspended inside something vast enough that its true outline is never visible. You are always *inside* a larger machine while standing in a small, specific, knowable spot of it: this grating, these four railings, this one dripping pipe joint at head height that you learn to duck.

**Density is sparse** — for all the noise and wet and engineering, the platform itself is mostly empty space. A valve cluster here. A control box, unlabeled, its indicator lights cycling through patterns that mean nothing without context, over there. A coil of chain looped on a hook, swaying very slightly though there's no wind, only pressure-differential draft. The emptiness between objects is where the fog pools thickest, where sound arrives slightly delayed, like the space itself has weight.

**Dynamic, active** — nothing here is static. Pressure cycles audibly through the pipes in slow pulses, three minutes on, one minute of relative quiet (relative only), then back. Somewhere a process vents, briefly turning the fog white and acrid before it clears. The lights flicker on a schedule that might be a brownout pattern or might be deliberate. Drips accelerate and slow with the temperature of unseen exhaust. The whole platform exists mid-process, never finished, never resting, a single held breath in a structure that is constantly, mechanically inhaling.

**Rules of the place:** nothing is explained. No signage beyond a few stenciled numbers, half-worn, on pipe collars — meaningless without the manual that went with them. The ladders lead somewhere, certainly, but the place offers no orientation, no map-logic, only more platforms like this one, each its own small wet bright loud pocket suspended in industrial fog, each disconnected from any visible whole. You learn the place by feel — which railings run warm, which drips are predictable, which clangs precede a vent-flare and which don't. There's a working theory that the whole structure is still being built somewhere above, and still running somewhere below, and you are standing in the part that's already finished and already operating, indifferent to whether anyone is there to hear it.
