# The Cistern Spires

There is a place where the mountains have not finished arriving. They came up too fast — within a geological eyeblink, a few hundred thousand years — and so their faces are still raw, sheared along fracture planes that haven't had time to round. This is one needle among them: a single vertical throat of rock, perhaps four hundred meters from base to crown, narrow enough that you could shout across the gap to its nearest sibling and be heard. The scale here is intimate despite the height — not a range, but a room with a very tall ceiling, walled in on two sides by twin sister-spires close enough to block the horizon.

**Stone, water, green.** The rock is a dark, wet basalt-like material, almost black when saturated, threaded everywhere with white veins of mineral deposit where water has been working upward and outward since the stone was born. It weeps constantly. Not dripping in the gentle sense — sheeting, in places, where springs have found horizontal seams and run them like spigots. The air at every elevation carries a fine suspended moisture; condensation beads on every cold surface, including the machinery. Vegetation has colonized with shocking density for something this young and this vertical: thick collars of broad-leafed plants ring every ledge wide enough to hold soil, vines drop forty, fifty meters in unbroken green ropes, and a kind of fat-leaved succulent grows directly out of the rock-weep zones, drinking straight from the stone. Moss is everywhere as a base layer, electric green where it catches light, black-green in shadow. The overall impression is of a tower being slowly reclaimed by a jungle that arrived before the tower had finished cooling.

**The industry.** Wedged into this vertical jungle, scattered rather than planned, is a small hydro-mechanical works — six or seven structures total, no more, none large, fit into ledges and notches like swallows' nests. A penstock pipe, riveted iron gone rust-orange against the black rock, runs down one full face of the spire, fed by a header tank bolted into a spring-source near the crown. Where it terminates, a turbine house — single room, corrugated roof, slick with condensation — produces a steady mechanical roar that the canyon-throat amplifies and throws back on itself. The sound here is the dominant fact of the place: water-rush, turbine-whine, the metallic clatter of a flywheel governor, and beneath it all the deeper hydraulic boom of overflow water finding the gorge floor far below. It is loud enough that the scattered workers — if there are workers; you sense them more than see them, a coil of fresh rope here, a lit lantern in a window-slot there, footprints in moss — must use hand signals at range.

Catwalks of black iron, beaded with rust and rain, span gaps between outcrops at three or four levels, half-swallowed by the vines already. A second, smaller structure near the base looks like a pump-relay station, its housing cracked open on one side to show a knot of pipe and gauge, abandoned mid-repair or simply left, the jungle indifferent to the difference.

**Vertical ecology.** Fauna is sparse but present in a way that rewards attention — a single dark bird species nests in the turbine-house eaves, undisturbed by the noise, perhaps even dependent on the vibration to deter something worse. Insects move in slow dense clouds at the mid-elevations where the spray hangs longest. Nothing larger; the terrain doesn't permit it, and the noise likely doesn't help. What you don't see is more interesting than what you do: no clear signs of the works' builders being still present in numbers, no smoke, no faces, only maintained machinery and the assumption of return.

**Atmosphere and light.** Normal daylight reaches in, but filtered — the spire-gap is narrow enough that direct sun only touches the rock face for a few hours around midday, and the rest of the time the light is a soft, even, slightly green-cast glow, bounced off wet leaves and wetter stone. Mist forms in low pockets by midmorning and burns down to nothing by early afternoon, then begins reforming as the temperature drops with evening. The warmth is humid, skin-damp, the kind that makes metal sweat and paper curl.

**Rules of the place.** It feels governed by patience: nothing here moves quickly except the water and the noise. The machinery's rhythm is slow and cyclical — the header tank fills, releases, refills, on an hours-long pulse you can track by listening to the turbine's pitch rise and fall. Erosion is young, so the rock hasn't decided yet exactly where its weak points are; you get the sense that in another ten thousand years this spire's exact silhouette will be quite different, that a ledge you're standing on is provisional. The jungle's growth and the water's patient carving are both, on the timescale that matters here, faster than the industry's wear. The machines are recent intrusions into an already-changing system, and the system is winning slowly, sweetly, draping iron in green at a rate too slow to watch but too fast to ignore over a season.

To stand here is to be in a loud, wet, warm, green throat of rock that hasn't finished being made, occupied lightly by people who built just enough machine to harvest the water's fall and then mostly went away, leaving the noise running on its own, patient, mechanical, perpetual, indifferent to whether anyone is left to hear it.
