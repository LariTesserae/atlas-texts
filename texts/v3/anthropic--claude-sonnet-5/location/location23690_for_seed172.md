# The Coil Yard

It occupies a fold of mountain that should not have a hollow in it, and yet here is the hollow: a chamber perhaps two hundred meters across, ribbed in dry frost, walled entirely in riveted iron plate that climbs into a ceiling lost in its own shadow. There is no sky here, no weather but the weather the machines make. The elevation outside is real — you would have had to climb a long time, through thin cold air and switchback paths of broken scree, to find the seam in the rock where the door is, if it can be called a door, a slot wide enough for a cart and tall enough for nothing else. Inside, the ground is grated steel, laid in young, sharp-edged plates that haven't yet been worn smooth by feet or weather, their bolt-heads still bright, their welds still raw-looking, like a wound that hasn't decided how to scar.

The cold is total. It is not the cold of absence but the cold of a place built to be cold on purpose — pipes sweat frost along their lengths, and where steam escapes a valve joint it freezes mid-plume into a static lace that never fully forms and never falls, hanging there as if time forgot to finish the motion. This is the place's first rule: nothing changes. The frost-plume is always at that exact stage of becoming. The gauges, when you find them, all read the same number they have always read. There is no decay because decay is a kind of change, and this place does not permit it.

The architecture is total construction — not a flake of native stone shows anywhere, even the floor of the hollow has been plated over, the walls plated over, so that the mountain is present only as a shape the iron remembers, a cavity the building fills. Girders cross the dark overhead at odd, unrepeating angles, as though designed by something that understood load-bearing only as folklore. Vegetation barely registers: a few sparse growths of a pale, wax-leafed lichen-thing cling to the warmest pipe-junctions, where waste heat bleeds through insulation, six or seven such colonies in the whole vast room, each no bigger than a hand, frozen at the edges, faintly luminous green in cross-section if you peel one back, which you should not do.

No animal moves here. No animal ever has. There are no nests, no droppings, no skeletons, not even insects drawn to the warmth — the absence is so complete it starts to feel less like emptiness and more like a precondition, a clause of the place's existing at all.

But the sound. The sound is the place's actual substance, louder than anything its size should permit — a continuous overlapping roar of engines that are not visible, hidden behind plate and grate, in walls within walls, driving belts you can hear slapping their rotation somewhere below the floor, a bass shudder that comes up through your boots and into your teeth, layered over with the shriller scream of something venting, something always venting, on a cycle so long you could mistake it for permanent. There is no quiet anywhere in the Coil Yard. Speech is useless past a few feet; people who work here — if anyone works here — must have a language of touch and lamplight, hand against shoulder, lantern-glow tilted in code.

Scattered through the floor, sparse and irregular, are the coils themselves: housings of dark alloy the height of a person, each ringed in copper gone the color of old blood, each humming at a pitch slightly different from its neighbor so that walking the floor is walking through a chord that never resolves, a held discord that has apparently been held since before anyone now living could remember, if anyone lives. They are spaced with strange evenness-that-isn't — close in some quarters, distant in others, the gaps between them filled with nothing but cold air and grate-light, so the whole room reads as mostly emptiness punctuated by these few enormous, working, unexplained objects.

The light is ordinary, almost defiantly so — caged bulbs strung along the girders at intervals, burning a flat warm-white that has no business looking so domestic in a place this inhuman, casting long grated shadows that don't flicker because the bulbs don't flicker, because nothing here flickers, nothing gutters, nothing is allowed the dignity of almost going out.

No one knows — or no one says — what the coils produce, or for whom, or where the noise goes once it leaves the mountain. There is a rule, unstated but absolute, that the engines do not stop. Whether they were started by people now long dead, or started themselves, or were always running, even before the iron was laid down around them, even before the mountain had this hollow in it at all — that is the kind of question the place itself refuses to entertain. It only roars, and holds its frost mid-fall, and waits, with the patience of something that has already arrived at the only state it will ever be in.
