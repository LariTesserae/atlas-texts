# The Hanging Mosses of Tarrenvale Cordillera

The plateau begins where the clouds usually end, a shelf of ancient stone thrust up into air thin enough to taste of iron and cold. From below, in the valleys that the locals — if there were any — would call home, this place is simply "the high wet country," spoken of with the particular reverence reserved for things too large to argue with.

## Terrain and Stone

The rock here is old in the way that mountains are old when they've stopped trying to become anything else. Basalt and a pale, water-loving limestone interleave in long terraces, each one stepped down from the last like the rungs of something built by a species with different ideas about scale. Millennia of rain have not eroded this stone so much as *negotiated* with it — every edge is rounded, every crack widened into a channel, every flat surface turned into a shallow basin that holds water like a held breath. The established quality of the erosion shows everywhere: nothing here looks new, nothing looks like it is still being violently shaped. It has settled into its final argument with gravity and water, and now simply persists.

Between the terraces, the ground is rarely dry. Groundwater sits close beneath the surface — you feel it through your boots as a kind of give, a sponginess to even the rockiest patches. Step off the harder stone and your foot will sink an inch into moss-cushioned ground that seems to exhale a thin mist with each compression. Springs emerge without ceremony from cracks in cliff faces, threading down in skeins that never seem to dry, feeding pools that feed streams that feed the wide slow rivers visible, on clear days, miles below.

## The Green Saturation

Vegetation occupies every surface that holds still long enough. This is not vegetation in patches — it is vegetation as the default condition of matter. Stone faces wear cloaks of moss four inches thick, the color of old jade, dripping at their lower edges into beards of lichen and small ferns that have rooted in the moss itself, plants growing on plants growing on rock. Where the terraces widen into something like meadow, the canopy of low, wind-bent trees closes overhead — a strange dwarf forest of gnarled conifers and broad-leafed things with waxy water-shedding leaves, none taller than twenty feet, all of them growing so close together that their root systems have fused into a single mat beneath the soil.

There is no negotiating with this density. Walking through it means walking *through* it — pushing fronds aside, ducking beneath low branches slick with condensation, finding your path chosen for you by whatever gaps the plant life has consented to leave. The air beneath the canopy is its own climate: cooler than the open terraces, saturated with the smell of wet bark and crushed leaf-matter, lit in shifting greenish patches where the light manages to penetrate.

## Weather of Its Own

At this elevation and this scale, the plateau makes its own sky. Clouds form along its leeward edge in the mornings, condensing out of warmer air rising from the valleys below, and by midday they have pooled into a slow white sea that drowns the lower terraces in fog. This happens with the regularity of breathing — clear summits, drowned valleys, clear summits again by evening, the whole cycle taking maybe six hours, and it has likely repeated itself, with only minor variations, for longer than anything currently alive here can remember.

The cold is a high-altitude cold, not biting but persistent, the kind that lives in your hands before you notice it's arrived. It keeps the water from ever quite drying and keeps the moss in a permanent state of glistening readiness. Rain, when it comes, doesn't so much fall as arrive — sheets of it moving across the terraces like curtains being drawn, audible minutes before they reach you as a hiss against ten thousand acres of leaf.

## Ecology, Saturated

The fauna here exists at a density that suggests every available niche has been filled, then filled again in vertical layers. Birds — many of them, calling rarely, communicating mostly in soft contact-notes rather than song — move through the canopy in loose flocks that seem to know which trees are fruiting before the fruit is visible to anything else. Beneath them, in the leaf litter and moss-mat, smaller things move with constant small rustlings: amphibians with skin like wet stone, slow armored insects, things with too many legs going about business that requires no haste.

Larger animals keep to the terraces' edges and the misty middle distance — herd creatures, hoofed and shaggy enough to shed the wet, grazing the rare open meadows in groups that move as a single grey-brown mass through the fog. Something hunts them, occasionally, somewhere out past the visible range — you hear it more than see it: a long-drawn call answered, sometimes, by silence, sometimes by the abrupt scatter-sound of a herd breaking and running across stone.

Nothing here is rare exactly, but nothing is loud either. The dominant sound is not silence but a kind of ambient murmur below the threshold of attention — dripping water, settling leaves, distant unseen movement — occasionally broken by something sharper: a bird's single cry, a rockfall loosened by root growth, the boom of air pressure changing as fog rolls through a gap in the terrace wall. The quiet here is not empty. It's *occupied* quiet, the silence of a packed room where everyone happens to be listening.

## The Iron Smell

And then there is the thing that doesn't belong, except that it does, somehow, without anyone having built it: a smell, on certain days, when the wind comes from the northeast — hot metal, oil, the ozone tang of something running hard. No structure has ever been found to account for it. No road, no foundation, no rusted machine half-swallowed by moss. The plateau is, by every visible measure, untouched — there is no path here that wasn't made by water or animal feet, no straight line anywhere in its geometry.

But the smell comes anyway, two or three times a season, strong enough that the birds go quiet and the herd animals lift their heads in unison toward the source. It lasts an hour, sometimes two, then fades back into wet stone and green leaf-smell as if it had never been. Those who've spent enough seasons up here stop asking about it. They simply note it, the way you'd note a change in the wind, and wait for it to pass — one more process in a landscape already running so many processes at once that one more mystery barely registers above the general hum of things growing, falling, dripping, and enduring.

The high country does not explain itself. It simply continues — wet, green, ancient, and quietly, persistently busy — the way it likely has for an age, and the way it shows no particular sign of stopping.
