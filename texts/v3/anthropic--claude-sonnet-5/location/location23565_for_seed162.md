# The Anvil Highlands

The plateau does not end so much as it *suggests* an end, somewhere beyond the curvature of visible distance, swallowed in a haze that is not quite weather. You are high here — high enough that the air carries a thinness it shouldn't, given how warm it sits against the skin, like a held breath that never cools. The ground beneath you is new rock, basaltic and raw, broken into plates with edges still sharp enough to draw blood if you're careless. Nothing has had time to round these stones down. They jut and overlap and stack themselves into low ridges, dark glass-skinned cooling-cracks fanning out from old wounds in the crust.

Despite the youth of the stone, life has moved in fast and thick. Vegetation crowds every fold and crevice — not a forest in any familiar arrangement, but dense mats of broad, rubbery leaves the color of bruised plums, vines that braid themselves into nets between outcrops, fungal towers the height of a person growing in clusters like organ-pipe colonies. The plants don't compete for light so much as for *space*, packed near-touching, layered three and four deep, so that walking through means pushing aside curtains of leaf and frond that part with a wet, reluctant sigh. Water is scarce here in any open sense — no rivers, no standing pools — but it lurks. Condensation beads on the undersides of leaves. Fog pockets sit in depressions like spilled milk, given off by something unseen, evaporating and reforming on a rhythm that doesn't match daylight. The dampness is intimate rather than abundant: in your collar, in the join of fingers, in the slow sweat the warmth pulls out of you.

The light is low — not night, not storm-dark, but a perpetual dusk-quality, as though the sun here is filtered through several things at once: altitude haze, spore-clouds, the strange refractive shimmer that hangs over certain stretches of ground like heat-warp but colder, wronger. In those shimmer-zones, distances lie. A ridge that should be forty paces away takes four hundred to reach, or three. A leaf might hang in two positions at once for the half-second it takes your eye to commit to one.

And there is machinery. Not buildings — nothing built, no walls, no shelter, no settlement — but *engines*, scattered across the highland like seeds that grew into something other than plants. Some run. Most found here are colossal, half-sunk into the rock as though they precipitated out of the stone itself rather than being placed — vast cylindrical drums veined with corroding pipe, turning slowly though nothing turns them, venting a low continuous exhalation of warm vapor that feeds the very mist drifting between the fungal towers. Gear-clusters the size of boulders mesh in lightless crevices, their teeth grinding so gradually that you only notice motion if you wait a full minute and compare. There is no factory, no foundry, no operator. The industry is ambient, distributed, native to the place the way a heartbeat is native to a chest — production without producer, output without evident purpose, just continuous strange labor going on in the dim light, half-grown into the cliffs, half-growing the cliffs in turn.

The fauna moves through and around all this in abundance: pale, many-legged grazers cropping the rubbery leaf-mats in shifting herds that number in the dozens, their footfalls oddly silent on the broken rock; flying things — not birds, something stiffer-winged, gliding between the engine-stacks on updrafts of vented heat — circling in numbers thick enough to dim the light further when they pass overhead in loose, wheeling masses; smaller things half-glimpsed in the leaf-curtains, multiplying eyes catching what light there is and throwing it back amber.

Yet for all this density of life and machine, the place is mostly quiet. Sound does not carry the way it should at this scale — the vastness should produce its own weather-roar, its own wind-howl across open plateau, but instead there's a hush, broken only by: the wet parting of leaves, the grinding gears at the edge of hearing, the occasional low engine-exhale, the soft many-footed passage of a grazing herd. Then, without pattern, a silence so complete it presses on the ears — followed by a single sound, enormous and brief, like a turbine spinning up and dying in one breath, from some engine-knot a mile off, gone before the echo can answer it.

This is a turbulent place, though the turbulence isn't weather in the conventional sense — it's *ontological*. The ground itself seems to renegotiate its own facts. Walk the same stretch of plateau twice and find the ridge has migrated, the engine-cluster has rotated its orientation, the herd grazing a slope has become a different species of grazer without any visible transition. Cliffs that were basalt an hour ago show seams of something that looks unmistakably like cooling magma still, faintly luminous, though the air around it carries no extra heat. Occasionally — rarely, but reported by anyone who spends long enough here — a fold of the landscape will simply *fail to resolve*, a patch of fog and leaf and rock that the eye cannot parse into consistent geometry, that the mind slides off of the way it slides off a word repeated too many times. People who walk into these folds do not reliably walk back out the same.

There are rules, of a kind, though none of them are written and none of them hold absolutely:

The engines do not stop, but they are not always in the same place twice — find one, and trust it will have somewhat relocated by your return, though it will always be "an engine" and always be doing approximately the same ambient, purposeless work.

The herds graze toward the shimmer-zones and never into them; whatever instinct governs the fauna here respects the unreliable geometry even where human judgment fails to notice the boundary in time.

Warmth pools low, in the leaf-mats and depressions, and rises in thin invisible columns near the bigger engine-stacks — following these columns is the nearest thing to a safe path through ground whose footing cannot otherwise be trusted, since erosion this young means crevices opening underfoot without warning, sharp-edged drops disguised by overgrowth.

And the quiet is not safety. The quiet is the place *between* events — a turbine-shriek, a re-sorting of the ridge-line, a fog-pocket exhaling itself into being. Anyone who has stayed here long enough learns to read the held breath of the silence as the truer signal, the warning that something — geological, mechanical, or stranger — is about to happen, born too recently to have learned restraint, too vast to be stopped, and entirely unconcerned with whether you are standing in its way.
