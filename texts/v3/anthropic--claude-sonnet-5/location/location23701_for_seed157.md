# The Hollow of Standing Water

There is a basin in the high country that the maps, if anyone drew maps here, would mark only as a depression between two shoulders of old stone. It is small — you could walk its perimeter in the time it takes water to boil, if you had a fire, if you had a vessel, if anyone here boiled anything. No one does. The basin holds itself closed, a room without walls, ceilinged in darkness even at noon, because the canopy above it is so total that what filters down is more memory of light than light itself — a perpetual dusk the color of wet slate.

## Ground and Water

The footing here is never dry. Not flooded, not a swamp exactly — the water sits *near*, an inch below moss, two inches below leaf-litter, surfacing in patient seeps wherever the established rock dips low enough to ask it to. Press a hand into any soft surface and water rises around your fingers within a few breaths. The stone — old, rounded, lichen-crusted in greens so dark they read as black — weeps continuously from hairline seams, not dripping so much as sweating, a slow exudation that has been going on, by the feel of the grooves it's carved, for longer than anything living here can account for. These weep-channels have worn smooth gutters into the rock, gutters polished to a shine that catches no light because there's no light to catch, but that you can feel under bare feet: glass-smooth troughs braided into a basin floor.

Condensation beads on every leaf-underside, every fern-spine, every strand of hanging moss, and falls — not as rain, but as a continual soft percussion, droplet by droplet, off thousands of points at once. This is the basin's only persistent voice.

## Vegetation, Wall to Wall

There is no negative space in this place. Ferns the height of a crouching person grow in stands so dense their fronds interlock, and beneath them a lower understory of broad, water-glossed leaves fills whatever the ferns leave open, and beneath *that*, moss and liverwort cover every remaining surface — stone, deadfall, the lower trunks of trees — so completely that the idea of "bare ground" doesn't apply here. It would be inaccurate to say the plants compete; they have, over a long established time, settled into a packed coexistence, tiered by height, each layer taking the light the layer above didn't want. Above it all, a slow-growing high canopy — trunks thick with epiphytes, no single species crowding out the rest, just decades or centuries of mutual, grinding accommodation — closes the basin's lid.

Nothing here is sparse. Nothing here is new.

## What Moves

The basin is, despite its silence, full. This is the central fact of the place: a hush that contains enormous, restless occupancy. Fauna saturates every layer of the vegetation — you sense it more than see it, because the dark and the cover hide almost everything from sight. Things move in the canopy, a constant soft traffic of shifted weight on branches. Things move at the leaf-litter, rustles that don't resolve into shapes. Pale, slow shapes glide just beneath the standing surface-water in the gutters and seep-pools, eyes or eye-analogues catching what little light there is in a brief flat gleam before submerging again. Insects exist in such numbers that the air itself has texture — a faint, constant, directionless hum underlying the percussion of condensation, so steady you stop hearing it as sound and start feeling it as pressure.

Nothing here seems predatory exactly, or if it is, the basin has reached an equilibrium so old that ambush and evasion have both become unhurried. Things are eaten; things eat; it happens in the dark, quietly, and the basin's overall stillness is never disturbed by it.

## Rules of the Place

This is a basin that does not change. Not seasonally, not generationally — the moss does not advance or retreat, the water level in the gutters does not rise or fall, the same soft ceiling of leaf-light governs every hour. Whatever processes brought it to this saturated, settled, packed equilibrium finished a very long time ago, and now it simply continues, the way a held breath can be sustained indefinitely if nothing requires the breather to exhale.

There are no constructions here, not even nests visible from the ground, not even cairns of shed shell or bone. If hands work here — and something in the texture of a few worn hollows in the softer stone suggests they might, suggests a hand-tool once scraped a basin into rock to catch a better trickle of water — they have worked so lightly and so long ago that the basin has absorbed the evidence the way it absorbs everything: folded it under moss, smoothed it with weeping stone, made it indistinguishable from what occurs without hands at all.

To enter the basin is to feel watched by a density of unseen life and met by an old, wet, patient quiet — a place that has finished arguing with itself about what it should be, and now simply, endlessly, is.
