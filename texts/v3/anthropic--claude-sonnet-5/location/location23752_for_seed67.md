# The Sump Engines of Aral-Vey

The horizon does not curve so much as repeat. For thousands of kilometers in every direction, the same low ceiling of warm steam hangs forty meters above a floor of riveted iron plate, and the plate goes on, and on, broken only by the regular intervals of condensation towers exhaling their slow grey breath into a sky that was built, like everything else here, on purpose.

There is no ground beneath the ground. Drill anywhere and you strike more structure — pipe, plate, ducting, the cooling ribs of some machine whose function was forgotten before the plate above it was laid. The builders, whoever or whatever they were, did not lay a floor on a world. They *made* the world, layer over layer, the way a wasp makes a nest from chewed paper, except the paper is alloy and the nest is the size of a continent and nobody has located its edges in any direction anyone bothered to record.

## Surface

The terrain reads as low and gently undulating, but the undulation is mechanical, not geological — long swells where some buried structure crowns just under the plate, shallow basins where condensate pools in painted iron troughs. Everything is young in the way a fresh weld is young: edges still sharp, plate-seams still proud, rivet-heads still showing the bright scratch of recent contact rather than the rounded blur of age. Nothing here has had time to erode because nothing here is allowed to erode — or perhaps it simply hasn't been long enough yet, on whatever timescale this place keeps.

Water moves everywhere and pools nowhere for long. It beads on cold piping, drips in slow percussion from elevated conduits, runs in thin sheets down condensation towers into gutters cut along every wall and floor-seam, as if moisture were not weather here but *waste heat made visible*. The air carries a constant low humidity, warm and faintly metallic, like breathing through a kettle's spout. Weeping rivets. Sweating valve-housings. Standing water never rises past the ankle because the drainage was engineered, long ago, to never let it.

## What Lives Here

This is the strange part, the part that resists the mineral logic of the rest: there is no vegetation, not one spore, not a single photosynthetic smear on any surface — and yet the place teems. Fauna beyond counting. Things that move through the steam-layer in flocks too dense to track individually; things that nest in the louvers of idle turbines; things with too many legs picking paths along handrails with the unbothered fluency of creatures who have never known a world without rails. None of them eat plants, because there are none. They eat each other, or they eat the residues of industry — mineral scale, oxide flakes, the slow bloom of corrosion byproduct that forms in damp corners — or they eat something this description cannot name because no one has yet traced the bottom of the food web down to its source.

They are dense without being loud. That's the unsettling music of the place: you can stand in a chamber with what must be thousands of individual creatures within sight — clinging to pipe-runs, drifting in the steam-haze, threading the grates underfoot — and hear almost nothing. A click. A soft chitinous shuffle. The drip of condensate. The biomass is overwhelming; the soundscape is a held breath. Whatever these things are, they were either built for silence or have lived so long among engines that silence became their only available register, the way prey animals on a wind-scoured plain stop singing because song carries.

## The Engines Themselves

The "industrial" of this place is not incidental backdrop — it is the place's organ system. Pump-stations the size of cathedrals stand at intervals with the regularity of cities on old maps, each one driving some portion of an unseen, continental circulation: water up, steam off, condensate down, repeat. Conveyor-ways run for kilometers between them, mostly stilled, carrying nothing, as though the cargo they were built for stopped arriving generations ago but no one thought to shut down the belts. Electric light comes from sodium-colored ranks of fixtures bolted to the underside of the steam-ceiling, throwing a warm, slightly orange, perfectly even light across everything — no shadow lasts long because no single light source is solely responsible for any patch of floor. It is daylight manufactured to spec, day after day, unwavering, because the dynamic of this world is fixed at zero: nothing changes here, not the light-level, not the humidity, not the low atmospheric murmur of distant turbines. It has reached some equilibrium and stayed there, perhaps for ages, perhaps since the moment of its making.

## Rules

- **Everything has a function, even if the function is now unknown.** No surface, structure, or object in Aral-Vey was not placed by design. Mystery here is not absence of cause but loss of the instruction manual.
- **Nothing decays past its threshold.** Corrosion happens, water flows, rivets weep — but the system maintains itself at a fixed level of wear, as if some unseen process resets or arrests entropy just shy of failure. The place is permanently *young* despite being, presumably, very old.
- **Density of life does not mean noise.** The fauna are legion and laconic. To be loud here is to stand out, and standing out, by the unspoken physics of the place, does not go rewarded.
- **The horizon is a lie of scale, not curvature.** You will never see the edge of this floor. Weather — true weather, fronts and pressure systems — forms in the steam-ceiling, contained entirely within the built atmosphere, because the scale is large enough to generate its own climate inside a structure that, technically, has a roof.
- **You are never outdoors, and you are never indoors.** Both words require a "natural" to oppose. Here there is only built and more-built, warm and wet and humming, low to a horizon that was riveted into place and has not moved, and will not move, for as long as anyone has been here to watch it not move.
