# The Singing Flats

There is no horizon here, because the horizon has been worn away along with everything else that might have stood tall enough to make one. This is the oldest land that still exists — not the oldest land that ever existed, which is gone, ground to suspension in the water that moves beneath everything, but the oldest *surviving form*, settled into a flatness so complete it reads as geometry rather than terrain. It goes on for thousands of kilometers in every direction you can't quite determine, because direction itself loses meaning on a plane this vast, this featureless, this finished.

And yet it is anything but quiet, anything but empty, anything but still.

## Ground

The substrate is a pale, mineral-slick clay-stone, the compressed sediment of incalculable cycles, polished by water that never fully leaves and never fully arrives — it simply *is*, perpetually, a film and a seep and a held breath of moisture across every surface. Where the stone shows through it is the color of old bone, of dried milk, of something between salt and skin. It weeps. Not dramatically — there are no springs, no gushing — but everywhere, constantly, a low exudation, so that your footing is always faintly tacky, faintly cool, faintly *alive* in the way only saturated ground can feel.

The flatness is not perfect. It is ancient-perfect, which is different: shallow basins a few centimeters deep, stretching kilometers wide, holding sheets of standing water so thin and so still they function as horizontal mirrors — until they don't, until the sky they reflect does something the sky should not do, and then the water shows that too, faithfully, without complaint.

## Sky and Light

The light is dim not from lack of sun but from an excess of *something else* — a perpetual high haze, water-loaded, that diffuses what daylight exists into a shadowless, directionless glow, gray-gold, the color of held breath. There may be a sun. There may be several, briefly, before resolving back into one — this is not consistent, and the land does not seem to require it to be.

The sky is where the dynamism lives most visibly. Clouds do not drift here; they *churn*, building into towering, continent-sized formations and collapsing within minutes, raining briefly and violently in isolated columns you can watch approach from kilometers off — visible curtains of water moving across the flats like the legs of vast, slow animals. Sometimes the rain falls upward. This is not a metaphor. There are places, unpredictable and unmarked, where you can watch water leave the ground and return to the haze in visible, glittering threads, and the air in those zones tastes like static and old pennies.

## The Sound

This is, before anything else, a deafening place, and that fact reorganizes everything else about it.

The sound is not one sound. It is the superimposition of an uncountable number of small sounds — the land is *packed*, saturated with presence even though almost nothing in it grows taller than your knee — and packed things, in quantity, are loud. Underfoot and at the edge of every shallow pool: a chorus. Reeds, low mosses, thready black-green vegetation that never rises far from the ground but covers every available surface in dense, overlapping mats, and within and beneath that mat, something — many somethings — producing a constant frictional, percussive, piping noise. Not quite insects. Not quite voices. A vast rubbing, clicking, exhaling chorus that swells and recedes in waves that move across the landscape faster than wind, as if the ground itself were breathing through ten million small mouths.

Layered over that: the thunder, near-constant, from the perpetually building and collapsing sky. Layered over *that*: a deep, almost subsonic groan that seems to come from the ground itself, ancient stone settling into configurations it has settled into ten thousand times before, each time slightly different.

There is no silence anywhere on the flats. Silence, when it does happen — and it does, in pockets, abruptly, like a held hand clamped over the world — is the most alarming sound of all, because it never lasts, and what follows it is never predictable.

## Ecology

Fauna is rare here in the sense of *large, individuated, nameable* fauna — you will go days without seeing a creature you could point to and call one thing. But this is misleading, because the packed density of the place is biological as much as geological. The low mats of vegetation are themselves colonial, continuous, possibly singular organisms stretching for kilometers, distinguishable from "ground" only by texture and the sound they make. What looks like sparse scrub is, up close, an interlocking lattice of root-analogs threaded into the wet stone so densely there is no soil, only structure and water and stone, indistinguishable from one another.

The rare true fauna — when you see it — tends to be wrong in some small, specific way. A wading shape with the correct number of legs but the incorrect gait. A flock-shadow crossing one of the standing-water mirrors with no visible flock above it. These are not explained. They are not aggressive, mostly; they seem as preoccupied with the turbulence of the place as you are, residents of a world whose rules they, too, are only partly subject to.

## The Breakdown

The weirdness here does not announce itself as spectacle. It is woven into the settledness of the place, which is the most unsettling part — this is *old* land, the oldest stable thing left, and yet stability and coherence have come uncoupled from each other entirely.

The standing pools sometimes show reflections that precede their cause by several seconds — you see the heron-shape wade through the mirror water a breath before it arrives, or it never arrives at all, and the mirror simply settles back to sky. Distances are not reliable: a feature that should take an hour to reach takes four, or twelve minutes, with no sense of having walked faster or slower, just an elastic disagreement between the land and your body's accounting of it. Twice a day, roughly — though "day" is generous, given the light — the entire visible flat seems to *fold*, very briefly, a fish-eye warp at the edge of vision that resolves before you can look at it directly, leaving everything in the same place but somehow recently rearranged, the way a card trick leaves the deck whole but altered.

The scattered structures — and there are some, rare, partial, an eroded foundation-line here, a single standing wall there, too worn to date, built from the same pale stone as the ground so that they seem to be emerging from it rather than placed upon it — do not always stay where you left them. Not far. A wall seen at midday from two kilometers off may, by the time you reach it, be three hundred meters left or right of its original bearing, and there is no good way to know whether the land moved or your memory did, and after enough time in this place you stop asking, because the asking is its own kind of erosion.

## Rules, As Far As Anyone Has Found Them

The water is older than the stone, in some sense that doesn't track with ordinary chronology — it behaves like it remembers states of the land that no longer exist, occasionally producing reflections, currents, or standing waves that correspond to topography long since worn flat.

Sound here is closer to a layer of weather than a byproduct of things happening — it has its own fronts, its own pressure systems, swelling and breaking independent of any single source, so that you can feel a wall of noise approach across the flat the way you'd feel a rainstorm coming, and brace for it the same way.

Nothing here resolves. The storms build and collapse without ever quite raining themselves out. The chorus swells and recedes without ever quite reaching a peak you could call a climax. The land has reached its final geological form and yet shows no sign of resting in it — as if flatness, total flatness, the erosion of everything down to one shared featureless plane, was not an ending after all, but the precondition for a different, stranger kind of beginning, one with no large shapes left to interrupt it, where every remaining form of change has nowhere to go but sideways, into the water, into the sound, into the increasingly thin membrane between what happened and what is, very loudly, still happening.
