The Terrace of Standing Stems

You reach it after climbing a long basalt shoulder, the rock still sharp underfoot, edges that haven't yet been worn into anything kind. There's no water anywhere — no trace it was ever needed — and yet the basin that opens above the climb is thick with green, fed by something other than rain.

The terrace sits high, a plateau of cooled mineral cut into rough shelves, each shelf no larger than a courtyard, a room, a small field. The whole place could be crossed on foot in an unhurried afternoon. It feels bounded — not by walls, but by the way the land simply stops at certain edges, dropping into haze.

What's built here was built without tools. That's the first wrongness you register. The settlement — and it is a settlement, dense with structure — rises out of the vegetation as if grown rather than made: walls of woven stem and dried resin, towers of stacked pod-husks fused at their seams, dwellings shaped like the inside of a fruit, hollowed and smooth. No seams show tool-marks. No surface bears a cut. Everything looks coaxed, pressed, encouraged into form by patient hands or patient roots — you can't tell which, and the distinction may not hold here.

The vegetation is lush in a way that feels almost architectural itself: broad fan-leaves layered three and four deep, stems thick as a thigh growing in close-set stands, their bark warm-toned, almost orange in the normal daylight that falls evenlyfrom a sky the color of old paper. Everything is close together — dense, near-touching, leaf brushing wall brushing stem brushing the flank of some grazing thing that doesn't startle. There's no current of air to stir it, so the layers hang motionless, stacked like sediment of green.

It's warm, the kind of warmth that sits on the skin without urgency, a held heat radiating up from the young rock as much as down from the light. The ground in the open patches is dry mineral, fine red-grey grit, hot enough at midday to shimmer faintly above it, though nothing here behaves like true desert — the moisture, whatever sustains the green, must come from below, drawn up through root and stem from some deep reservoir the surface never sees.

Fauna is abundant but the place is, strangely, almost silent. Small barrel-bodied animals move between the stem-houses in loose unhurried groups, grazing on low fronds, their feet broad and soft enough to make no more than a papery whisper against the grit. Something larger moves at the terrace's far edge, browsing the high leaf-tiers, its presence betrayed only by the slow sag and recovery of a stem under its weight. Birds, if they are birds, perch in long unmoving rows along the roof-ridges of the stem-towers — dozens of them, packed shoulder to shoulder, utterly still, as though displayed rather than resting. None of them call. The quiet here isn't empty; it's attentive, the kind of silence that suggests everything present is listening rather than speaking.

There is no tech, not even the suggestion of a wheel or a blade — but there is unmistakably settlement, unmistakably order. Paths run between dwellings in patterns too regular to be accidental, paths kept clear of growth though nothing visible clears them. Husk-towers cluster in formations that suggest streets, plazas, a logic of gathering and dwelling — built, you'd swear, by hands, except you never see hands, never see anyone building anything. The structures simply are, complete, and perhaps still slowly becoming more complete, growing by increments too slow to watch, the way a vine finds a trellis without anyone witnessing the finding.

The rules of this place, as far as they can be guessed: nothing here is finished, but nothing here is in any hurry. Change happens at the pace of seasons or longer — a wall thickens, a tower gains a story, a stand of stems closes a gap that was open last year, if there is a "last year" that means anything on this terrace. The young, sharp erosion of the rock underneath suggests the whole plateau itself rose recently, in some geological sense of recently, and the settlement followed almost immediately after, as if life here cannot tolerate an unbuilt surface for long.

Toward the terrace's broken edge, where the shelf ends and the haze begins, the stem-dwellings thin and stop, giving way to a fringe of taller, wilder growth — un-housed, unworked, leaning out over the drop as if curious about it. Stand there long enough and you'll notice the held quality of the air, the way even the breath of the place seems paced to something larger than a day. Nothing rushes here. The grazers will still be grazing when you've gone. The birds, if you wait, may not move at all.
