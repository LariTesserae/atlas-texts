# The Lowmarsh Conurbation

It has no other name worth speaking, because everyone who lives there simply calls it *the Floor* — a low, flat, water-threaded sprawl that has no edges visible from inside it, and which has, over centuries, forgotten it was ever anything but this.

## Terrain and Ground

The elevation never rises more than a few meters above the waterline anywhere within it, and the waterline itself is unstable — not tidal, not seasonal, but *systemic*, governed by something deep in the engineered substrate beneath the city that nobody alive still fully understands. The ground is not soil. It was soil once, perhaps, or whatever passed for soil on this world before vegetation failed to take and something else took its place — a packed mineral hardpan, gray-bronze, riddled everywhere with capillary fissures that weep groundwater upward in sheets after the heat builds. By midday the whole Floor sweats. Walkways shine. Foundations show dark tide-lines climbing their lower courses, evaporating, climbing again.

There is no plant life to speak of — no green anywhere, no root system holding anything together — and yet the ground holds, because it has been built upon so continuously, so densely, for so long, that the construction itself has become the erosion-resistant skin the world never grew naturally. Buildings are the topsoil now. Foundations are the bedrock's replacement. Where structures have been demolished or have fallen, the bared hardpan underneath looks almost wrong, too smooth, like a wound that hasn't scarred over yet because nothing has been laid on top of it.

## Structure and Density

This is the part that overwhelms first: there is no unoccupied space. Not "little" unoccupied space — none. Towers don't stand alone; they merge above the fourth or fifth story into skybridges, conduits, hanging market-decks, lattices of habitation that other towers grow into from the opposite direction until whole districts become a single fused organism of architecture, three and four structures deep in places, one built through and around and inside the bones of the last. Older levels — established, settled, decades or centuries former — sit at the bottom, weathered smooth, their ornament worn into suggestion. Newer growth erupts from them at odd angles, bright-edged, unweathered, still sharp.

The materials are not familiar. Some surfaces are clearly metal, oxidized into soft blues and ambers. Some are a fused ceramic-glass nobody quarries anymore because the technique that made it isn't fully understood even by its inheritors — engineering that exceeds current explanatory physics, structures that flex impossibly under load, that seem to redistribute their own weight, that occasionally *heal* visible cracks overnight through some embedded self-repair logic still faintly humming after generations of disuse and reuse. Entire support pylons seem to drink the standing water at their bases and exhale it, cooler, somewhere above — a building doing what a tree would do, if trees existed here.

## Water

It is everywhere without being a flood. Canals run below and through and sometimes directly underneath load-bearing structures, channeled with obvious ancient intent though no one alive built the channeling. Condensation beads on every cool surface in the warm air and drips in syncopated, arrhythmic chiming from a thousand awning-edges and pipe-joints at once. Marsh-edge logic governs the city's lowest tier: things are damp that shouldn't be, walkways are slick, certain plazas exist permanently ankle-deep and have simply been built around, accepted, populated anyway — markets conducted from raised plank-stalls over standing water, children and fauna alike wading through it with the unconcern of people for whom this has simply always been true.

## Sound

This is the place's dominant fact. It is *deafening*, all the time, from every direction, compounding: water dripping, water rushing through hidden channels, the structural hum/groan/flex-creak of buildings that breathe and resettle under their own impossible engineering, the calls of dense fauna populations nesting in every available ledge and conduit-gap, market-noise compounded across a hundred fused levels stacked vertically so that sound from six stories up arrives undiminished at street level, mixed with sound from six stories below rising back up through grates and shafts. Machinery somewhere is always running, some of it visible, much of it not, embedded in walls whose function has outlived anyone's memory of installing them. There is no silence anywhere in the Floor. People who leave it report the outside world as eerily, unsettlingly quiet.

## Fauna

Abundant, adapted, opportunistic — creatures that have made every ledge, conduit-mouth, water-channel, and dead architectural cavity into habitat. Nothing here evolved alongside plant life; whatever ecology exists has built itself instead around the city's own metabolism — waste-heat vents, water channels, the warm exhaust of failing-but-still-running ancient machines, the food-economy of a packed human population. Flocking things roost in the high lattice-gaps and stream out at dusk in numbers that visibly darken light for whole minutes. Eel-bodied things move through the canal systems too fast to be fished properly. Things live *in the walls* — not pests, exactly, more like a second resident population the city's first population has made an uneasy permanent peace with, because evicting them has never once worked.

## Light and Atmosphere

Daylight is ordinary, unremarkable in itself, but it arrives filtered, fractured, bounced — falling unevenly through the fused upper lattice in shafts and patches, so that any given lower-level plaza experiences its own private weather of light and shadow that has nothing to do with the actual sky, which most residents of the lower tiers rarely see directly at all. The warmth of the air, combined with all that standing and rising water, gives everything a soft haze toward midday, a thickness to far sightlines, condensation forming and dispersing on a daily rhythm independent of any season because the Floor, at this scale, makes its own weather — humidity domes, localized downpours born from its own vapor that fall on one district while the next sits dry, micro-storms spinning up in the gaps between towers and dying within the hour.

## Dynamism — the Turbulence

Nothing here is finished and nothing stays in one state. This is the Floor's deepest organizing truth: it is being built and unbuilt simultaneously and continuously, structures rising in one quarter while others, fatigued or flooded or simply outcompeted for space, are stripped and recycled into the next thing going up two streets over. Whole districts can change character within a generation. The ancient self-repairing infrastructure occasionally does something its builders intended that nobody now predicts — a channel reroutes itself, a tower's substructure decides to redistribute load and an entire block tilts six degrees over a season, forcing evacuation, re-leveling, re-settlement. Market economies boil and collapse and reboil in the space of days. It has the energy of an anthill that never stops being kicked — not chaos exactly, because it has rules, an established equilibrium of its own, just one whose stability is *motion itself* rather than stillness. The Floor doesn't hold a shape. It holds a *rate of change*, and has for so long that the rate of change is the most settled, most ancient, most reliable thing about it.

## What People Say About It

Those born in the Floor say there's no such thing as quiet, and don't trust places that have it. They say the buildings are listening, half-joking, because of the way the old self-healing material seems to respond to stress before stress occurs. They say you should never sleep on ground level during the warm months because the hardpan sweats and the damp gets into your bones. They say the things in the walls were here before the people were, which is probably true. And they say, if you ask why no one has ever mapped the whole Floor edge to edge — why no one knows how large it actually is — that the question doesn't really make sense to them: the Floor is not a place with edges you reach. It's a place with density you keep finding more of, the further you go.
