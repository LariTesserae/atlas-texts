A high shelf of land, no bigger than a few terraced fields laid end to end, holds itself above the world in a fold of mist that never quite burns off and never quite thickens to rain. This is a small place, contained, its edges marked not by walls but by where the ground simply stops rising and falls away into cloud. You could walk its full circumference between one meal and the next.

The terrain is old in the way of things that have stopped changing quickly — humped and settled, basalt-boned, its surface a patchwork of mossed stone shelves stepping up toward a blunt summit nothing has bothered to call a peak. Water moves through everything here without ever forming a lake or river worth naming. It beads on leaf-undersides, threads down rock seams in dark capillary lines, surfaces as small seeps that vanish into root-mat before going anywhere. The rocks weep continuously, a slow black sweat that keeps every surface faintly slick, faintly cold to the touch even in the day's warmth.

Vegetation crowds close but not densely — broad-leaved trees with smooth pale bark grow in loose stands, well-spaced, their canopies overlapping just enough to dapple rather than darken. Between them: ferns waist-high, fat-leaved succulent things growing straight from cracks in stone, hanging mosses that drip rather than sway. The lushness is real but unhurried — nothing strains for light here, there is enough for all of it, so growth has settled into a kind of patient abundance rather than competition. Air moves through the gaps freely. You are never enclosed, only accompanied.

The light is even and clean, neither bright nor dim, falling through the cloud-layer with a diffused, shadowless quality, like the whole plateau sits inside a lantern someone forgot to light fully.

And then the sound. This is the place's strangeness, the thing that doesn't match its emptiness: it is loud. Not with voices, not with anything you can locate — there is almost no fauna, just the occasional pale insect or a single dark bird crossing high and silent — but the air itself seems to carry a sound, a low continuous roar-hum that seems to rise from the stone, from the water moving inside it, from some resonance in the rock's hollow places. Standing still you feel it as much as hear it, a vibration under the sternum. It never gets louder or quieter. It has presumably been here as long as the moss has, which is to say: a very long time, and it isn't going anywhere.

No structures stand. No path has been cut, no stone stacked. If people have ever come here, they left only the faintest suggestions — a cairn-shaped boulder that might be natural, a worn hollow in a rock shelf that might be a seat or might be erosion, a scattering of fire-cracked stones half-sunk in moss near one seep, ash-grey, old enough that new growth has closed over half of them. Whoever made that fire used hand tools, simple blades, nothing more. They are long gone, or they come rarely, on cycles slow enough that the plateau forgets them between visits.

Time moves slowly here. The mist thins and thickens across days, not hours. The seeps swell after rain and shrink back over weeks. A fallen tree might lie half-rotted, half-reclaimed by moss, taking a generation to finish disappearing. Nothing rushes. The loud, ceaseless hum is the only urgent thing in the place, and even that is changeless — a strangeness that has stopped being strange through sheer persistence, the way a held note eventually becomes silence to the ear that's grown used to it, except here it never quite does.

To stand on this shelf is to feel suspended — above the lowland weather, below nothing, ringed by cloud, accompanied by trees that don't crowd you, water that never pools, and a sound with no source you will ever find, no matter how long you stand still and listen for it.
