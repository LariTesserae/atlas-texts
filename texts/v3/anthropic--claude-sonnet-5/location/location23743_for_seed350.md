# The Drowned Works

The water does not fall here so much as it *insists* — a vertical sea, warm as blood, pressing down through a canopy so thick and young that entire trees have not yet learned to bend correctly under the weight. They bow at strange unfinished angles, trunks pale and fast-grown, bark not yet hardened, leaves the size of doors slapping against each other in the deluge with a sound like wet machinery. Because there is wet machinery, somewhere beneath all this. You can hear it before you can place it.

This is low ground — a flooded basin, regional in reach, the kind of place where a valley used to be before something filled it in and called the filling a forest. The land underneath is young: raw red-brown earth, sharp little ridgelines that haven't yet been worn into anything graceful, exposed root-shelves still crumbling at the edges like a wound that hasn't scarred. Erosion hasn't caught up to whatever happened here. Whatever happened here was recent. Whatever is happening here is not finished.

Vegetation crowds in at saturation — fern-things the size of houses, fungal shelves layered up tree trunks like ledgers, vine-mats so dense underfoot that you don't walk on ground so much as on a mattress of living rope, three feet of it, soft and giving and faintly warm from its own rot-heat. Nothing moves through it on legs. There is no animal life here — no birds in the canopy, no things rustling, no eyes in the dark. The absence is its own kind of pressure, because everything about the density and the warmth and the wet says *there should be something living in this*, and there isn't, and the silence-shaped-hole where the fauna should be makes the place feel like a stage set after the actors have been removed but before the set's been struck.

Except it isn't silent. It is the opposite of silent. It is deafening.

Threaded through the trunks, half-swallowed by buttress roots, bolted into outcrops of the young stone — industry. Pipes the width of felled trees, sheathed in something like iron and something like coral, sweating condensation that isn't condensation. Turbine-housings the size of barns, three-quarters reclaimed by climbing fungus, still turning. Still turning. Conveyor-skeletons strung between trees at impossible heights, empty now of whatever they carried, swinging and groaning in the rain like hanged things. Cabling as thick as a torso runs along the forest floor under the vine-mat, and where it surfaces it hums — not electrically, not quite, but with a frequency that you feel in your molars. The built world here is scattered, not concentrated: no factory complex, no town, just outposts and stations and single structures dropped into the green at intervals, half-claimed, still working. Still working *for what* is the question nobody can answer, because there's no one left scattered among the scatter. Just the work, continuing.

The noise is the forest's true weather. Engines that have run past their tolerances into some second register of sound, throbbing underneath the static roar of rain on a million leaves. Metal flexing and complaining somewhere unseen. Pressure-vents screaming open and shut on schedules that don't sync to anything. The water itself seems to carry sound the way it carries warmth — thick, immersive, directionless — so that you cannot tell if the grinding industrial heartbeat is coming from your left or from inside the ground or from the sky, which is the same gray-bronze color whether you look up through canopy or across through rain, because there is no real distinction here between sky and water and roof. Light arrives dim and strained, filtered three or four times over before it reaches anything, the color of old brass, of a lamp behind a curtain behind a waterfall.

And then the rules stop holding.

A turbine-housing a quarter mile off turns the wrong direction relative to its own driveshaft, and has always turned that direction, and you remember it turning the right direction a moment ago though no moment has actually passed. A stretch of pipe disgorges steam upward into the rain, and the steam refuses to disperse — it stands there, a held breath, a held column, for minutes, for longer than minutes mean here. Twice you pass the same fallen stanchion from two entirely different directions of approach. The vine-mat underfoot occasionally pulses, once, like a single slow heartbeat traveling under acres of ground cover, and then stops, and nothing follows from it. Sound arrives slightly before its source — you hear a valve shriek, then watch it shriek, the two events separated by a half-second that should not exist. The basin is *turbulent* in the way weather is turbulent, except the instability isn't atmospheric, it's causal: structures rebuild themselves out of order, decay running in both directions simultaneously, so that you'll find a station rusted to lace standing fifty feets from one gleaming as if freshly poured, and they are — you become increasingly sure — the same station.

There is a working theory, unproven, unprovable, that the place is still being built. Not built once and now decaying — built *continuously*, an act that has not stopped since whatever original catastrophe submerged this valley and breathed industry into it instead of letting it simply drown. The factories were never finished arriving. The flood was never finished falling. The forest was never finished growing into its frame. Everything here is mid-verb, suspended in its own becoming, and the deafening sound is the sound of construction and collapse occurring in the same cubic meter of warm wet air, forever, with no one left to call it either one.

You do not so much walk through the Drowned Works as get *included* in a sentence that is still being written, in a language made of turbines and rain and a green so dense it has its own weather, under a sky the color of an engine that has run too long to ever properly stop.
