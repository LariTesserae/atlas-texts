# The Cloud Forest of Telgrethane Sky-Shelf

There is a place, high on a continental shelf where the air thins and cools, where the ground gave up being ground some unrecorded age ago and became instead a canopy — a single, vast, vegetal mass stretched over the bones of a plateau, fed perpetually by a ceiling of standing rain. Call it a forest if the word can stretch that far. It is more accurate to say the entire visible world, from horizon to horizon, is plant: trunks like cooling-tower columns, root-mats thick as glacial ice, leaves the size of sailcloth, all of it stacked into tiers that rise into the falling water until the upper terraces simply dissolve into mist and you cannot tell anymore where the foliage ends and the sky-flood begins.

**The water** does not fall so much as *occupy*. It is less rain than a permanent atmospheric saturation — a wall, a held breath of moisture that drifts and curtains and occasionally thickens into true downpour, then eases back into a gray suspension you walk through like a held liquid. Nothing here is ever dry. Bark sweats. Stone, where stone shows at all beneath the roots, weeps continuously. There are rivers, but they run *sideways* through the canopy as often as down — channels of water resting in giant cupped leaves the size of valleys, spilling tier to tier in slow cataracts that take days to fully drain from top growth to root-floor.

**The vegetation** is not undergrowth and overstory in the ordinary sense — it is structural. Whole geological-looking formations are plant: ridges that look like sedimentary cliffs are actually root-conglomerate grown and compressed over centuries into stone-hard lattices, function as cliffs, weather as cliffs, but are alive at the cellular level all the way through. Canyons exist where vegetable mass simply never grew, leaving negative space between titanic biomasses, and you can stand in one of these gaps and feel agoraphobic despite a forest in every direction, because the nearest growth-wall might be a kilometer off across a gulf of wet air. This is the sparseness folded into the totality: vegetation absolute in volume, but distributed with huge intervals — groves the size of countries separated by open shafts of mist where nothing grows, where the rain-wall is the only occupant.

**The machines** are the wrongness, and they are everywhere once you start looking, though they never announce themselves as separate from the plant-mass — they are *grown through*. Massive iron-toned structures that function exactly like refineries, like pumping stations, like turbine halls, sit fused into the root systems, vine-strangled and rust-streaked, but *operating*. Pistons the size of cathedral naves move within trunk-cavities. Somewhere below the leaf-floor, something like a furnace breathes orange light up through translucent bark, and the whole tier of forest above it is warmed a few degrees, visible as a faint shimmer-line in the mist. There is no record of who or what built this industry into the living wood — there are no other artifacts, no walls, no roads, no built thing that isn't also a plant. It reads as though the forest itself, at some point in its long, unchanging history, metabolized industry the way it metabolizes minerals: absorbed engines as a kind of organelle. The machinery does not evolve, does not expand, does not break down. It simply runs, forever, at the same output, as much a fixed feature of the place as the cliffs of root.

**The sound** is the loudest kind of constant: the basso thrum of those buried engines, the percussive crash of water dropping tier to tier, the groan of trunk-architecture flexing under its own colossal weight, and laid over all of it, the shrieking, hooting, chittering traffic of fauna using the canopy-shafts as highways. It is loud the way a waterfall or a foundry floor is loud — not chaotic, but a dense, continuous wall of noise you stop hearing as separate sounds after the first hour, the way you stop hearing your own pulse.

**The fauna** moves through this in abundance but never in crowds — sparse-but-everywhere, the way the vegetation itself is sparse-but-everywhere: massive grazing things that look like they eat root-bark and excrete back into the soil-mass they came from, gliding or swimming things adapted to move through standing rain-curtains as easily as air, packs of small quick things that live entirely inside the iron-and-bark machine-organs, denned in the warm spaces between pistons. Nothing predates the machinery. The machinery isn't food, isn't prey, isn't even quite noticed by the ecology — it's geology to them, infrastructure they live around the way anything lives around a cliff or a cave.

**Light** comes down normal and gray-gold through the standing damp, diffused by a billion droplets into something shadowless and directionless — no real noon, no real dusk, just a sourceless even glow that brightens and dims with the thickness of the rain-curtain rather than any motion of a sun you can locate. 

**Temperature** stays cool, almost cold near the upper tiers where wind moves freely between rain and root, warmer in the buried furnace-pockets, but the gradient is stable, fixed, has apparently always been exactly this.

**Nothing here changes.** This is the place's deepest law, more fundamental than its water or its green: whatever process built this fusion of forest and foundry reached, at some point, a perfect equilibrium, and it has held that equilibrium since — not dying, not growing, not evolving, not eroding past its current weathered, established, long-settled shape. The rivers run the same paths every day. The engines have run at the same pitch for longer than anything living can measure. The fauna populations neither boom nor crash. Erosion has worn the root-cliffs into their final, comfortable shapes and stopped. It is a world caught not at its beginning and not at its end, but in the middle of a held note — vast, vegetal, mechanical, raining, and utterly, permanently the same, world without end, amen, hum of turbine, hiss of falling water, on and on into a horizon that never resolves because there is always more forest, more mist, more engine-light glowing up through wet and ancient bark.
