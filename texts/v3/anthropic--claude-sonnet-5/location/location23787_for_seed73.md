# The Cloud-Root Cordillera

The mountains here do not end where mountains usually end. They keep going up past the place where rock should give way to thin air and finished business, up into a permanent collar of cloud that never burns off, never thins — a held breath of vapor that wraps each peak like a hand cupped around a candle. From below, in the warm valleys, you cannot see the summits. You only see where the green stops climbing and the white begins, a line drawn by altitude itself, patient and absolute.

It is hot here. Hot in the way of a held thing, a place sealed by its own geography against any cooling wind. The cloud-collars trap warmth as readily as they trap moisture, and the valleys steam in the mornings, exhaling what the night condensed. Every surface sweats. The rock faces — vast, dark, terraced flanks of something like basalt but veined with a faint, deliberate luminescence — weep continuously, threads of water finding the same grooves they've found for centuries, grooves worn smooth and certain, not raw. This is old erosion, settled erosion. The mountains have made their peace with the water running through them. Nothing here looks newly broken.

## The Vertical Forest

Vegetation does not stop at any sensible elevation. It climbs the cliff faces in dense mats, root systems threading into cracks that fill with cloud-moisture and never fully dry, holding canopy at heights where canopy has no business existing. Broad-leafed trees with bark like wet slate grow sideways out of vertical stone, their canopies forming green shelves a thousand feet up, with smaller trees, ferns, and a hanging undergrowth of pale vine cascading off the shelf-edges in curtains forty feet long. The layering is total — moss on bark, fern on moss, orchid-analogues rooted in the fern, insects living in the orchids, all of it close enough to touch its neighbor, a saturated stack of green going up the cliff like rungs.

Between the vertical forest-shelves, waterfalls drop from the cloud-line itself — not sourced from any visible lake or snowmelt, but seemingly condensed directly out of the permanent mist above, falling in long unbroken ribbons that vaporize partway down into drifting curtains of spray, then re-form lower as streams, feeding the marsh-floor of every valley between peaks. The valley floors are wet ground, near-bog, threaded with slow black water and stands of reed-like growth taller than a person, the established kind of wetland that has clearly been a wetland for a very long time, with its own deep peat and its own fixed channels.

## What Moves

The place is loud — not chaotic, but full. Birdcall analogues echo cliff to cliff in long delayed repeats, the architecture of the gorges turning every cry into a small chorus. Things move through the canopy-shelves in numbers: gliding fauna with membrane-wings that catch the warm updrafts rising off the steaming valley floor, riding thermals from shelf to shelf rather than flying conventionally, descending in long lazy spirals to drink at the black marsh-water before climbing again. Larger things move slower, low to the ground in the wet brush — heavy-bodied browsers with shell-like dorsal plating, beaded with condensation, leaving trails through the reeds that fill with water within the hour and look, the next morning, as though they were never made.

The abundance is dense without being chaotic — everything has its layer, its niche, its time of day. Dawn brings the spiral-gliders. Midday belongs to the things in the canopy. Dusk is when the marsh-floor browsers come out, and the sound changes register, the birdcall giving way to a lower, resonant calling that seems to come from the black water itself.

## The Old Marks

Built things are rare and strange, scattered like dropped objects rather than placed with intention you'd recognize. A platform of fused stone juts from one cliff-shelf, anchored into the living rock, its surface inlaid with a lattice that still, faintly, glows — drawing a slow current from somewhere, powering nothing visible, simply persisting. Elsewhere, a structure that might be a bridge or might be a frozen gesture spans two peaks at a height where the cloud-collar should make such a thing invisible from below, except that it isn't: it holds its own pocket of clarity around it, a sphere of undisturbed visibility a few hundred meters wide, as if the mist has been politely asked to stay back and has agreed, indefinitely.

These remains don't cluster. They don't suggest a city or a civilization in the way ruins usually do — one platform per mountain, perhaps, one bridge spanning one particular gap, an occasional standing post of dark material driven into a cliff-shelf with no visible purpose, humming very faintly at a frequency the local fauna seem to avoid by a wide, instinctive margin. The tech, where it appears, does not look built so much as grown into agreement with the mountain — it bends light slightly, it sheds water without ever rusting, it persists through erosion cycles that should have claimed it a thousand years ago, and persists still, untroubled, while the rock around it keeps weathering at its own unhurried rate.

## The Rule of the Place

Nothing here changes quickly. The cloud-collars have sat at the same elevation, by all evidence, for an extremely long time; the river channels in the marsh below have carved their banks into permanence; even the glowing lattice-platforms seem to operate on cycles measured in years, not days, their light brightening and dimming on some calendar too slow to witness directly. This is a landscape that has finished arguing with itself. The water knows where to fall. The forest knows how high it can climb before the air gets too thin even for it, and stops there, in a clean line, every time. The old structures have made their accommodation with time and don't appear to be in any hurry to lose it.

What strangeness exists here is structural, not eventful — built into the bones of the place rather than happening as incident. The clarity-spheres around the high structures. The luminescence under the rock-skin. The way certain post-markers hum and certain animals will not cross within a hundred meters of them. The cordillera does not narrate itself. It simply continues, hot and wet and densely alive, climbing into its own permanent weather, indifferent to whether anyone arrives to read the few sentences it has chosen, here and there, to leave legible.
