# The Canopy Steppes of Halvarrun

The first thing that strikes you, coming up out of the lowland mists onto the high plateau, is that the ground here is not ground at all. It is a roof.

Halvarrun sits at altitude — a vast tableland lifted enough that the air thins and cools at dusk, though by midday the sun presses down through gaps in the canopy with a heat that makes the resin-bark sweat. But the soil itself, the actual mineral floor of this place, may lie thirty meters down, forty, lost beneath an interlocking architecture of trunk, root-arch, and frond that has been building itself for longer than anyone has counted. You do not walk on Halvarrun so much as walk *through* it, along the sparse threadwork of trails that wind across root-bridges thick as roads, under boles wider than houses, between hanging curtains of pale vine that sway though there is no wind you can feel.

## The Substrate

This is a place where vegetation has become terrain. There is barely any bare earth left to find — what little water exists here arrives as fog-drip and short, violent monsoon bursts that last an hour and vanish, so the plants have learned to hoard it themselves: barrel-trunks swollen with stored sap, leaves cupped into funnels, whole sections of bark grown porous and spongy so they drink the air's damp before it can evaporate in the heat. Water is rare enough that you can go a day's walk hearing only the trickle of condensation working its way down some interior channel of bark, never finding the source.

The erosion here is old, settled, established — this is not a landscape still finding its shape. The great root-arches have the smoothness of things shaped by ten thousand years of weather and traffic: rubbed pale on the topside where feet and claws have passed, mossed dark in their undersides where things that prefer dark have made homes. Whole limbs have died and become bridges, become caves, become the floors of clearings where younger growth has pushed up through the rot of the old. Nothing here looks new. Everything looks like it has always been arriving at exactly this form.

The scale defies easy reckoning. This is not a forest; it is closer to a second sky, a continent-spanning lattice that generates its own weather — fog pooling in its lower folds at dawn, thunderheads building above its high canopy by early afternoon, rain that may fall on one shoulder of the plateau while the next valley over bakes dry. Walking a day might cross only a fraction of a single root-system's territory. There are travelers' tales of trunks whose far side has never been mapped.

## Life, Overwhelming

And yet for all this immensity, the plant-mass itself feels sparse in its punctuation — vast trunks set wide apart, broad clearings of root and lichen-floor between them, space enough that you rarely feel crowded by the architecture itself. It is what *moves* within that space that overwhelms.

The fauna here exceeds reckoning. Things flash through the canopy gaps in numbers too fast to count — feathered, scaled, membrane-winged, things with no obvious analog, moving in flocks and shoals and undifferentiated swarms. Ground-things lumber across the root-bridges in herds that take an hour to pass. Things live *inside* the trunks, you realize, when a bole the width of a tower suddenly exhales a cloud of clicking, chittering motion from some hollow chamber within. The sound never stops. It is, frankly, deafening: a continuous, layered roar of call and counter-call, the bass throb of something enormous somewhere using the trunks themselves as drums, the insectile shimmer-static that never quite resolves into silence even at what passes for night. You learn to shout to be heard at arm's length, or you learn the hand-signs that the few human travelers here have developed instead.

The strangeness sits exactly here, in the ecology — relationships between species that don't map to anything tidy. Creatures that seem to *farm* sections of bark-fungus, herding smaller grazers toward it and away. A vast pale animal, antlered with what might be a developed lichen-symbiote rather than bone, that stands motionless in clearings for days at a time until something unseen makes it move. Things that call back in fragments of other species' calls, a confusion of mimicry so dense that no sound here can quite be trusted as identification. The forest does not feel hostile, but it feels *attentive* — aware of you in a way mere biomass shouldn't be, with too many eyes, in too many places, tracking your particular shape of movement.

## The Scattered Hand

People exist here, but lightly. The built environment is scattered, faint, the work of hands with simple tools — rope bridges lashed with fiber, platforms wedged into root-forks, the occasional carved totem-post gone gray and soft with rot, marking a trail-junction or a boundary nobody living quite remembers the reason for. There are no walls. There is no metal beyond what might be carried in on a traveler's person, no permanent fires lit, because the canopy mass would rather not be tested that way and the people who live at its margins know it. What structures exist are built to be reabsorbed — lean-tos of woven frond, granaries on stilts, a single rope ladder vanishing up into a hollow that some family has called home for three generations, its entrance marked only by a faded fetish of woven bark-thread meant to ward off whatever moves through the canopy at the hour between dusk and dark.

These are not conquerors of the place. They are tenants, permitted by scale and inattention to live in its gaps, and they know the rules without needing them written: don't fell a living trunk, don't drum where the deep callers might hear and answer, don't trust a clearing that's too quiet, don't drink standing water without testing it first against your own skin. Move during the loud hours, because predators favor the lull. Listen for the specific layered silence that means something enormous nearby has stopped to notice you.

## At Rest

Toward what passes for evening, the heat doesn't break so much as thicken — the air goes gold-green through the high leaves, full of drifting spore-motes and the lazy spirals of huge soft-winged things riding thermals up from the canopy's heated crown. The deafening noise doesn't stop here; it only changes register, the daylight chorus handing off to the night chorus in a transitional hour when both run simultaneously, a sound like two rivers crossing.

And somewhere below, in the dark spaces between root and root, the water — what little there is — gathers itself drop by patient drop, and waits for the next short violent rain to come and renew the whole improbable, ancient, roaring machine of it.
