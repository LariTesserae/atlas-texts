# The Stillward Range

The mountains here are old beyond the meaning of the word — not old like stone is old, but old like a held breath that has been held so long it has become a kind of silence in itself. They rise to elevations that should be impossible to breathe at, and yet the air at their shoulders is thin but mild, scrubbed of weather by something other than altitude. They were not built by tectonics. Nothing here remembers tectonics. They were *settled* into this shape, the way a face settles into its final expression — every peak ground down past youth, past drama, past the sharp adolescence of younger ranges, into long convex curves the color of old bone and rust-grey iron. Erosion finished its work here so long ago that the wind doesn't even bother anymore. There is nothing left to take.

And yet.

Despite the stillness of the rock, the sky above the Stillward is never the same twice. This is the contradiction the place is built on, if "built" is even a word that applies: a profoundly ancient terrain under a violently unfinished sky. Storms assemble themselves up there with the speed of thought — not storms of water, since there is none, but storms of *pressure*, visible as standing ripples in the air itself, places where light bends wrong and the horizon briefly doubles. Updrafts spiral into towers a kilometer wide, hold their shape for an hour, then unravel into nothing. The atmosphere over the range behaves like something still being decided, draped across a landscape that finished deciding eons ago.

The valleys between peaks are vast — walking floor to floor would take days, and the scale of the place makes the rare traveler feel less like a hiker and more like a mite crossing the back of something sleeping. Almost nothing grows. What vegetation exists clings in thin grey-green threads to wind-sheltered crevices: lichen-things, leafless and slow, that may take a century to spread a hand's width. There's no canopy, no understory, nothing resembling a forest — just rock, scattered low growth, and distances that swallow scale.

Fauna are rare enough that seeing one is an event remembered for years. Pale, long-legged creatures move along the high ridgelines sometimes, alone, never in groups large enough to call a herd. Nobody is entirely sure what they eat. Their tracks are the only proof they're not stories.

Scattered through the range — never clustered, always alone, kilometers apart — are the structures. Not ruins, exactly, because ruin implies decay, and decay implies something time can still do to them. These don't decay. A slender black arch stands across one ridge, holding nothing, supporting nothing, and the air passing through its frame arrives on the other side measurably colder, for reasons no instrument brought here has been able to explain. A half-buried lattice the size of a small hill, in a valley three days from anywhere, hums at a frequency too low to hear but felt in the teeth, and the lichen near it grows in spiral patterns nowhere else does. These are not ruins of a civilization that ended. They feel more like instruments left running, tending the sky-storms above with a patience indistinguishable, from the ground, from indifference.

There is almost no sound. Wind passes over rock that's too smooth and too old to whistle. What you hear instead, if you stay still long enough, is the occasional deep concussion of the upper atmosphere doing something to itself far overhead — a soft thunder with no flash, no rain, arriving seconds or minutes after whatever caused it, rolling down the mountainsides and dying in the valleys.

People who come here — rare, and usually only once — describe two competing impressions that never quite resolve into one. The ground says: *everything that was going to happen here already happened, a very long time ago, and what remains is the shape left behind.* The sky says: *nothing here has been decided yet, and won't be, maybe ever.* The structures, ancient and impossible and still faintly, inexplicably warm to the touch in places, seem to be the only things old enough and strange enough to stand in both timescales at once — anchored to the dead calm below, listening to the unceasing argument above.
