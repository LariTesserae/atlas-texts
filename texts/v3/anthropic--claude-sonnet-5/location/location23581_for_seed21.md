# The Steaming Shelf

The plateau arrives without warning, a wound of basalt thrust up through younger rock, its edges still sharp enough to draw blood if you fell against them. This is high ground, but not old ground — the stone here remembers being somewhere else, deeper, darker, before something folded the world and pushed it skyward. You can see the violence in the rock's grain, the way strata that should run flat instead buckle and tilt like pages of a book slammed shut too fast.

It is hot here in a wet way — not the dry crackle of desert but a thick, breathing heat, heat with weight to it. Steam rises constantly from fissures in the basalt, threading upward in slow ribbons that catch the light and scatter it. The air condenses on every surface; stone sweats. Touch any rock-face and your palm comes away slick. Small pools collect in basin-hollows — not lakes, nothing so large — each one rimmed with mineral crusts in colors that shouldn't occur naturally: a faint bismuth sheen, threads of verdigris, a deep wine-dark stain that pulses faintly when shadows pass over it, as though the mineral itself is reactive to light.

## What Grows

Vegetation comes in clusters, not carpets — lush where it exists, absent everywhere else, so that the plateau reads as a series of small green rooms scattered across grey stone. Broad-leafed succulent things with leaves like cupped hands, gathering condensation and funneling it down into root-bulbs swollen with stored water. Between them, low fern-analogs with fractal leaf patterns that seem almost too precise, as though grown to a specification rather than evolved. There's a quality of *design* to the growth here that unsettles before it charms.

Some of the plants hum. Not loudly — you'd miss it if the place weren't otherwise so quiet — but if you put your ear near certain stalks, a low resonant frequency comes through, like a held breath given pitch. Botanists, if any came here, might call it sap cavitation, fluid moving under pressure through specialized channels. But the regularity of the tone, the way clusters of the plants seem to harmonize with each other across distances of twenty or thirty meters, suggests something closer to communication.

## What Was Built

Scattered through the green rooms are the remains — though "remains" implies decay, and these don't decay, not visibly, not at any rate erosion this young could account for. Partial structures. A archway, freestanding, its underside etched with a geometry that doesn't quite resolve into language but clearly isn't decoration — too repeated, too rule-bound. A flat plinth of some material that isn't basalt, isn't anything you'd find in a geology textbook, smooth as poured glass but warm to the touch, warmer than the ambient heat would explain. Embedded in one plinth, a hairline lattice glows faintly after dusk — not bioluminescence, not firelight, something engineered, still running on power from a source no one has located, doing a job no one can identify, patient and indifferent to whether anyone is here to see it.

These structures don't cluster into anything like a village or a ruin-field. They're spaced apart, the way the vegetation is spaced apart, the way everything here seems to prefer room to breathe. A single building's worth of fragments scattered across a kilometer of stone. You start to feel each one might be a punctuation mark in a sentence too large to read from the ground.

## What Moves

Fauna is rare enough that an encounter feels like an event. A lizard-analog, scaled in plates that shift faintly iridescent as it crosses between shade and steam-light, has eyes mounted in a way that suggests it's tracking something other than predators — looking, perhaps, at the hum-frequencies the plants put out, or at gradients of heat the human eye can't parse. Once, maybe twice in a long walk across the shelf, something large moves at the edge of vision among the steam-columns — a shape too tall and too quiet, gone before the eye commits to it. Whether predator or something else entirely, the plateau keeps its own counsel.

Insects, when they appear, move with unnatural precision — straight lines, sudden stops, as though following sightlines invisible to anything without their particular eyes. They don't swarm. They process, singly or in twos, between one green room and the next, like couriers.

## The Rules of the Place

Water rises here rather than falling — that's the first thing you learn, watching steam thread up from rock that should be too young, too freshly thrust-up, to hold this much trapped heat and moisture. The water table sits unnaturally close to the surface, as if the plateau is less a landform than a lid, set down over something still cooling, still venting.

The second thing you learn is that the engineered fragments don't interact with each other the way ruins should. The archway doesn't align with the plinth. The lattice doesn't seem aimed at anything. It's less like the wreckage of one structure and more like pieces of several different unfinished structures, set down in proximity by something with very different ideas about distance and time than the creatures now walking among them.

Sound carries oddly. A footstep on the slick rock travels further than it should, bouncing between basalt faces, arriving from a direction it didn't originate. The quiet between these sounds is not empty — it has texture, a kind of held tension, as if the plateau is mid-exhale and has been for ten thousand years.

Nothing here decays at the rate it should. The young rock, sharp-edged and fresh, sits beside artifacts of an engineering that makes the rock look hasty and improvised by comparison. Time moves through this place at more than one speed, and the seams between those speeds are where the steam rises thickest, where the plants hum loudest, where the light, ordinary as it is, seems to bend very slightly around the wine-dark mineral stains — bending toward something none of the human instruments here are built to register, but that the lizard-analogs, eyes cocked at an angle, watch as if waiting for it to arrive.
