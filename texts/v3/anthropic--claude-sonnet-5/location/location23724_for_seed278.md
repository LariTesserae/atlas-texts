# The Tableland of Quiet Engines

The plateau goes on past the curve of what you'd call a horizon, if horizons here meant anything other than a suggestion. You climbed for two days, maybe three, to reach this shelf of rock, and now there's nowhere higher to climb to that would tell you more. The land is flat in the way old things become flat — not by design but by erosion's enormous patience, wind and the rare hard rain taking the same stone in the same place for a span of time that doesn't translate into anything human. Whatever mountains stood here once are memory now, rendered down into long ridges like the backs of buried animals, into shallow basins, into scree fields gone smooth at the edges.

The cold sits on everything like a second atmosphere. Not brutal — not a cold that kills quickly — but constant, settled into the rock itself, so that even the few hours of weak grey light each day-cycle don't warm the surface so much as make it tolerable. The light itself is dim, filtered through a high haze that never quite clears and never quite thickens into proper cloud. Sometimes you can find the sun's position. Mostly you can't.

Vegetation is a rumor here. Lichen crusts the leeward sides of boulders in patches of rust and ash-green. Where damp gathers — in the bottom of a wind-carved hollow, where condensation beads overnight on the undersides of stone shelves and slides down into cupped basins — a fringe of wiry grass holds on, grey-green, stunted, spaced so far apart from its neighbors that each tussock seems to be making its own private negotiation with the ground. There are no trees. There is no canopy, no understory, none of the layered architecture of fuller places. Just rock, lichen, the occasional grass-ring around a moisture pocket, and space.

So much space. The scale of this place is the first thing that undoes you and the last thing you stop noticing, because you can't actually hold it in attention for long. The plateau has weather systems that begin and end without ever reaching you — you'll see a curtain of rain three days' walk distant, a grey smear hanging from the haze-ceiling down to the ground, moving along its own track, never arriving. The land is vast enough to generate its own indifference.

And it is *full of animals.*

This is the wrongness of the place, the thing that takes longest to understand. By every measure of available water, of plant matter, of anything an ecology should need to sustain itself, this plateau should be nearly empty. It isn't. Herds move across the open ground in numbers that don't make sense against the sparse forage — long-legged grazers with backward-curving horns, hides the color of the rock they cross, moving not in tight defensive masses but spread thin and patient, walking for the sake of walking, grazing on lichen and the wiry grass and, it seems, on something else, something you can't see, some sustenance the land offers that isn't visible to you. Above them, things wheel in the haze — not birds exactly, wider-winged, slower, riding thermals that shouldn't exist in air this cold. At dusk the ground itself seems to shift in the middle distance, a slow boiling of smaller creatures, burrow-things, surfacing in their hundreds to do something unhurried before going under again.

None of it is loud. That's the second wrongness, sitting right alongside the first. A herd this size should be a thunder of hooves, a chorus of calls. Instead there's a hush over the whole tableland, broken by isolated sounds that seem swallowed before they travel far — a single low call, descending, unanswered; the click of hoof on stone; wind finding an edge of rock and drawing one thin note out of it before losing interest. The animals move through their abundance in near silence, as though sound itself were another scarce resource being rationed out here, like water, like warmth.

And under all of it, woven into the rock at intervals that seem to follow no map you can read, there is a sound that isn't animal and isn't wind: a deep, slow throb, felt more than heard, rising up through the soles of your feet from somewhere beneath the plateau's skin. It comes in long intervals — minutes between pulses, sometimes longer — patient in the same register as everything else here. There is nothing built on this land. No tower, no wall, no road, no fence, nothing raised by hand or tool meets the eye anywhere across that vast grey distance. And yet the throb persists, mechanical in its evenness, industrial in a way that has no business existing without smokestacks or rails or any of the apparatus such a sound should require. Somewhere below — under the lichen, under the scree, under the long-worn bones of the dead mountains — something with the rhythm of an engine turns over, slow, vast, and very old, and the plateau has simply learned to hold it the way it holds the cold, the way it holds the herds, the way it holds the silence: as a condition of being here, unexplained, unhurried, and entirely unbothered by your need to understand it.

The damp, when you find it, is always small and always exact — a basin of trapped dew, a film of frost burning off in the thin late-morning light, a vein of damp rock where some deep moisture works its way to the surface only to be taken by the cold or the wind within the hour. You learn to read these pockets the way the herds clearly do, the way the burrowing things do, converging on them in their sparse and patient geometry. There is just enough water here to keep things alive. There has been, it seems, for longer than rock remembers. There will be, the plateau suggests without insisting, for a good while yet.

You stay as long as you can stand the cold and the strangeness of being one warm, breathing, hurried thing in a landscape that has agreed, on some scale below words, to slow down and wait.
