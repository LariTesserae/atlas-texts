# The Cistern Terraces

The plateau sits high above whatever ground level once meant, and you reach it by climbing through a thousand small rooms that are not rooms but basins — stone-walled, shoulder-deep, packed edge to edge across the whole visible extent of the world. There is no path between them. There is no between. The builders, whoever or whatever they were, worked under the apparent conviction that empty space was a mistake to be corrected, and so they corrected it, cell after cell after cell, until the entire highland became a single contiguous fabric of hand-cut basins sharing walls, sharing weight, sharing water.

Because there is water everywhere. It rains somewhere out of sight, perpetually, or it doesn't need to — every basin holds some, and what one basin holds it gives a little to the next, through chutes no wider than a wrist, carved with a patience that suggests centuries of someone's single life spent on tool against stone. The sound this makes is not a roar. It is mostly quiet — the architecture absorbs and gentles everything — but if you stand still you hear it: a thin continuous percussion, basin into basin into basin, water finding its unhurried way downhill across a structure with almost no downhill left to find, because the terraces have been built so level, so deliberately, that the water seems reluctant to move at all. It pools. It waits. It transfers itself in small grudging increments, the way a held breath leaks out.

The stone is pale and the light is enormous — a high, bright, cool light, the kind with no real source you can locate, just an even saturating glare bounced a thousand times between wet rock and standing water until the whole terrain seems to glow from inside itself. Every basin throws back a disc of sky. Walk far enough and you stop seeing stone at all, just a regional checkerwork of mirrors, packed rim to rim, each one slightly different in tilt and tone, none of them reflecting anything but the same pale bright nothing above.

Nothing grows here. No moss softens the cut edges, no lichen blooms in the damp seams — the wet would seem to invite it and nothing has accepted the invitation. The stone is too young, perhaps; the chisel marks are still sharp underfoot, the basin lips still show fresh-cut facets that haven't weathered round. This place was made recently, on whatever timescale applies to a thing this large, and it has not yet had time to be colonized by anything smaller and slower than its builders — or else something about the water itself, mineral-heavy, faintly metallic on the tongue if you tried it, makes it inhospitable to growing things. No insects cross it. No birds rest on its thousand small ponds. The silence has the particular quality of a place built for a purpose that didn't include anyone else being invited.

The tools that made this were simple — you can tell from the working. No two basins are quite identical, no perfectly true right angle anywhere; the cuts are confident but not precise, made with hand chisels and stone hammers and an enormous, almost incomprehensible expenditure of human time rather than mechanical force. There is no metal anywhere in evidence, no fitted joints, no wheel-tooled surfaces. Just thousands of individually worked hollows, each one slightly different, each one full of water, climbing the high plateau in slow ascending tiers toward some summit you can't quite see from any single point because the glare off the water erases the horizon long before your eye gets there.

What is it for? That's the unease underneath the bright quiet calm of it. A reservoir this dense, this total, with no fields below it to feed and no animals to water and no visible settlement at its base — it has the logic of agriculture without any agriculture in sight, the form of infrastructure serving an absence. Maybe the builders are still coming. Maybe they already left, having finished only the storing and never the using. The basins don't seem to be filling further or draining further; the water at any given pool sits at the same quiet level today as it did, you suspect, the day the last chisel mark was cut. A slow world. A patient, finished, purposeless plenty, glowing under a sourceless cool light, listening to its own thin percussion, waiting for a thirst that may never arrive to claim it.
