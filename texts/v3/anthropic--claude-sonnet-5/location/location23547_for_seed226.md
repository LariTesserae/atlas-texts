# The Terrace of Standing Water

The mountain has not finished deciding what it wants to be here. You can feel that in the rock underfoot — basalt and pale sedimentary stone still sharp at the edges, not yet rounded by the centuries of weather that will eventually come to soften it. This is young ground, geologically speaking, though the people who built on it have been here long enough to forget the place was ever otherwise.

A single broad shelf of high terrain, perhaps the size of a modest village commons stretched lengthwise — bounded on three sides by rising stone and on the fourth by a drop into mist. The air up here is cool, thin enough to notice, thick enough to drink. Water seeps everywhere without ever pooling into anything large: runnels between the paving stones, a constant faint trickle-sound underneath the louder noises of the place, moss furring the undersides of every ledge and lintel. The rocks themselves seem to weep — not dramatically, just a persistent damp sheen, like the mountain is still cooling from something.

## What Stands Here

Stone and timber structures cluster close along the terrace, built with the patient stubbornness of hand tools and simple iron — adzes, wedges, rope-and-pulley work, joints pegged rather than nailed. No wheel-cut precision, no right angles trued by instrument: walls lean very slightly, roofs are thatched or shingled with split wood, and yet the whole settlement holds together with the kind of stubborn permanence that comes from people fixing things constantly rather than building them perfectly the first time.

But here is the strange part: there are far fewer buildings than the noise would suggest. The construction is genuinely urban in character — terracing, channeled water, a central structure with a bell or gong housed in a wooden tower, narrow stone streets cut to drain rain toward the cliff edge — yet the density of *things* is low. Long gaps of bare wet stone and low ground-cover separate one cluster of structures from the next. You could stand in the middle of this place and see almost nobody, while still hearing what sounds like a hundred.

## The Sound

This is the place's most unsettling quality, set against how empty it looks. The sound is loud — genuinely loud, in a way that doesn't match the few figures you can actually see moving between the buildings. It's not voices, mostly. It's percussive: looms or pump-mechanisms knocking somewhere unseen, the gong-tower sounding its slow interval, water moving fast through wooden flumes, things creaking and dripping and settling. Sound seems to gather and amplify against the stone walls, bouncing along the terrace until you can't locate its source. A person doing one small task with a mallet half a kilometer off can fill the whole shelf with noise.

## Green

Vegetation crowds in everywhere the buildings don't claim — not wild exactly, but unmanaged in a lush, indifferent way: broad-leafed plants you don't recognize, ferns unfurling at impossible density along every damp wall, moss thick enough to sit on, small flowering things colonizing every crack between stones. It has the feel of a garden nobody is quite tending anymore, or one that's outgrowing its boundaries faster than hands can cut it back. Vines have started up one side of the gong-tower. Someone has, at some point, made a half-hearted attempt to clear a path through a stand of broad ferns near the cliff edge, and the ferns are visibly winning.

## Who and What

Fauna is rare to the point of eeriness — you might see a single dark bird working the cliff updrafts, hear something small moving in undergrowth and never see it, find a print in damp moss that nothing explains. The settlement's quiet population (you sense more people than you see, hidden in workshops, behind dripping eaves) seems to coexist with this absence without comment, as though the thinness of animal life up here is simply understood, unremarkable, the mountain's own business.

The light is ordinary, daylight-grade, but it comes through a sky that never quite commits to clear or overcast — pale, diffuse, the kind of light that makes wet stone gleam without glare and makes the green look almost lurid against grey rock.

## The Unusual Thing

What unsettles, slowly, is the mismatch: the sound argues for a thriving, populous place; the visible emptiness argues otherwise; the architecture argues for a settlement old enough to have layered itself into the rock, while the rock itself argues that none of this should have been stable enough to build on yet — the ground is too new, too unworn, too recently arrived at whatever shape it's settling into. Cracks in the foundation stones haven't widened into anything yet; they're still hairline, still deciding their path. The terrace might be sinking a hair's-width a year, or rising — nobody you'd ask would know, and the gong sounds at its slow interval regardless, marking time for a place that geology itself hasn't finished writing.

It changes slowly here, if at all. Moss creeps a little further each season. The vines climb the tower's east face and nobody removes them. The sound continues, patient and enormous, filling a place that otherwise holds almost nothing at all.
