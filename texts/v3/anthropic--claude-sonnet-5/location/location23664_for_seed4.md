# The Hollow of Sleeve Moss

You find it by smell first — cold green, wet bark, and underneath that, something animal and warm-blooded, a thousand small bodies breathing in the dark.

It is not large. A single fold in low ground, no wider than a fishing village, sunk just below the surrounding marsh-flats so that the cold water seeps toward it and pools at the edges in black mirrors that never quite go still. The walls of this hollow are not stone. They are moss grown to the thickness of architecture — pillars of sphagnum and peat-fern fused into trunks, archways of liverwort thick as cathedral doors, a whole settlement woven into and out of and *as* the vegetation, so that you cannot say where the plant ends and the built thing begins. Houses are not built *in* the green. The green was coaxed, over some short span of years too brief for proper erosion to have touched the bark-walls, into the shape of houses. Everything here is young. The fungal mortar between woven branches still smells of rain. Roof-thatch has not yet silvered with age — it is still the deep wet brown of something cut last season.

There is no real light. What exists comes low and diffuse — a bioluminescent fuzz clinging to certain moss-beards, pale as held breath, and here and there a tallow lamp behind a window of scraped bladder-skin, the flame small because the makers here have only fire and blade and woven things, nothing more. Hand tools rest against doorframes. A digging stick. A bone needle the length of a forearm. No metal rings against metal; nothing here rings at all.

The settlement is sparse — you can walk a long while between one cluster of moss-homes and the next, through open wet ground where only ferns stand sentry, before another cluster rises from the dark. This spareness is deceptive. It suggests emptiness. It is not empty.

It is full of *animals.*

This is the strangeness of the place, the thing that bends the air: an ecology pressed to its absolute limit, fauna beyond what the visible food and space could possibly sustain, as though every creature within a hundred miles funneled itself into this one low wet pocket and decided to stay. Pale newts thread the standing water in coils, dozens per pool. Insects move as a single shifting skin over every leaf-surface, a low continuous whisper-static that is the closest thing this place has to ambient sound. Furred shapes — not quite voles, not quite something else — nest in the moss-walls themselves, riddling the architecture with tunnels so that the houses breathe with movement, walls subtly pulsing if you watch long enough. Birds you never see, only hear: short clicks and one long descending note, repeated every few minutes from someplace up in the wet dark canopy that the moss-architecture has grown into, as if the buildings have a ceiling made of living tree the people never bothered to clear.

And yet it is *mostly quiet.* The fauna is overwhelming in number, not in noise. Everything here has learned restraint, low murmurs, soft-footed transit between water and moss and den. Sound moves in pulses — a flurry of wingbeats, a held silence after, the insect-static returning to fill the gap, then nothing again. The quiet doesn't feel hollow. It feels like a room full of breathing people pretending to be one body asleep.

The people of this hollow — small in number, primitive in craft, urban in their stubborn clustering — live as if folded into the animal mass rather than against it. Their walkways are raised on woven root to keep above the standing water and the nightly traffic of foraging things below. Their granaries (if you can call woven moss-pods *granaries*) are guarded less by walls than by an understanding: certain creatures are fed deliberately at the threshold, a tithe, so the rest of the structure goes untouched. Whether this is animal husbandry or animal worship is not clear to an outsider, and possibly not entirely clear to them either. The rules of this place are young rules, freshly made, still being tested — you can see the failed experiments at the hollow's edge, the half-collapsed moss-houses abandoned when some arrangement with the fauna didn't hold.

Cold sits over everything like a held breath. Not killing cold — the kind that keeps the water from freezing but makes every surface faintly weep, condensation standing on moss-bark, on bladder-skin windows, on the hairs of your own arm. Mist doesn't rise so much as simply already be there, low and constant, thickening toward the black mirror-pools.

You could stand at the hollow's lip and see almost nothing — a dim huddle of green shapes, a few small lights, motion at the threshold of sight in every direction. You could stand an hour and start to understand that the ground itself is alive in layers: water beneath, root and peat above that, moss-architecture above that, animals running through and over and inside all three strata at once, and somewhere in the seams, holding it together with hand-tied lashings and quiet ritual, the few human shapes who decided this was a place to stay.

Nothing here is old. Nothing here is finished. The moss is still growing into the doorframes that were cut for it.
