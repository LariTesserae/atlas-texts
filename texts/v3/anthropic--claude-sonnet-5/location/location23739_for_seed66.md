# The Shelf of Tarrindown

The plateau announces itself a hundred kilometers out as a smudge of brown haze sitting above the haze of everything else — a tableland that the local atmosphere has learned to bend around, generating its own weather where the warm wet air off the lowlands hits the cold lip of the rise and gives up its water in long, grey, low-hanging curtains. You climb to it through switchback roads cut into rock the color of old teeth, and the air changes before the view does: thinner, cooler at first, then warming again unexpectedly as you crest onto the shelf itself, where something — geothermal vents, deep mineral chemistry, the bodies of ten million creatures, possibly all three — keeps the plateau's basin running ten degrees hotter than its rim.

There is almost no plant life here. What vegetation exists is vestigial — pale lichen-analogues crusting the lee sides of stones, fed by spore-fall and not much else, and a flat black fungal mat that follows the drainage lines like a stain, almost certainly older than the city. The world here decided early to do its business in mineral and animal terms, skipping the green entirely.

## Stone and Structure

The plateau's bedrock is a stratified grey-violet sediment, soft enough to quarry but tough enough to hold a load, and the city of Tarrindown — though "city" undersells the thing, which sprawls across nearly the whole high shelf in overlapping districts built up across centuries — has been carved into it as much as built on top of it. Walk through the old quarter and you are walking through rooms that are also geology: ceilings show the same banding as the cliff faces outside, walls weep faintly where groundwater finds old seams, and runnels have been deliberately routed along carved channels in the floor so that the city's endemic dampness becomes a feature rather than a flood. Every doorway has a sill raised four inches against the wet season. Every basement has a sump.

Newer construction — the foundries, the stockyards, the loud iron-ribbed processing halls down toward the lower terraces — is built up rather than carved in: riveted plate, brick kilned on-site from the plateau's own clay, smokestacks that vent into the perpetual low cloud cover until you can't tell which haze is industrial and which is weather. The built environment is dense almost everywhere, structure leaning against structure, walkways slung between rooftops three and four stories up where the ground level has become too crowded, too loud, or too occupied by something with too many legs to be worth contesting.

## The Fauna Problem

This is the plateau's defining fact: there is so much animal life here that the ecology reads less like a place with wildlife in it than like wildlife that has, as an afterthought, organized some stone around itself. The basin's warmth and the high water table support a food chain whose biomass density would be implausible anywhere with normal predation pressure, and predation pressure here is apparently not normal — or rather, the city itself has become the apex predator, several centuries running.

At ground level: drays, a domesticated grazing-beast the size of a draft horse with leathery flanks, kept in such numbers that whole quarters are dedicated to their stabling, their waste, their slaughter, their hide-processing — the smell of them, ammoniac and warm, underlies the whole city like a second atmosphere. Above them, in the rafters and roof-spaces of every district: roosting colonies of gliding scavengers, thousands strong, who pour out at dusk in spiraling columns to strip the processing-yards' offal and pour back in at dawn, their wings making a sound like rain that isn't rain. In the wet channels and sumps: eyeless burrowing things, pale, numerous, mostly harmless, occasionally not. In the deep cisterns: something larger that nobody quarries near anymore, by long-standing unspoken agreement.

The city's infrastructure has been built, in large part, in negotiation with this. Streets have animal-priority hours. The foundries time their loudest operations to coincide with roost-hours, when the gliders are gone and won't be startled into the machinery. Children are taught the eleven warning calls before they're taught to read. It is not a hostile ecology, mostly — it is simply a saturated one, and "overwhelming" describes density, not malice. You are never anywhere on the plateau where you cannot hear, smell, or feel the press of other living bodies, and you learn quickly that this is just the ambient condition of being here, like noise, like the wet rock.

## Sound and Industry

Tarrindown is loud in the specific way of a place that has stopped trying not to be. Foundry-hammers keep a percussive bassline through the daylight hours; chain-drives and belt-driven mills add a higher, grinding overlay; the drays bawl in their stockyards; the gliders' wings hiss at dusk and dawn; vendors and dock-loaders and the cable-car operators on the terrace lifts all shout over the top of it, because shouting is the only way to be heard. Underneath everything, almost subsonic, the plateau's water moves through its channels and culverts, a continuous low percolation that the older residents claim to be able to feel through the floor before they can hear it.

The engines here are industrial in the unglamorous sense — steam and combustion plant, belt and gear, electrified only in the newer central districts where overhead lines now string between the rooftop walkways like a second, deliberate weather. Production is at scale: hide, bone, fat, fiber, mineral salt leached from the wet rock, ore hauled up from shaft-mines that go down through the plateau's edge into the warmer dark. Smoke stands over the lower terraces in a permanent brown ceiling that the rain — frequent, soft, more mist than storm most days — never quite manages to wash out.

## The Pace of It

For all its noise and crowding, Tarrindown changes slowly. This is a settled place, established across what feels like geological time relative to a human life: the carved quarters were carved by ancestors of ancestors, the drainage channels have been deepening their grooves at a fingernail's pace for centuries, the roost colonies have occupied the same rafters since before anyone living can document. Erosion here means refinement, not destruction — water and animal traffic wearing the city's hard edges into something closer to a worn tool than a ruin. Industry expands at the edges, terrace by terrace, decade by decade. Nothing here happens quickly except the weather, and even the weather is a fixed habit: warm wet air rising, cooling, falling as mist and drizzle against the rim, running off down the channels that have known exactly where to take it for longer than the city has existed to use them.

It is a place with the feel of an old, loud, overfull room that everyone inside has simply learned to live in — not uncanny, not really strange, just thick: thick with bodies, thick with damp warm air, thick with the accumulated, unhurried weight of a very long time spent figuring out how to share a high stone shelf with everything that already lived there first.
