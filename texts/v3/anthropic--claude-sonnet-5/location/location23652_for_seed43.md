# The Sunken Warren of Kelth-Adar

Below the dry crust of the upper world, where the heat of day never reaches and the cold of night never bites, lies a hollowed settlement that has been carved, packed, and shored into permanence over generations. This is Kelth-Adar — though no single mouth says the name the same way twice, and no single chart agrees on its true extent.

## Terrain and Material

There is no soil here. There is no rock left unshaped. Every wall is rammed earth or fitted stone-block, every floor tamped clay polished by feet, every ceiling a low vault of mud-brick or timber-and-thatch buried under more earth above. The builders — whoever they were, generations back, and whoever they remain, still adding rooms — worked with what was at hand: clay dug from the warren's own excavation, timber dragged down from above before the entrance shafts narrowed too much to pass logs through, fired brick from small kilns whose smoke-holes pock the surface world like old wounds.

The place reads as established — not new-cut, not raw. Edges have been rounded by ten thousand shoulders brushing past. Steps are worn into shallow bowls in their centers. Threshold-stones are grooved by feet that have crossed them for longer than memory holds names. This is erosion not of wind and water but of *use* — the patient grinding of a place by the bodies that live in it.

Scale is intimate. No chamber exceeds the size of a modest room above-ground; the largest hall might hold forty bodies pressed close, and that is the cathedral here. Most spaces are smaller — niches, cells, corridors barely shoulder-width, junction-rooms where five tunnels meet around a single firepit. The warren spreads not by being large but by being *many* — chamber after chamber after chamber, threaded by crawl-passages, stairs cut into raw clay, ramps worn slick.

## Density and Crowding

This is the warren's defining fact: nothing is empty. Walls bear shelf-niches stacked three and four high, each holding tools, bundled cloth, dried foodstuffs, the small idols of the unnamed faiths. Ceilings are strung with hanging baskets, drying racks, looped cordage, nesting-shelves for the warren's birds. Floor space is claimed by sleeping mats, low tables, coiled rope, stacked brick waiting for the next expansion. People move through their own homes by practiced negotiation, sidestepping what is always there.

The fauna press into this density too. Small warren-fowl roost in ceiling baskets, their clucking a constant undertone. Insects — beetles, crickets, a chittering moth-analogue with pale wings — live in every crack of mud-brick, kept semi-in-check by lizard-things that dart along the warm walls. Rodents thread the wall-gaps despite every effort to seal them out, and are in turn hunted by warren-cats, slim and silent, tolerated as pest-control and treated half as livestock, half as kin. Every level of this place has its tier of creature, stacked the way the storage-niches are stacked.

## Water

Water is not absent, only rare and precious in its rarity. Condensation beads on the cooler stone of the deepest chambers and is licked off by lizard-things and caught in shallow grooves cut beneath certain walls — a slow architecture of catchment. Somewhere below the warren's lowest known level, a seep feeds a cistern-chamber, walled in fired brick, where the water is black-still and echo-loud when a drop falls into it. Rain, when it falls above, finds its way down through certain shafts after the worst storms, and these are marked, feared a little, valued for the brief flush of water they bring.

This dampness — never flood, never drought, always a little present — has shaped what little vegetation survives: pale fibrous fungus cultivated in clay trays along certain walls, valued as food and dye both; a hardy creeping moss that colonizes the dampest corners and is encouraged rather than scraped away; root-vegetables grown in earth-filled troughs fed by the condensation channels, their leaves thin and starved for a light they only get from oil-lamps and the occasional shaft of borrowed sun.

## Light

No sun reaches most of the warren directly. Light comes from oil-lamps — squat clay vessels with floating wicks — set into wall-niches at intervals, their flames small but numerous enough that no corridor is ever fully dark. In the hub-chambers, larger fires burn in pits beneath smoke-holes that channel up through the earth to the surface, and these rooms glow amber-orange, throwing huge soft shadows that move with the crowd. A few shafts — narrow, angled, lined with pale clay to catch and bounce what light enters — bring weak daylight down to certain upper chambers at certain hours, and these spots are prized, fought over, gardened.

## Sound

The warren is loud, and the loudness is structural — sound doesn't dissipate here, it bounces, gathers, layers. Voices in adjoining chambers blend into a permanent murmur-roar. The clack of looms, the scrape of grindstones, a child's shriek of laughter, the percussion of someone's hammer fitting brick to brick, the warren-fowl's clucking, the lizard-things' dry rasping calls, the constant drip-echo from the cistern-chamber carried strangely up through stone — all of it merges into an ambient roar punctuated by sharper, closer sounds. Silence, when it happens, is noticed immediately and treated as faintly wrong, a held breath.

## Ecology and Rhythm

Life here runs on a rhythm independent of any sun. Sleep-cycles are kept by habit and by the rationed dimming of certain lamps at certain hours, called "night" by convention. Children are raised collectively in the warmer central chambers near the great firepits. The dead are interred in the walls themselves, sealed into niches that become, over years, simply part of the architecture — so that to live in Kelth-Adar is to live inside the bodies of those who built it, layered like everything else.

The ecosystem is a managed one: warren-cats kept for rodent control, warren-fowl for eggs and feathers, fungus-trays for food and dye, moss for moisture-indication (its color shifts subtly with humidity, and the old keep watch on it as others might watch sky). Predation is small-scale and constant — cat on rodent, lizard on insect, occasional larger thing (unseen, only inferred from absence and from claw-marks too big for any catalogued resident) said to roam the deepest, unlit, unclaimed tunnels that the warren has not yet annexed and may never.

## Rules of the Place

Tech remains primitive by necessity and by a kind of stubbornness — metal is rare, traded up from below or down from above at great cost, reserved for tools that must hold an edge. Most construction, most craft, is hand and fire and clay and patience. This is not poverty so much as a chosen ceiling: the warren has had the chance to change and has mostly declined, finding its rhythms adequate.

There is an unspoken sense that the warren is not finished — that somewhere, always, someone is digging a new chamber, shoring a new wall, and that the total extent of Kelth-Adar is known by no single inhabitant. The deep tunnels, unlit and unclaimed, are where the unusual lives: stories of chambers that hum on certain still nights, of brick walls warmer than the fires that should explain it, of a draft that smells of rain in a place no rain has ever reached. Nothing confirmed. Nothing disproven. The warren tolerates a little mystery the way it tolerates the moss — watched, lived-with, never fully explained.
