# The Sunken Works

Below the green ceiling—some forty feet of warm water, lit from above in the color of bottle glass—the old works go on standing.

This was a town once, or a town's industrial quarter: brick chimneys, iron-frame sheds, the long low halls where things were made. The flood that took it didn't tear it down. It came slow, over years they say, a rising table of water that crept up the foundations a finger's width at a time until one day the lamps in the lower windows simply didn't light anymore, and nobody thought to mourn it because everyone had already moved their lives upward, room by room, year by year. So the town went under whole. Established. Settled into its new state the way a tooth settles into a socket. Nothing here feels like ruin — it feels like residence.

**Terrain and material.** The streets are still streets, cobbled, narrow, running between buildings that lean together at the rooflines the way old buildings do when they've stood close too long. Silt has filled the low places — the gutters, the cellar stairs, the gaps under loading docks — but the architecture itself holds. Brick gone the color of a bruise, soft green-black where algae has taken the mortar lines and turned them into something like veins. Iron has rusted into lace; some beams have rusted into nothing at all, leaving holes in the structure like missing teeth, perfectly outlined by the rust-stain ghost of where they were. The cobblestones are slick with a felt of weed, walked on by nothing, currents moving through them in slow tidal breaths that never quite become a current.

**Density.** This is the strangeness of the place: it is packed. Not with people — there are none — but with growth and machine in equal, crowding measure. Kelp-analogues, broad-leafed and the deep glossy green of rainforest canopy, have rooted in every gutter, every roof-seam, every open window, and grown upward toward the green light in columns so thick that whole streets are corridors of leaf, brushing your shoulders on both sides. Between the leaves: gear-housings, flywheels gone to coral-crust, smokestacks still upright and now home to columns of slow bubbles that rise from some process underneath that has never stopped. The factories kept running. That is the thing nobody who hasn't seen it will believe. Sealed boilers down in the foundations, fed by some original fire or by a colder process that replaced it, still turn wheels nobody built to run underwater. You can feel it in your sternum before you hear it: a bass thrum, patient, slow, the heartbeat-rate of a machine built for centuries, not seasons.

**Light and atmosphere.** Dim, bottle-green, filtered twice — once by forty feet of water, once by the canopy of broad leaves that has grown up to meet that surface light and steal most of it. What gets through arrives in shafts, moving slowly, swinging like the slowest pendulum as the surface above ripples. In those shafts, particulate hangs and drifts — leaf-matter, rust-flake, the fine sediment of a hundred years of settling — so each beam of light looks solid enough to lean a ladder against. Outside the shafts: a green-brown dark, soft-edged, in which the shapes of chimneys and gable-ends emerge only as you're nearly upon them.

**Sound.** Mostly quiet, the quiet specific to deep water — a pressure-quiet, a held-breath quiet, your own pulse the loudest thing in it. Into that quiet comes, irregularly, the works: a clank transmitted through stone and water both, felt more than heard, when some gear far below completes its long slow revolution. A creak of a chimney swaying half a degree in a thermal current. The wet sound of a leaf-column shifting against brick. No birds, because there's no air for them. No bells, though some of the towers still hold them, and once in a very long while — a year, a decade — a chime current finds the right angle and a bell tolls once, underwater, a sound like a struck stone wrapped in cloth, and it carries for blocks and dies.

**Ecology.** Fauna is rare but present, and strange for being so quiet about its presence. Pale eel-things coil in the gear housings, drawn by the warmth the machines still give off. Crab-analogues, slow and big-clawed, graze the algae off the cobbles at a pace that makes the erosion itself look hasty. Once in a while something larger passes far off in the leaf-corridors — never seen whole, just a darkening of one shaft of light, a leaf-column swaying after it's gone. Nothing thrives in abundance. The ecosystem here has the patience of the architecture: a few residents, well-suited, settled in for the long duration, not multiplying so much as enduring.

**Tech.** This is the heart of the wrongness, the 2.4 humming under everything: this is not a drowned ruin in the usual sense, where machines rust into stillness and nature reclaims at leisure. The machines are still going. Somewhere below — sub-cellar, sub-foundation, deeper than any diver here has gone and come back able to describe — the original works persist, sealed, lightless, running some process whose product nobody alive remembers the use for. Flywheels the size of houses turn at the rate of a held breath, once a minute, smoothly, in oiled silence broken only by that occasional transmitted clank. Pipes still carry something warm — not hot, just warm, blood-warm, which is why the water itself sits seven degrees above what the latitude should give it, why the vegetation up top is lush instead of sparse, why this whole sunken district is a warm green lung in a colder sea. The factories are feeding their own afterlife. They make the climate that lets the leaf-columns grow that feed the crabs that the eels eat. The machine has become the ecosystem's furnace, unintentionally, accidentally, and gone on doing it long after whatever it was built to do stopped mattering to anyone.

**Rules of the place.** A few things are understood, by the rare diver or surveyor or salvage-crew that comes down here with lamps strapped to their chests:

You do not go below the third sub-level of any standing structure, because the floors there are not floors anymore, they are the tops of machine-housings, and they are warm, and they sometimes move.

The bells, when they ring, are not a warning and not a welcome — they are just the building doing what it was built to do, on a schedule too slow for any one visit to notice twice. Hearing one is considered lucky in the way that hearing thunder directly overhead is lucky: rare, awesome, faintly dangerous-feeling though it costs nothing.

Leaf-columns sway away from the machine-warmth and toward the surface light in a spiral, so the local way of orienting yourself, when the brick has all gone unreadable with growth, is to read the twist of the nearest leaf-column like a grain in wood — it always knows two directions at once, up and away-from-heat, and between those two you can place yourself.

And the town, whatever it was called, kept no other name down here. The works don't need one. They've been running long enough that the name was the first thing to erode — long before the brick, long before the iron, the only thing about this established, settled, patient place that didn't survive the flood.
