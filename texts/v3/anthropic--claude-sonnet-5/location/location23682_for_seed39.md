# The Raw Range

The mountains here are still being born. You can feel it in the way the rock comes apart under your boot — not weathered to dust, not smoothed to anything, but fresh-broken, sharp-edged, the color of a wound. Strata jut up at improbable angles, slabs the size of houses tilted against each other like a structure mid-collapse or mid-construction; it's hard to tell which. The peaks above — and they are genuinely high, high enough that the air thins and your breath turns visible and ragged — haven't been worn into the gentle, ancient curves of old ranges. They're jagged, vertical, freshly thrust, still trailing the violence of their making. Scree fields lie at unstable angles of repose, not yet settled into permanence. Stand still long enough and you'll hear a faint, irregular ticking from somewhere upslope — small stones finding their final resting places, one at a time, the mountain still deciding its own shape.

Water moves through this place but doesn't pool with confidence. It hasn't earned its channels yet. You find it seeping from rock faces in dark vertical stains, the stone weeping in thin black ribbons that freeze at the edges and run liquid at the center. Where it gathers, it forms shallow braided threads across flat shelves of stone — not rivers, not yet, just water looking for a course that doesn't fully exist. Mist sits low in the long, open valley below, condensing on every cold surface, beading on lichen, on metal, on your own sleeves. The cold keeps it all suspended in a kind of perpetual half-state: not quite freezing, not quite flowing, water caught between forms the way the land is caught between geological ages.

Vegetation is sparse and opportunistic — wind-flattened cushion plants wedged into cracks, threads of pale lichen spreading across the broken rock in patches like old bruises, the occasional hardy shrub bent permanently sideways by a wind that apparently never stops. Nothing tall. Nothing that argues with gravity or weather. The plants here have made peace with scarcity.

And yet — life. Improbable amounts of it, given how little grows. You hear it before you see it: wingbeats overhead, a heavy, percussive sound, large birds working the updrafts along the cliff faces, riding the thermals that the broken terrain throws off in unpredictable columns. Herds move along the valley floor far below — you catch them as dark, shifting smudges, hundreds of them, grazing the sparse turf and lichen with the patient thoroughness of animals built for scarcity. Smaller things dart between rocks closer at hand — quick, nervous, gone before you're sure you saw them. The cold doesn't seem to have thinned the ecology so much as concentrated it, packed it into fewer and fewer footholds where every available patch of green or every ledge wide enough for a nest is intensely, competitively used.

Then there is the sound that doesn't belong to any animal: a deep mechanical pulse rolling up from somewhere beyond the ridgeline, regular as a heartbeat, audible only when the wind drops. Industrial — unmistakably so, the rhythm of pistons or turbines, the distant suggestion of something vast and engineered grinding away. But there's nothing built here, nothing visible — no structure, no road, no tower breaks the skyline anywhere within view. The sound seems to come from inside the mountain itself, or from beyond the next range, carried for impossible distances through the thin cold air and the rock. It never resolves into a source. It simply continues, a low industrial drone laid underneath the wind and the ticking scree and the cries of the high birds, as though the planet had swallowed a factory whole and was still digesting it.

Light here is filtered and uncertain — the sun, when it exists at all, comes through high grey cloud as a diffuse silver smear, throwing almost no shadow, flattening the jagged peaks into a strange two-dimensional menace. Visibility stretches for kilometers in the open valley below, ridge after ridge dissolving into mist-grey distance, and yet up close everything feels close-walled, vertiginous, the next outcrop always looming nearer than expected.

Nothing here is finished. The mountains are still rising, the water hasn't decided where to run, the scree is still settling, and underneath it all, something enormous keeps pulsing — patient, mechanical, and utterly unaccounted for.
