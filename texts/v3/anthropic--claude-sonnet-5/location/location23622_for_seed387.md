# The Cistern Works

There is a hollow beneath the world, and inside the hollow, someone built a city, and the city is drowning, and the city does not mind.

## What It Is

Call it a chamber, though chamber suggests something too small. It is a held breath of stone—a cavity below the surface of wherever this is, vast enough to swallow a basin's worth of black water and still leave room above for iron and smoke. The ceiling is rock, sweating, unseen, lost somewhere above the steam-haze. The walls curve away into dark on every side, so that the place reads less like a room and more like the inside of something that was once alive and has since been gutted and refitted with pipework.

Warm water fills most of it. Not clean water—mineral-thick, the color of weak tea, moving with a slow turbulence that never quite resolves into current or stillness. It rises and falls against the architecture in surges that have no visible cause: maybe tidal, maybe mechanical, maybe the breathing of something the builders never bothered to name. Catwalks and platforms and squat brick structures rise out of it like a settlement built by people who expected the flood and built anyway, on stilts, on pilings, on the bones of older, taller things now three-quarters submerged.

This is not ruin. This is **maintained**. The erosion here is the erosion of long use, not abandonment—rust patterns that have been scraped and repainted a hundred times, joints that leak and get repacked, stone stairs worn into smooth troughs by feet that still climb them. The place has settled into what it is. It has been this way long enough that "this way" is simply how the world works, down here.

## Materials

Black iron, mostly. Riveted plate, the rivets themselves crusted white with mineral bloom where the warm water laps against them. Brick the color of old liver, glazed in places with a slick film. Brass fittings, green-black, polished bright only at the handles and valve-wheels where hands grip them daily. Chain. An enormous quantity of chain, hanging slack from pulleys into the water, disappearing into it, hauling up things nobody outside this place will ever see.

There is no wood here that isn't sealed in pitch, because wood does not survive what this place does to it.

## The Industry

Tech sits high here—not advanced, but relentless. Engines churn somewhere below the waterline, their housings visible only as humped iron backs breaching the surface like the spines of sleeping animals, each one venting a thread of steam that joins the general haze. Conveyor lines run along gantries bolted into the rock, hauling buckets of something up out of the water and dumping it with a wet clatter into hoppers that feed into furnace-mouths glowing dull orange in the gloom. Pump stations the size of houses thud in syncopated rhythm, a polyrhythm with no fixed tempo, because each pump answers to its own pressure and none of them are synchronized and nobody has ever bothered to synchronize them.

This is the source of the sound. The sound is the whole point, almost—it arrives first, before sight, before smell, a wall of mechanical noise that does not let up: the boom of pistons, the shriek of unlubricated wheels, the percussive slam of water against hull-plate, the roar of vented steam, the groan of chain through pulley, layered so densely that individual sounds stop being distinguishable and become instead a single continuous pressure against the ear, a thing you feel in your sternum more than hear. Conversation, if it happens, happens by gesture, or by shouting directly into an ear from a distance of inches, or not at all.

## The Settlement

People built here—built up, built around, built into. A town's worth of structure crowds the available dry footing: narrow housing blocks stacked three and four stories where the rock permits, walkways slung on cable between them, ladders bolted vertically up support columns, doorways that open directly onto black water with no railing because everyone who lives here already knows not to fall in. Lanterns hang at every junction, oil-fed or gas-fed, throwing a dim orange light that does not so much illuminate the place as suggest its outline—you see the nearest twenty feet clearly and everything past that as silhouette and steam-shadow.

It is built dense in plan but the volume around it is enormous and empty—the population a scatter of figures across a townscape that fills the eye, suspended within a cavern whose true size dwarfs everything man-made inside it. Look up from any walkway and the buildings stop a few stories above you; look further up and there's only dark, steam, the suggestion of ceiling, the sense of standing at the bottom of something far bigger than the works that occupy it.

## Ecology — What Lives

Almost nothing, and what does live here is strange for it. No plants; nothing photosynthesizes in a hole this deep and dim. What passes for life are the things that have adapted to warm mineral water and the constant violence of machinery: pale eel-like things that move through the flooded lower levels and are sometimes hauled up tangled in the chain-buckets, dead or half-dead, and tossed back; a kind of skin of grey biofilm on standing water that the workers scrape off the intake grates by hand, cursing; rumors—always rumors, never confirmed—of something larger living in the deepest flooded shafts below the lowest platform, something the engines were maybe built to keep asleep, or to feed, or both. Fauna is rare enough that a sighting is an event, talked about for days, embellished in the retelling until nobody believes the original report anymore.

## The Turbulence

Nothing here is stable for long. The water surges without warning, sometimes high enough to swamp the lower walkways, and workers move with the practiced unconcern of people who've timed their lives to a rhythm that isn't quite rhythmic. Pressure blows out a seal somewhere on the pipe network every so often—a gout of scalding steam, a scramble, a repair crew descending on ropes, the rest of the place barely pausing because this happens often enough to not be remarkable. Furnaces flare unevenly. Platforms get rebuilt, extended, abandoned, repurposed, the skyline of the place subtly different month to month, the engine deep below seemingly dictating an ongoing renovation through nothing but vibration and breakdown. It is a bazaar's energy translated into industrial terms: noise, crowding, transaction, repair, urgency, all conducted at the bottom of a stone throat filled with hot water, by light too dim to see the far wall by.

## The Unease

There's a wrongness held just below the threshold of the uncanny. The engines run, but no one you ask can quite explain what they're refining, what's coming up the chain-buckets, what the furnaces are fed. Ask and you get an answer—"water-coal," "deep-salt," "the take"—but the answers don't quite cohere into a single explanable industry, as if everyone down here knows their one job and nobody holds the whole picture, or as if the whole picture isn't meant to be held. The warm water that fills the chamber doesn't behave quite like water should: it surges on no tide anyone can name, and very occasionally, between the clatter of the machines, something in its movement against the hull-iron sounds almost rhythmic—almost like breathing, slowed down, vast, coming from very far below the lowest platform anyone has ever built.

No one mentions this. The pumps keep going. The lanterns keep their small dim orange ground against the dark, and the chain keeps hauling its black water up into the light, link over link, forever, because the works have always run, and the works are not going to stop now.
