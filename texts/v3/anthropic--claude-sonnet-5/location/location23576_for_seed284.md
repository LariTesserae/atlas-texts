# The Needle-Stack of Khoreth Vant

It occupies less space than you'd think possible for what it contains — a single basaltic spire, maybe four hundred meters of vertical throw packed into a footprint you could walk around in twenty minutes. The rock is dark and young, still showing the conchoidal fractures of stone that hasn't had the centuries to soften. No water has ever touched it. No water ever will — the air here is bone-cool and bone-dry, the kind of dry that makes metal want to seize and skin want to crack, and what little moisture exists in the system is recycled industrially, jealously, through pipe and condenser.

## The Stack

The spire is hollow-ish, in the sense that something has been done to it — not carved so much as *occupied*, level after level, by a settlement that grew the way crystal grows: opportunistically, along whatever fracture or ledge presented itself, until the whole needle is wrapped and threaded and tunneled with structure. Galleries cantilever off natural shelves. Catwalks bridge gaps that were never meant to be bridged. Lower levels are squat and load-bearing, true to the rock; upper levels get spindlier, riskier, balanced on outcroppings that look like they're holding their breath.

There is no vegetation because there has never been a reason for any to take hold — no soil, no still water, nothing but mineral and the things people have bolted to it. What color exists comes from oxidation stains, scorch-marks, and the queasy sodium-orange of the work-lights strung along every accessible edge.

It is dim, always — the spire stands in some kind of permanent shadow-country, whether from atmospheric haze, eclipse-light, or geography unknown to its residents, so the industrial lighting isn't decoration, it's survival. Strings of it run up the spire like a fuse that never finishes burning.

## The Engines

Tech runs hot here, comparatively — not advanced so much as *committed*. Pistoned things. Belt-driven things. Furnaces fed by whatever combustible the place mines out of its own bones, venting up through chimney-shafts that double as structural members. The whole spire thrums at a frequency you feel in your molars before you hear it: a low industrial pulse, engines somewhere mid-stack doing something continuous and necessary, gear-trains turning lift-cages on chains thick as a forearm.

But "mostly quiet" governs the felt experience despite all this machinery — the engines are deep-throated and constant rather than loud, a bass hum absorbed into stone rather than ringing off it. Sound doesn't carry far in the dry air. Voices clip short. Most of what you hear close-up is small: chain-link settling, a hand-cart's wheel, the click of someone's breathing-mask valve.

## What Moves

Despite the hostile rock and the absence of anything resembling a plant, the place teems. Fauna here doesn't graze — it scavenges, parasitizes, or was *brought*, generations back, bred to subsist on industrial byproduct: oil-fed grubs in the lower furnace levels, leathery cliff-roosting things that glide gap to gap on heat-thermals kicked up by the chimneys, swarms of small chittering scrap-pickers that follow the work-crews like gulls follow ships, eating dropped solder and packing-grease. Higher up, something larger nests in the unused upper galleries — residents speak of it the way port towns speak of weather, an acknowledged hazard rather than a myth.

The ecology, such as it is, runs entirely on what the settlement produces and discards. Cut the engines and the spire would starve in a season.

## Instability as Default Condition

Nothing here is settled, and that's not a flaw in the place, it's the operating mode. Construction is constant — new galleries bolted on as old ones are condemned, lift-chains rerouted weekly, whole work-levels reshuffled depending on which seam is currently productive and which has played out or cracked. The young, sharp-edged stone is unstable in ways the engineers respect and resent in equal measure: rockfall is routine, shafts get abandoned mid-dig when a wall starts weeping dust, and at least once a season — locals say, with the weary shrug of people describing weather — a whole tier of scaffold-construction comes down in the night, engines and all, and has to be rebuilt by morning shift.

There's a particular sound for that, distinct from the ambient machine-hum: a long groan traveling down through the rock, felt before heard, that empties every catwalk in about four seconds flat. People who've lived here long enough claim they can tell from the pitch which face is going.

## The Unusual Edge

Nothing about Khoreth Vant breaks physics, but it sits slightly askew of comfortable. The orange work-light, filtered through the perpetual haze, throws shadows that seem to lag a half-beat behind the things casting them. The scrap-picker swarms move with a coordination that looks almost deliberate, wheeling as one organism rather than a crowd of small things. Long-timers swear the engines, deep in the furnace-levels, run on a rhythm that occasionally — rarely — falls into sync with each other across levels that shouldn't be able to hear one another, and when that happens the whole spire seems to hold still for a moment, the turbulence pausing as if listening to itself.

Nobody's found a cause. Nobody's stopped the engines long enough to check.
