A high shelf of rock, no wider than a chapel floor, balanced near the top of the world.

You reach it by a goat-track that gives out a hundred meters below, after which there is only the rock itself to climb — wet, dark, ribboned with old mineral stains the color of rust and tea. The shelf sits below a final spire that goes up further still, lost sometimes in moving cloud, and the air at this height has a thinness to it that makes the lungs work for what they're used to getting free. Cool air, never cold enough to bite, always carrying a faint suspended wet — not rain exactly, more a condensation that never fully resolves. Mist beads on every surface. The stone weeps from cracks where seep-water has been finding its way down for longer than anyone has watched. Where the weeping has run long enough, moss has taken hold, and where moss has taken hold, something thicker followed.

That's the strange grace of the place: this high, this exposed, and yet it's green. Not lush like a lowland valley — lush like a single overfull room. A tangle of dwarfed trees, wind-bent and old beyond their height, grow out of cracks where soil has accumulated over centuries, gripping the rock with roots that look more like clamped hands than anything botanical. Beneath them, waist-high ferns, a low blanket of broad-leafed plants holding the night's water in cupped centers, lichen in every shade of grey-green-orange furring every rock that holds still long enough. It should not be this green at this height. It is anyway, because the wet never fully leaves, and the established angle of the rock has caught and held enough soil over enough centuries to let roots set deep into the cracks and not let go.

The scale of the place is intimate despite the vastness around it. The shelf itself you could cross in two hundred paces. It has edges — real ones, where the green stops and the rock drops away into white nothing or far-below cloud-tops or, on clear hours, an impossible drop to valleys so far down they look like a separate, smaller world. Standing at the center of the shelf, you are enclosed by its own small ecology, and at the rim, you are suddenly looking down at the entire planet's worth of air.

The sound here is enormous, disproportionate to the emptiness of the place. Wind comes up the cliff face below in long throats of stone and arrives at the shelf as a roar that the dwarf-trees break into a thousand smaller hisses and rattles. Birds — and there are many, the place is thick with them, more here than anywhere a person might expect at this elevation — keep up a constant competing noise: harsh territorial calls from raptors nesting in the spire above, the chitter and burst-flight of smaller birds nesting in the scrub, a layer of insect sound underneath that never quite stops, even in the cool. Some unseen large animal — hoofed, by the sound of stone displaced — moves through the brush at the shelf's far end on its own business, and you hear it more than you ever see it. Echoes do strange things up here; a falling rock two valleys over can sound like it's dropping at your feet.

And yet for all that noise, there is almost no density of presence. The shelf is empty in the way a cathedral is empty — full of sound and air and meaning, but you could walk it for an hour and meet nothing that wanted to be met. The animals keep their own circuits. Whatever built the few structures here is long gone.

Because something did build, once. At the leeward end of the shelf, tucked where the spire's bulk cuts the worst of the wind, there's a low ring of fitted stones, no higher than a knee, the kind of structure thrown up by hands that had only other stones and maybe a metal blade to work with. Nearby, a flatter stone bears a shallow depression worn too symmetrical to be entirely natural — a basin, maybe, for catching the seep-water, or for something else now unguessable. A second structure, half-collapsed, leans against the spire's base like it was built to use the rock as one whole wall, the way someone short on material and long on need will do. No roof remains, if there ever was one. Whoever came up here came rarely, stayed briefly, and left only this. The moss has been working on the stones for what looks like centuries. Nothing about the place says return.

The processes here move with a patience that is also, paradoxically, frantic. Cloud comes up the cliff face in waves, swallows the shelf whole for ten minutes, leaves it beaded and saturated, then peels back to blazing thin-air sunlight that dries everything to a crisp brightness within the hour, before the cycle starts again. The birds time their hunting to the cloud-gaps. Insects surge during the dry windows and vanish during the wet ones. The dwarf-trees lean a little further from true with every storm, and one, near the ruined wall, has finally given up entirely and lies along the ground, still half-alive, sending new green shoots straight up from its fallen trunk as if nothing at all has happened to it.

There is something faintly unusual about the place beyond its simple improbability — a sense, hard to source, that the shelf has been a destination for longer than the ruins suggest, that the basin-stone was shaped for a purpose more specific than water-catching, that the spire above, when the cloud parts just right and the low sun comes in sideways, throws a shadow across the shelf that lands, every time, in the same place relative to the stone ring, as if the whole arrangement were a kind of patient instrument, built once, used a few times, and then abandoned when whatever question it was built to answer stopped being asked.

But mostly it is just this: a green and noisy room in the sky, soaked in mist and birdsong, perched above an enormous fall of air, indifferent to the few marks made on it, going on with its small loud business under a high cool light that never quite warms it through.
