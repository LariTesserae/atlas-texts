# The Sump Galleries

Low ground, lower than it should be — a basin the rain found and never quite left. The land here doesn't drain so much as *seep*, water rising through gravel and pale clay until the boundary between ground and puddle becomes a matter of pressure rather than geography. Step in one place and your boot stays dry. Step a hand's width over and you're shin-deep in standing water the color of weak tea, threading between knuckles of grey stone worn round and close together — close enough that in many places you walk hopping rock to rock, never touching the wet ground at all.

No green here. Whatever vegetation might once have rooted in this basin is long gone, and what fills the gap instead is mineral and fungal-adjacent: black and ochre crusts on the stones, slick mats of something between algae and mold spreading in the shallows, faintly ribbed like the soft underside of a tongue. They don't photosynthesize in any visible way — there's not light enough for that, the sky here a permanent dim ceiling of cloud, the basin sitting in a kind of perpetual late-afternoon-before-storm. The crusts seem to feed on the seep itself, on dissolved minerals, on whatever the industrial works upslope leach down into the water table.

Because there *is* industry here, scattered and old but still running. Small structures — squat brick and corroded iron, a dozen of them spread across the basin with no obvious plan, connected by raised plank walkways gone silver-black with damp. Pump houses, mostly. You hear them before you see them: a low mechanical breathing, pistons working somewhere underground, drawing water up and pushing it elsewhise, keeping the basin from drowning itself entirely or perhaps just feeding some deeper process nobody's explained. Pipes run along the ground at odd angles, half-submerged, sweating where warm metal meets wet air. Condensation drips from every overhang in a constant soft percussion — not rain, just the air giving up its water onto anything cold enough to take it.

The density is in the stones themselves, really — they crowd the basin floor close as cobbles, no two alike, none more than waist-high, packed shoulder to shoulder so the whole terrain reads like a held breath of held-back boulders, paused mid-collapse for centuries and just... staying that way. Erosion has had its full say and settled into agreement with itself: every edge here is rounded, every surface lichen-stained or water-stained into the same weathered consensus. Nothing looks new. Even the pump houses look like they grew there.

Fauna moves through the gaps. Not birds — nothing flies in this damp dim air — but low pale things, eyeless or near enough, that thread between the stones with the confidence of creatures who know this place by touch and smell rather than sight. Some are clearly aquatic, finned, navigating the seep-channels like fish navigate streams. Others are stranger: jointed, many-legged, climbing the wet stone in unhurried processions, feeding on the algae-crusts, occasionally on each other. You hear them more than see them — a constant faint scuttle and slip beneath the pump-breath, the basin's actual pulse. Abundant, the parameter says, and it's right: lift any stone and there's a clatter of retreat, a sense of a population far larger than its visibility suggests, packed into this small bounded place like the stones themselves.

The strangeness sits in details that don't resolve. Some of the algae-mats glow, faintly, only at the edges, only sometimes — bioluminescence or chemical reaction or nothing anyone's bothered to name. Some of the stones aren't stones; closer inspection finds machined surfaces beneath the lichen, a corner too regular, a seam. Old infrastructure, drowned and crusted over, indistinguishable now from the geology except where you catch it mid-transition — half stone, half forgotten purpose. The pump houses run on principles no one currently walking these planks seems fully able to explain; they simply tend them, oil the visible joints, listen for the engines to go wrong, and the engines mostly don't.

It's a small world, this basin — walkable in an hour, edge to edge, bounded by a low rise of the same wet grey stone that hems it in like a bowl's lip. Beyond that rise, presumably, dry ground, but few reasons given here to go look. Sound stays close: water-drip, distant piston, scuttle-click, and under all of it a profound hush, the quiet of a place that has settled into its working rhythm and intends to keep it indefinitely. Cool air. Wet hands. The basin breathing through its iron lungs, and everything small and pale and many-legged living in the spaces between the breaths.
