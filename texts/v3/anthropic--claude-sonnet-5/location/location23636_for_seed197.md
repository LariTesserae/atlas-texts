# The Vine-Town of Murmuth's Hollow

There is a place that was a town once, or perhaps will be a town again, or perhaps is a town in some grammatical tense that human language has not bothered to invent — and now it is a town wearing green, a town that the green is wearing, the distinction having long since dissolved into something more honest than either word alone.

## What Remains of the Streets

Low walls. That's the elevation telling you something: this is not a hilltop, not a high place from which to survey conquest. The hollow sits in a shallow basin, almost a bowl, and the buildings — what's left of them, what's *become* of them — rise no higher than two or three stories, their roof-lines softened into suggestion by the mass that's claimed them. You can trace where a street used to run because the vegetation, even at total saturation, still respects the old logic of passage. Plants grow thick everywhere else, but along the route where feet once moved, something — gravity, memory, the dumb persistence of disturbed soil — keeps the canopy a little higher, a little more arched, so you walk through a green tunnel that was once a lane between cobblers and granaries.

The buildings are stone and old timber, the kind of construction that speaks of settlement-scale ambition once: a town hall, a well-house, a row of what must have been shops with shared walls. Established erosion means none of this is fresh ruin. The edges have gone soft decades ago, maybe centuries. Stone corners are rounded like river pebbles. The timber that hasn't rotted to humus has silvered and set into a kind of permanent half-decay, neither falling further nor holding firm — just *there*, the way a settled thing is there, unbothered by its own slow dissolution.

## The Green That Is Everything

Total vegetation doesn't mean jungle exactly — it means the plant life has become the substrate of the place, the medium through which everything else is expressed. Walls are not stone-with-ivy; walls are ivy-that-remembers-being-stone, the masonry now more scaffold than substance, vines load-bearing in ways that should not be possible and are. Roofs are not thatch or tile gone mossy — they are canopy, a continuous low ceiling of broad warm-climate leaves (something between banana and elephant-ear, but veined with a faint bioluminescent blue that has no business existing and exists anyway) that catches the rain in cupped pools and lets it down slow, drop by drop, all day.

Moisture is intermittent here — a damp world, not a wet one. Water collects in leaf-cups and old stone troughs, runs down vine-ropes that have braided themselves into something like gutters, pools in the hollows of doorsteps. It rains in short violent bursts that pass through the canopy as percussion before reaching the ground as mist. Between rains, condensation beads on every leaf-surface and the air carries that thick warm dampness of a held breath.

The warmth is gentle but constant, the temperature of a held hand. No extremes. Everything grows because everything always can.

## The Emptiness Within the Fullness

Here is the strange part, the part where the numbers refuse to resolve into a single comfortable image: despite all this mass of green, despite the totality of vegetation-as-architecture, the *density* of the place — its presence-per-volume — is almost nothing. The vegetation is voluminous but hollow. The vines form chambers, the broad leaves form vaulted rooms, and within these green cathedrals there is mostly air. You walk through corridors of living wall into rooms of living ceiling and find them cavernous, underfilled, a townful of generously empty halls. It is a settlement built at hamlet-scale — you could walk its entire extent in twenty minutes — and yet each room within it feels like a held breath of space, vines arcing overhead the way ribs arc in a cathedral, and almost nothing standing in the middle of the floor.

This held-emptiness is unsettling in a way that's hard to name. The fullness is structural, peripheral, environmental. The center of every room is void.

## The Animals That Should Not Be Heard This Way

Fauna is abundant — genuinely abundant, a teeming biomass of birds, things-like-birds, insects in dense moving clouds, small mammals that move in startling unison like a single nervous organism distributed across forty bodies — and yet the sound this produces is not abundant. It is *total*. Deafening, the parameter insists, and once you're inside the hollow you understand why the word had to be used at full strength: every creature here is loud beyond its proportion to the silence around it. A bird the size of a fist produces a call that should belong to something the size of a cathedral bell. The insect-drone isn't background hum; it's foreground roar, a wall of frequency that seems to come from inside your own skull as much as from the canopy.

The strangeness compounds: sometimes the sound arrives before its source. You hear the heavy beat of wings forty seconds before anything crosses your sightline. You hear a chorus of small birds erupt from a room you have not yet entered, and when you enter it, empty — the room is one of those hollow ones, void at its center, and the sound is already receding from a doorway on the far side that you are certain you have not yet reached.

This is the reality-breakdown asserting itself, gently but insistently: sound here does not always obey sequence. Cause sometimes follows effect. A flock that you watch fly *into* a stand of vine-wall will, three breaths later, produce the sound of having flown *out* of it, from the same spot, the air rippling slightly as if rewinding something that never finished playing forward.

## The Logic That Doesn't Hold

This is a populated emptiness, an established ruin still actively becoming — both processes occurring at once, weathering and overgrowth working not against each other but as if they were the same single motion viewed from two angles. The town is decaying into the plant life and the plant life is constructing itself into the town's shape, simultaneously, with no contradiction felt anywhere in the system.

The light is normal — daylight, real and warm, filtering down through gaps in canopy in the ordinary dapple-pattern you'd expect of any warm sunlit grove — except that it sometimes pools wrong. A patch of sunlight will sit motionless on the ground for an hour past where the gap in the leaves has already closed, the light orphaned from its source, persisting like an afterimage that has decided to become real. Or a room with no visible opening above will hold a single beam, perfectly vertical, sourceless, illuminating nothing in particular, just present, like a memorial candle for an aperture that used to exist.

Doorways occasionally do not connect to the rooms on their other side in any stable way — walk through the cobbler's threshold (you know it's the cobbler's by the rusted last still hanging, vine-wrapped, from a ceiling hook) and you may emerge into the well-house three streets over, or into a room that matches no architecture the town seems to otherwise contain, windowless, vine-vaulted, holding one perfect pool of collected rainwater and absolute silence — the only silence in the whole hollow, a silence so total after the deafening abundance everywhere else that it feels like its own kind of scream.

## No Tools, No Hands, No Memory of Hands

There is no technology here, not even the residue of simple tools beyond what the old buildings imply from some prior age outside the place's current logic. Nothing moves anything. No one shapes anything. The town-shape persists the way a fossil persists — not maintained, not used, just *held* in its established form by the accumulated patience of erosion and growth working in tandem, century after settled century, while abundant unseen animals scream through hollow green rooms and the rain comes in pulses and the light forgets to leave when it should and somewhere, always, just out of the visible street, something very large moves once, slowly, and is never seen, only heard — a low groan of vegetable structure shifting its great weight, settling further into whatever shape it is still, even now, becoming.
