# The Stacked Terraces of Vhalkurun

The mountain is gone. What stands in its place is a thing that remembers being a mountain — the same extreme rise, the same vertiginous shoulders climbing into bright haze — but every surface of it is laid stone, poured concrete, riveted iron, terraced brick. There is no bedrock anywhere within reach of the eye. Somewhere beneath all of it there may still be granite, but no one living has touched it, and no one particularly wonders.

## Form

Vhalkurun rises in eleven thousand vertical meters of ringed terraces, each ring a settlement-shelf wide enough to hold streets, and each shelf retained by walls of fitted stone four and five meters thick, weeping faint mineral stains where condensation gathers and drips in slow brown ribbons. The terraces spiral rather than stack neatly — a long ascending switchback road of a continent, so that walking the rim path from base to summit is a journey of months, not days.

Between terraces, ladders of iron and stone stairwell-towers connect level to level, worn smooth-edged by feet but never worn through — the erosion here is the erosion of long use on durable material, not decay. Things are old. Things have settled into the shapes that centuries of weather and weight press things into. Nothing looks new, and nothing looks like it is failing.

## Materials and Atmosphere

Every wall is built stone or fired brick or cast iron, sealed where needed with a black pitch-glaze that beads moisture rather than absorbing it. Air is warm, thick with humidity that never resolves into proper rain — instead a fine mist drifts through certain terrace-bands at certain hours, condensing on cold iron railings, dripping from leaf and gutter, pooling in carved cisterns that the terrace-keepers tend like shrines. Higher up, the air thins and dries; lower down, near the river-terraces where aqueducts feed cisterns, the dampness is constant and the smell is green and mineral.

Light here is bright and strange-angled, refracted by haze that clings in low terrace-folds and burns away on the high shelves, so the mountain seems to wear different weathers on different rings simultaneously: brilliant clarity at the crown, soft fog two thousand meters down, gold late-afternoon light pouring sideways through gaps in the upper retaining walls.

## Vegetation and Fauna

Lushness here is cultivated, not wild — there is no wild, everything is built — but the built world has been planted into densely. Terrace-gardens cascade over retaining walls in green sheets: vines bred thick and rope-strong to bind the stonework, fruiting trees in stacked planter-rings, moss cultivated deliberately on north-facing walls to slow the wind and hold the damp. Roof-gardens connect to roof-gardens by plank bridges, so that above the street level there is a second, leafier city of its own.

Fauna moves abundant through this layered green: birds nest in the thousands of ledge-niches built deliberately into upper walls (a practice as old as the terraces themselves, half pest-control, half tradition), goats and small hoofed things graze the high garden-rings, fat river-fish move through the cistern-channels, insects hum thick in the warm wet pockets near aqueduct outflows. Nothing here is unmanaged, exactly, but much of it has been left long enough that it behaves as though it were wild.

## Sound and Industry

The place is loud in the particular way of old, slow industry — not chaotic but constant. Waterwheel-driven hammers in the lower forge-terraces beat a steady percussion that carries upward in the dense air. Gear-trains in the cistern-houses turn with a low continuous groan, lifting water shelf to shelf, terrace to terrace, in a relay that has run unbroken for generations. Bells mark terrace-time — each ring has its own bell-tone, slightly detuned from its neighbors, so that a chorus of near-harmonics rolls down the mountain at dawn and dusk. Beneath it all: voices, hooves, cart-wheels on stone, the endless wet drip of condensation finding its way to drains that have channeled it the same way for centuries.

## Rules of the Place

Nothing here changes quickly. The terraces were built once, long ago, by methods half-forgotten even to those who maintain them, and what exists now is maintenance, accretion, slow reinforcement — a wall repaired here, a stair re-laid there, gardens replanted in their same beds generation after generation. Vhalkurun does not grow outward or upward anymore; it simply continues, ring upon ring, in the established shape it found centuries back.

The one unusual law of the place, spoken of without much alarm: water always runs uphill somewhere on the mountain. Not everywhere, not obviously — but every terrace-keeper knows a channel, a pipe, a stretch of gutter where the damp moves the wrong way against gravity, slow and faint, like the mountain breathing inward once for every dozen times it exhales correctly through its proper downhill drains. The engineers built around this rather than against it; entire cistern systems are designed to make use of the upward stretches, and the priest-mechanics who tend the gear-houses near those points are paid slightly better and asked slightly fewer questions.

No one remembers if the mountain shape was chosen because it could be terraced this thoroughly, or whether the terracing itself, applied long enough, is what made something that was once merely tall into something this total — built rim to rim, base to crown, with not one handful of original earth left exposed to the bright, damp, droning sky.
