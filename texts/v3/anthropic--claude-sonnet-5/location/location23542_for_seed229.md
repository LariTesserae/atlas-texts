# The Lung-Garden of Karassi Tor

The plateau sits high—high enough that the air thins and tastes of mineral and chlorophyll in equal measure—but it never sees rain. Water is a rumor here, a thing the old terraces were built for and then abandoned when the builders learned the plants didn't need it, not the way water-plants need it. What feeds this garden moves through the ground itself: a slow grey weeping of mineral oils from fissures in the basalt, oils that the lush growth drinks through root-mats thick as cable. The soil, where you can see it, is dark and waxy, almost soap-textured, and it never quite dries to dust the way you'd expect at this elevation.

## The Terrain

Karassi Tor is small—you could walk its boundary in an afternoon, if the walking were easy, which it is not. It's a single high shelf of folded grey-green stone, eroded into rounded humps and sudden short cliffs, the kind of erosion that comes from age rather than violence—wind-softened, settled, comfortable in its shapes the way an old animal is comfortable in its bones. The whole plateau tilts slightly, draining toward a lip on its eastern edge where the rock simply ends in a long fall into haze. No rivers ever carved this place. It carved itself, slowly, against wind and time and the patient pressure of roots.

Between the stone humps grow groves—not forests, nothing so continuous—isolated stands of broad-leafed growth in colors that shouldn't exist together: bruise-purple fronds beside acid yellow-greens, deep arterial reds threading through waxy blue-black leaves like veins. The vegetation is lush within its groves but the groves themselves are scattered, islands of green riot separated by long stretches of bare warm stone where nothing grows at all, as if the plants have agreed among themselves where the boundaries lie.

## The Sound

This is the loudest quiet place you will ever stand in. There is no water-sound, no rush, no babble—and into that absence pours everything else. The vegetation itself makes noise: stem-chambers that fill with gas and release it through valve-like apertures in long groaning exhalations, a sound like the plateau itself breathing. Seed-pods detonate on cycles measured in hours, not days—a percussive popping that rolls across the stone and triggers answering pops from groves a hundred meters off, a call-and-response that never fully stops. And over all of it, the fauna: things with membrane wings that shouldn't be able to fly in air this thin, screaming territorial calls between cliff-perches; ground-things that drum on the hollow stone with hardened knuckle-pads; something large and unseen that produces, every twenty minutes or so, a low call like a held note on a bone flute, answered from somewhere on the other side of the plateau.

## The Fauna

Abundant beyond what the sparse, scattered vegetation should support—which is the first sign that something about this place's rules doesn't match ordinary expectation. Lean grey-skinned grazers with too many joints in their legs move between groves in loose herds, eating not leaves but the gas the plants exhale, mouths cupped upward like they're drinking from the air. Nest-colonies of membrane-wing things roost in the cliff-faces, their guano staining the rock in radiant streaks of blue-green that seem to glow faintly after dark, a bioluminescence borrowed or stolen from something they eat. Small quick scavengers, doglike but wrong in the hip and the eye, follow the herds and follow each other and occasionally follow the few human travelers who pass through, at a distance, curious rather than hostile.

The strangeness deepens here: animal behavior on Karassi Tor runs in bursts of frantic activity followed by long stillness, and the bursts seem to correlate with the gas-cycles of the plants rather than any rhythm of light or dark. Something is feeding something, on a clock that has nothing to do with a sun.

## Light and Atmosphere

The light is dim even at midday—the plateau sits often beneath a haze that the locals (the very few who pass through and call it anything) describe as breath-fog, a low ceiling of cloud that doesn't rain because there's nothing in it to rain, just the suspended exhalation of ten thousand gas-chambers venting slowly into still high air. Light comes through this haze diffuse and shadowless, the color of old brass. At what should be dusk, the bioluminescent stains on the cliffs and the slow drift of phosphorescent spores from certain groves take over, and the place becomes a dim garden of cold blue-green light pulsing very slightly out of sync with itself.

## The Built Things

Scattered, faint, old. A scatter of low stone walls, waist-high, arranged in patterns that suggest old terracing for crops that may have needed water and so failed, or may never have been water-crops at all—nobody now is sure which. A handful of single structures: a windowless dome of dry-fit stone with a smoke-hole that hasn't seen smoke in generations, three standing stones leaned together at angles too precise to be accident, a half-collapsed lookout perched on the eastern lip where the plateau falls away, facing out into haze at nothing anyone living has named. Hand-tools turn up sometimes, wedged in root-mats—blades of hardened wood, a few corroded simple-metal edges, evidence of people who worked this stone with their hands and small fires and nothing more, and who are gone now, or elsewhere, or simply quiet.

## The Turbulence

Nothing here holds still long. This is the place's deepest rule: equilibrium is temporary and everyone knows it. Groves grow with visible speed in the gas-rich microclimates near root-weeps, then over-mature and collapse within a season, their wax-soil souring, the grazers moving on, the scavengers following. New groves erupt elsewhere on cycles no one has mapped. The seed-pod detonations sometimes cascade—one grove setting off the next setting off the next in a wave of percussive noise that crosses the whole plateau in minutes, a green detonation-storm that leaves spent husks and fresh seed-litter and excited, gorging fauna in its wake. Cliffs shed rockfall when root systems finally crack the stone they've been patiently working for decades. The bone-flute call changes pitch, season to season, as if its source is also changing, growing, becoming something else.

To stand on Karassi Tor is to stand somewhere that is always mid-transformation, never finished, and—despite its age, despite the long settled erosion of its stone—never at rest. It is an old place that refuses to be a still one. The few travelers who come here, drawn by rumor of the gas-flowers or the bone-flute thing or the blue-green cliffs, tend to describe it the same way afterward: *alive in a way that felt like being watched by something patient, hungry, and in no hurry at all.*
