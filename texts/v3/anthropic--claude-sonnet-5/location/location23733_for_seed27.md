# The Lignate Heights

The water left this place so long ago that the word for it survives only as a root in older compound nouns—*hydra-still*, the dead engineers called the era before. What remains, at this altitude, on this ancient and near-flattened plateau, is wood. Not forest in the old sense—forest implies floor, canopy, gaps. Here there is no gap. The vegetation does not grow *on* the terrain. It *is* the terrain, has been for longer than erosion can properly account for, its growth and dying and growth again having worn itself down into something like geology.

## Substrate

The plateau extends past sight in every direction, a slow swell of high ground gone soft-edged with age, and across all of it: lignified mass, layered the way sediment layers, except every stratum was once alive and most strata still are, somewhere inside. Cut a cliff face anywhere here—and the cliffs exist, where old growth has slumped and calcified into bluffs forty meters tall—and you'd find rings within rings within rings, fused trunks the width of plazas, hollowed and re-grown and hollowed again, a heartwood archive going down into a dim that no one has bottomed out.

The surface you walk on is canopy, but canopy gone load-bearing: a meshed lattice of branch-as-beam, root-as-foundation, bark-as-pavement, silvered grey-brown in the low light, cool and slightly springy underfoot like something that remembers being soft. In long-settled districts the wood has been coaxed—by means current physics doesn't fully license, by tools that read growth-intention out of cellular fields and *suggest* rather than cut—into towers, bridges, terraces, whole municipal districts grown rather than built. A bell-spire here is a single redirected trunk, four hundred years in the making, still faintly, glacially thickening.

## Light and Air

The sun, where there is one, arrives filtered through whatever canopy-layer sits above the current one—because the mass is tiered, three or four strata of living crown stacked atop each other like a building with floors of leaf. The light that reaches the inhabited terraces is dim, green-grey, the color of light through closed eyelids. It does not change much through the day; the cycles here are slower, marked less by sun-angle than by the staggered blooming of different tiers, each on its own decades-long clock.

It is cool always, the kind of cool that comes from biomass holding its own temperature against a thin, high atmosphere. No water moves through this place in any liquid form—what the plants need they pull from deep mineral veins or from the air itself, through aerial root-mats that hang in curtains between the tiers, drinking humidity that barely exists, fed in places by misting arrays the engineers seeded centuries back and that still, somehow, run.

## Sound

It is not quiet. It is, in fact, loud—the loudest kind of quiet there is. Wood groans constantly under its own shifting weight, a low-frequency creaking that never fully resolves, like a ship the size of a country. Through this baseline drone: the abundant fauna. Things that are not quite birds, having long since traded flight-feather for glide-membrane, shriek and clatter between perches. Burrowing things move audibly through the punk-soft deadwood layers below the walking surface, a constant subsonic rustle. Somewhere—always, everywhere, never locatable—something large exhales, once, every several minutes, a sound the settlements have stopped reacting to.

The built sections add their own noise: chimes grown rather than hung, resonance-chambers carved into living trunks that the wind (what wind there is, thin and high-altitude) plays like instruments, tuned over generations into a kind of unintentional, ever-shifting music that the residents claim to no longer hear, though visitors always do.

## Settlement

Sparse, this far up, this far gone in time. Towns sit like burls along the spine of the plateau, scattered with real distance between them—not for lack of room but by old law, some half-remembered ordinance about letting the growth-mass breathe between population nodes. Each town a cluster grown around some original founding tree, now indistinguishable from its descendants, terraces spiraling up and out, walkways that are roots that were asked, long ago, to go a certain direction and have been complying ever since.

The technology here doesn't look like technology. It looks like more wood, until you notice the seams of soft light running along certain grain-lines, or the way a door dilates without hinges, or the fact that some structures hum at a frequency that makes your fillings ache, doing something to gravity or growth-rate or time-within-the-wood that no one bothers to explain to outsiders because the explanation requires four other things explained first and the residents have stopped finding it interesting.

## Ecology

Abundant fauna, strange fauna: grazers that eat bark and excrete a kind of soil substitute that the trees then drink back up through aerial roots—a closed loop, water-absent, running entirely on dead matter and patience. Predators that are mostly limb and patience themselves, ambush-things that have evolved to look exactly like a particular forty-year stretch of dead branch, and do, until they don't.

Things that may not be fauna at all but a mobile growth-state of the plant mass—nobody's fully settled the taxonomy, and the disagreement has been going on, companionably, for generations, in a town two ridges over that has built its entire university around the question.

## Rule of the Place

Nothing here is finished growing. That is the one rule that holds. The cliffs are old, the architecture is centuries-settled, the culture has the unhurried, deeply-aged texture of a civilization that stopped being surprised by itself a long time ago—and underneath all of it, every beam, every street, every silent tower, the wood is still, almost imperceptibly, becoming something else. Ancient is not the same as finished. It is just very, very slow.
