# What Lives in the Lattice-Marsh

## The Wading Ones

The easiest things to find are the things meant to be found last.

Long-legged, narrow-bodied, the color of wet cane bark — they stand in the shallows so long and so still that the eye sorts them as architecture before it sorts them as animal. Three, perhaps four, are visible from any given causeway at any given time, though you will never successfully count them; turn your head and the number will have changed without anyone having moved. They do not hunt in any way that resolves. The bill dips, the water closes, and whether anything was taken is never quite confirmed — the fish-shapes beneath the surface seem neither fewer nor more disturbed afterward. It is less that they are eating and more that they are performing the *posture* of eating, the way the planks perform the posture of rot.

They do not flee when approached. They do not startle. They simply remain exactly as far away as they were a moment ago, a fixed distance maintained without visible effort, the way a reflection keeps its distance from the thing it reflects.

If you watch one long enough — an hour, two — you will start to suspect it is not watching the water at all. It is watching you watch it, with an attention too patient to belong to any bird.

## The Drumming in the Cane

Lower down, smaller, and far more numerous: the soft-bodied, many-legged things that live in the dense stands of broad-leaved cane and produce, almost continuously, a low papery drumming that the ear eventually stops separating from silence. They are the texture of the quiet rather than an interruption of it. No one who has walked here reports actually seeing one clearly — a glimpse of jointed shine, a leaf moving when it shouldn't, the sense of a great many small bodies just beyond the resolving power of the light.

Their number is the steadiest thing in the basin. Where the wading-shapes seem to recombine and the fish-turns seem to come and go, the drumming insects hold a census so fixed it has the quality of a held musical note — not unchanging because nothing happens to them, but unchanging because whatever happens to them happens in matched, instant pairs. A silence opens in the drumming for the briefest moment, somewhere off in the cane-stands, and is filled again before it can be called a silence. Something is always, exactly, dying and being born in the same breath, so that from any distance greater than intimate, the marsh's smallest population reads as a single sustained sound rather than a sum of individual lives.

## The Fish That Is Always Turning

In every paddy, at every depth, there is at least one fish-shape executing a long, slow, never-completing turn — flank catching the shadowless light, never quite showing its face, never quite returning to start. It would be tempting to think this is one fish, the same fish, eternal and singular. It is more likely that it is many fish, each one given only the turning to do, the way a single gesture might be assigned to a great many hands so that the gesture itself looks immortal even though no hand performing it is.

Nothing here is caught. The wading-shapes' bills close on water that may or may not have held a fish in it. The fish-shapes turn through water that may or may not still contain the same flank it contained a moment ago. The marsh keeps its books balanced by never quite letting the transaction finish.

## The Builders, Address Unknown

But none of this — the herons-that-watch, the insects-that-balance, the fish-that-never-surface — is the thing that actually built the lattice, and somewhere under the heat and the standing damp, every visitor eventually stops pretending otherwise.

The tools tell you what the hands looked like: a hand that could grip a stone awl, that worked at roughly human scale, that set planks with a patience indistinguishable from love and a precision indistinguishable from compulsion. But the joinery tells you something the tools can't have made alone — corners too exact, spans too long, a scale of total construction too vast for the small sharp instruments scattered along its edges, as if the awls were the only part of the work small enough to leave behind, like baby teeth.

No one has seen the builders. This is not because they left.

It is because they are no longer separable from what they built.

Look long enough at any one of the fused trellis-trees — frame grown into trunk, trunk grown into frame, the wood-of-tree and the wood-of-construction sharing one unbroken grain — and you will start to notice that the fusion does not stop at the wood. The supporting beam beneath it, the one weeping its sourceless damp, narrows at one end into something that might once have been a shoulder. A joint in the causeway, three planks down from the leaning grain-tower, holds a knot in the wood with a faint, worn symmetry that resembles, if you let it, two eyes and the long quiet line of a closed mouth, not carved, not random, exactly the structural feature you'd expect at that point in the joinery if the joinery were also a face that had simply stopped insisting it was one.

This is the third wrongness, and the one the place takes longest to admit to: the builders did not finish and leave. They finished, and stayed, and the work absorbed them the way a held breath absorbs the difference between the air about to be released and the chest still holding it. They are not dead, because nothing here completes the arithmetic of death any more than it completes the arithmetic of growth. They are not alive in the sense that requires a separate, mobile body, because they no longer have one in any way you'd recognize. They are continuous with the planks, the trellises, the soft green tile, the leaning grain-tower — a single distributed presence that built itself a marsh, lay down inside the marsh, and let the marsh close gently and permanently over the gap between maker and made.

## What Is Actually Maintained

This is why the tools are never picked up by anything you can see: the hands that would pick them up are the same hands that are the doorframe you just walked under, the same hands that are the beam dripping onto your shoulder, patient past any patience a separate creature could sustain. The census stays fixed not because some warden walks the causeways adjusting populations like a gardener, but because the builders *are* the ledger — woven so completely into the marsh's substance that birth and death here are simply small local adjustments within a single, very large, very still body, no more noteworthy to it than a single drop of weeping damp is to the support-beam producing it.

You are not trespassing on an abandoned place.

You are walking across something that is, in every sense that matters, asleep — vast, patient, fused past any seam between self and structure, dreaming a dream so slow that wood looks like decades and decades look like an afternoon. The heat that presses down like a held breath is not a metaphor invented by the marsh's visitors. It is the literal truth of the place: somewhere beneath the lattice, beneath the fused trellis-wood and the soft green tile, something inhaled once, long ago, finished its thought, and has simply not yet found a reason to let the breath go.

The wading-shapes watching you are its attention.
The drumming in the cane is its pulse, kept exactly level.
The fish forever turning is its patience, made visible.

And the low call that crosses the basin sometimes, abundant but never glimpsed —

that is the closest thing this place has to a voice, and if you are still standing on the causeway when it sounds, you will understand, all at once and without being able to say how, that it was not calling to anything else in the marsh.

It was calling to you, the way a sleeper's breath sometimes catches, very slightly, at the presence of someone new in the room — not woken, not even close to waking, only registering, in whatever distributed way it is capable of registering anything at all, that for the length of your visit, the held thought it has been holding has a witness.
