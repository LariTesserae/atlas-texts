**The Tenders**

There are perhaps a few hundred of them spread across the whole basin, where once — you can tell from the size of the dormitory blocks, the long-cold barracks-rows with their hundred identical doors, the chapel-sized canteens now used for storing parts — there might have been thousands. They are not a mystery in the way the place around them resists mystery. They are simply few, and they are exactly what the work made them.

You'd find one, eventually, if you waited at a junction long enough or followed a pipe-run to wherever it terminated in a furnace-house. A woman, say, in her fifties or older, though the heat and the haze make ages hard to read — skin gone the same soft red-brown as the brick, loosened at the throat and the backs of the hands, sweated-through clothing that's stopped being cotton-colored and become basin-colored, a uniform brown-gray that matches the chimneys and the clay-pan both. She moves without hurry. There is no version of this job that rewards hurry. She has a satchel of small tools worn shiny at the buckle, a long iron key or two on a ring at her hip for valves that haven't been relabeled in a generation, and she walks a route she could walk asleep — has, probably, on the bad nights — checking gauges that mean something only to her, listening to the breathing of a particular engine the way you'd listen to a sleeping person to make sure they were still sleeping and not something worse.

She does not mind the animals. This is the first thing to understand about her and about all of them. The heavy ground-birds that roost above her furnace and streak its bricks white have been there longer than she has worked there, and she has long since stopped seeing them as separate from the building — they are simply what the chimney-top looks like, the way moss is what a roof looks like elsewhere. She steps around the fat amphibious things sunning on the warm flue-stones without really registering the step. When something heavier slaps into a drainage pool at night she doesn't startle; she's catalogued the weight and shape of every splash this basin makes, the same way she's catalogued every knock and shudder her machinery is allowed to make before it means trouble.

What she is, mostly, is a relationship — between herself and a small number of vast slow patient machines that were built to require a workforce ten or twenty times her present number, and which she has learned, over years, to keep alive anyway, through a kind of triage of attention. She cannot tend everything. So she has learned which parts of the works can be left to rust into lace, decorative and useless, and which parts must be oiled and walked and listened to weekly or the whole regional breathing apparatus stutters. This knowledge is not written anywhere. It lives in her, and in the dozen or so others like her scattered through the haze, each with their own quarter of the basin, each rarely seeing the others except at the rail junction where supplies come in twice a month, where they trade news in the short flat sentences of people who don't get much practice talking.

They are not unhappy. This is the surprising part, if you could ask one and get an honest answer. There is a peace available to a person who has been given an enormous, half-emptied, animal-thick, slow-breathing machine and told: keep it alive, however few you are, however long it takes. It asks nothing of her that isn't concrete. The gauge reads what it reads. The valve turns or it doesn't. The birds roost where they roost, indifferent to her, and she is indifferent back, and between the two indifferences there is something almost like company.

At night she goes back to whichever room in whichever dormitory-row she's claimed as her own — far too large a building for one person, most of its hundred doors shut on rooms she's never entered — and she sleeps to the same sound the whole basin sleeps to: the engines breathing low under the haze, the drip of condensate off the pipe-runs counting out a rhythm with no fixed period, and underneath all of it, settling toward silence as the animals settle too, the sense of a great deal of patient life — mechanical and otherwise — being kept, by very few hands, exactly as it has always been kept.
