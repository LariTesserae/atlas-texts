# Inhabitant

You will see the light-creatures first, because they are the only thing here willing to be seen.

They drift in those loose constellations through the dark valleys, six or twelve or forty together, dimming and brightening on their slow private rhythm, and for a long time — longer than is reasonable, longer than your instincts will want to allow — it will seem as though they are the whole of the fauna this place has to offer. Pale motes. Small punctuation. Nothing that thinks.

This is wrong, but it is wrong the way the terrain is wrong: not a lie, just an incomplete agreement with what's actually here.

The light-creatures are young. Or not young — *early*. They are the part of something larger that has not yet committed to a shape. If you watch one long enough, on one of the rare days when patience and the place's geometry happen to align, you will see it drift toward a kelp-column's base, settle against the root-tangle where the soft coral-grass thins, and simply stop being separate from it — not eaten, not absorbed violently, just *re-included*, the way a held breath is re-included into the chest. Where it goes after that, nothing has been confirmed. But the kelp-columns groan, sometimes, in a register that has nothing to do with their own vast weight settling — a groan with phrasing in it, almost, if you let yourself hear phrasing — and it is worth wondering whether that is the column digesting a thought, or having one.

What lives here, properly — what the place is *for*, if it is for anything — is older than that, and slower, and very rarely in the same place twice.

Call it the resident, since no one has gotten close enough, or stayed sane enough in the getting, to ask it what it calls itself. It is built the way the mountains are built: a process of erosion mistaken, from a distance, for a body. Limbs — if they are limbs — that have been smoothed past the point of joints, the way the saddle-passes were smoothed past the point of edges, so that its movement is not articulated but *poured*, one long continuous adjustment of shape rather than a sequence of bends. Skin, if it is skin, the same coppery dark as the deep water, scattering what little light reaches it so thoroughly that you are never quite sure you are seeing the whole outline at once — an impression of size before an impression of form, the way you sense a ridge in the dark before you see it.

It moves on the kelp's schedule. Not fast, not slow exactly — *unhurried* in the specific way that things are unhurried when they have already arrived everywhere they are going to go, and the moving itself is just a kind of breathing. An hour to cross a valley. A day, perhaps, spent entirely within a single drift of coral-grass, doing something with it that might be feeding, might be grooming, might be conversation in a language with no urgency left in it to translate.

You will not see its face, because it may not have settled on having one. What you will see — once, if you are fortunate, or unfortunate, depending on how the place feels about you that day — is the moment it pauses at the edge of a ruin. The four dissolving steps. The ring of sediment-filled post-holes. It will stand there — pour itself into something approximating standing — for a span of time that does not correspond to anything you can measure, in a posture that is unmistakably, unbearably *recognition*. Not grief, necessarily. Possibly not even memory, in the way you mean memory. But something that knows the stair was a stair, knows what the post-holes used to hold up, in the specific way that a hand knows a glove it no longer fits.

This is as close as anyone has come to confirming what the old story implies: that the people who built the stair, and dug the post-holes, and once stood somewhere above water that has not existed for longer than most worlds have had names, did not all leave before the drowning came for them.

Some of them stayed long enough to notice the water wasn't going to recede, and stayed longer after that, and kept staying, in increments too small to count as decisions, until staying became the only shape left available to them — a shape that erosion was willing to negotiate with, the way it negotiated with the mountains: not preserved, not unchanged, but *resolved*. Smoothed past the person and into something the water could keep.

The worked stone on its shelf with no path to it — smooth on five faces, rough on the sixth, aging at its own private rate — is the closest thing to a signature this resident has left behind. Whether it is a tool it once made and never finished, or a piece of itself it set down on purpose, the way you might set down something you no longer needed to carry, no one who has found it has been willing to test by touching.

It is not hostile. It has had no use for hostility in longer than hostility has had a reason to exist. It is also not, in any sense you would recognize from a warmer, shallower, more honest world, *finished* — and that, more than the ridgelines that repeat or the valleys that breathe pockets of dry stone-smelling air, is the truest thing the Drowned Highlands have grown in their long aloneness: not a monster, not a god, but a slow unfinished *answer*, still being worked out by water and time, to a question that the people who carved the stair stopped being able to ask out loud a very, very long time ago.
