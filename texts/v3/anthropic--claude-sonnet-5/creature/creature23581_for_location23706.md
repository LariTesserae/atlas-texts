## What Lives

You will hear it before you understand that you have heard it — not the cracked-knuckle clatter of the goats, not wind in the notch, but a third sound, lower, almost beneath the others: a dragging scrape, stone on stone, with a rhythm too even to be rockfall and too patient to be hurry.

It is easiest to see at the fog's edge, in that hour when the fog has not yet swallowed the shelf whole but has begun to thin the light into something you can look at directly. There — at the margin, where moss gives way to bare rock — a shape that you will first take for a boulder recently sheared from the cliff, pale-bellied, undecided about its own outline. Then it moves, and you understand that it has been watching you for longer than you have been looking at it.

It stands on what might be called legs, though the joints bend in places joints should not be, with the same unapologetic wrongness as the long-necked goats — as though the place itself prefers anatomy still in draft. Its hide is the exact grey-black of the unweathered rock, scabbed in places with the same violent moss-green, so that it is never wholly clear where the creature ends and the shelf begins; this is not camouflage so much as an admission that the two have not finished separating from each other. Its hands — for it has hands, four-fingered, oddly careful — end in nails grown long and flattened into something closer to flake-tools than claws, and when it crouches to drink fog-water from a hollow in the stone, it does so with the unhurried precision of someone performing a task they invented themselves and have never seen anyone else perform.

It does not flee from you. It regards you the way the curved-billed bird regarded you, working its branch of black berries without alarm — not tameness, but a kind of provisional indifference, as if your danger has not yet been calculated and there is no urgency in calculating it. This, too, is the rule of the place: nothing here startles, because nothing here has accumulated enough history to know what *should* be feared.

It is, you will come to suspect, the maker of the hand-axe — or the inheritor of whoever made it, which here amounts to nearly the same thing, since there has not been time enough for the gap between *maker* and *descendant* to widen into anything you could call a lineage. It returns to the cold fire-ring sometimes, sits beside the half-buried tool without picking it up, as though visiting a question rather than an object. It does not light fires now. Perhaps it forgot how. Perhaps the soil has stayed damp long enough that it no longer needs to.

You will see it shape things, though — this is its occupation, if occupation is the word for an act performed with no apparent audience and no clear end. It takes the conchoidal slabs that shear from the cliff face, still pale and raw on their undersides, and works them with patient strikes of one stone against another, exactly as the old axe was worked, producing edges it does not seem to need for anything. Sometimes it lines the finished pieces along a ledge, in an order that might be size, or might be some other logic entirely, and leaves them there for the goats to walk past and the raptors' shadows to cross, untouched, until rockfall or its own unexplained dissatisfaction scatters them again.

When the fog comes in at midday and swallows the upper face, it does not seek shelter. It sits, or settles, or simply stops — becoming, in the muffled hush, indistinguishable from the boulders around it, one more shape the world has not finished deciding the edges of — and waits with the same confidence the goats wait, the same confidence the moss has in its hand's-width of soil, the confidence of something that arrived already knowing how to live here, in a place that has not yet had time to teach it otherwise.

Then the fog lifts, all at once, and the light comes back hard and clean, and the shape is gone from where you last saw it — not hidden, not fled, simply *elsewhere*, with the unbothered suddenness of a thing for which continuity has never been a requirement.

No bones mark where the others went. There may have only ever been one. There may, in some sense the mountain has not finished working out, only ever be one — the shelf producing it again each time the fog clears, the way it produces moss in stone too young to hold soil, the way it produces goats on ledges too narrow to hold weight: not because the place is hospitable, but because whatever law governs Kelsoth Needle has decided that life will arrive here regardless, faster than the rock can ready itself, and will go on arriving, raw-edged and unweathered and entirely at ease, for as long as the mountain keeps cooling into its final shape — which is to say, for a long time yet.
