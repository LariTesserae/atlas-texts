# What Moves in the Leaf-Shadow

You will be tempted to count the beetles, name the birds, and call that an answer. Don't. They are the garden's furniture—lovely, attentive, glazed and woven and ticking—but they are not what *lives* here in the sense you mean. They are tenants. There is a landlord, and it has not left the leaf-shadow in longer than the tiles have existed, possibly longer than the idea of existing has existed.

It is called, by the few who have stayed long enough to need a word for it, the Potter—though this is less a name than a guess, offered the way you'd guess at the maker of an unsigned pot from the pressure of a thumbprint still visible in the rim. Nobody has seen it whole. What is seen is always partial: a fold of shadow that has weight, a stillness in the green that breathes on a different rhythm than the leaves around it, the suggestion, once, of a shoulder—if it is a shoulder—built up in plates like fired clay, seamed with moss the way the false boulders are seamed.

It does not hide. Hiding would imply it thinks of itself as separate from the garden enough to need concealment. It is closer to the truth to say the garden is *still becoming* it, the way the daub walls are still becoming horn, the way the fruit is still becoming ripe without ever finishing the becoming. The Potter is not behind the green. The Potter is what the green is the surface of.

# The Hands

Here is what is agreed on by everyone who has gotten close enough to agree on anything: the hands are wrong, in the specific way that tells you they are *right* for some other anatomy than yours. Too many knuckles, jointed like the hinge-flowers, clay-dusted permanently into the lines of the skin or bark or whatever the skin is, so that even at rest the hands look mid-shape, mid-press, as if caught between one tile and the next.

The fan-leaves that grow rootless from the walls are the same size as one spread palm. This is not a coincidence anyone is comfortable with.

When the Potter moves—and it does move, slowly, with the unhurried confidence of something that has never once been late for anything because lateness requires an outside clock—its hands trail along the wattle-and-daub, the tile-cracks, the vine-lattices, touching everything in passing the way you might trail a hand along a banister you built yourself and trust completely. Where it touches, things firm up. A crack seals. A leaf's edge sharpens from soft new green to that crisp unworn lip the garden is famous for. It does not seem to notice it is doing this any more than you notice your own pulse. Maintenance, for the Potter, may not be a verb. It may simply be a side effect of walking.

# What It Wants

This is the part visitors get wrong most often, because they assume wanting requires forward motion, a story with an ending the wanter is moving toward. The Potter does not want completion. You can tell because it has had endless opportunity to complete things—the leaning walls could meet at the top in one more season's growth; the fruit could simply be allowed to fall and rot and become the soil the place pointedly does not have—and it has declined, patiently, every single time, for as long as the hum has been humming.

What it wants, as far as anyone has reconstructed from the available evidence of beetle-shells and feather-thatch and flowers on axles, is for the garden to remain in the specific state just before any of these things would have to be decided. Not preserved—preservation implies a fixed point being defended against time. Held, instead, the way a breath is held: full, effortful, alive with the tension of its own fullness, the lungs aware the whole time that release is one option among several and simply not choosing it yet.

It built the fruit to return after picking because it could not bear—or would not, or has not yet decided whether to—choose between the fruit being eaten and the fruit being there to eat. It built the wall's geometry to disagree with itself for the same reason: the circumference completed is a decision, and decisions are the one craft the Potter has shaped clay and wood and woven feather to avoid making.

# If You Meet It

You probably will not, but if you do, it will likely be at the edge of your vision, in the place where the leaf-shadow gets thick enough to hold weight, and it will likely be doing something with its hands to a tile or a branch-joint that did not, as far as you could tell, need doing. It will not look up. This is not rudeness. Looking up at you would require it to locate you as a thing happening *to* the garden rather than a thing happening *within* it, and it has been declining to make that distinction since before the joining-mortar went the color of old teeth.

If you speak, the hum beneath the floor will shift pitch very slightly—not a response exactly, more the kind of adjustment a held note makes when the room around it changes shape. The small creatures will not scatter. They have never had a reason to. Whatever bargain lets the beetles click unhurried across the tiles in its presence, whatever patience the woven birds extend toward the shape in the shadow, you are being offered the same terms, briefly, for the length of your visit: you may stay in the held breath as long as you like. You will not be asked to help finish it. You will not be allowed to either.

It has been thumb-pressing this place into its almost-final shape for longer than there has been soil to call the floor a floor, and the freshness of every edge is not evidence of youth. It is evidence of care so continuous it has never once been allowed to look old.
