# Those Who Climb the Falling

They call themselves — in the four or five tongues that have been overheard and badly reconstructed by the rare survey that bothered to listen — something that translates roughly to *the light-debtors*, or *those who owe the green*. It is not a flattering name. They did not give it to themselves out of pride.

## Bodies Built for Catastrophe

They are not large. Largeness is a poor strategy in a place where the floor you are standing on may simply cease to exist by evening. What they have instead is grip — hands and feet both, fingers and toes equally long, equally capable of closing around a vine-causeway in the half-second between the first tremor and the second. Their skin runs pale, almost root-colored, a damp under-color that never tans because there is no sun to tan under, only the shadowless pearl-glow that asks nothing of pigment. Their eyes are too large for the rest of the face, an old adaptation to a light that has no glare to squint against but plenty of vastness to need to read — judging distance across kilometers of green-gold haze, judging which far-off tremor in the canopy means *nothing, a bird* and which means *run, climb, now*.

They are quiet by necessity and have made a virtue of it. Loud things, in a place this acoustically vast, summon attention from animals too large to have ever been properly seen. So they have developed a language that is mostly not sound — hand-shapes flashed across a clearing, weight-shifts on a vine-causeway that travel as readable vibration to anyone holding the same line, a held stillness that means *wait* more clearly than any word could. The sound-language they do have is sparing, used at distance, in single struck notes not unlike the bird-calls that cross the canopy — and it's an open question, never quite settled even among themselves, who learned the call from whom.

## Living on Things That Are Already Falling

They do not build, in the sense that building implies permanence, because nothing here rewards permanence. What they do instead might be better called *visiting upward.* A family — and family is loose here, reconstituted often, because people are lost often — will find a trunk-system old enough to feel trustworthy, climb it over a season, lash a shelter into a fork using vine-fiber and their own teeth, live there for a year or three or ten, watching the slow creak of growth around them, listening for the particular deepening groan that means the wood beneath them has started to lose an argument with its own weight. Then they leave. Not always in time. The ladders and lashings and cold fire-rings scattered through the dark are not abandoned monuments; they are simply what's left after a place stopped being safe and the people who knew it stopped being there to say so.

Because of this, their relationship to the forest is not one of harmony, despite how that word wants to attach itself to anything old and quiet and green. It is closer to tenancy under a landlord who is not malicious, only enormous and indifferent and constantly renovating. They watch collapse the way coastal people watch weather — with genuine respect, real fear, and a body of inherited, half-superstitious knowledge about which groans mean *settling* and which mean *go.* Children are taught the sound of a trunk-system about to fail before they are taught much else. Those who don't learn it don't get old.

## What They Eat, What Eats Them

They lick dew like everything else here, carry hollowed gourd-knots that catch the slow drip of stump-pools, chew the fiber-pulp of certain young root-shoots for the thin sugar in it. They hunt almost nothing, because almost nothing is large enough to be worth the risk of hunting; mostly they gather, climb, wait, and trade — among themselves, across the rare meeting of two families on a shared causeway — small worked things: a knapped sliver of stone, a sinew cord, a particular twist of woven fiber that holds knots better than the others.

The pale many-legged thing that lives entirely inside one trunk-system is, to them, not vermin and not livestock but something closer to a neighbor whose habits you learn and accommodate — they know which trunks have one and avoid driving their lashings too deep into that wood. The larger thing, the one that shakes a vine-causeway a quarter mile without ever showing itself, is spoken of carefully, referred to sideways, almost never named directly, the way you don't say the word for a flood while standing in the floodplain. Nobody alive has described it well. Several have died near where it was, which is not the same as dying because of it, though the distinction has stopped mattering to anyone's grief.

## What They Believe About the Light

Because they have no sun and have never had one, the absence isn't a wound the way it would be for us — it isn't *missing* anything. But they have theories, told differently by every family, about where the glow comes from, and almost all of the theories agree on one thing: that the light is *owed*. That the green takes something from the dark below — the old root-soil, the dead, the calved-away cliffs of amber wood-flesh — and gives back more than it took, brightness instead of substance, and that this is not generosity but a kind of cosmic error the forest cannot stop making. Some hold that the dead become light eventually, filtered up through root and trunk and leaf over the generations-that-are-decades, which is less a comfort than it sounds, since it means the brightness overhead is, among other things, ancestral, watching, accumulating, and never coming back down to be anyone again.

They do not worship this. They are not pious people; piety is a luxury of places stable enough to support a cosmology that doesn't need updating every few years. Mostly they just live underneath it, climbing when they can, falling sometimes, listening always for the groan that means *move*, leaving their ladders and their cold fire-rings and their abandoned shelters scattered thin across a darkness made bright by a debt no one alive remembers incurring — and getting on, in the meantime, with the strange, brief, vertical business of being people in a place too large and too fast to ever be entirely theirs.
