**The bird.**

It has no name outside the people who tend the turbine house, who call it, when they bother, *the hammer-bird* — a clipped, private name, used the way you'd name a tool. It is small, the size of a fist, plumage the exact non-color of wet basalt: a black that resolves, in good light, into deep oxide-brown. Its eyes are disproportionately large, pale-ringed, built for the perpetual dusk of the spire-throat. It does not sing. What it does instead is time its calls — three sharp clicks, evenly spaced — to the turbine's idle pitch, so that for years the workers swore the machine itself was making the sound, until someone finally climbed the eave with a lantern and found the nest tucked into the lee of the flywheel housing, eggs trembling very slightly, perpetually, against the bare metal.

This is not incidental. The hammer-bird breeds on the machine's pulse. Its clutch-cycle tracks the header tank's hours-long fill-and-release rhythm with an accuracy nobody has explained; chicks hatch within a day of a tank-cycle's peak pressure, as if the vibration through the iron were a second incubating warmth, a heartbeat borrowed wholesale from someone else's body. When the works fall silent — and sometimes they do, for a season, when no one comes to clear a blocked intake — the birds don't leave. They simply stop breeding, and wait, perched along the cold pipe, until the water starts moving through it again.

What they eat is the insects.

**The insects.**

Slow, dense, glittering at the mid-elevations where the spray hangs longest — clouds of a small soft-bodied fly that never seems to bite anyone, never seems to land, simply hangs in the green light like sediment suspended in still water. They are, on close inspection, mostly translucent, their interior anatomy visible as a faint red thread of gut. They breed in the moss-water, in the standing droplets that bead on every leaf and every cold rivet, and so their numbers track the humidity exactly — thickest at dawn, when the condensation is heaviest, thinning to almost nothing by the dry hour of early afternoon when the mist burns off. They are, in effect, a visible barometer. The people who live here read them the way sailors read cloud.

**The tenders.**

There are perhaps five of them, perhaps seven — the number is hard to pin because they are rarely in the same place twice, and the spire does not encourage headcounts. They live light: a hammock slung in a dry notch, a lantern, a coil of rope kept oiled against the wet, a set of hand tools wrapped in oilcloth against the rust that wants to claim everything in a season. You would mistake them, at a distance, for vegetation themselves — they wear deep green, mud-green, moss-green, not from any uniform but from laundry that has simply lost the argument with its surroundings over the years.

They do not speak much, not because they are unfriendly but because the canyon eats words below a certain volume and amplifies them strangely above it, so a normal conversational register simply doesn't survive the trip. Instead they have built, out of necessity, a small private language of the hands — a flat palm held at the turbine house for *running clean*, a fist for *trouble*, two fingers walked along an invisible pipe for *check the seam*. Newcomers learn this language before they learn anyone's name. It is taught at rope-length, across gaps, by people who may not get close enough to shake your hand for a week.

Their work is maintenance, not progress. Nobody here is trying to build the works larger. They patch, they clear silt from the intake, they replace a worn governor-flywheel bearing with another scavenged from the cracked-open pump-relay station at the base, robbing one dying machine to keep another alive a while longer. They have made an uneasy peace with the jungle's claim on the iron — they cut vine away from a catwalk's footing but leave it draped along the handrail, because pulling all of it free seems, somehow, like asking for an argument the rock would eventually win anyway. There's a working theory among them, never stated outright but evident in how they build, that the spire is lending them the works rather than hosting them — that the basalt is being patient with the iron the way it's patient with everything else, and that the wise thing is not to overstay the loan.

**What's in the tank.**

There is one more thing, spoken of even less than the rest, mentioned mostly as a joke that nobody quite laughs at. The header tank near the crown, where the spring-source first pools before the long fall down the penstock, is dark, deep, lined in a slick algae-black that never seems to clear no matter how often it's skimmed. Tenders who've climbed the ladder to check the float-valve say the water moves wrong sometimes — a slow turning current with no obvious source, as if something at the bottom were breathing very slowly, on its own cycle, indifferent to the tank's. No one has gone down to look. The float-valve has never failed. The water keeps coming when it's wanted, and that, on this spire, among people who have learned to ask very little of a place that is still actively deciding what shape it wants to be, is considered answer enough.
