# The People of Khulm-Atar

You notice the legs first, if you've come up from the lowlands. Calves like knotted rope, a forward lean built into the hip that makes flat ground look almost like a mistake to them—watch someone from the high tiers walk across a level courtyard and there's a hitch in it, a readiness for a slope that isn't there. Children here learn stairs before they learn words for them. By six they can run a switchback descent at a speed that stops visitors' hearts, bare feet finding purchase on schist worn glass-smooth by ten thousand other feet doing the same thing for ten thousand other childhoods.

The chest is the other tell. Lungs built generation on generation for thin air, ribcages a little broader than you'd expect on otherwise spare frames—people here are not large, food and warmth being what they are, but they are *deep*, built front to back rather than tall, the way a good lamp is built around the flame it has to keep alive. Outsiders arriving at altitude gasp and stagger for days. Khulm-Atari children gasp for about an hour, around age four, and then never again in their lives, as if the mountain only asks the question once.

Faces are weathered in a specific way—not the leather-and-wrinkle of desert sun, but a tightness around the eyes, a permanent slight squint against light that never gets diffused, and a windburn that sits high on the cheeks like a blush that never fully fades. Most have dark hair, straight, often kept short or bound tight against the cold rather than left to do anything decorative with it; decoration, when it happens, happens on cloth, not bodies—the dyed wool everyone wears in layers, indigo and a rusty madder-red and a yellow made from lichen scrape, colors chosen specifically because the gray-black stone offers so little of its own.

## Hands

If you wanted to know a Khulm-Atari's whole life story you would ask to see their hands, not their face. Stoneworkers' palms carry a permanent gray dust worked so deep into the skin's creases it survives washing, survives years, becomes essentially tattoo. Cisterers and ice-scrapers have a different mark—chapped, cracked at the knuckle in a way that never fully heals between one frost season and the next, because the work is morning work, cold work, done before the sun gets a chance to help. Weavers' fingers are nicked along the same three spots on each hand, an occupational scar so consistent you could identify the trade blind. Crane-tenders have a callus across the base of all four fingers from rope, distinct from the callus a goat-herder gets from the lead-line, which sits higher, near the first knuckle.

There is no embarrassment in this legibility. A person's hands are read the way you'd read a tier-marker on a building—not gossip, just information, the kind you'd want before asking a stranger for help or trade.

## Niches, Filled Completely

The narrowness-and-fullness you'd notice in the animal life holds just as true for the people. Khulm-Atar does not produce generalists. A child is watched in the first years for what they gravitate to—the kid who lingers at the ice-gutters, the one who can't stop touching wool, the one who already, at five, sits too still and too long watching the older masons work a fracture-plane—and from that watching a trade gets suggested, gently, by an aunt or a tier-elder, and is rarely refused, because refusing means asking the mountain to support one more idle mouth on terraces that can't extend themselves to cover idleness.

This produces people who are extraordinary within absurdly small domains. There are women, by their forties, who can tell from the sound of a single dropped pebble which of the eleven water-channels in their tier has begun to silt, and where. There are old men whose entire expertise is the angle of lintel stones relative to noon sun in the third month of the year, and who are consulted, with real solemnity, by builders three tiers away. Nobody finds this strange. The mountain doesn't waste a niche, and neither, by long habit, do they.

## Speech and the Shape of Silence

They are not a loud people, and the architecture has trained them further into quiet—you learn early that a raised voice in a stone canyon comes back to you from four directions, garbled, and that the polite volume is the one that reaches exactly the person you're speaking to and no one in the next courtyard. This has produced a way of talking that outsiders sometimes mistake for coldness: flat tones, short sentences, long pauses that aren't searching for words but simply unbothered by silence.

But watch a gathering at a communal oven, off the record of any visitor's expectation, and the warmth is there, just calibrated differently—it comes out as a kind of deadpan, dry humor delivered with no change in expression, jokes about the weather, about the obstinacy of goats, about the relative idiocies of tiers above and below (there is a long, good-natured tradition of inter-tier mockery, the upper tiers calling the lower ones soft for their thicker air, the lower ones calling the upper ones mad for their thin one). Affection is shown in maintenance, mostly—a daughter quietly re-mortaring her mother's threshold before winter, a husband walking an extra half-mile to check a particular cistern not because it's his but because his wife mentioned, once, months ago, that the lid seemed loose.

## Elders, Children, and the Inheritance of Unfinished Things

Age confers a specific and very literal authority here, because so much of what holds the city up was begun by someone now old or dead and finished by someone not yet born. An elder isn't valuable for wisdom in the abstract; they're valuable as a *repository of intention*—the only living person who remembers which exact stretch of half-built terrace wall their own grandfather meant to extend, and in which direction, and why. Losing an elder before that knowledge is passed on is treated with something close to architectural grief: a real, structural loss, not just a personal one.

Correspondingly, children are raised with an unusual, unsentimental awareness of being temporary custodians of long projects. It's not framed as burden—Khulm-Atari children don't seem more burdened than children anywhere—but it does produce a kind of patience you don't often see in the young. A nine-year-old here can watch a crane being slowly repaired over an entire season, contribute four afternoons of unglamorous rope-work to it, and feel a real, calm satisfaction at having moved a multi-generation project forward by some small fraction, without needing to see it completed. *The mountain finishes nothing in one life* is taught early, not as resignation, but as the basic arithmetic of being one of many hands on one very long task.

## What They Notice That You Don't

Live there a while and you start to see the small competencies that mark a native: the way a Khulm-Atari's eyes flick automatically to the upper crags at certain hours, checking raptor behavior the way a sailor checks cloud; the instinctive half-step adjustment everyone makes on a frost-slick stair without seeming to look down at all; the unconscious tally kept of exactly how many days since the last good rain, visible in how casually or carefully someone handles water in front of you.

They are, in total, a people shaped by the specific arithmetic of their stone—narrow niches, filled completely; long tasks, carried partially; quiet voices, in a place where sound travels too well to waste; deep lungs, low expectations of comfort, and an unhurried, unembittered relationship with the fact that nothing they personally build will ever, in their own lifetime, be finished. They do not seem to find this sad. They seem, if anything, mildly amused that anyone would expect otherwise.
