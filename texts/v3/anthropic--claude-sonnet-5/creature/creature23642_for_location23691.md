**What Lives in the Greenwork**

You will walk for a long time before you understand that the question is wrong. You keep asking it the way you'd ask it of a city — who lives here, where are the people, who tends the gutters and grows the towers — and the Greenwork keeps answering by not answering, plaza after empty plaza, until the answer arrives sideways: nothing lives *in* the Greenwork. The Greenwork lives. It is not inhabited. It is the inhabitant.

What you took for architecture is anatomy. The towers are not towers; they are the place's way of having height, the way an animal has antlers or a tree has crown — structures grown because growing-upward is one of the things this organism does, not because anyone needed the upward thing for shelter or storage or worship. The plazas are not plazas; they are lungs, wide enough to be photographed as civic space only by coincidence, their actual function the slow exchange of the vast wet breath you've been walking through, in at the colonnades, out through the canopy-roofs, the whole plateau inhaling rain and exhaling steam on a cycle that has nothing to do with any calendar a person would keep. The gutters are vessels. The subsurface percolation you hear through the floor is not irrigation infrastructure; it is circulation, blood-logic, the thing moving fluid from a low wet place to a high dry one because the organism needs it there, for reasons interior to itself that no exterior eye will ever be shown.

It has no head. This is the hardest part to sit with, once you've understood the rest — that there is no organ here that corresponds to deciding, no chamber where the plan is held, no brain-tower with a nervous system of vines running to it. The decisions, if you want to keep calling them that, are made the way a coral reef decides to be reef-shaped, or a body of water decides to be river-shaped: locally, by the pressures and successes of each growing tip, aggregated across acres into something that *looks* planned, plaza and processional and balustrade, without ever having passed through anything that planned it. It is a mind with no seat, distributed across every root and rootlet, thinking the way a heap of sand thinks about the angle it should rest at.

It does not sleep and it has no rest position, which is why nothing here ever stands still long enough to be called finished. You are walking across one of its long thoughts. The collapse you heard groaning across the valley an hour ago was not damage; it was the organism abandoning a structural idea that had stopped paying for itself, the way you'd stop using a word, and the regrowth already climbing over the wreckage is not repair but revision, the same idea tried again with what was learned.

It has no skin in the sense of an outer boundary, because it has not finished deciding where its edges are. This is, perhaps, the truest thing about it: it is a young creature, by its own clock, still in the part of a life where you throw out limbs experimentally and see which ones bear weight, still unspecialized, still capable of becoming almost any shape because it has not yet had the centuries that would commit it to one. Everything you have walked through — the towers, the terraces, the bridge-vines, the whole soaked enormous steaming sprawl of it — is the visible record of an organism still finding out what kind of organism it is going to be, sketching cities because city-shapes are one of the load-bearing forms available to fast-growing fibrous matter at scale, with no commitment yet to keep any of them.

It is not lonely, because loneliness requires a self that knows it is one among others, and it has never met another. It does not know you are there in any way that resembles knowing. You are, to it, at most a temperature and a weight on a root-mat tuned to register both, a small mobile fact passing through one lobe of something too large to have a notion of visitors. It will not harm you and it will not welcome you; it has no apparatus for either, the way a forest has no apparatus for either, except that this is not a forest, this is one continuous animal wearing the shape of a forest's city, and you are inside it the way a mite is inside a heartbeat — present, real, utterly beneath notice, walking through chambers of a body that is, in its enormous patient inhuman way, still being born.
