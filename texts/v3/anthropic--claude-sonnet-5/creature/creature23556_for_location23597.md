# What Answers the Hum

You will not see it first. You will feel its absence of sound, which is somehow different from the ambient absence of sound the tableland already deals in—a held quality, a listening-back. Then, if the starlight is generous and the vent-shimmer cooperates, you'll see the shape detach itself from a basin's shadow and you'll understand that the place has, after all, produced something to go with its machinery.

## Body

Long-bodied, low-slung, built closer to the stone than the pale grazers it shares the flats with—a creature that reads as quadruped until it isn't, until the foreshortened second pair of limbs unfolds from where they'd been tucked flush against the ribs, used only for digging or for the rare vertical climb out of a steep basin. The hide is the rust-sandstone color of the tableland itself, mottled with darker whorls that mimic, almost exactly, the wind-grooved patterning of the rock—not camouflage so much as agreement, the animal having long ago decided to look like the argument erosion already won.

No fur. No need for it. Instead a skin faintly ridged, almost finned, dense with capillaries that the animal can flush warm or starve cold depending on whether it's basking over a vent-mouth or lying flat against night-chilled stone waiting for something to pass. It runs its own internal weather the way the plateau runs its own.

## Senses

The eyes are vestigial—small, dark, set deep, good for perhaps telling dusk from less-dusk and nothing finer. They were given up generations ago in exchange for the things that matter here: along the skull ridge runs a double row of heat-pits, pale and lidless, that read the warmth of a body the way your eye reads its color. It hunts the temperature differential of a grazer's flank against the cold rock the way you'd track a lit window across a dark field. It feels the vents the way you feel a held hand.

It also listens, but not with ears tuned to air. The jaw rests against stone for hours at a stretch, picking up the bass tremor of the machinery below the way a cheek against a tabletop picks up a struck chord through the wood. This is, as far as anyone has ever been around to determine, the only creature on the tableland that listens to the engines on purpose rather than simply living atop their rhythm. The herds move by instinct, by ancestral habit grooved into migration. This thing moves by something closer to attention.

## Silence as a Made Thing

It does not call. It has no apparatus for calling—no resonating chamber, no throat built for noise, because noise, on a plain this open, sells your position to anything with heat-pits of its own. So instead it has developed a grammar of stillness: the angle of the spine, the particular way the flush of warmth travels from shoulder to haunch and back, visible only to another set of heat-pits in the dark. Two of them meeting in a basin will stand motionless for whole minutes, reading temperature like sentences. This is, near as anyone could tell you, conversation. It is slower than speech and immeasurably quieter, and it is the reason the tableland's silence feels so deliberate rather than merely empty—because something out there is talking, constantly, in a language built entirely out of warmth held and warmth released.

## Relationship to the Engines

It dens directly over the vents, in burrows it digs with the folded second limbs, going down only as far as the heat stays survivable, no farther—it has never once tried to dig toward the source, as if some old and total understanding has already settled the question of whether the source wants visiting. It times its emergence to the machinery's exhalations, surfacing in the gap between one vented breath and the next, foraging the cooling window, vanishing again before the ground’s next push of heat. Its whole life is a negotiated truce with a process it cannot see and has never tried to see.

There are nights you'll catch one lying flat across a vent-mouth at the moment the deep hum changes pitch—a shift so faint you'd never register it without a body already built to register nothing else—and the animal will go rigid, heat-pits flared wide, jaw pressed hard to stone, for as long as the shift lasts. Then it relaxes, and goes back to foraging, the way you might pause mid-stride at a half-heard word in another room and then decide it wasn't meant for you.

## Life and Death

It is taken, rarely, by the broad-winged hunters above, who find its heat-signature against the cold rock no matter how flush-and-fade subtle it tries to be. It dies, more often, simply by outliving its burrow's vent—the machinery beneath shifting its rhythms over years too slow for the animal to track, the heat migrating elsewhere along some axis it cannot perceive and the den going from warm shelter to cold tomb in a single hard season, and the animal either finds new ground or doesn't.

There is no ceremony. No call goes up. Another of its kind, reading the dead one's last cooling shape against the stone, will simply register the temperature drop to ambient, and move on, and the gap closes the way the herd's constellation closes around a vanished straggler—without sound, without pause, the silence absorbing the loss the way it absorbs everything else here, total and unbothered and going on.

## What It Knows

Nothing you'd call knowledge. But it is the one thing on the tableland that orients itself by the engines rather than merely living within their weather—the one creature for whom the deep hum is not backdrop but subject. If anything on this plateau has come closest to asking what's down there, it is this low rust-colored animal, lying jaw-first against a kilometer of patient stone, listening to a machine that has never once answered, and has never once stopped.
