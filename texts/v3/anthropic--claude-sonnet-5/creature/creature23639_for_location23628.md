## The Terrace-Folk

They are built for stairs, first of all. Generations of switchback climbing have given them a particular gait — short-strided, flat-footed, weight carried low — and calves and lungs disproportionate to their height. Outsiders coming up from the rail-head, even fit ones, find themselves winded by the fourth terrace; a Hallow Mere native will pass them at a steady unhurried climb, carrying two buckets on a yoke, breathing through the nose, not even flushed. Height itself is unremarkable here, but build runs compact and dense — there's no advantage to verticality in a world of low eaves and interlocking roof-lines, and a great deal of advantage to being able to turn sideways in a culvert-tunnel without breaking stride.

Their skin carries the place's two climates at once: pale and faintly damp-soft from the chest-high fog and the long hours in glasshouse shade, but weathered hard at the hands, throat, and forearms from lye-vats, furnace-doors, and wet rope. Hands especially mark a person's terrace — tannery hands stained tea-brown to the wrist, dye-shed hands blue or rust-orange depending on the mordant, furnace hands scarred pink-white in patches where spark and slag have had their say over a working life. A person's trade is legible at a glance, the way a tree-ring is legible, and nobody bothers asking what terrace you work because they can simply look.

Clothing is layered, oiled, and endlessly mended — wool under waxed canvas under a final shedding-layer of greenhouse-fiber cloth that sheds condensation the way a leaf does, beaded rather than soaked. Color is muted by necessity (dye is product, not decoration, and you don't wear what you're meant to sell) except for small fierce flashes — a woven cord, a dyed cuff-edge — that function as terrace-badges, kin-marks, guild-marks, read instantly by anyone local and invisible to anyone not. Boots are resoled rather than replaced, sometimes four and five times, the upper leather outlasting generations of sole.

## Voice and Manner

They speak as the fog asks them to: low, economical, pitched to carry six feet and no further. Visitors mistake this for coldness or secrecy. It is neither — it's acoustic courtesy, the same instinct that keeps a person from slamming a door in a sickroom. Whole transactions happen in murmur and gesture: a hand-flat meaning *hold*, two fingers tapped on a railing meaning *clear*, a particular not-quite-nod that means *I see the wall is going on the ninth terrace too, yes, don't ask me when it'll be fixed.* Children are taught the hand-vocabulary before they're taught to shout, because shouting on a stair-tunnel just brings the foreman, and the foreman is busy.

Humor runs dry and structural — jokes about the cable-line, about which terrace flooded this month, about the oxen's opinions of the weather — delivered flat, often mid-task, often not smiled at by anyone, including the teller, until a beat later when someone snorts despite themselves. Grief and celebration both tend toward the same hush; a wedding procession up through the terraces is recognizable mainly by the number of people quietly following one path at once, not by noise.

## Hands That Do Two Jobs

Because nothing on the terraces is single-purpose, no person is either. The woman tending the vine-trellis on the foundry's south chimney-face is also, by virtue of standing exactly there twice a day, the one who notices when the flue-draw changes pitch and reports it before the boiler crew does. The boy minding the cable-oxen knows the fodder-greenhouse rotation better than the greenhouse-keeper, because he's the one hauling cut feed up six flights every dawn and watching what's ready before the official harvest log says so. Trades blur at the edges on purpose; the works can't afford specialists who only know one terrace, not when a wall can go down on the ninth and stay down for six months and the only people who can reroute the water-channel around it are whoever happens to understand both stonework and hydraulics, regardless of what their guild-mark says.

This produces a particular cast of mind: practical, lateral, faintly impatient with anyone who insists a job is "not mine." Status accrues not to those who command the most narrow expertise but to those who can be sent anywhere on the terraces and make something work with what's already lying around — a reputation built terrace by terrace, repair by improvised repair, until certain names get invoked the way a tool gets invoked: *get her, she fixed the south reservoir gate with nothing but a cart-axle and a prayer.*

## Sleep, Eaten in Pieces

Because the works never stops — boilers don't bank themselves at night, water doesn't wait for daylight to find the weak mortar joint — sleep here is shift-broken and communal. Worker housing is stacked dense along the mid-terraces, rooms barely wider than the cot in them, but doors are left unbolted within a household-block by long custom, because someone is always coming off a night repair crew at three in the morning soaked through and needing the nearest dry floor, and the etiquette of the place simply assumes you'll find a floor and no one will mind. Whole generations have grown up understanding sleep as a resource shared rather than owned, the way the water is shared, the way the greenhouse heat is shared up the chimney-vines. People nap in five-minute increments standing against warm flue-walls. It looks, to a stranger, like exhaustion. To the terrace-folk it's simply Tuesday.

## What the Fog Has Taught Them

They've developed an unhurried relationship with disorder that outsiders mistake for fatalism but is closer to a kind of literacy. A boiler failure sending steam up four terraces and erasing a street in white is not, to them, a crisis-event; it is Tuesday's weather, metabolized the way the place metabolizes everything else — noted, worked around, eventually repaired, rarely discussed afterward except as a landmark in time (*before the ninth-terrace wall went, after the steam-out*). They don't expect finished. They've never seen finished and have stopped imagining it as a state worth wanting. What they want, instead, and tend with real devotion, is *held* — the wall held another season, the reservoir held through the thaw, the greenhouse glass held against last week's hail. Permanence isn't on offer here and they know it bone-deep; competence in the face of that, quiet and constant and never quite caught up, is the only inheritance the terraces actually pass down.
