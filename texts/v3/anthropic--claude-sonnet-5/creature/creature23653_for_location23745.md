They are called, in the closest words a stranger has ever found, the Standing — and the name is less a name than a description, the kind given to something whose nature simply is its only proper noun.

You will not catch one moving. That is the first thing to understand. Their motion belongs to the register of root-growth and stem-thickening, the register the eye is built to discard as "nothing happening." A Standing individual, full-grown, resembles less an animal than a kept thing: a trunk-thick lower body rooted ankle-deep in the grit, bark-skinned, ringed faintly the way a stem rings, tapering upward into a torso braided from many thinner stem-limbs that have learned, over some unmeasured span of growing seasons, to separate into hands. The hands are the youngest part of them, the most finished, the only part that still bears the look of intention rather than accretion — four or five fibrous digits, blunt, warm to the touch, patient enough to press a wall flat over the course of a decade and never once consider it slow.

Their faces, where a face would be expected, are closer to a fruit's hollow than an animal's — a soft concavity ringed with short pale filaments that don't see so much as feel the air's faint chemistries, the moisture-breath of the basin, the heat-signature of a grazer's flank against a stem two courtyards over. Sight, as you would use the word, is not how the Standing know their terrace. They know it through contact: foot-root to ground-root, stem-wall to stem-wall, the whole plateau wired with a slow continuous pressure-sense, so that a hand laid on a tower in one quarter can feel, faintly, the lean of a stem-house being thickened three shelves away. This is why nothing here startles. This is why the grazers don't flinch from you — they were never strangers to the network; they are read, distantly, the way you might register a held breath in another room.

The barrel-bodied grazers are not separate from the Standing's purpose, though they are genuinely their own creatures, born and dying on their own slow terms. They were chosen, long before any memory the Standing retain as story, for their habit of cropping exactly the growth that would otherwise crowd a doorway shut, and they have been kept close so long that their wandering has become indistinguishable from tending. They do not know they garden. The Standing do not correct them. It works, and working is its own justification here.

The birds along the roof-ridges are stranger, and closer kin than they look. They do not migrate, do not call, because they were never quite separated from the colony's listening — they are the part of the Standing's sense that reaches upward, into air the root-network cannot touch. Each row is posted the way an eye is posted in a socket: necessary, motionless except when necessity moves it, packed shoulder to shoulder because a single watcher would be a blind spot and a hundred are a horizon. What looks like display is vigilance. What looks like silence is a kind of conversation conducted entirely beneath the threshold you can hear.

At the terrace's far, hazier edge, the something larger that sways the high leaf-tiers without ever quite showing itself is the oldest Standing on the shelf — old enough that motion has mostly left it, old enough that its lower body has gone fully into root and its upper body has thickened into something closer to a husk-tower than a person, slowly being built into and around by the very dwellings it once, centuries of seasons ago, pressed into shape with younger hands. The Standing do not die the way you would recognize dying. They root deeper, slow further, and eventually become load-bearing — a wall, a tower's spine, a shelf's deepest foundation — and the settlement simply continues growing from where they stopped being able to walk. This is why no surface bears a tool-mark. There were never tools. There were only hands, and then walls that used to be hands, doing the same patient work at a pace too long for the word "labor" to survive contact with it.

They do not build for shelter from weather, since there is none worth sheltering from. They build, as near as the gesture can be translated, the way a stand of stems closes a gap — because an unfilled space on a young plateau is simply an instruction not yet carried out, and they intend, given the centuries the basalt still owes them, to carry out all of it.

If you stay until the light goes old-paper amber at its lowest angle, you may feel, very faintly, a hand's worth of pressure pass through the ground beneath your own feet — not a threat, not even quite an acknowledgment. Just the network, taking note that something new has stood, for a while, among the standing.
