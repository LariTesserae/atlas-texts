What lives here does not live *in* the canopy so much as *of* it, the way the engines are no longer separate from the wood that's grown through them. You will not see them first. You will hear them — or rather, you will hear the canopy do something slightly different than wind and animal-roar and structural creak, a thread of order pulled through the noise-floor, and only later understand that the thread was deliberate.

They are built low and long, built for the understructure rather than the open sky — four-limbed in the manner of something that climbs more than it walks, with a fifth and sixth limb reduced to short, strong hooks along the underside of a narrow torso, for hanging from fiber-lattice while both true hands are occupied. The skin is not skin in any sense you'd recognize from a wet world — a tight, faintly translucent integument, sap-fed rather than blood-fed, the same clear oily fluid that runs the trunks running just beneath it, so that in the strobing dapple-light they seem to flicker between solid and something closer to wet glass. Where blood would flush a wound, theirs simply seals, resin-quick, leaving raised pale scar-ridges that the old ones wear in dense maps across their forearms and throats — a record, if anyone were reading it, of every fall, every storm-passage, every too-close pass to an engine's heat-vent.

Their eyes are small and mostly useless and they have made peace with that. Light here is no kind of language — it changes meaning every few seconds as the upper canopy churns, gold to black to gold — so they don't navigate by it. What they have instead, in place of sight, is a long ridged organ running the length of the spine and up through a crested, hollow-boned skull, exposed slightly at the surface in raised dark vanes — not unlike the wind-cane architecture overhead, and not by coincidence. They feel the canopy's chord-structures the way you'd feel a hand on your back. Pressure-storms that flatten outsiders against the decking and reduce all speech to nothing are, to them, *information* — direction, distance, the rough size and health of distant trunk-masses, the location of others of their kind a kilometer off, all carried on a roar that would otherwise mean nothing but danger.

They do not build, because there is nothing here that needs building. What they do is *tend* — though even that word may overstate their understanding of what they tend. The engines fused into the deep boles are older than any oral history they carry, and the relationship has long since stopped being one of operator and machine and become something closer to symbiosis grown stupid with time: they clear vine-growth from certain grilles because the grilles run warmer and cleaner when cleared, and warmer grilles mean a good place to lay a clutch through the cold thin nights at altitude; they pack mineral-rich rot-mat into certain intake housings because, generations back, someone learned that doing so quiets a particular engine's worst register, the one that otherwise vibrates wrong in the breeding season and drives the clutch-young to chew through their own shells too early. None of them could tell you what the engines are *for*. They would likely find the question strange. The engines are not for anything. The engines are terrain, the way the cliffs of dead leaning trunks are terrain, and you tend terrain because terrain that is tended is terrain that doesn't kill you.

They live sparse and far-flung, the way the storms and engines and rookeries are sparse and far-flung — small kin-clusters, rarely more than a dozen, holding to a given fold of understructure for generations until a great trunk's slow century of dying finally takes their decking down, at which point they migrate, unhurried, toward the next hollow bole that sounds, through the spine-ridge, like home. They eat the sap directly, tapping bored holes at the fiber-junctions with a hooked single tooth kept for the purpose, and the flying things — the chitin-and-feather thousands that erupt screaming when the storms roll through — are both their great predator-rival and their great food source, depending on the season and which of the two is currently weaker.

They do not seem to suffer the noise the way you would. It is reasonable to suspect they could not survive its absence — that a silence, a true one, would be the only environment here actually hostile to them. When the full-volume moment comes, the storm-roar and animal-roar and structural-roar fusing into the single continental sound the place insists on, you will see them not flinch but still — limbs locked into the lattice, spine-ridge flared open to its fullest, reading the chord the way you might close your eyes to listen better to music, except for them this is the only kind of seeing that has ever told them anything true.
