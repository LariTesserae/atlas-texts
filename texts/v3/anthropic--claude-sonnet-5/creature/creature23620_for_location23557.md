# The Folk of Lowmere

You will smell them before you reason out where they are — peat-smoke and wet wool and something sweeter underneath, crushed reed-pith, maybe, or the waxy smell of the salve they work into their hands against the damp. They keep to the walkways out of long habit, not caution; their feet know the slick boards the way your tongue knows the inside of your own mouth, without looking, without thought.

## Build and Bearing

They run small and broad — not stunted, but compact, low-slung, built for moving under low thatch-eaves and along narrow plank-ways without bruising themselves on the world. Generations of stooping have given the older ones a permanent slight forward set to the shoulders, not quite a hunch, more like the curve of a hook waiting to be hung from. Hands are wide and thick-knuckled, perpetually a little swollen from the wet, the skin gone soft and pale and faintly puckered, like a thing too long in a bath, except where decades of rope-work and willow-stripping have built up a yellow-hard callus across the palm pads.

Their skin, in general, carries the basin's colors. A greenish cast in the hollows — not sickness, just the long result of living inside a chlorophyll haze, light filtered through leaf for an entire lifetime. Damp-flush in the cheeks. Networks of fine broken veins from the engine-heat worked at close range for years. The old ones go mottled at the hands and forearms, brown and green and rust-orange in patches, until you cannot be entirely sure where the liver-spots end and the iron-stains from the cistern-tanks begin.

## Dress

Wool and oiled canvas, always, regardless of season — the basin has no season, only degrees of wet. Garments are layered loose, meant to be shed or added through a working day that swings between peat-engine heat and standing-water chill within the same hour. Everything is the color the basin makes things: moss-drab, rust-brown, the deep green-black of old willow-bark, the dull orange of algae-rust. Boots are the one item kept fanatically maintained — soled thick, greased weekly, because the walkways punish bad footwear and the bog beneath punishes bad boots worse. Older workers wear a kind of woven reed over-mantle, shrugged on for the worst drips, that from a few feet away looks like they've simply grown a second, shaggier roofline of their own.

## Hands and Trades

Everyone here has a trade written into their body. The loom-workers have a permanent slight crook to two fingers from the shuttle-catch. The peat-cutters and stokers have forearms ropy and dark, eyebrows gone thin and singed-looking from a life spent near the stacks. The cistern-tenders, who spend their days wrist-deep in the filter-tanks, have hands stained a faint permanent green-grey no amount of washing lifts, and a particular patient squint from years of peering down into water trying to judge its clarity by lamplight. The wheelwrights and smiths are the loudest and largest of the lot, the only ones who seem to relish the noise rather than simply live inside it, and they're easy to find — they're the ones not flinching at the waterwheel's clack.

Children, when you see them, are barefoot on the warm walkways even when the adults aren't, faster and more sure-footed than anyone, treating the slick boards and the standing black mirror-pools as their own private country. They vanish into the reed-thatch gaps between buildings the way swamp-fauna vanishes into the fringe-trees, and reappear somewhere else entirely, always a little muddy, never having fallen in.

## Temperament

They are not unfriendly, but they are unhurried in a way that can read as remove — the basin's patience seems to have worked itself into the people the same way the damp has worked itself into the brick. Questions get answered eventually, after a pause that isn't suspicion so much as a different relationship with time. They measure things the place's own way: not by the hour, but by the wheel's turn, the stack's smoke-color, how far the evening mist has crept up the third channel. A request that seems urgent to an outsider will be received with mild, genuine puzzlement — *urgent* is not a category that has much purchase here, where the rope-walk and the cistern-filter and the pollard-cut have all been running on their own slow correct schedule since before anyone living can remember a day it wasn't so.

They are proud of the place without being especially demonstrative about it — proud the way you're proud of a joint that still bears weight after forty years. They speak of the works in the proprietary plural — *our wheel, our stacks, our wet season* — and regard the works-house's three chimneys with something close to filial feeling, scolding their scorch-blackened brick the way you'd scold a relative for not dressing warm enough.

## What They Don't Talk About

There is, beneath the unhurried warmth, a certain wariness of the dry world above the basin's rim — not hostility, just a settled, generational sense that things up there move too fast, dry out too easily, don't *last* the way things here last. Newcomers who try to hurry the work, or speak of replacing the willow-walls with something more modern, get the same patient, slightly pitying look the basin itself seems to give the rain: *that's not really necessary, but you go ahead and see.*

And there's an old habit, never quite explained to outsiders, of leaving a cup of the cistern-water out at the marsh-fringe each evening, at the boundary where the swamp-oak starts and the worked ground ends — for the birds that won't come in, they'll tell you, if you ask. Though no one has ever actually seen a bird drink from it, and the cup is always empty by morning regardless.
