The ones who live here are called, in their own tongue, something that translates badly as *the Sleeve-Born*, or sometimes *those-who-stayed-when-it-was-easier-to-leave* — a name with the shape of an old joke worn into something closer to an oath.

They are small-statured, not through any blight but through generations of crouching: doorways grown low, ceilings shaped by moss that climbs only so high before it needs trimming, walkways meant to be crossed bent-kneed and quiet-footed so as not to startle what nests below. A tall person would learn to fold here within a season, or leave. Their skin carries the same low sheen as everything else in the hollow — not sickness, but the simple fact of never being fully dry, of sleeping in air that beads on every surface. Joints ache early. Old women here have wrists swollen with a damp-ache by thirty winters, and they do not call it illness, they call it *paying rent*.

Their hands are their craft and their wealth. Bone needles worn smooth at the eye from decades of thread, digging sticks polished black with handling, fingers permanently faint-stained green from moss-work — the coaxing of architecture out of growth, a skill passed mother to child as patiently as language, because it *is* a kind of language: which strain of sphagnum will thicken into wall, which liverwort will arch and hold, which fungal thread to introduce at the seams so the mortar knits instead of rotting. They do not call this farming or building. They call it *asking.*

They are quiet because the place demands it and because, after enough generations, quiet becomes simply how a person is built. Children here are taught to laugh in exhales rather than sound, taught the difference between the insect-static that means nothing and the half-second gap in it that means something is moving where it shouldn't. A Sleeve-Born child knows the call-patterns of a dozen unseen birds before they know their numbers past twenty. They listen with their whole bodies, a held-stillness you'd mistake for shyness or suspicion, but which is really just fluency in a language made of breathing in the dark.

They feed the thresholds — handfuls of grain-mash, fish-skin, fat-rendered scraps — not as worship exactly, though something reverent has crept into the gesture over the short years this place has existed. It started, the elders will tell you if you sit long enough at a lamp-window, as bribery. Plain and desperate: *don't eat the granary, eat this instead.* But bribery repeated enough times, generation after short generation, calcifies into rite. Now there are particular words said at the moss-pod thresholds, particular hours, particular hands that are allowed to do the feeding — usually the old, whose joints ache too much for moss-work but whose hands are steady enough for this. They do not call the voles or the newts or the unseen canopy-birds gods. But they do not call them *not*-gods either. The distinction has not yet been needed.

There is a wariness in them around outsiders that isn't hostility so much as arithmetic — they know, with the clear unsentimental knowledge of people living at the absolute edge of what their ecology can bear, that one wrong addition to this hollow could collapse the whole fragile bargain. A new mouth. A disturbed nest. A granary opened to the wrong appetite. You will be watched closely, fed sparingly, asked to keep your voice low and your steps light, and you will be trusted only as far as you demonstrate you understand: this is not abundance. This is scarcity, dressed thick and green and breathing, balanced so finely that nothing here has had time to grow old.

They themselves have not had time to grow old, as a people. Their oldest stories are about the founding — about arrival, about the first failed moss-house at the hollow's collapsed edge, about whoever it was that first noticed the water pooling here never freezing and said *here, this one, we stay here.* They tell these stories the way other peoples tell creation myths, except theirs happened within living memory of someone's grandmother, and so they tell them with a strange mix of reverence and embarrassment, the way you'd tell a story about your own mistakes.

What they are, in the end, is *new* — new enough that their rules are still being written into the moss itself, new enough that the half-collapsed houses at the hollow's rim are not ruins but rough drafts, new enough that none of them, watching the green climb slow and patient into the doorframes they cut last season, can yet say with certainty whether what they are building is a village or a kind of careful, ongoing, mutual surrender to everything that already lived here first.
