## What Tends the Reach

You are wrong, eventually, to think nothing lives here. It only lives wrong — inverted, recessed, keeping to the spaces between the spaces a person would look.

Call them Tenders, because no one ever named them properly and the word fits what little is known. They are small — no larger than a forearm, segmented, the color of the wet basalt they move through, which means you can stand a meter from one and not see it until it moves, and even then the moving looks like a trick of the steam. They are blind in the absolute sense: no eyes, not even the memory of eyes, just a smooth continuous hide over where a face would otherwise organize itself. They don't need sight. They have grown, over however many generations a thing like this requires, an intimacy with pipe and valve and gauge so total it amounts to a second nervous system laid over the city's own — they feel a pressure drop in a line three towers away the way you'd feel a hair touch your arm.

They live inside the works, not beside them. Wall cavities, the dead space beneath floor grates, the long dark intervals of pipe-bridge interior where the condensation collects — these are the Tenders' country, and they almost never surface into the open air you walk through. When they do, it's only ever the seam-work: a valve needing a hand at the exact moment of failure, a gasket replaced before it has finished cracking, a lens behind a window wiped clear of a film that hasn't fully formed yet. They anticipate rather than repair. This is why nothing in Anhalt's Reach is ever found broken — the Tenders arrive slightly before the breaking, as if the maintenance and the decay are racing each other on a track too fine for a human eye to read, and the maintenance, by some old margin, keeps winning.

No one has ever caught one still. No one has ever caught one working, either — only the consequence: a warm floor where dust should be, a console humming through a window with no one behind the glass. The "pale eyeless thing" travelers report between the cooling towers is very likely a Tender caught crossing open ground, doing something rare and probably urgent, low and fast and gone before the eye finishes registering it as a shape rather than a flicker.

What they eat, if they eat — what they are, if "are" still applies after however many centuries of this — is unrecorded. The going theory among the rare people who've stayed long enough to develop theories is that they were never separate from the works at all; that somewhere far back, in whatever century built the first tower, the maintenance crews were merged into the system they maintained, by choice or absorption or simple slow attrition, until the line between worker and machine wore as thin and smooth as the gravel road. The Tenders, in this telling, are not a population living *in* the city. They are the city's hands, gone feral and intimate at once — still loyal to the job, having long since forgotten, or never needed to know, who the job was originally for.

Above them, barely, are the steam-moths — the only thing in the Reach that anyone would call wildlife without flinching. Filamentous, the size of a thumbnail, the color of nothing in particular, they gather at vent-mouths and pipe-joints where the warm exhalation is steadiest, riding the thermals in slow drifting columns like ash that's decided not to fall. They are likely not moths in any real sense — no wings to speak of, more a fringe of cilia that catches updraft — and likely not alive in any sense that would survive close examination, but they move with intention, clustering thicker around vents that are about to vent harder, thinning before a pressure drop, as if they read the same anticipatory signal the Tenders read, only from outside the walls instead of within them. Perhaps they are what the system sheds. Perhaps they are what the Tenders look like before they finish becoming Tenders, or after they finish unbecoming them. No one has dissected one. The attempt, by the one traveler known to have tried, reportedly yielded nothing — the moth simply stopped existing between the fingers, the way a held breath stops existing once released.

And beneath both — beneath the towers, the rails that curve before they arrive, the steam that shouldn't rise this far from a fault line that shouldn't be this hot — is the thing the theory gestures at and never quite names. Not a population. Not even, probably, a creature in the sense of having edges. Something old enough that "alive" and "running" stopped being different categories for it long ago, breathing slow and vast somewhere under the plateau's whole weight, and feeding — without malice, without even noticing, the way a body doesn't notice the nail it's keeping faintly warm — this entire reach, these towers, these rails, the Tenders, the moths, the steam, the heat, the very stubbornness of the maintenance, as one long unconscious exhalation that has been going on, comfortably, for longer than the concept of a city has had any business surviving.

It has not been disturbed. The roads make sure of that. They curve, every time, well before they arrive.
