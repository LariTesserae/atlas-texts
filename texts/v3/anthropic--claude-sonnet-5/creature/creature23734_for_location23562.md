## The Tenders

You will see them before you understand that you have seen them, because they do not move the way the rest of the chamber moves. Everything else here obeys the slow gyre of the water, the systolic pulse of the walls, the held-and-collapsing logic of a fluid keeping time half a beat late. The Tenders ignore all of it. They travel the dry geometry—those straight threads of light laid invisibly across the wet room—and where they pass, the water does not part so much as *decline to be relevant*.

They are tall in the way a held breath is tall: an elongation rather than a height, three meters of vertical incident with almost no width to match. The body is a column of the same translucent sheath-material that wraps the conduit-stalks, and for a long disoriented moment you may mistake one for a stalk that has decided to walk. It hasn't decided. It has simply never agreed to be rooted in the first place. Light beads travel up and down inside it exactly as they do in the vegetation, and you will eventually understand that the stalks are not their crop, or their architecture, or their cattle. The stalks are their *kin*—a sessile caste of the same lineage, settled into the floor at some point in a life cycle you will never be shown the rest of, doing the slow patient work of running current through the chamber's information, while the mobile caste moves above them doing something else.

What the mobile ones do is tend. Hence the name you'll reach for, lacking their own. They reorient platforms by degrees over the course of an hour with a touch that doesn't touch—a hand, if it's a hand, held near a strut, and the strut deciding, the way the water decides to hold its shape a half-second too long. They visit the wall-machines at the forty-second pulse and lay themselves flat against the basalt during the systole, as if checking a pulse against their own, as if the breathing of the room and the breathing of the Tender are meant to be compared, reconciled, kept in phase. When the comparison fails—and you will feel it fail, a stutter in the chord the stalks ring out—a Tender will go very still, every light-bead in its body slowing to a crawl, for as long as it takes to think whatever passes for a thought in something built less like a brain and more like a switchboard.

They do not have faces in the sense you'd recognize, but they have a *forward*, marked by a denser cluster of light-traffic near the top of the column, and when one turns its forward toward you, you will feel measured rather than seen—your temperature, your displacement, your rate of consuming the water's good order, all logged somewhere in that traffic and forgotten or kept according to rules you won't be told.

They are recent. This matters more than it should. Their sheaths show no scarring, no patched seam, no worn translucency gone milky with age, in a chamber where nothing has had time to erode. You begin to suspect that "recent" is not a phase of their existence but its entirety—that they are made fresh, sharp-edged, the way the basalt throat was made fresh, by some process upstream of this room that you will never see, and that the disagreement visible in the architecture—platforms that don't share a logic, doorframes that lead nowhere, stairs holding angles water shouldn't permit—is the disagreement of successive shipments of Tenders, each batch solving the chamber's problems its own way, none of them staying long enough, or being similar enough, to defer to the last.

There is no aggression in them, and no welcome either. You are, to a Tender, a piece of unscheduled motion in a room that runs on schedule—not quite an intrusion, more an unaccounted variable, the kind that gets a slow pass of attention and then is filed. They will not follow you. They will not lead you. If you stay still long enough near one of the conduit-stalks, you may see a Tender pause beside its rooted kin, forward-cluster dimming to match the stalk's slower pulse, in what might be conversation, might be feeding, might be grief, might be none of those words doing any useful work at all—

—and then it will straighten, and move off along its dry invisible line, into the packed fullness, toward the next platform that needs to be told, gently, without words, what angle to hold against an ocean that was built, like everything else down here, to be lived in rather than crossed.
