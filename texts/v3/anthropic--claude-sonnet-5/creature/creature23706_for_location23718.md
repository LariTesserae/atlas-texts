# The People of the Reach

They are built the way the trees are built — bent, but not broken, and stronger for the bending. You can tell a Reach-born person from a flatlander by the legs first: thick through the calf, slightly bowed, toes splayed wide from a lifetime of bare feet finding purchase on uneven stone. Children learn the lean of the spire before they learn to talk, the way other children learn to nurse — it's not taught, it's absorbed, a correction made constantly and invisibly by the inner ear until the body simply knows which way is *down* even when the ground insists otherwise. Flatlanders who climb up for trade stagger for days. The Reach-born find this hilarious and try not to show it.

**Hands matter more than faces here.** A person's worth and history are read in calluses, in the particular roughness of palms that have spent decades reweaving vine-railings, in fingers permanently stained from beetle-oil or clay-work. Old age is not hidden but worn forward — the elderly are the ones whose hands know the most, and they are deferred to constantly, consulted on everything from cistern repair to naming disputes, because they alone remember which retaining wall has begun to creep, which terrace edge took three generations to widen a handspan, which root-and-wall systems are load-bearing and which only look it. Memory, here, is structural. To forget which wall your great-grandmother laid is to risk pulling the wrong stone.

**They speak loudly, always**, even in what they consider private conversation, because the spire has trained their voices the way it has trained their legs. A Reach-born person dropped into a quiet flatland room will seem to be shouting. They aren't. They've simply never needed to learn quiet — sound here travels up and down the open shaft regardless of intention, so privacy was abandoned generations ago as a lost cause, and something else grew in its place: an elaborate, almost ceremonial *politeness of overhearing*. You do not act on what you hear from another terrace unless it was meant for you. You pretend not to have heard the argument on terrace nine, the grief on terrace fourteen, even as you adjust your own plans around it. Discretion here isn't silence — there is no silence — it's a kind of practiced deafness, a social skill as essential as climbing.

**Their eyes are adapted to the bronze dimness**, slow to startle, quick to catch small movement — the flicker of a scavenger-bird sizing up a meal, a child's foot finding a ledge in failing light. They squint at open flat-country sun and call it *raw light*, something faintly indecent about it, too much, too even, nothing to filter it through. They prefer their light slanted, gold, broken by leaves and stone. Lantern-lighting at dusk is not chore but ritual, done by the youngest old-enough child on each terrace, a small rite of passage repeated every evening of every life — the first lantern you light alone is remembered the way other cultures remember first words.

**Status moves sideways and downward, not up.** There is no single ruler of the spire — the terraces are too separate, too sound-confused, too proud of their own small distinctness for that. Authority belongs to water: the cistern-wardens of each terrace, almost always elder women, who track the careful arithmetic of what is gathered and what is owed below. Because the system drinks high and gives low, the upper terraces depend entirely on the goodwill and competence of the people above them, and the highest terraces — the ones closest to the unclimbed faces where the cough-call sounds at night — carry a strange dual status: most exposed, most vulnerable, but also most trusted, since everyone below survives on what they choose to pass down. A cistern-warden's word, on matters of water, overrules anyone's, even visiting flatland traders who don't understand why nine hundred people on a needle of rock take a clay jar's worth of dew so seriously.

**They are extraordinary climbers without ever calling it that.** There's no word in their language, as far as outsiders can tell, that separates *climbing* from *walking* — a path that happens to run vertical for thirty feet is still just a path. Children race the ladder-chutes the way flatland children race down streets. Rock-hyrax-things are pets, rivals, and occasional protein depending on the season and the household; a child who can outclimb one on the vine-railings by age eight is considered simply competent, nothing remarkable.

**They are patient in a way that looks, from outside, almost like fatalism, and is not.** Nothing is finished here — not a wall, not a terrace, not a life's work — and this has settled into temperament. A Reach-born person mid-task, interrupted, does not seem to mind; the task was never going to be finished by them anyway, only continued, and someone will continue it after them too. Grief is real and deeply felt, but it is shaped by this same logic: the dead are not gone so much as folded into the structure, their walls still standing, their cisterns still gathering, their names attached permanently to the stretch of terrace they widened or the gutter-channel they kept clear. Ancestors are not worshipped exactly, but they are *present*, constantly, underfoot and overhead, in every stone that is still doing its job.

**They fear, mildly and permanently, the upper dark.** No one builds above the last terrace; no one goes there past dusk; the cough-call from the unclimbed faces is treated the way flatland people treat weather — not a monster exactly, not a god, just a fact of the place you arrange your life around. Mothers invoke it gently to keep children from climbing too high too fast, not with terror but with the same tone they'd use to say *the wind is sharp today, wear something*. It has lived above them longer than anyone's memory, the way the spire itself has, and there's a kind of family resemblance assumed between the two: the unclimbed dark and the unfallen stone, both older than naming, both simply *there*, both something you build your whole life downward and around rather than against.

And running under all of it, in every story told to every child before they can walk the terrace-paths alone: the conviction, never argued because never doubted, that they are not separate from the spire so much as its current inhabitants — its newest growth, no more permanent than the soil filling its cracks, no less wanted. The spire drinks the sky and bleeds it back through them. They have simply arranged themselves, generation on generation, to be standing in the path of that long slow exhale, palms cupped, grateful, in no hurry at all.
