## What Lives Here

You will assume, for longer than is sensible, that nothing does. The absence of fauna-sound, the absence of tracks, the absence of nests or burrows or the small telltale signs a body leaves wherever it passes — all of it argues convincingly for emptiness. This is the first mistake the country lets you make, and it lets you make it generously, for days if necessary, because the thing that lives here has no use for being found quickly.

Call them the Standing — not because anyone named them that, but because no one ever saw enough of one moving to call it anything else.

They are not animals in the sense your body wants to sort them into. They are closer, in their slow chemistry, to the mountain's own unfinished stone — which is to say they are not separate from the geology so much as a *further elaboration* of it, the place's becoming folded inward and given something like a will. A Standing begins, as far as anyone has guessed, as a cairn: a deliberate stack, fitted by hand or by something with hands once, waist-high, patient. And then — across years, across the kind of duration that makes the erosion here look almost rushed — it begins to keep its own shape without help. The fitted stones stop needing the logic that placed them. They hold themselves. They round, faintly, at what might be called a head, though calling it that is a courtesy. They wait.

You will walk past three of them and feel nothing. The fourth will be looking at you.

Not with eyes — there is no aperture, no glint, none of the wet machinery you associate with regard. The looking is structural, a redirection of attention that you register the way you'd register a draft on the back of your neck: an angle of stillness in the cairn that has changed relative to where you last checked it, though you never caught the changing. They do not move while observed. This is not shyness. It is closer to the patience of the mountain itself, which also does not move while you're watching the ridgeline, and also has gotten everywhere it has ever needed to go.

Up close — if you are ever foolish or determined enough to get close, which most who come here are not, sensing correctly that closeness is not invited — the stones of a Standing are warmer than the surrounding rock by a degree you can't account for, and they hold, faintly, the same low tuning-fork resonance as the canyon-throats, as though each one were a single sustained note in the larger held chord, a body built entirely out of the place's own unceasing sound. Touch one and you feel it less as a tremor than as a held breath you've interrupted. They do not react to touch. They have never, in any account, reacted to anything quickly.

What they do, across years: they finish things. A drystone wall left open on one side, the rest of you assumed, was left open by builders who departed before completing the work. This is not quite true. The walls are left open *for the Standing* — gaps sized for one of them to occupy, slow gatehouses at the heads of passes, sentinels grown into their posts rather than placed in them. Given decades, a Standing migrates the way the mountain migrates — by means too gradual to witness, only to discover. A wall you walked through last season has, this season, one fewer gap. Somewhere not far off, there is one more cairn than you remember at the head of the next pass, fitted just a little more perfectly than weather alone should allow, facing a direction that has nothing currently in it to watch.

They do not feed in any sense you'd recognize, which is consistent with there being no game, no green specifically grazed, no carcasses, no waste. What sustains them seems to be whatever sustains the rest of this place — that same unnamed channel that feeds the moss without rivers, that holds the false summits up without true strata beneath them. They are not predators. They have never, in any report anyone has carried back out, harmed a single traveler through force. Their danger is entirely a danger of attention: to be noticed by a Standing, to feel that structural regard settle onto you and not lift, is to find your own sense of duration beginning — very slowly, very patiently — to slip into their register. People who linger too long near a cluster of them report time stretching the way the valleys stretch, distances folding the way distances fold here, until they cannot say whether an hour or a season has been spent standing in a hollow being watched by stones that have not, in that whole while, performed a single visible motion.

Those who stay long enough are not killed. They are *outlasted*. The mountain is patient and the Standing are its patience given shape, and a human span of attention — bright, urgent, quickly bored — simply cannot hold a vigil as long as theirs. Eventually you look away. Eventually you walk on, certain nothing happened, faintly unable to account for the lost time, faintly aware that something has been added to your weight or your slowness that wasn't there when you arrived. Some travelers, rarely, do not look away in time. What becomes of them is not recorded, except that the cairn-counts at certain passes are higher than the old maps say they should be, and some of the newer stones, if you find them before moss has claimed them, are fitted with a precision that looks less like masonry and more like accommodation — as if the stack had been built, this one time, around something that was already, patiently, willing to stay.
