# Those Who Keep the Shelves

They are called, by outsiders who rarely stay long enough to learn better, the *Tor-folk* — though within Anvirra Tor itself no one uses that word, because no one has ever needed a name for the only kind of person there is.

They are a heavyset people, built low and broad the way the mountains are low and broad — not tall, rarely lean, with thick wrists and thicker necks, the kind of bodies that come from generations of stairs rather than flat ground. Their skin runs sallow-pale, almost the color of the mist itself, the result of a sun seen mostly in hour-long exhalations rather than full days. Their eyes, by contrast, tend dark and deep-set, adapted to reading distance and depth through grey air — Tor-folk can judge a switchback's grade or a cable-car's position through fog thick enough to blind a lowlander, the way a sailor reads swell. Their hands are their most particular feature: broad, calloused, faintly stained — soot-black at the nail-beds for the foundry-families, peat-brown for the shelf-farmers, slate-grey and permanently chalky for the wall-menders and stair-cutters. You can read a person's whole lineage in their palms before they've said a word.

They dress in layers, always layers, because the weather changes faster than a single garment can answer for — quilted underlayers in oiled wool, overcoats of waxed canvas or boiled leather, hoods less for the cold than for the wet, which finds its way into everything eventually. Color is muted by necessity and then by habit: lichen-green, iron-grey, the deep brown-black of wet bark. A bright color, on the rare occasion one survives the dye-pots and the damp, is treated almost as an event — worn on high-shelf feast-days, remarked upon, remembered for years.

## Work as Inheritance

Tor-folk do not choose trades so much as receive them, the way they receive a shelf-level address or a particular slant of cistern-light through a particular window. A family's relationship to the mountain is older than any living member of it. The foundry-families have tended the same engines for six generations, as the text below already notes, and they speak of their machines with the unselfconscious intimacy of people discussing elderly relatives — *she's running hot today*, *he doesn't like the cold mornings anymore*. The cable-tenders, who ride the funiculars up and down the shelf-stacks in all weather, develop a particular reputation for taciturnity and a particular skill at silence; two cable-tenders can ride together for an hour-long ascent and exchange four words, and consider this a satisfying journey. The wall-menders, who keep the retaining stone that holds each shelf's soil in place, are regarded with a quiet, almost religious respect — theirs is the work that keeps the thickets growing and the towns from sliding into the mist below, and a bad wall-mending season is spoken of the way other cultures might speak of a failed harvest.

Children are not so much raised toward a trade as soaked in one, present at the foundry-mouth or the cistern-edge or the stair-cuts from infancy, absorbing the rhythm of the work before they have words for what they're absorbing. It's said — though said the way all things in Anvirra Tor are said, without much insistence — that a foundry-child can sleep through any noise except the one specific irregularity that means *the engine's about to need attention*, and will wake instantly for that and nothing else.

## A Theology of Increments

Tor-folk are not, on the whole, a devout people in any organized sense — there are no grand temples here, no single accepted cosmology — but they hold a diffuse, practical reverence for *accumulation*: the moss creeping over the cistern lip, the soil gathering grain by grain in a stone seam, the centuries of footsteps that softened stairs into ramps. Their closest thing to scripture is a body of sayings about patience and sediment — *the shelf doesn't rush*, people say, of almost anything, a piece of advice and a shrug at once. Death is spoken of, when it's spoken of at all, in the same vocabulary: a person doesn't die so much as *settle*, finishing their accumulation, becoming part of what the next layer is built on. Burial here is vertical and literal — the old shelf-cemeteries are terraced like everything else, generations stacked quite plainly atop one another, the oldest graves lowest and longest grown-over, indistinguishable now from the rock.

This produces a population markedly unbothered by things that would alarm a visitor — the hour that becomes three, the schedule no one can explain, the unrecognized tools surfacing from the deep shafts at dawn. Tor-folk regard the mountain's small inconsistencies the way they regard weather: not a mystery to be solved, but a condition to be lived inside. *It's always been like that* is offered, in Anvirra Tor, not as evasion but as a complete and satisfying answer.

## The Sound They Make

Because sound behaves so strangely on the shelves — swallowed in one street, thrown bizarrely loud from three terraces off — Tor-folk have developed a culture oddly attentive to *which* sounds carry and which don't, and a corresponding fondness for bells, horns, struck iron: things built to be heard precisely because voices so often aren't. Foundry shift-changes are marked by bell rather than word. Funicular departures are called by horn. Within households, conversation tends low and close, almost conspiratorial, an adaptation to walls that swallow speech meant to stay private and betray speech meant to carry — you learn, growing up here, exactly how loud to speak for exactly which purpose, and outsiders are immediately legible by how badly they get this wrong, shouting where a murmur would do, murmuring where only a bell will be heard at all.

They are, in total, an unhurried, watchful, undramatic people — fond of strong dark drink brewed from fern-root and roasted grain, fond of long silences shared rather than endured, deeply loyal to shelf and trade and lineage, and entirely incurious about the deep workings under the lowest visible terrace, the older brick, the unrecognized tools — not from lack of imagination, but because they have lived their whole lives on a mountain that stopped being young before any word existed to describe it, and they have learned, the way the moss has learned, the way the fern has learned, simply to grow into whatever room the old stone leaves them.
