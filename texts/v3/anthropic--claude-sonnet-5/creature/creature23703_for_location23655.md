# Those Who Live in the Scaffolding

They call themselves, when pressed, simply *ridge-folk* or *stack-folk*, depending on which cluster raised them — but mostly they don't call themselves anything. The need doesn't arise. Nobody visits.

You'd know them first by the layering. Coats over coats over canvas, not for fashion but because the temperature here refuses to commit to a season, and a body needs options. Underneath, they run lean and wiry, built for catwalks and ladders rather than open ground — broad-shouldered for hauling, narrow everywhere else, with the permanent slight stoop of people who have spent generations ducking under low pipe-joints and stairwell overlaps. Their skin carries a faint, perpetual dampness, not sweat exactly, more like they've absorbed some of the mountain's own condensation into the pores. Soot collects in the creases of their hands and never fully washes out; by middle age, the lines of a person's palms are stained dark enough to read like a second set of fingerprints, a record of furnace-years.

Their eyes are the tell. Pale, often — washed-out grey, amber, a watered hazel — adapted to the gauze-light the way deep-water things adapt to pressure. Ridge-folk squint outdoors and see perfectly well in furnace-glow that would blind a lowlander. Children born here are sometimes slow to focus in true daylight, on the rare clear day when the cloud ceiling thins; their parents don't worry about it. The eyes catch up, eventually, or they don't, and either way the child learns the ladders just fine.

## The Trades

Work here runs in family lines, not because the settlements forbid mobility but because the knowledge required is too specific to learn quickly and too dangerous to learn carelessly. A furnace-tender's children become furnace-tenders. A rail-walker's children walk rail. The trade isn't just mechanical — it's a literacy. You learn which gantries hum wrong before a strut fails. You learn the particular pitch of bird-cry that means weather is coming through the cloud ceiling, a front, a pressure-change that will make the pipe-bridges sweat harder than usual. You learn, above all, to read the *settledness* of a place — whether a street has finished deciding it exists, whether a stairwell's overlap has drifted closer to your floor this year than last.

This last skill has no formal name but everyone has it to some degree, the way everyone in a coastal town can read tide without consulting a chart. Outsiders — on the rare occasion one climbs this far — are warned off certain streets gently, almost apologetically, the way you'd warn a guest about a loose floorboard. *That one's still thinking. Go around.*

Shifts are long, structured around furnace-cycles rather than any clock, and the rhythm of the work has worn itself into the rhythm of the bodies doing it: slow-building exertion through the heat-climb, a long simmer of maintenance and watching, a cooling-down period when the settlement's residential tiers fill with quiet, unhurried talk. Nothing here is rushed, including the people. Urgency is reserved for the rare moments the mountain actually moves.

## What the Children Are Taught

Before arithmetic, before reading, ridge-children learn the rules of the in-between, recited almost like rhymes:

*If the landing looks the same twice, don't argue with it.*
*A shadow that leads you is worth following. A shadow that follows you, isn't.*
*Count the street before you walk it. Count it again at the far end. If the numbers don't match, you weren't where you thought.*

This is not taught as danger so much as etiquette — the way you'd teach a child not to interrupt, not to stare. The anomalies are neighbors, of a kind. Rude to point at, ruder still to panic over.

There is a kind of dry affection in how they talk about the unfinished places, the same affection a person might have for a relative who's a little strange at gatherings. *Oh, that landing. It's always been like that.*

## The Ones Who Came Back Different

There is a category of person here that nobody discusses directly but everybody recognizes — those who spent too long near an unfinished street, or fell asleep in a stairwell overlap, or worked too many seasons at the edge of a settlement where the rock itself hadn't decided its final shape. They come back mostly the same. Mostly.

A man might return with a shadow that's a half-second slow, trailing him gently through life like an afterthought that never quite catches up. A woman might find, late in years, that mirrors render her with one detail wrong each time — a part on the opposite side, a scar that migrates ear to ear — and that nobody else in the room ever notices but her. There's a family three settlements over, it's said, where every third child is born with a sixth finger that nobody remembers being added, present in the earliest infant photographs, absent from none of them, and yet absent too in some account someone's grandmother insists she remembers.

Nobody treats this as tragedy. It's filed alongside frostbite, alongside the rattling cough that comes from furnace-smoke — an occupational hazard of living somewhere still becoming itself. You adjust. You live an ordinary life slightly askew, the way the mountain lives an ordinary geology slightly askew, and the two unfinished things keep each other company.

## The Old Ones

At the oldest end of life, in the upper residential tiers where the furnace-heat doesn't quite reach and the moss grows thickest on the windowsills, there are elders who have stopped correcting the inconsistencies in their own stories. They'll tell you about a sibling who both did and did not die in a rockfall forty years back, with no contradiction in their voice, as if the fact simply resolved itself differently depending on which version of the telling you'd asked for. They are treated with a particular gentleness by the younger ridge-folk — not pity, something closer to reverence, the recognition that a long enough life lived this close to the mountain's unfinished seams eventually stops drawing a hard border between a person and the geology around them.

Some, very old, are said to walk out one day toward a street that's never resolved, on a morning when the fog sits low and undisturbed, and simply not return — not as a death, exactly, nothing the settlement marks with grief or funeral. More like a retirement. More like a long-overdue admission that they, too, were always part of something still cooling, still settling, still — like the peaks above the permanent cloud — patiently, unhurriedly, finishing.
