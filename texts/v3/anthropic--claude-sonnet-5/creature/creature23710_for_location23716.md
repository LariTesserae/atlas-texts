## The Tenants

You hear them before you understand what you're hearing — that "soft thud of something heavy-bodied moving along a branch above." It takes longer than it should to realize the thud has a rhythm, a give-and-pause that isn't falling, isn't blundering, but *deciding*. Something up there is choosing its branches.

They are called, by the handful of people who've ever needed a word for them, hollow-bears, though they are not bears, not anything close — the name is just what the shape suggests from below, in bad light, at a distance: a low, broad-backed silhouette, moving without hurry, never quite where you expected the sound to come from. Up close they are stranger than the silhouette allows. The body is barrel-thick, low to the branch, built for clinging rather than running, with four wide limbs ending in pads that splay and grip rather than claws that pierce — climbing apparatus borrowed less from anything that hunts than from anything that simply refuses to fall. The skin beneath the coat is thin, damp, faintly permeable in the way of something that drinks as much through its hide as through its mouth; in the wettest months you can see it darken and swell very slightly, like a sponge taking on water, and in drier weeks it dulls and tightens, visibly thirstier.

The coat itself is the part that unsettles, once you look closely. It is not fur in any clean sense — it is felted, matted by design rather than neglect, and growing in it, rooted in it, is a low static turf of the same mosses and liverworts that coat the rock walls. The hollow-bear does not wear camouflage. It cultivates it. Spores settle into the damp undercoat in infancy and never leave; by adulthood each animal carries a small fixed garden on its back, individual as a fingerprint, slightly different shades of green-black-gray depending on which colony of moss took first and held. They are, in the most literal sense, mobile terrain — patches of the hollow's own walls, grown legs, grazing slowly through the strata they otherwise resemble.

Their eyes are large, dark, deep-set, built for the perpetual gray-green dusk and useless in anything brighter; on the rare day when full sun finds its way down through a gap in the canopy, they're said to simply stop, hunch, and wait it out, blinking, until the light moves on. They do not see you so much as *register* you — a slow tracking attention, unbothered, unafraid, the look of something that has not needed to fear anything in this hollow for longer than it has needed to remember why.

**What they do, mostly, is tend.** They move along fixed circuits — root-tunnel to standing deadwood to canopy fork and back — grazing the fungus gardens that grow in every joint and hollow, but grazing in a way that prunes rather than strips, leaving the mycelial mat intact, cropping only the fruiting bodies, the way a careful hand might pick rather than pull. In the process their fur picks up spore-dust from one colony and deposits it, hours later, in the damp crook of another tree entirely — an unconscious, ceaseless seeding, repeated by every animal in the hollow, all the time, for longer than any of them have been alive. No hollow-bear has ever planted anything on purpose. The fungal city wears their fur like a road network and has, over generations, simply grown to depend on the route.

They are not solitary, but they are not exactly social either — each animal or small kin-cluster holds a loose, overlapping range of root-tunnel and branch-fork, marked not by scent or sign but by *call*: that short, low note you hear and then, after a long pause, hear answered from somewhere else in the hollow. It isn't a greeting. It's closer to a sounding — a way of confirming the shape of the dark by listening for who else is standing in it, spaced out just far enough that the grazing never overlaps, never strips any one garden bare. The pause between call and answer can run to minutes. Neither animal seems troubled by the wait.

They breed slowly, in the wet hollows of the oldest standing deadwood — the closest thing the place has to a nursery district — and the young stay matted into a parent's coat-moss for the better part of a year, riding the slow circuit of grazing routes before they're felted with growth of their own and sent, eventually, to find an unclaimed fork in the canopy and start a territory, a garden, a coat, from scratch.

**Beneath them, smaller tenants keep the same architecture at a finer scale.** The gill-grazers — pale, many-legged, no longer than a fingernail — move across the underside of every bracket fungus in slow exploratory grids, eating the spore-dust the hollow-bears miss, themselves grazed in turn by something smaller still that you would need to kneel in the leaf-litter and wait, motionless, a long time to ever actually see. The whole hollow runs on this nesting of small diligent appetites, tier below tier below tier, with the hollow-bear simply the largest and slowest and most visible link in a chain that has no clear bottom.

You will likely never see one clearly. You'll hear the thud, catch the slow swing of a low branch settling after weight has left it, glimpse — just at the edge of where the gray-green dusk gives out into shadow — a shape that might be a heavy-bodied animal or might be a particularly mossy burl of old wood, and by the time you've decided to look harder it will have resolved into stillness, indistinguishable, patient, willing to wait far longer than you are to find out which it was.
