# Those Who Tend

They are pale — not sickly, but the pale of something grown in shade for generations, skin gone the color of the limestone itself, faintly luminous in places where it's thinnest, at the wrist, the temple, the place behind the knee. Not blind, not even close — the children are taught to read gauges before letters, after all, and that requires eyes — but their eyes have widened over time, the pupils sitting large and dark and permanently a little dilated, drinking in whatever light the sodium lamps and the glowing lichen offer. In sunlight, on the rare occasions someone climbs the long shafts to the surface, they squint and weep and call it beautiful anyway, then climb back down within the hour, uneasy in all that unfiltered glare.

Their hands are the most expressive part of them. Generations of reading pressure by feel — palm flat against warm iron, fingers tracing the faint shudder of a valve about to turn — have made their hands almost a second face, mobile and articulate, used to communicate across the chamber's strange acoustics where raised voices come back distorted and wrong. A flat palm pressed twice against a pipe means *wait*. A trailing finger along a rust-stain means *I remember when this didn't reach so far*. Children learn hand-talk in the same breath as gauge-reading, often before they learn to speak in full sentences, because sound carries badly here and touch carries true.

They dress in layers that shed water rather than keep out cold — oiled wool, waxed canvas, leather gone soft and dark with decades of damp — because the chamber's chill is mild but its wet is constant, and a body that stays dry-skinned here is a body that's found some trick the others haven't. Many wear small brass or tin disks at the throat or wrist, stamped with the sigil of whichever boiler or condensation tower their family has tended for generations; it's not quite a surname, not quite a trade-mark, more like a kinship claimed with a machine rather than a bloodline, though the two have long since tangled together — the Pellings have stoked the third boiler so long that *Pelling* and *third-boiler-keeper* are nearly the same word.

**Temperament**

They are patient in a way that looks, from outside, almost like indifference — but it isn't. It's a patience trained into the body by a place where the water table moves over years and the rust creeps over decades and a flywheel takes minutes to complete one slow groaning turn. Urgency is rare and treated with mild suspicion, the way an aboveground village might regard someone who laughs too loud at a funeral. Decisions are made the way the boilers are stoked — discussed, banked, returned to, never rushed, because nothing here has ever rewarded rushing and several things have punished it.

They are listeners before they are talkers. The chamber's acoustics have shaped this — a culture where you learn to let a sound finish before responding to it, where the dripping percussion underneath every conversation has trained generations of ears to filter signal from noise, to catch the difference between *just water* and *water plus something else*. When the pipes hum their single sustained note, work stops, briefly, everywhere in the chamber, without anyone calling for it — heads turning toward the sound the way sunflowers turn, a whole population pausing to listen to something they've long since stopped expecting to understand. When the fish in the cisterns surface in their wordless unison, the same stillness moves through the human population above the waterline, an answering hush, as if the people were just another species in the chamber's ecology, responding to a cue no one has named.

**Roles and Days**

Labor sorts loosely by temperament rather than strict inheritance, though inheritance shapes it too. The *stokers* are broad-shouldered and slow-moving, comfortable with heat and long waiting, feeding coal or peat into fireboxes on schedules that have the unhurried gravity of liturgy — and indeed the stoking is spoken of in faintly religious terms by everyone, even those who'd deny believing in anything: *we feed it because it has always been fed, and a thing fed that long becomes owed something.*

The *gauge-readers* are leaner, quicker-eyed, prized for the steadiness of their hands and their ability to sit motionless for an hour watching a needle that might not move at all, and to know — by some accumulated, almost unconscious arithmetic — the difference between a needle that's merely still and one that's stuck.

The *fish-tenders* work the cisterns, harvesting the fat blind fish with nets of woven wire, an occupation so undemanding it's mostly given to the very old and the very young — a way of keeping both groups present and useful without expecting too much, since the fish basically tend themselves, breeding and feeding in the dark cisterns in numbers that have never needed managing, only collecting.

And then there are the *listeners* — not an official role, never assigned, but every settlement seems to produce one or two: people who simply sit, often near the south wall where the backward clock hangs, and listen to the chamber the way another culture's elders might watch the sky. They are the ones who first notice when the humming note changes pitch, when the fish surface a half-second later than usual, when a new rust-stain has started somewhere it shouldn't have. No one calls them priests. Everyone treats them a little like priests.

**Belief, If That's the Word**

They do not worship the machines, exactly, but they do not *not* worship them either. The prevailing attitude is something closer to tenancy — the sense that they live inside something old and half-understood, that their stoking and tending and gauge-reading is less *operation* than *cohabitation*, a long-running negotiation with a structure that might once have had a purpose and now simply has a rhythm, and the rhythm is enough to organize a life around. Asking *what is this machinery for* is considered a child's question — charming, eventually outgrown, like asking what the rain is for. The honest answer, which most adults arrive at and few say aloud, is: *it is for this. For us being here, listening to it, in the dark, together.*

They check the backward clock every day not because they believe it tells correct time but because the checking is the point — a small daily proof that someone is still paying attention, that the chamber is still being watched the way you'd watch a sleeping animal you weren't entirely sure was harmless. Marriages are sometimes blessed, half-jokingly, half not, by holding the couple's clasped hands against a warm pipe until they can feel the same pulse of pressure move under their palms together. Children are named, sometimes, for whichever rust-fall was nearest the wall when they were born — *third-rust-fall-children*, a whole loose cohort of them now grown, scattered through the chamber, doing the slow unglamorous work of keeping something old alive without ever being told, quite, what it would mean to let it stop.
