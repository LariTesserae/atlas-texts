# What Walks There

The first one you see, you don't see at all—you mistake it for a heat-mirage thickening near a fire-bowl, the kind of shimmer the eye discounts automatically. It's only when the shimmer *steps*, decisively, onto the next plate of stone, that you understand you've been watching a body the whole time, and that the body has been watching you back, in its way, for longer than you'd like.

Call them, for lack of anything they've offered themselves, the **standing ones**—though they rarely stand still, and "ones" undercounts them by an order of magnitude you'll never finish correcting. They are the plateau's only population, and they are legion.

Up close—closer than is comfortable, because they permit closeness, they do not flinch from you the way living things flinch—a standing one resolves into something like a quadruped, though the count of legs refuses to hold steady under examination. Four when it's still. Six when it's walking. None, distinctly none, in the half-second after it crosses one of the slow gear-cracks in the stone, where it seems to flicker out of leggedness entirely and progress by some other method, a kind of patient falling-forward, before reassembling its gait on the far side like a sentence that stuttered and recovered.

Its body is heat made dimensional. Where the air above the fire-bowls bends light, this creature *is* that bending, given a hide—if hide is the word for a surface that has no temperature gradient across it at all, that is exactly as hot at the core as at the edge, a uniform shimmer-flesh that your eyes report as motion even when it's holding still. Look at it long enough and you start to see the geometry underneath: a ribwork of something harder, hexagonal, the same basalt-rust as the plateau floor, visible only at the joints, where the heat-flesh thins to show its skeleton is the land itself, quarried and folded inward rather than grown.

Its eyes are not eyes. They are the two coolest points on its body, small dark patches in the shimmer where the gradient drops, and they track you the way a furnace's draft-vent tracks a change in air pressure—not with intention exactly, but with the kind of responsiveness that intention evolved to imitate. There is no mouth. There has never needed to be a mouth. It does not eat the world; it is already made of what the world is made of, and it metabolizes nothing you'd recognize as fuel—possibly it doesn't metabolize at all, possibly it simply *runs*, the way the gas-fissures run, the way the fire-bowls burn, on the same unauthored schedule that turns the plateau's stone gears without anyone having wound them.

Some standing ones, when they pass near enough, you can hear: a slow bellows-rasp from somewhere in the chest-region, in through the same surface it exhales through, no separate openings, the whole hide breathing as one diffuse lung. Underneath the rasp, fainter, almost embarrassed to be there: a gear-click, evenly spaced, like a mechanism counting something down or up, you can't tell which, and it never finishes counting in any span of time you're willing to wait through.

They travel in herds that are not herds—no leader, no edge, no visible reason for the boundary of "this many, together" rather than some other number. They move toward the haze at the plateau's lost margins with the unhurried certainty of things that have never once needed to arrive anywhere, because arrival, for them, may not be different in kind from the walking. You watch one herd take most of a day to cross your sightline, and by the time the last of them clears the ridge, you've lost track of whether they were leaving or whether the ridge had been, the whole time, quietly walking toward *them*.

They do not eat. They do not drink. They do not, as far as anyone has stayed long enough to determine, die—though sometimes one will simply stop mid-stride, settle its shimmer-flesh back down into the rust-red stone it rose from, and become, again, indistinguishable from the plateau. Not a death. A sentence ending where the page happened to run out, with the clear sense that the same words might continue somewhere else, in some other standing one, at some other unscheduled hour.

What they're *for* is the question the plateau declines to answer. There is no predator here for them to be prey to. There is no scarcity for them to compete inside. They are not pollinators, not decomposers, not engines of any visible economy—and yet there are so many of them, so constantly, that "ecosystem" feels like the wrong shaped word entirely, a word built for places where things need each other. The standing ones don't need. They simply persist, in numbers that have nothing to do with what the land could support, as if population, here, isn't a measure of resources at all, but a measure of how thoroughly the wrongness has taken—as if every fissure-vent and every ratcheting stone-gear and every fire that burns on nothing eventually had to produce, as a kind of overflow, a body to go with the burning.

Stand near one long enough, in the heat, in the roar that never resolves to a single source, and you start to suspect the relationship runs backward from how you assumed it. The plateau did not make engines and then, incidentally, creatures to wander among them.

The standing ones are what the engine, run long enough without a purpose, eventually learns to grow.
