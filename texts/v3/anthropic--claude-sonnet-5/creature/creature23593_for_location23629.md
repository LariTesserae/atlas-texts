**The Scattered Hands**

Call the first one Mosseth-of-the-Long-Seam, though that is not a name so much as a location with a person attached to it — here, identity and geography have not yet finished separating from each other.

She is small, by the standard of a world with sun in it — perhaps four feet at the shoulder-hump, built low and dense, with a spine that curves forward into a permanent stoop, because nothing here has ever needed to look up. Her skin is the color of the rock she sleeps against: a grey gone faintly red at the joints, where blood runs closest to the surface and where, over generations, something like pigment has migrated to better drink in the coal-light's heat. It is not sight she has in her eyes — those went small and clouded back when her people decided, without ever deciding anything so much as simply *stopping*, that eyes cost more than they returned. What she has instead are the plates: two raised, leathery ovals on her brow and a third, smaller, at the base of her throat, dense with thermal nerve-endings, reading the world the way a hand reads a face in the dark. She "looks" at you by turning these toward you, a slow patient orientation, like a flower that has never seen the sun finding it anyway, by feel.

Her hands are the part that hasn't changed, the part that gives her people their borrowed name. Five digits, opposable thumb, nails worn to blunt use rather than grown to points — hands built for exactly the kind of fine, unhurried, repetitive work that fills a life with nowhere to rush toward. She has spent this evening — though there is no evening, only the hour when the herd-murmur thins and she judges enough sleep-time has passed — knapping a blade from a fist-sized chunk of obsidian-glass formed where a coal-seam met cooling water that no longer exists. Each strike is small. She has been three days at this one blade. There is no reason to be faster.

She lives alone, in the sense that her dwelling — a half-ring of stacked stone closing one side of a natural alcove, the coal-seam itself forming the warm back wall — holds only her, her mate having gone three migrations ago to seam-country two hundred kilometers east, following the herds the way his family always has, the way hers stayed put the way hers always has. They will not see each other again, in all likelihood, and neither of them experiences this as tragedy. Distance, here, is not exile. It is simply the shape the world takes between two warm places. He sends nothing back, because there is no sending; she does not wait, because waiting implies an arrival that the architecture of this country does not promise. What they had, they had. The coal-seam does not require renewal to keep glowing.

She eats what the herders' word translates roughly as *seep-meat* — small mineral-grazing things, eyeless, soft-shelled, that she catches with a snare of fire-hardened — not wood, there is no wood — but a tough cartilage cord stripped from the leg-joints of larger grazers and cured for weeks in gas-bloom smoke until it stiffens like rope. She does not hunt so much as wait at the places the herd reliably passes and take what crosses close enough; this is not laziness, it is the only sane economy in a world where everything moves on a schedule older than language and is not going to be hurried for anyone's hunger.

Her religion, if it can be called that — her people have no word that separates belief from observation — is built on the seams themselves. She believes, the way she believes the floor is down, that the long branching lines of glowing rock are *old in a way that watches.* Not a god exactly. Closer to an ancestor so distant the relation has gone past kinship into something like weather. When she dies — and she will die here, in this alcove, of nothing more dramatic than slowing down until she stops, the way the old do in a place with no predators large enough to hurry the matter — her body will be laid directly against the seam, not burned, not buried, simply returned to the warmth at the speed warmth prefers, which is to say slowly, over weeks, while the mineral chemistry that built her unbuilds her again into the soil that isn't soil. Her children, if she has living ones nearby, will not mourn in any way you would recognize as mourning. They will simply, for a time, sit closer to that particular stretch of seam than to any other, the way you might linger near a phone that used to ring.

She has language — sparse, mostly tactile, built as much from pressure and proximity as from sound, since sound carries strangely in the vast warm dark and a shout here means something closer to *emergency* than *hello*. Most communication happens at brushing distance: a hand laid on a forearm, held a beat longer than passing requires; a particular cadence of footstep that means *kin* rather than *stranger* rather than *herd-animal too close*. She has perhaps four hundred words. She does not need more. Nothing here has happened for the first time in longer than her language has existed to describe it.

What she does not have, and could not conceive of wanting, is the idea that anything should be otherwise. She does not wonder why there is no sky. She does not wonder, particularly, whether the seams were placed on purpose, because the question "why" implies a story with a beginning, and she has never stood anywhere a beginning might have happened. Ask her, if you had four hundred words in common and the patience to spend them, what she thinks she is *for*, and she would not understand the question as a question. She is for sitting near the warmth. She is for making blades that take three unhurried days. She is for being, eventually, laid down against the long red seam and given back.

It is not resignation. Resignation requires having wanted something else. She has simply never been offered the premise that there was anything else to want, and in the entire history of her entire enormous, lightless, patient country, nothing has ever happened to suggest that premise was missing anything at all.
