They are few, and they move like the basin trained them to: unhurried, low-shouldered, ears half-tuned to a noise so constant it stopped being noise generations ago and became weather. You'd see one before you heard them coming, usually, because the chuff and drip and birdsong eats footsteps whole. They wear loose, oiled cloth gone soft and faintly green at the seams—not dyed that way, just lived that way, the basin's damp finding every fiber eventually and deciding to stay.

Call them tenders rather than workers, though there is work, plenty of it, the kind that never finishes and was never meant to: clearing a gutter of fern-root, coaxing a sticking valve with the flat of a hand and a few words spoken more out of habit than belief that the machine understands. They don't fight the vegetation so much as negotiate with it. You'll see one kneeling by a vent-pipe, gently unwinding a vine that's gotten too ambitious, draping it instead a few feet off where it can still drink the warm air without throttling the joint — not removed, just redirected, the way you'd correct a child rather than punish one.

There's a particular quality to how they hold time. Ask one a question and there's a pause before the answer, longer than is comfortable, as if they're checking the answer against some slower clock running underneath the conversation — the boiler-clock, the moss-clock, the four-hour breath of the gantry vents. They've absorbed the settlement's patience the way the ironwork absorbed the settlement's growth. Nothing here is rushed because nothing here has ever rewarded rushing; the cup punishes haste with a slipped foot on wet stone, a fern-frond to the eye, a misjudged valve that vents early and scalds.

Physically they run damp-flushed, faces a little ruddy from the basin's trapped warmth, knuckles soft-cracked from handling wet rope and wetter iron. Old-timers, if there are any — and the place is young enough that "old-timer" might mean someone who arrived eleven years ago instead of three — develop a faint stoop from decades of ducking under low pipe-runs and leaning into the same three doorways. Their hands know plants and machines with the same unconsidered fluency; ask one to name the difference between pruning a vine and bleeding a valve and they might not immediately see what you're asking.

They don't startle at the heron-thing in the creek, or at the chittering economy of small animals thick in the fern-shade. There's an unspoken arrangement: the basin's fauna gets the runoff, the warmth, the moss-shelter, and in exchange it simply doesn't bother anyone, going about its business with the same indifference the tenders extend right back. A few of them have taken to leaving food scraps near the same three spots out of habit more than charity, the way you'd top off a cistern that's never quite empty but never quite full either.

What you won't find among them is alarm, or wonder, or much in the way of story-telling about how strange this all is — the ironwork grown around by root and vine, the plants curling toward heat instead of away from it. They've stopped finding it strange, if they ever did. They were raised on it, most of them, or arrived young enough that the basin's logic became their own before they'd developed a competing one. Ask why the philodendron wraps the boiler joint instead of withering near it, and you'll get a shrug, maybe a half-answer about minerals in the runoff or warmth being warmth wherever you find it, and then they'll go back to whatever slow task brought them outside — content, in the particular way of people who have made a long peace with a place that is still, visibly, making up its mind about what it wants to become.
