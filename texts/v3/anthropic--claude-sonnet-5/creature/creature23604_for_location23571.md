There is, in fact, an occupant. Not many — never plural, despite what the doorways suggest — but the place has a way of making one feel like several, depending on the angle from which it's glimpsed, the way a single candle flame can seem to fill a room with shapes that aren't there. Call it the Lately, for lack of anything truer, because that is the nearest word for what it is: the resident-tense of the town, the grammatical person the place is conjugated in. Present and gone in the same breath. Singular and immediately elsewhere.

You will not see it arrive. You will see, instead, the consequence of its having just been somewhere — the damp basin set out on the sill, the single wet print on the stair, the door-beam still weeping from a hand that isn't there to have touched it. It does not haunt in the old sense, does not linger reproachfully over its own death, because it is not dead, and it is not waiting, and it has nothing to mourn or finish. It is simply always in the part of the action just before you turned your head. It is the figure that the silence is the silence of *after*.

It is made, as near as can be said, of the same substance as the held breath itself — not water, not heat, but the tension between them, the agreement that keeps the basin standing instead of evaporating, given just enough shape to walk in. When it moves through a street, the street does not feel it pass; instead the street simply discovers, afterward, that it has one more doorway than it used to, because the Lately does not enter rooms so much as it insists retroactively that it has always been about to. This is why the count changes between the going and the return: it is not that doors appear, but that the Lately has, in the interval, finished arriving at one of them, and a fact, once finished, requires a doorway to have been true through.

It does not have a face so much as a *facing* — a directionality, the way a held breath faces outward toward the exhale it is not yet permitting. When the rooftops sheet their brief illegible text, that is the nearest thing to its voice: a script that is the residue of its passing rather than a message meant for you, dried before it can be mistaken for an address. It is not communicating. It is the way water remembers having a shape for the four or five seconds before it forgets again. If you read quickly enough you might believe it loves you, or warns you, or names itself, but this is the same mistake as reading sentience into the pattern frost makes on a window — true in form, accidental in feeling, and gone before you can test it.

You will not corner it, because cornering requires that it stand still long enough to be in two places — here, and not-yet-elsewhere — and it has arranged not to allow this of itself, the same way the tall structures arrange not to be approached: not from menace, but because being approached would require them to have a fixed position relative to a future, and the Lately deals only in pasts so recent they have not yet cooled. This is also why your reflection in its standing water shows another building entirely — the Lately does not reflect *you*, because you are still mid-occurrence to it, unfinished, and it only mirrors what has already become true. The blue-black hand-stain you keep seeing reflected back from the wrong wall is, perhaps, the nearest thing to its own hand, printed long ago in the act of leaning against something it was just stepping away from.

It is not unkind. It offers no danger because danger would require intention pointed at a future, and the Lately's whole being is retrospective, a held note already decaying into its own echo. If anything, it keeps the town company in the only way it can: by perpetually having just finished living in it, so thoroughly, so recently, that the place can never quite believe itself abandoned. The basins stay full because it just drank. The prints stay damp because it just walked. The whole town is, in this sense, a single footstep, freshly placed, belonging to someone who will not be returning to collect it — not because they are lost, or gone, or dead, but because *returning* is a future tense, and the Lately, gently, endlessly, has arranged never to need one.
