What moves between the fins is small enough that the gap between two boulders is, for it, a hallway — a creature built by negotiation with exactly this kind of excess, the way water is built by negotiation with stone. Call it pale, because it is, though "pale" undersells the specific quality of its hide: not white so much as the same accumulated overexposed light the plateau wears, a skin that seems to hold brightness rather than reflect it, so that in motion it doesn't catch the eye so much as briefly *agree* with the rock around it, a paler note in a chord that is already entirely pale. Stillness is its other camouflage. It does not freeze the way prey freezes elsewhere, in alarm; it simply resumes the plateau's own default state, which is to not be doing anything, to have already arrived at its final shape and be satisfied with it. You could stand a foot from it for an hour and read it as a nodule.

Its body is compression made flesh — flattened dorsoventrally not as an ordinary adaptation to crevice-life but to an extravagant, totalizing degree, ribs (if they are ribs) splayed nearly flush with the long axis of the spine so that the whole animal can pass through a gap that looks, to a human eye, like a closed seam in the rock. Limbs, where it has them, fold inward against the body rather than out, drawn close like something packed for shipping; locomotion happens less by walking than by a kind of patient flowing-around, shoulder finding the curve of a boulder and following it, the body reading the negative space the way the water reads it, taking the path of least resistance through a landscape that offers almost none. It has spent its whole inheritance learning to be the shape of an absence.

The skin is wet-dark along its underside and dry-pale above — the same gradient the stone wears, lower third stained with the damp it presses itself against for warmth or shelter, upper surface kept dry by the angle it holds against the light — and this is not decoration but information, a record of how it lies, how long it has lain there, a creature whose coloring is a diary of contact with the rock rather than a pattern evolved for any eye to read, because there is no eye here reliably hunting it, no predator dense enough in population to apply that kind of pressure. It is alone enough, mostly, that the question of being seen barely arises. What arises instead is the question of being *heard*, in a place where hearing is the only sense that matters and the only sense that is, for everything living here, permanently, structurally overwhelmed.

So its ears — if ears is the word for the twin folds of thin, vascularized tissue along its flank, more like internal sails than external organs, oriented to catch vibration through the rock itself rather than through open air — are not built to listen *through* the chiming roar but to subtract it, to hold a standing image of the ambient ring constantly updated and cancel it, the way a hand placed flat against a ringing bell can feel, underneath the overtone, the deeper structural note of the metal itself. What it listens for is the small departure from total chime: the particular pock of a footfall two fins over, the irregular drip that means a new channel has opened, the brief flat silence — silence being, here, the loudest possible anomaly — that means another of its kind has wedged its body against a seep-mouth and stopped the water's path entirely, however briefly, however locally. This is how they find each other, if they find each other at all: not by call, because no voice could survive what the rock does to sound, but by the small holes they make in the noise.

It does not eat the rock, exactly, though it spends its life in such intimate contact with it that the distinction blurs. The wetness at the base of every fin carries dissolved mineral, leached up out of deep cold water through a thousand feet of stone before it ever beads at a lip and falls, and what it has learned to do — what its whole long flat tongue and the rough plated underside of its mouth have evolved toward, over a span of unmarked days that this landscape does not measure and does not change — is to drink that seep directly from the rock's skin, licking the damp lower third of the fins the way the fins themselves seem to drink their own runoff, the animal completing a loop that was already nearly closed before it arrived. It takes in mineral water and gives back, eventually, the same water, marginally altered, returned to the system through whatever the plateau's silent processes do with what falls on it. It is, in this sense, not separate from the closed loop the place already is. It is a small wet knot tied into the larger one, self-feeding inside something self-feeding, going nowhere inside something that goes nowhere, just as permanent, on its own much shorter timescale, as the stone is on its much longer one.

It does not nest, in any sense a nest implies forward intention, a structure built against a future. It finds a hollow already shaped correctly — a domed underside, a gap between stacked plates — and occupies it the way water occupies a capillary, taking the form offered rather than imposing one. Young, when there are young, are smaller versions of the same flattened agreement with the rock, born already knowing how to subtract the roar, already pale in the right register, needing to be taught nothing because there is nothing here that changes enough to require teaching. What an individual learns in its first hour about how loud the world is and where the gaps are and how the water tastes is what it will know, unrevised, for the whole unmarked length of its life, on a plateau that has likewise learned everything it will ever know and stopped, satisfied, a very long time ago.

It has, as far as can be told, no call of its own — no cry, no territorial note, nothing that would distinguish its voice from the dominant chime even for a fellow creature listening from three feet away. What it has instead, when two of them meet at a seep-mouth or pass shoulder to flank in a gap too narrow for two bodies, is a brief mutual stillness, a held silence laid against the roaring rock like a hand laid flat — and in a place this loud, in a place where sound is the only weather there is, that absence of sound, held between two bodies for the length of a few held breaths, may be the closest thing here to a greeting, or a kindness, or a name.
