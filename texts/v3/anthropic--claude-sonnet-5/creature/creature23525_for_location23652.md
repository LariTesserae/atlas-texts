# The Kelth-Adari

They are smaller than the surface remembers people being — not stunted, exactly, but *economized*, generation by generation, by ceilings that never rose to meet them and corridors that never widened. Adults stand a head or more below what the old stories (the ones that drift down from traders and exiles) describe as average. It is hard to say if the warren shaped its people or its people shaped the warren first and have simply kept faith with the choice ever since. Either way, a tall stranger in Kelth-Adar walks stooped everywhere, forever apologizing to lintels, while the warren-born move through their tightest passages without breaking stride, without looking up, the way water finds the shape already cut for it.

**The Eyes and the Skin**

Lamp-light is gold and small and never quite enough, and the Kelth-Adari eye has answered this over centuries the only way an eye can: by growing patient with dimness. Pupils sit permanently a little wider than a surface-dweller's, even in the brightest hub-chamber firelight, giving warren-folk a faintly nocturnal cast that unsettles outsiders — a sense of being looked at by something that sees a half-step further into the dark than you do. Eye color runs pale: washed grey, weak amber, a blue gone milky, as if pigment itself were a luxury better spent elsewhere.

The skin tells age the way tree-rings tell it, but in clay rather than wood. Dust works into the creases of knuckles, the folds behind ears, the soft skin at the wrist, and it does not fully wash out — there isn't water enough to spare for that kind of thoroughness, and besides, no one tries that hard. An infant's skin is merely pale. By middle life it has gone a soft, ground-in grey, deepest in the hands and feet, the parts that touch the most wall and floor. The very old are called, with real affection, "fired" — as if the kilns had finally gotten to them too, the way they got to the brick.

**Hands, Feet, Backs**

Whatever else changes, the body remembers its work in its calluses. A brick-layer's palms are flat and hard as the brick itself. A weaver's fingertips carry the permanent stain of fungal dye, blue-black under the nail no matter how often scrubbed. Lamp-tenders have one favored thumb burned smooth and nearly numb from decades of pinching wicks. Feet go bare or near-bare in most chambers — sandal-soles are saved for the colder lower levels and the rare trips toward the surface shafts — and the soles harden into something closer to horn than skin, reading the floor's temperature and grain the way an eye reads a room.

Backs curve. Not dramatically, not in every body, but there is a characteristic warren stoop, shoulders rounded forward, head carried slightly bowed, the unconscious posture of someone who has spent a life ducking. The old joke that you can tell a person's home-chamber by the precise angle of their spine, and the joke is not entirely a joke.

**A Loudness That Is Also a Kind of Quiet**

They are not a hushed people, despite — or because of — the constant roar. Voices in Kelth-Adar pitch to be heard over looms and grindstones and warren-fowl and the eternal murmur bleeding through every shared wall, and so ordinary speech here would sound, on the surface, almost like shouting: full-throated, unselfconscious, layered into the ambient din without apology. True privacy doesn't come from lowering the voice — that would only sound suspicious, a wrongness, like the unnerving hush of an empty corridor. Instead, the Kelth-Adari have grown a second language with their hands: a quick, economical grammar of finger and wrist, taught to children almost as early as speech, used for the things meant for one set of eyes only — a private joke passed across a crowded firepit, a warning, a courtship, a grief too raw to say aloud into a room that will only echo it back distorted. To watch two warren-folk converse is sometimes to hear one conversation and watch an entirely different one running beneath it on their hands.

**Kinship Without Edges**

Children belong, in the day-to-day sense, to whichever adult is nearest the central firepit chambers at the relevant hour, and biological parentage is a fact people know but rarely organize their lives around. This is not coldness; it's architecture. There isn't room, in a forty-body hall, for a family to be a closed thing. A child is minded by whoever is free, fed by whoever is cooking, scolded by whoever witnesses the offense, and grows up with an enormous, diffuse sense of *whose* it is — everyone's, loosely, and no one's, tightly. Pair-bonds and households exist, but they are softer-edged than the surface imagines them, more like gravitational centers than walls.

Old age is not removed from the center of things; if anything it migrates toward it. The fired-skinned elders hold the firepit-adjacent niches by quiet consensus, partly for the warmth their joints now need and partly because the warren has decided, without ever voting on it, that the people closest to becoming wall should sit closest to where the children are made.

**The Dead Underfoot, Overhead, In the Walls**

There is no graveyard because there is no need for one; the wall is the graveyard, has always been the graveyard, will keep being the graveyard for as long as Kelth-Adar is dug. The newly dead are washed, wrapped in fungal-dyed cloth, and sealed into a niche chosen — sometimes years in advance — near a chamber the person loved or worked or raised children in. Over a generation the niche-seal weathers to match the wall around it; over two, it more or less *is* the wall; by the third, a person leaning their tired shoulder against that stretch of corridor has no idea, or every idea, depending on how closely their family kept the count.

This produces an attitude toward ancestry that is wholly unsentimental and wholly intimate at once. The dead are not worshipped from a distance — they are *touched*, daily, by the very act of living in the rooms they built and sealed into. Small offerings are left in wall-niches without much ceremony: a pinch of dried fungus, a thread of dyed wool, the kind of thing you'd hand a relative in the next room rather than burn for a god in the sky. The unnamed idols mentioned in the warren's older corners are not, mostly, depictions of gods at all — they are stylized, worn-smooth little figures of *someone*, a particular dead someone whose actual name slipped out of memory generations back while the gesture of leaving them something kept going out of sheer habit, because stopping would feel like an unkindness to a wall that has never once stopped holding the roof up.

**Roles Worn Like a Second Skin**

Trade in Kelth-Adar is less a job market than a set of inherited postures. The brick-layers and diggers — broad-shouldered, perpetually clay-caked past the elbow — are the ones still pushing the warren's edges outward, and they hold a particular pride that borders on recklessness; it is mostly their dead whose niches sit nearest the unclaimed dark. The moss-readers, often older women and men with exceptionally patient hands, tend the damp corners the way others might tend a sickbed, watching color-shift the way a sailor watches sky, and their warnings about a coming dry spell or a coming flush are taken with real seriousness. The lamp-tenders keep the warren's invented night, walking fixed rounds with a wick-trimmer and a flask of oil, dimming by agreement, never by sun, and they are some of the only people in Kelth-Adar who can tell you, unprompted, exactly what hour it "is." The cat-wardens — usually children, or the elderly who've taken it up as a second childhood — know every warren-cat's territory and temperament by name and treat them as something between livestock, tool, and difficult relative.

And then there are the deep-diggers, the ones who go past the lit and claimed warren into the unlit dark where the walls are sometimes warmer than the absent fire should explain, where a draft sometimes smells, impossibly, of rain. They go in pairs or not at all, mark their path in chalk and touch, and come back — most of the time — with nothing but a story and a slightly different look in those wide pale eyes. They are given a wide berth and a great deal of unspoken respect, the warren's version of people who've stood at the edge of the map and chosen, so far, to come back from it. No one stops them. No one entirely trusts what they say they've seen. The warren has room in its theology, such as it is, for a little permanent unresolved mystery — it tolerates the deep-diggers' stories the way it tolerates the moss on the wall: watched, lived-with, never explained, and somehow, by not being explained, made part of the architecture too.
