# The Tenancy

Call them what they call themselves, when pressed: the Tenancy. Not citizens, not a tribe — tenants, in the oldest and most literal sense, people who hold a lease on a structure that could call it in at any hour, and know it. There are perhaps three thousand of them threaded through the spire at any given time, though nobody's done an honest census in a generation, because a census implies the population sits still long enough to be counted, and the Tenancy does not.

## The Body of the Place

They are lean in a way that reads as deprivation to outsiders and isn't, quite — wiry, economical, built for climbing and for squeezing through gaps rather than for force. Skin goes early to leather: the dry air cracks it at the knuckles and the corners of the mouth no matter how much condensate-grease gets rationed out, and most adults past thirty have the seamed, sun-starved look of people who've spent decades under sodium-orange light and almost none under anything else. Eyes run pale and wide-pupiled, adapted to the perpetual dim, which means a trip to true daylight — on the rare supply-run down and out from under the shadow-country — leaves a Tenant squinting and miserable for days, calling it "the glare-sickness" with real resentment, as if the sun were an inconvenience the world owes them an apology for.

Hearing is the prized sense, and it's trained early. Children are taught the engine-pulse before they're taught to read — taught to feel it in the molars, in the floor of a foot, taught to sort the *good* groan of routine machinery from the *other* groan, the long traveling one that means a face is about to go. There's a saying: *deaf is dead*. It is not metaphorical. Most adults can tell you, with unsettling precision, which tier is failing from three levels away, the way a sailor reads weather in the color of the horizon.

Breathing-masks are everyday wear, not equipment — slung at the throat when not in use, fitted to the face like a second feature when work demands it, and there's an entire small economy of valve-repair, filter-trade, and mask-customization that functions as both craft and a minor form of personal expression. You can tell a fair amount about a Tenant from how they've decorated the thing that keeps them breathing.

## How the Stack Sorts Its People

Altitude is class, more or less, though nobody phrases it that way out loud. The lower levels — squat, load-bearing, true to the old rock — house the oldest families, the foundry-tenders, the engine-minders, people whose work is continuous and unglamorous and whose galleries have stood, in some cases, for three or four generations without major collapse. Lower-stack people are conservative by trade and temperament; they distrust new seams, new scaffolding, anything spindly, and "load-bearing" is the highest compliment one Tenant can pay another.

The upper galleries are where the young and the reckless go — scaffold-riders, seam-prospectors, the crews who bolt new structure onto outcroppings that look like they're holding their breath, because that's where the productive seams currently are and where the money and reputation get made. Upper-stack work pays better and kills more people. Everyone knows the trade and almost everyone makes it anyway, at least once, at least young.

Between the two is the great churning middle — the chain-riders, the cart-pushers, the furnace-feeders, the scrap-pickers' handlers, the mask-menders, the lift-cage operators who spend their whole working lives hauling other people's labor up and down four hundred meters of dark stone on chains thick as a forearm. This middle is where most of the Tenancy actually lives, and it is, by unspoken consensus, where the place's real character resides: practical, quick to mourn and quicker to move on, fond of a particular gallows humor that outsiders find unsettling and locals find load-bearing in its own right.

## A Relationship With Collapse

Loss here is routine, and the Tenancy has built an entire emotional architecture to metabolize it quickly, because it has to. When the long groan comes — and it comes, on average, more than once a season — the catwalks empty in about four seconds, an evacuation reflex bred so deep it doesn't pass through conscious thought anymore; toddlers learn to flatten against rock at that sound before they learn most words. Afterward there is a short, sharp, practical grief — names spoken once at the next shift-meal, never to be spoken again as anything but a memory attached to a tool or a debt — and then the morning crew rebuilds. The dead, where retrievable, are given to the furnace, which is not regarded as desecration. It is regarded as the dead being useful one last time, which, in the Tenancy's working theology, is the closest thing to an honorable end available.

There's no large religion to speak of, but there is a body of small, very specific superstition: spit on a new bolt before it's set; never name a gallery until it's survived its first season; never let a scrap-picker swarm settle on you, because it's a sign the rock under your feet has already decided. None of it is treated with great solemnity. All of it is observed without exception.

## The Listening

Then there's the Hush — what outsiders, if they ever heard of it, would call superstition, and what the Tenancy treats as simple fact requiring no further comment. Every so often, rarely, the deep engines fall into sync across levels with no mechanical link between them, and for a handful of seconds the whole spire goes still — the turbulence pausing, the bass hum resolving into something almost like a single tone — and every Tenant who's old enough to know better stops what they're doing and listens.

Nobody can explain it. Nobody's stopped the engines long enough to find out, because there's a strong, unspoken consensus that finding out is not the point and might be actively unwise. Instead there are Listeners — usually older Tenants, often retired from heavier work, ears too good to waste — whose actual job, paid in shared meals and deference, is to sit near the furnace-levels and notice when the Hush is coming, and tell people, and otherwise say nothing about what they hear in it. Ask a Listener what the engines are doing when they sync, and you'll get a shrug, a long look, and maybe — if you've earned it — something like: *They're not doing anything. They're just, for a second, all in the same place.*

It is the closest thing the Tenancy has to a sacred experience, and it has built no temple around it, no liturgy, nothing but the simple discipline of going quiet when it happens and going back to work the instant it stops — because the spire does not pause for long, and neither, ever, does the Tenancy.
