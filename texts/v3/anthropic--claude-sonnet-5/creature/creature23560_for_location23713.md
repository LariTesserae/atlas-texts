They are called, by the handful of outsiders who have ever spoken of them at all, the tenants — though the word implies a lease, an arrangement with a landlord, and there is no one left to hold that role but the grove itself.

You will not see one on a first pass through. You may not see one on a fifth. What you'll get instead is what's already been described: a paleness at the edge of vision, low among the roots, gone by the time your eyes finish adjusting to where it was. This is not because they are rare, exactly — though they are not many — but because they are slow in a way that reads as absence. A thing that moves at the speed of a dripping leaf does not register to an eye tuned to catch motion. You walk past them the way you walk past the hour hand of a clock.

When one does resolve, finally, into something you can hold in your vision long enough to describe, what you find is a body built for exactly this place and nothing else. Long-limbed, but not in proportion to anything you'd call a frame — more a string of soft, knuckled lengths, jointed at intervals that don't quite match where joints should be, the better to lie along a root or drape through a gap between fused fronds without announcing themselves as a separate object from the vegetation. The skin is pale in the specific way of things that have not seen direct sun in a very long lineage of generations — not white so much as the color of water itself, faintly translucent at the thin places, blue-grey where something darker runs close beneath the surface. In patches, especially along the back and the outsides of the limbs, the pallor is broken by rust — not dirt, not pigment, but actual oxide, ground in and grown into the skin over years of contact with the pipework, until it stops being residue and starts being a kind of marking, individual to each one, the way a wood grain is individual. You could, if you were inclined to that sort of grim accounting, read a tenant's age and travels in its rust the way you'd read a tree's age in its rings.

The face, if you want to call it that, is mostly a face's absence: no need for it. Eyes are present only as a faint paired darkening beneath the skin, vestigial, light-sensing rather than seeing, enough to register the green-gold shift of canopy-light into evening and no more than that. What does the real work is the skin itself, all over, faintly sensitive to vibration and temperature and the particular mineral taste of water — they read the grove the way a deaf man reads a floor through his feet, constantly, full-body, all the time. The mouth, low and small and near the underside of what would be a chin if there were a head distinct enough to have one, is built for slow grazing: the dinner-plate flowers, the algae-skin on standing pools, the thin sweet exudate that beads at certain pipe-joints where mineral water and old lubricant have made something almost edible between them. They are not predators. There is nothing here to predate. They eat the way the leaves eat, by sitting in the path of a good thing and letting it arrive.

What they do — what fills their unhurried days, if day means anything under that shadowless light — is maintain, in the most marginal and unconscious sense of the word. A tenant's long fingers, spending an idle hour draped over a valve-housing, will worry at a seized bolt the way a person worries a hangnail, not toward any purpose, and three years on that valve will turn a half-degree easier than it did. A tenant resting its weight against a cracked pipe-joint for a long sleep will leave, when it finally moves, a thin scar of secretion sealing the crack a little tighter than before. They do not know they are doing this, any more than you know you are breathing. It is closer to a tic than to labor. But multiplied across however many tenants there are, however many decades each one lives — and they live long; nothing this slow burns fast — it adds up to exactly the slow improving custodianship the grove seems to exhibit. They are not the grove's gardeners. They are something more like its white blood cells: present at the site of every small wound, doing something about it without ever convening to decide to.

They do not seem to speak, in any sense you'd recognize, but they are not silent either. At the long intervals when the deep structural clank sounds from somewhere in the grove's iron marrow, every tenant within hearing — and hearing, for them, may mean half the grove — goes still in a way that reads, unmistakably, as attention. Some have been seen, in the half-second after, to flex a hand or curl a limb in something that might be a flinch, might be a greeting, might be neither, only the body answering an old vibration the way a hung bell answers a struck one beside it. Whether this is communication, ritual, reflex, or memory of a function some ancestor tenant actually performed, generations back, when the machines still needed a hand on them — no one has stayed long enough among them to find out. They do not mind the question. They do not appear to notice it's being asked.

They breed rarely and, as far as can be told, without ceremony: a slight swelling, low on the body, over a span of what must be years, and then, in the silt at the bottom of one of the brown-clear pools, something small and stiller than its parent, pale even by tenant standards, eyeless from the start rather than merely undersighted. It will not move on its own for what might be a full season. It will lie in the cistern-mud as part of the grove's substrate, breathing the same wet air as the roots above it, until one day it isn't there, and somewhere nearby there is one more long pale shape draped patient over a pipe, indistinguishable, already at work without working, already a fixture, already nearly invisible.

Nothing about them is dangerous. Nothing about them is, in any active sense, even alive enough to alarm — they will not flee from you and they will not approach you; they will simply continue being where they are, at the speed they are, for as long as the grove continues being what it is. But once you have seen one clearly — really seen it, held it in focus long enough to understand what you're looking at — you cannot walk back through the Sump Engine Groves the same way. Every root looks, after that, like it might uncurl. Every drape of pale stem in shadow might be tissue or might be limb. The grove was already unsettling for being a machine that learned to grow. It is worse, somehow, gentler and worse both, for being tenanted — for having, all along, something patient and pale and rust-marked lying in its dark places, holding it together one unconscious decade at a time.
