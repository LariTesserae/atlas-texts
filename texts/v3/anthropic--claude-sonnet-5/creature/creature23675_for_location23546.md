They are called, by the few outside who still remember the Works exist, *the Soaked* — a slur that became, the way these things do, the name the inhabitants use for themselves with a kind of grim pride. They do not call themselves anything else. There was no founding migration, no single people who arrived; there is only the population that didn't leave fast enough when the water came, and their children, and their children's children, who never knew the place dry and so never thought to call it ruined.

**The Body, Reshaped**

Three generations in, the water has started to write itself into them. Not dramatically — they are still recognizably human, still breathe air, still drown if held under too long — but the margins have shifted. Children learn to hold breath before they learn to read, because the floor of the nursery-tier is sometimes a swim and sometimes a crawl depending on the hour, and nobody has the luxury of teaching the skill later. By adulthood most Soaked can hold a slow two-minute submersion without panic, longer if they've trained for the deep-galleries. Their hands and feet show the cumulative damage of decades of wet-work: skin gone soft and pale and faintly translucent at the fingertips, a permanent low-grade wrinkling that doctors elsewhere would call maceration and the Soaked simply call *hands*. Eyes adjust early to the sodium-orange cones and the blue dark between them; Soaked children have unsettlingly good night vision and unsettlingly bad tolerance for true daylight, on the rare occasions anyone surfaces far enough to see it. Joints ache young. Lungs, conversely, run strong — the lung capacity of a Soaked diver in her thirties would read as athletic-elite anywhere else, and is, here, just what you need to fetch a dropped tool from a flooded sub-level without it becoming a funeral.

They are quiet people, not from temperament but from acoustic necessity: shouting in the Works either vanishes into the white roar or — worse — carries a kilometer to something in the dead galleries that was better left unaware of you. Soaked speech has accordingly gone clipped, low, heavy on gesture, heavy on a particular dialect of taps and clicks against pipe and rail that lets them talk *through* structure when the open air is too loud or too dangerous to talk across. A whole second language, more felt in the molars than heard, exists for exactly this: *one tap, hold — wait. Two-three, fast — drop, now. Long drag — something's coming, don't ask what.*

**How They Live**

Population clusters at the oases — the still-running turbine halls, the active filtration plants, the conveyor junctions that haven't yet gone fully dead — for the same reason the fauna does: heat, light, breathable air-pockets, food. A "town," such as the word applies, is a few hundred people strung along the catwalks and dry shelves of a single still-functioning system, living in the lee of machinery the way an older world's villages clustered at a river bend. The dead zones between oases are commute, not home — traversed in teams, never alone if it can be helped, with weighted rope and a count of taps agreed on before anyone enters the silence.

Work is, overwhelmingly, *maintenance of a purpose nobody remembers*. They keep the pumps running because the pumps running is the difference between an oasis and a drowned gallery; they patch turbines whose design schematics rotted out of memory two generations back, working from inherited intuition and trial and the scar tissue of past mistakes. There is a caste of sorts — not formal, but real — built around this: the *Line-Walkers*, who maintain the conveyor systems and have made an entire ethic out of the rule never to expect cargo at the end of a belt, only ever more belt; the *Listeners*, older Soaked with ruined knees who've traded mobility for an encyclopedic feel for the structure's groans and settlings, who can tell you from three tiers away whether that clang was the building shifting or something walking on it; the *Divers*, young and reckless, who go into the flood corridors for salvage, for eel-meat, for the occasional intact fixture worth cannibalizing, and who die more than anyone else and are mourned the loudest for it.

**Belief, Such As It Is**

They are not a religious people in any structured sense, but they have rites, and the rites are about water and machinery in roughly equal measure, because the two are, to a Soaked, basically the same category of thing: forces older than memory that you do not control, only negotiate with. The dead are given to the deep tiers, not burned or buried — there's neither fuel nor earth for either — sent down through a stress-crack with a weighted line and a name spoken once, because the rule is you say it once and the water keeps it after that. Children are told, not quite as metaphor, that the *something* glimpsed sometimes in the deep flooded levels — the wake, the pressure-change, the tier-wide shudder — is not a monster to be killed but a neighbor to be *unbothered*, the same lesson taught about the turbines: don't stop it, don't anger it, don't go looking for its face. A running machine, to the Soaked, carries a whisper of sanctity that a dead one doesn't; "it still runs" is the closest thing they have to a blessing, said of pumps, of elders, of anything that has, against all reasonable expectation, kept going.

**What They Are, In the End**

They are not heroic and they know it. Nobody is rebuilding anything; nobody believes the water will recede, or that the Works will ever again hold the throughput it was built for. What the Soaked have instead is an extraordinary, unglamorous competence at *persisting inside a machine too large to understand and too dangerous to abandon* — at reading its moods, riding its cycles, harvesting its oases, and staying quiet in its silences. They move through the drowned tiers the way the bat-fish flocks move through the atria: easily, between two mediums that would each, alone, kill a less-adapted thing, going where the warmth and the light and the noise of working machinery tell them it's safe to be alive, and trusting, the rest of the time, to a soft-skinned hand on cold steel, and a single tap, held, that means: *wait. Listen. We're still here.*
