They are, before anything else, a people of the hands and the eyes, because the ears gave out generations ago — not from any single event but from the cumulative arithmetic of living inside a sound that never stops. Hearing loss here is not a disability so much as a developmental stage, like losing milk teeth. By sixty most have lost the upper registers entirely; by seventy, conversation at any distance is theater, all gesture and mouth-shape. Children are taught to read lips in the crèches alongside their letters, taught it the way other towns teach reading aloud, because reading aloud is precisely what doesn't work here. A whole secondary language of hand-shapes has grown up in the gaps between words, used at the foundry, on the stairways, across the wider plazas where leaning close isn't possible — quick economical signs for *water's up*, *valve's blown*, *mind the step*, *I love you*, *not yet*, *soon*. Strangers from quieter places find the silence-within-the-noise unsettling: a hundred people in a market square, all gesturing, all reading each other's mouths, the engines thundering underneath like a held assumption nobody bothers to mention anymore.

Physically they run lean and weathered, sun-creased rather than sun-burnt, skin taking on a faint permanent sheen from the mineral content of everything they touch — the cistern water leaves a fine bloom on hands and forearms that doesn't wash off so much as get redistributed, and old-timers have a soft chalky quality to their knuckles, a kind of fossilization-in-progress that the young find slightly repellent and will, of course, develop themselves in time. Salt rimes their eyebrows by midafternoon. Their hands are the most expressive thing about them and the most damaged — burn-scarred from foundry work, callused from valve-wheels, missing a finger-joint here and there with the unremarked frequency of a place where machinery is old and not always forgiving, and where you do not stop the work to mourn a finger.

Labor is generational and inherited less by choice than by terrace: families cluster by elevation, and elevation correlates roughly with trade. The lower terraces, nearest the foundries and the unexplained machinery that draws water from depths nobody has measured, belong to the engine-tenders — a caste, more or less, though they'd resist the word — people who marry within their own thudding neighborhoods and pass down not just jobs but a kind of inherited fluency with specific machines, the particular cough of pump-engine four, the way valve-stack nine needs to be leaned on just so. They speak of their engines with the proprietary, faintly affectionate exasperation of someone discussing a difficult elderly relative. The upper terraces, quieter by maybe a decibel or two — though no outsider could tell the difference — house the cistern-wardens, who monitor water levels and basin clarity and have a reputation, not entirely joked about, for being a little strange: solitary, prone to crouching at cistern mouths for long stretches doing nothing but listening to the breathing, the way the cool air comes up timed to engines three terraces below in a synchrony nobody has ever managed to explain by pipe or pressure alone.

It is the cistern-wardens, mostly, who maintain the town's central unspoken rule, the one about the plinths. Every child learns early — not through lecture but through the flinch of an adult's hand on their shoulder — that the bare foundation-stones scattered in the gaps between buildings are not for climbing, not for building on, not for asking about past a certain point. The official explanations are thin and contradictory: unstable ground, old property disputes, superstition. Nobody believes the official explanations and nobody offers better ones. There's a working theory, held by almost everyone and stated by almost no one, that those particular sites sit over something the original builders deliberately chose not to cap — a vent, a throat, an old seabed fissure wider or deeper or *different* than the others — and that the town's long bargain with the upward-seeping water depends on leaving a certain number of mouths uncovered, untaxed, alone. It isn't discussed at dinner. It is simply observed, with the same unexamined consistency as not whistling indoors or not pointing at the hawks.

They are not, on the whole, a morose people, despite the noise and the salt and the missing fingers and the slow erosion of their hearing — there's a dry, economical humor that thrives in a place where you must conserve breath to be understood, where a single well-timed gesture across a market square can set a whole terrace laughing without a sound carrying past the third row. They marry late and bury early relative to the gentler lowland towns, but they hold their dead with unusual care, washing the body in cistern water before sending it down through the same iron stairways the living use, as though death here is simply a further descent, one more terrace down, into the machinery and silence underneath everything that makes the noise above possible.

At dusk, when the steam goes gold and the engines settle into their evening rhythm, the old ones gather at the cistern mouths — not to talk, mostly, since talking is work, but to sit in the warm down-draft of mineral-smelling air and watch the moths gather at the relief valves, hundreds of them, pale and silent, spiraling in a hush that the whole rest of the town has long since stopped being able to hear.
