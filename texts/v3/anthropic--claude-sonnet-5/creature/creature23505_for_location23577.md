The first thing you'd be tempted to call them is frogs, because something in you needs a word and that's the nearest one available. But watch longer and the word stops fitting. They are too big for it — haunched things the size of dogs, the size of low stools, squatting half-submerged in the black pools with their backs breaching the surface like wet stones that breathe. Their skin has the same unfinished quality as the ground itself: mottled, glistening, shifting between a liver-dark and a deep mossed green depending on how the dim light catches it, as if pigment hasn't fully settled on a decision either. Their throats work in slow bellows, and it's their low booming notes you hear under everything else — the foundational sound of the place, the bass the rest of the chorus is laid over.

But they are not only frogs. Look at the foreclaws, too articulated, too dry-looking for something that lives wet — long, curved, faintly keratinous, more like something that should belong to a climbing thing. Look at the faint suggestion of feathering, not feathers exactly, along the ridge of the spine — a fringe that might be decorative, might be vestigial, might be on its way to becoming something it hasn't become yet. They sit in the water and they also, when you're not quite looking, are three branches up in the canopy, settled in the crook of a paddle-leaf with the same patient haunch. The same animal. It has not decided between water and height, so it keeps both.

There are smaller cousins of these — fist-sized, faster, skittering in numbers along the leaf-litter, the source of that peripheral shiver you keep almost-catching. And there are larger ones, glimpsed rarely, whose weight you feel in the dip of a branch overhead before you see any part of them at all, whose eyes — when the leaf-shadow gives them up for a second — sit disconcertingly forward on the face, paired and depth-finding, more predator-built than the squat froggish body around them would suggest.

They do not seem to hunt one another. They do not seem to hunt much of anything with urgency — there is no chase here, no real fear-flight, just a constant unhurried repositioning, a population that breeds and crowds and calls and shifts strata but never thins. You don't see carcasses. You don't see bones going soft into the black soil, though the soil is clearly soil enough to take them. Whatever ecology should follow from this many mouths and this much green never quite completes itself. They eat; nothing is seen to be eaten away to nothing. They call; nothing answers with alarm, only with more calling.

It's the eyes that stay with you longest — that wet shine at every level of the place, paired lights low in the pools and high in the leaf-shadow, all of them belonging, you come to suspect, to variations on the same unsettled animal. Not many species crowding one habitat. One species, still negotiating, at every height and every wetness at once — as undecided about its own shape as the ground is about being ground, as the basin is about being water or land, as the whole seethe is about being jungle or swamp or something that hasn't been named because it never had to stop becoming long enough to earn one.

They are, you realize eventually, the place's actual tense. Not inhabitants of a finished world but the held breath itself, given haunches and a throat and that low boom that never resolves into silence — proof, wet-eyed and patient in every story of the canopy, that nothing here intends to finish arriving.
