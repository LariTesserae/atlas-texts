## What lives there

You will not see it arrive. You will see its consequence — the cane-stand trembling four meters off where nothing else moved, the print in the moss already losing its edges, the pelt-color (grey going to dishwater-blue, exactly the shade of the cane at dusk) gone before your eye has finished registering that there was a color at all. This is not because it is rare, particularly. It is because it has had a very long time to become exactly the size, weight, and color of not being seen.

Call it, for lack of any name given to it by anyone who stayed long enough to need one, a **comber**.

It is low — never higher at the shoulder than your knee — and long in the body, built less like a quadruped than like something that used to be a quadruped and has since been stretched and flattened by the demands of moving through collapsing architecture instead of over open ground. Its feet are the first thing you'd notice if you ever got one to hold still: broad, fan-toed, padded with a dense fibrous tissue that mimics, with uncanny fidelity, the give of the root-mat itself. It doesn't walk on the mat so much as negotiate with it, distributing its weight across each footfall the way a hand distributes weight across a page it doesn't want to tear. This is why the prints spring back. The comber is not light. It has simply spent longer than the plateau's recorded ice ages learning to be unfelt.

Its coat is oiled, dense, double-layered — an undercoat for the wet cold, an outer guard-hair for shedding the constant damp before it reaches skin — and colored in long unbroken gradients that match, stalk for stalk, the blue-green-to-ash transition of the cane around it. No spots, no countershading in the usual sense; the comber's whole body is essentially a long smear of camouflage tuned to a single, specific, ubiquitous plant. It is an animal that has bet everything, evolutionarily, on this one stand of reed never disappearing. So far the bet has paid out for longer than memory.

It is blind, or very nearly. There is no advantage to sight in light this level and this filtered, in a place whose visual landscape rewrites itself every few nights anyway — what good is memorizing a path that won't exist by morning? Instead its skull tapers forward into a long sensitive muzzle ringed with stiff bristles, and beneath the fur along its spine and flanks runs a second, denser array of the same bristle, root-set deep enough to feel transmitted vibration rather than just air movement. The comber reads the plateau the way you might read a held string — tension, slack, the particular shudder of a fungal column two days from failing versus one that's sound for another year. It does not get caught in the collapses. It has never needed to be fast, only early.

It eats slowly and constantly: moss where the moss pools deep enough to hold grubs and the slow soft rot that grubs imply; the pithy interior of fallen cane, opened with foreclaws built more for splitting fiber than for digging; the spongy, slightly sweet flesh just inside a fungal column's rind, harvested only from columns it has already felt dying, so that "harvest" and "scavenge" are, for the comber, the same word. It is, in this sense, the plateau's only mourner and its only undertaker — the one creature that attends every collapse, just slightly ahead of it, just outside the blast radius of falling cane, feeding on what the architecture gives up before it gives up the rest of itself to the next stand's roots.

It does not call. None of them do — there is no comber-song, no territorial cry, nothing pitched where your ear could catch it. What they have instead, transmitted not through air but down through the packed root-mat that is, after all, one continuous structure for kilometers in every direction, is a low, periodic, deliberate flex of the chest and forelimb — a controlled, percussive *push* against the mat, repeated, modulated, sent out the way you'd send a signal down a wire. A single comber's signal wouldn't carry far. But there are more of them than you would ever guess from how rarely you see one, spaced out across the plateau at some old, agreed distance, each one relaying what it last felt — a failing column to the east, clear ground to the north, the press of something larger moving wrong through a stand three ridges over — on down the line, mat to mat, until the signal has traveled farther than any single creature's voice ever could.

It is possible — no one has stayed long enough to be sure, and the comber would certainly not announce it — that this relay is what you hear as the engine-exhale, the ozone tang, the long mechanical sigh that never resolves into a source. The chest-push, magnified by enough root-mat acting as a single drumhead kilometers wide, might sound, from the outside, very much like something vast and built breathing in a valley you'll never reach. The metal smell might be nothing more than the comber's own scent-marking, oiled and oxidized, laid down along signal-routes the way a hand might worry the same doorframe for a hundred years. Or it might not be that at all. The plateau keeps its own accounting, and the comber — small, blind, patient, older by temperament than the young violent cane it lives inside of — has no obligation to settle the matter for you.

What's certain only is this: something here has outlasted every stand of reed it was ever born into, has felt every collapse a full season before it happened, and has decided, across more generations than the ice-combed valleys below can date, that the correct way to survive a world this unstable is to never be caught standing on the part of it that's about to fall.
