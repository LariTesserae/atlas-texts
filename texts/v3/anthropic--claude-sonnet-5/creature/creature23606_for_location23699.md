## Inhabitants

You will see them before you understand that you have seen them — a economy of movement so unhurried it reads, at first, as stillness misremembered. The dust-toned garments are not clothing in the sense of a thing put on over a body, not quite; they drape and fold in a manner consistent with cloth, but the way they catch the directionless light suggests something closer to a second skin grown to resemble cloth, the way the stone grew to resemble masonry. Underneath — when the wind, rare and soft, lifts a hem or sleeve — there is an impression of too many joints, or too few, an anatomy that seems to have been negotiated rather than evolved, settled on as a workable compromise between a body and the act of standing upright in a place where upright is not always a fixed direction.

Their faces, on the occasions one turns toward you, offer little to hold onto. Not blankness — there is clearly attention there, a weight of regard that lands on you like the held-breath wind — but the features arrange themselves with the same unhurried imprecision as the streets: an eye where you expected a mouth's shadow, a stillness around the brow that might be patience or might be the simple absence of any need to express it. They do not startle. Nothing here startles. When a terrace they are crossing folds quietly into the one beside it, they continue walking, and arrive, and this arrival is not treated as a small miracle but as the unremarkable conclusion of an unremarkable errand.

It is difficult to determine their number because the city does not hold population the way other places do. They seem to be sparse not because they are few, exactly, but because they are *distributed* — across the terraces, yes, but also, it begins to seem, across the discontinuities themselves, so that the figure you watched climb out of sight on one stairwell and the figure who appears, slightly later, on a stairwell two geometries over, may be continuations of a single unhurried errand rather than two separate lives. They do not need a city sized to hold a crowd because they do not occupy the city all at once. They occupy it the way the mountain occupies its own height: provisionally, recursively, with the unconcern of something that has never needed to finish arriving.

What they do, in the doorways and plazas and at the heads of stairwells that end in open air, resembles tending. Not labor exactly — there is no visible making, no visible repair, the stone needs none of that — but a kind of attendance, as if their function is to be present at the city's seams, to stand witness at the places where one geometry concedes to another, the way a held breath needs someone to notice when it becomes stillness. The door standing open three tiers up, the one that gives onto darkness: someone is keeper of that door, though you will never see them enter it, only ever see them nearby, facing it, in the attitude of someone listening to a room rather than someone about to use it.

The long-necked slate-colored bird that stands on parapets and is then simply gone — this, you come to suspect, is not a separate species so much as a posture the inhabitants themselves can fall into, at the very highest tiers, where the architecture has stopped pretending to be for bodies and the inhabitants, accordingly, stop pretending to need bodies built for streets. It is what one of them looks like when it has climbed past the last useful stair and decided, in the manner of the mountain deciding how tall it wants to have been, to become briefly a different kind of thing — something that can be motionless without it costing anything, something built to stand at the edge of three hundred feet of open air and find that a reasonable place to wait.

And the tone that moves through the stone underfoot, gone before you can decide it happened — this, almost certainly, is speech. Not a voice raised, but a voice so slow that its sentence may take a generation, the city's true population conducting, in the register of the stone itself, a conversation that the dust-robed figures and the parapet-birds and the folding terraces are merely the visible punctuation of. They are not separate from the city's argument with its own geometry. They are how that argument manifests at the scale of something that can be looked at, briefly, before it recedes, the way the summit recedes, into a tier of itself you have not yet learned to call by the right name.
