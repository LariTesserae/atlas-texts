# Those Who Tend the Unfinished

They are called, in the nearest translation anyone outside the shelf has managed, the **Ördûni** — though that name flattens something that resists flattening, since what lives here was never quite one thing to begin with.

You see them first as scale problems. A doorway sized for a human-shaped body, and through it, glimpsed only briefly, something that has to fold itself nearly in half to use the same threshold the boulevard outside was built wide enough to never require. This isn't error. It's accommodation, the architecture's way of admitting that more than one kind of intelligence keeps house here, and has for longer than anyone has bothered to count.

## The Door-Folk

The smaller of them — human-scaled, more or less, though "scaled" undersells how loosely that holds — move along the gantry-rims and the half-finished foundations with the unhurried attentiveness of people checking on something that is mostly fine without them and will go on being mostly fine. They're slight, long-limbed in a way that reads as adaptation to thin air and a lifetime of climbing wet stone — fingers a little too long, knuckles a little too many, skin with a faint sheen to it that isn't sweat and isn't oil, something the body produces to keep the perpetual damp from finding purchase in the lungs. Their eyes have gone pale, not blind but tuned to flat grey light, pupils that contract slow and stay wide, built for a sky that can't agree with itself.

They carry instruments that hum a half-tone before they sound — tools that read the city's growth the way a physician reads a pulse, not directing it so much as listening for where it's about to go and nudging, very slightly, very rarely. This is the whole of what counts as their labor: not building, but *attending*. They walk the seams where wall is still deciding to be wall, lay a hand or an instrument against the warm not-quite-stone, and either let it continue or, on the rare occasion something is growing toward a configuration that won't hold weight, redirect it with a touch so light it looks like blessing rather than correction.

They don't live in dense clusters. They live in the gaps the city leaves on purpose, threading themselves through the boulevards built too wide for them, into chambers built exactly to their measure, as if every structure here secretly contains a person-shaped room somewhere inside its inhuman shape, and finding it again each evening is simply what going home means.

## What the Boulevards Were Widened For

The wide ways weren't built for the door-folk, and the door-folk will tell you so without much concern, the way you'd mention which rooms in your house are technically your sibling's. Something larger moves through this city on its own schedule — glimpsed at dusk more than dawn, low-slung, many-jointed in a way that makes "many-jointed" sound too tidy, built closer to the ground than its height would suggest because it spends as much time leaning into the rock as standing free of it. It is, near as the door-folk reckon, *kin* — not pet, not livestock, not separate species so much as a different expression of the same patient lineage, the way a tree and its root system are both unmistakably the tree.

These ones do the heavier listening. Where the door-folk read the city's growth at the scale of a wall or a gantry, the larger kin seem to feel it at the scale of the shelf itself — pressing themselves flat against new foundations for hours at a time, utterly still, in postures that look like grief or prayer and are probably neither. When they move, the city seems to lean very slightly toward wherever they've gone, as if their attention is itself a kind of nutrient.

They are not unfriendly to the smaller kin. They are something closer to elders who have stopped needing to speak, and the smaller ones simply grew up understanding the silence.

## What Answers from the Rock

The chittering that starts at dusk is not these two. It comes from deeper — something small enough to live in the pore-spaces of stone that hasn't finished setting, something that the city's slow growth doesn't displace because it has learned to live exactly at the pace the rock changes, migrating cell by cell through seams as they widen, the way a creature might migrate through a forest as the seasons turn the canopy. No one among the door-folk has ever held one. They don't try. The rock-dwellers' chittering is taken, casually and without much examined faith, as a kind of weather report — a sign the substrate beneath a given quarter is stable enough to be worth lying down on. When the chittering goes quiet in a district, the door-folk start avoiding it, and within a season that district is usually mid-transformation, walls breathing harder, the city deciding something new about itself.

Whether the rock-dwellers are a third intelligence, an instinct of the larger kin extended into the stone, or simply the place itself developing a nervous system one neuron at a time — nobody asks. It isn't considered an urgent question. Time, here, is abundant; certainty is not required to keep living well.

## A Population That Doesn't Need to Be Large

There are not many of any of them, not in city terms. The plazas built two hundred meters across with a single structure at their center aren't empty because the builders died or left — they're sized the way they are because a handful of attentive lives, properly distributed, is what a place like this actually requires. The Ördûni do not seem to experience this as loneliness. They experience it, if their unhurried movement through all that generous space is any evidence, as room enough to do the work properly — the work being, in the end, simple: to keep living inside something still being made, to notice when it needs a hand, and otherwise to let it become whatever it is in the process of becoming, on a timescale that doesn't require either party to hurry toward an ending.
