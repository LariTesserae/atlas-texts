**The Wardkeep families.**

You would expect, given the place, to find hermits — solitary cultivators gone half-feral among their own greenery, or else squatters who drifted in after the town's official reason for existing dried up with the river. You would be only partly right, and the part you're wrong about is the more important part. The Greenhouse Wards are inhabited, generation-deep, by a small cluster of families — perhaps thirty or forty people in all, counting the children, counting the very old — who did not drift in but were *born* to this arrangement, who hold a kind of hereditary tenancy over which conveyor gets oiled, which gas-lamp gets its mantle replaced, which vine gets allowed to thicken into a load-bearing limb and which gets cut back before it can make that claim. They are not the descendants of whoever decided, a century back, to let the plants in. They are the decision's continuation. Nobody among them could tell you the original reasoning, but every one of them could tell you, without having to think about it, whether a particular stretch of fig-root is this year due for trimming or another decade of quiet, structural trust.

**Appearance and bearing.**

They are unremarkable to look at in the way that people who work outdoors in cool, damp, filtered light tend to become unremarkable — weathered rather than worn, a little stooped from decades of walking through understory that never quite lets a person stand at full height, hands marked less by calluses than by the fine, permanent green-black staining that comes from handling wet moss and oiled iron in the same afternoon. They dress practically: waxed canvas, leather aprons gone soft and dark, the kind of close-buttoned coat that keeps out damp without trapping heat, because the ward is never hot enough to need shedding clothing and never wet enough to need true waterproofing — only that持续, in-between dampness the place itself runs on. Many keep a short hooked blade at the belt, less a weapon than a tool for one specific job: testing whether a vine has gone structural yet, the way you'd tap a wall to hear if it's load-bearing.

There is a particular stillness to how they walk through the canopy-dark lanes — not caution, exactly, but a kind of practiced unhurriedness, as if hurrying through a system that runs on decades would be a category error, a misunderstanding of what kind of place this is and what kind of attention it wants.

**Work, and what counts as work here.**

Their labor is maintenance in the truest, oldest sense: not building, not really repairing, but *tending the accommodation*. A wardkeep's day might include relighting a lamp the mechanism failed to catch, checking the tension where a vine has been allowed to take over a failing lintel's job, walking the conveyor's rail twice to make sure the slats haven't been overtaken faster than the schedule can tolerate, climbing the gasworks tower's wrapped flank to check that the woody vine sheathing the hot chimney hasn't dried out and turned from insulation into kindling. None of them would call this gardening, exactly, and none of them would call it engineering either. The closest word, if you pressed one of the older ones for it, might be *husbandry* — herding a relationship rather than building a thing, the same way a shepherd doesn't build a flock.

Children here are put to small versions of this work early — not from cruelty but from the practical fact that the ward's whole logic has to be learned by feel, over years, the way you'd learn the moods of a large, slow, mostly benevolent animal. A child's first job is usually the dry fountain in the market square: clearing the fern-choke from the basin every few weeks, not because the fountain will ever run again, but because — this gets explained to every child the same way, in almost the same words, generation on generation — *a clearing has to be kept a clearing, or it stops being one, and then you don't have a clearing anymore, you just have less of the green than somewhere else.*

**Temperament.**

They are quiet people, but not withdrawn — quiet the way the ward itself is quiet, a baseline hush punctuated by specific, known, unalarming sounds. They speak economically, in the manner of people whose work doesn't require much narration, and they have a settled, unbothered relationship with the strangeness of their own home that can read, to a passing visitor, as either wisdom or a kind of mild incuriosity. Ask one of them whether it troubles them that the vines are *doing a job* — that the moss insulates on purpose, that the root system seems, in places, almost too conveniently distributed for pure accident — and you'll get something between a shrug and a correction: *of course it's doing a job. That's the arrangement. What did you think we were keeping?*

There is no priesthood here, no formal rite around any of this, but there is something rite-adjacent in the small repeated gestures — the way an elder will rest two fingers against a particular thick vine-trunk before climbing past it, not in supplication exactly, more the gesture you'd make checking a colleague has clocked in for their shift.

**The old ones, and the unusual note in them.**

The eldest among the wardkeep families have a quality that younger visitors notice and can't quite name — a slowness to their responses that isn't infirmity, a stillness in company that goes a beat past comfortable, as though some part of their attention is always running on the ward's own patient clock rather than a conversation's faster one. They sleep, it's said, with their windows unshuttered to the leaf-press even in the cool months. They are the ones who seem to know, without checking, when a vine has quietly finished a decades-long job of taking over a failed beam's weight — who'll mention it in passing, the way you'd mention a neighbor's recovery from a long illness. Whether this is simply the deep, unglamorous expertise of people who've watched one place closely for seventy years, or something closer to what the ward itself seems to be doing to its architecture — a slow, patient incorporation, tenant becoming a little bit of the tenancy — is not a question anyone in the Wards finds urgent enough to ask aloud. They have work in the morning. The conveyor runs at its hour regardless.
