# The Roost-Kin

They have no agreed name, because nothing here was around to name them — but if you spent long enough among the gantries, dodging the drip-patterns, learning which rail-lengths are safe to grip and which are runneled slick, you would start calling them what your body calls them before your mind catches up: *the kin*. Not out of affection. Out of the same instinct that names a recurring weather. They are simply what is here, in such quantity that the word for them becomes a word for the place itself.

## Body

Pale — not white, but the pale of something that has never needed pigment because no eye selecting for camouflage ever passed through this generation or the last ten thousand. The pallor has a faint waxy sheen, condensation-slick, as if their skin were just another cold surface the room had condensed onto. They are roughly the size of a folded umbrella when at rest, larger when the wing-membranes — four of them, vestigial-seeming, asymmetric, more like ailerons than true wings — unfurl to glide the short distances between perches. They do not fly far. They don't need to. The architecture brings the next perch within reach if you wait.

Too many limbs for what limbs are typically for: a forward pair clearly built for gripping pipe and rail, hooked and strong; a rear pair more vestigial, dragging slightly, vestigial the way a snake's hip-bones are vestigial — evidence of a use phased out by the environment doing the work instead. Between these, smaller manipulator-digits, three or four per side, fine enough to work condensation-blooms apart from gasket seams, to groom crystalline residue from each other's flank-fur, to do small precise things in a place built at industrial scale.

Their heads are mostly mouth and ear. The eyes are an afterthought — small, lightless-environment eyes, milky, more light-sensing than image-forming, because there has never been enough light here to make seeing worth the investment, and because the ears do the seeing's job better. Ears like furled umbrellas themselves, oversized, constantly swiveling, layered with fine cartilage ridges that the engineers — if engineers there were — clearly tuned to specific frequencies, the way a tuning fork is tuned, not by chance but by design.

## Voice

The shriek-click-thrum that fills the Works is not noise to them. It's address. Each kin has a voice slightly different in pitch from its neighbor — close enough to blend in the wall-of-sound from outside, distinct enough that, inside the roost, they are calling each other by something like a name. A mother (if that word even applies to whatever reproductive logic governs them) can find her clutch in total dark and total din by pitch alone, the way you'd find your own name spoken in a crowded room.

The bass drone of the engines is not separate from this. It's the floor under their music — literally, the register their calls are pitched against, the way a singer pitches against a held drone-note. When certain pipes hum at their particular triggering frequency, the whole roost answers in unison, one bar of unbearable unified sound, and then disperses back into chaos, like a held breath let go. No one knows what the unison is for. The kin don't seem to know either. It happens the way a yawn happens — contagious, automatic, satisfying, purposeless to the one yawning.

## Feeding

They lap condensation directly from cold metal — that constant bead-and-drip is, for them, a standing buffet, always replenishing, never quite gone. But the real feeding, the deep feeding, happens at the crystalline blooms: the mineral residue that crusts certain pipes where the chemical-green vent-glow leaks hardest. The kin gather there in dense, patient clusters, working the crust apart with their fine manipulator-digits, and there is something unsettlingly deliberate in the way they tend those blooms rather than simply stripping them — picking around the edges, leaving the center crystal intact, returning days later to find it regrown.

This is the detail that, if you stood and watched long enough, would start to bother you: the crystal blooms thicken faster, grow more reliably, in exactly the spots the kin tend most carefully. As though the tending isn't harvest but husbandry. As though somewhere in the original design, the relationship was inverted on purpose — the machine built to produce a crop, the kin built to cultivate it, both of them mistaking the arrangement for an ecosystem when it was always, from the first rivet, a farm.

## Roosting and Rank

They do not roost at random. The gantry-joints nearest the warm conduits hold the dense, settled clusters — old kin, you'd guess, by the crusted residue worked permanently into their flank-fur, the slow-blinking milk-eyes, the way they don't startle at near passes the way the outer-ring kin do. Move outward from the warm joints and the roosting gets looser, younger, more reactive — juveniles who haven't yet earned, or aren't yet trusted with, a place near the heat.

There's no visible fighting over this. No display, no blood. Whatever sorts them into rings happens too slowly and too quietly to watch — the way sediment sorts itself by weight in still water, not through contest but through settling. You get the sense that the structure of the roost predates every kin currently living in it, that they are filling positions, not creating them; that a kin who dies near a warm conduit is, within the hour, occupying that exact same square foot with a different body.

## What They Do With Stillness

For a population pressed into a space at total capacity, deafening, restless-seeming, they are — at the center of it — strangely unbothered. They have never known the roost less full. They have never known the engines quieter or the drip-rate slower. There is no memory among them, individual or collective, of a Condensation Works any different from this one, because there has never been a different one to remember. What looks, from outside, like chaos at maximum tolerance reads, from inside the roost, as simple permanence — the kin equivalent of weather, of gravity, of the fact that water runs down and not up.

This is perhaps the strangest thing about them, stranger than the extra limbs or the milk-eyes or the unison-call: they are not surviving the Condensation Works. They are not enduring it, adapting to it, pushing against its limits the way a creature in an evolving world might. They are simply, fully, the shape the Works has always had room for — exactly as many of them as there are pipe-joints to hold, exactly as loud as the architecture can carry, exactly as hungry as the crystal regrows to feed. Tuned, like everything else here, to a dial that was set once and then welded fast.

They will be here, unchanged, in this exact density, calling in this exact register, for as long as the engines run. Nothing in the room suggests the engines are capable of stopping.
