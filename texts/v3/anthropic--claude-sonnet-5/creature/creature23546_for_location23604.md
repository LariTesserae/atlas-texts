# What Answers the Echo

## The Many, the Small, the Heard-Not-Seen

Most of what fills this hollow with noise is small enough to die in your palm and built for exactly one thing: knowing the dark better than the dark knows itself.

Call them, for lack of any name they'd recognize, the **chitterlings** — though that word does a disservice to the dozen or more kinds that travel under it. Some are many-legged and fast, chitin-cased, moving in the quick scuttling bursts you catch at the edge of vision; these graze the fungal shelves and lichen-mats, scraping them down to the rock with mouthparts built like tiny rakes, then scattering at the first vibration of something larger. Some fly — badly, erratically, more falling-with-control than true flight — on membranous wings grown translucent from disuse of color, navigating by the click-trains that ring constantly through the vault. Their eyes, on the rare animal that still has them, are milk-pale and nearly useless, vestigial windows kept open out of habit rather than need. What replaces sight is a whole second nervous architecture built around *return* — every one of these creatures is, in some fashion, talking to the ceiling and floor and far walls constantly, and listening to what comes back.

They breed fast, die fast, and exist in numbers that the sparse green shouldn't support — until you understand they aren't really living off the lichen at all, not primarily. Dig a few inches into any damp patch and you find the truth of the place: a soft gray underweb of fungal mycelium, threaded through the broken rock for kilometers, drawing some slow nutrient seep down from above and turning it into biomass nobody above ground would recognize as a food source. The lichen and the pale stalks are just the parts of that web that happen to show. The chitterlings graze the hidden mat as much as the visible growth, and so the hollow feeds far more mouths than its garden should allow.

Above the chitterlings, larger things move with less hurry — pale quadrupeds the rough size of dogs or small deer, thick-bodied and slow-blooded, that browse the fungal shelves in unhurried clusters and lumber off into the gray distance when the rock shivers underfoot. These are the "slow gray mass" glimpsed at range: heavy-lidded, short-legged, with broad flat feet adapted to the loose rubble and long drooping ears that do for them what eyes do for surface animals. They are placid, herd-minded, and almost entirely undefended, which is why something else has learned to hunt them.

## The Hunters Between

Thinner shapes move through the herds at a respectful distance, low to the ground, built long rather than broad — the hollow's predators, sparse the way everything substantial here is sparse, because density above a certain size simply cannot be supported twice over. They hunt by waiting in the gaps between clusters of noise, in those long quiet stretches of bare stone the chitterlings and grazers cross only when they must, and they kill quickly, without ceremony, because there is no cover here to make a hunt last. Their calls — the long low rolling ones that return as echo over echo — are not communication alone. They are reconnaissance, mapping a kilometer of empty dark in a single cry, the predator listening to its own voice come back changed by everything it touched on the way.

## The Ones Who Carry Hands

And then, passing through all of this, rarely, leaving almost no trace but never quite none — the tool-makers.

They are not numerous. You could walk the hollow for what felt like a season, by whatever measure of time means anything down here, and never see one — only find, near a damp seam or a fungal shelf, the discarded evidence: a flake struck true off a stone core, an edge worked with patience no chitterling possesses and no slow gray grazer could conceive of. They are upright, or upright enough — bipedal in their better moments, knuckling along on longer forelimbs when speed matters more than dignity — and their bodies show the same compromises as everything else here: reduced eyes, pale or absent pigment, an enlarged and acute sense crowded into ears or whiskered snout or some sensory apparatus that doesn't map neatly onto a surface animal's face.

What separates them isn't size or strength — the slow grazers outweigh them, the predators outpace them. It's the forward motion of thought made visible in stone: the same flake-shape struck again and again across the rubble, a standard they're holding themselves to even with no one to show it to. They pass through in what might be small kin-groups, judging by clustered tool-finds, and they do not stay. No shelter, no hearth-mark, no worked wall. They strike an edge, use it, discard it, and move on into the dark toward the next damp patch, the next fungal shelf, the next stretch of grazing slow-things to take down with a sharpened flake and a patience the hollow has otherwise forgotten how to need.

Nobody knows — not even, perhaps, they themselves — where they're going, or whether "going" is the right word for movement with no destination across a hollow that has no edge. They simply pass, leave a mark, and pass again, the one thread of forward intention drawn taut through a world that has otherwise settled, completely and forever, into its own circling equilibrium.
