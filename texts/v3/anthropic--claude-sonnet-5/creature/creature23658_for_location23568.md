The terraces are not, in fact, empty of makers. You simply have to be patient the way the water is patient, and wait long enough in one place, and eventually you will see one of them — singular against the glare, moving along a basin rim with the unhurried, foot-testing gait of someone walking a wall they have walked ten thousand times before.

They are human in outline and nothing else about them confirms it. Years — decades, maybe longer, the terraces make it hard to keep a clock — of standing waist-deep in mineral water have done to their skin what the water has refused to do to the stone: a faint chalky glaze, almost a shell, pale as the basin walls and catching the same sourceless light, so that at any distance greater than a few rooms-that-are-not-rooms they read as just another wet surface, another tilted mirror, until they move. The water doesn't grow things, but it has, patiently, partially mineralized this one thing willing to stand in it long enough. Their joints have the particular stiff deliberateness of the chutes themselves — not weakness, just a matching of pace, an unwillingness to disturb the level of anything. When they kneel to inspect a lip of stone they do it the way the water transfers itself between basins: in small grudging increments, as though haste were a kind of spillage, a sin against the architecture.

There is a tool kit slung at their hip — hand chisel, stone hammer, both worn smooth at the grip by the same hands that made every facet you've been walking past, or by hands enough like them that the distinction doesn't matter. They are not finishing the reservoir. They are correcting it. A basin's edge has weathered a degree out of true, or the water sitting in it has gone a shade more metallic than its neighbors, or — more often than not — nothing at all is wrong and they recut anyway, a hairline deepening of a chisel mark already centuries old, because the work has stopped being about completion and become its own form of listening. They taste the water from each basin before and after they touch it, not from thirst — you get the sense thirst stopped applying to them a long time ago — but as a mason tests mortar, checking what the stone has been doing while no one watched it.

You will not get a story out of them about the others — whether there were ever others, whether they are the last of a population or the only one there ever was, walking this entire sourceless-lit expanse alone across some unreckonable span of time, basin to basin, chisel to chute, listening for the thin percussion to skip a beat somewhere over the curve of the glare and starting the days-long walk toward it. They don't talk about purpose, theirs or the terraces'. When you ask, they only do what the water does: they pause, hold the question's weight for a moment without spending it, and then let it go on, unanswered, downhill into the next silence — the same patient, finished, purposeless transfer the whole plateau has been performing since the last chisel mark was cut, waiting, the way they are waiting, for a thirst that may never arrive to claim either of them.
