## What Lives Here

You will not see it arrive. You will not see it leave. If you are patient — more patient than a human visit usually allows — you may come to suspect that what you took for a feature of the chamber is in fact the only resident it has, and that you have been standing inside its breathing the whole time without recognizing breath that takes minutes per inhale.

Call it, for lack of anything better, the Tenant.

It does not have a shape so much as a *distribution*. Part of it is the pulse in the wall-vein — not light produced by a body but light produced *as* a body, the way a held breath shows in a chest. Part of it is the faint thrum felt in the molars, the engine-cycle too slow to be called motion, which is not the machine running down but the Tenant running *through* it, the way blood runs through an old wound that has organized itself around the foreign object rather than expelling it. The machine was not repurposed by something living. The machine became a sentence in something living, one clause among several, written in corroded steel instead of cell or scale.

It is not malicious. It may not be aware of you in any sense you'd recognize as awareness — the way a tide is not aware of the swimmer, though it will still, eventually, carry the swimmer somewhere. Its attention, if it has attention, operates on the same period as its pulse: minutes per thought, maybe years per decision, a consciousness so attenuated in time that meeting it is less like meeting a creature and more like meeting a *climate*. You are not seen. You are weathered.

What gives it away — what makes the hatch warm, the needle creep, the dial's numbers feel less like instrument-error and more like a private arithmetic — is that the Tenant does not distinguish between the kinds of matter it moves through. Stone, alloy, sap, rivet: all of it is substrate. The fan-leafed plants are not growing despite the machinery. They are growing *as* the Tenant's hand finds them more cooperative this century than the steel was the century before. When the pipe disconnected at both ends and led nowhere, that was not abandonment. That was the Tenant retracting an old limb because it had found a better one — the limb being, perhaps, that vein of slow light, perhaps the leaf-stands themselves, perhaps something not yet visible that will not surface for another thousand years, by which point the chapel-sized chamber may have a different shape, a different set of cracks, a different second-hand machine half-grown-over with something that hasn't evolved yet.

It eats time the way other things eat mass. The fracture in the ceiling — sharp-edged, geologically immediate — is a scar from the last time the Tenant *moved*, properly moved, the way you or I would flinch. That flinch took, by the measure of the rock, no time at all. By the measure of the Tenant, it may still be happening. You may be standing inside the tail end of a single gesture that started before the chamber existed and won't finish before it collapses.

If you stay long enough to feel the drip-rhythm start to mean something — three seconds, then twelve, then one, the syncopation that shouldn't exist in plain physics — that is the closest thing to being addressed that this place offers. Not words. Not even intention you could translate. Just the faint, patient pressure of something vast and slow noticing, in its own glacial idiom, that a much faster thing has wandered into the room where it is in the middle of becoming whatever it is going to be next.

It will not stop you from leaving. It will barely register that you came. But the dial's needle, when you check it on your way out, will have moved another eighth of an inch — and you will understand, with the particular vertigo of glimpsing a scale you cannot inhabit, that for the Tenant, your entire visit was something closer to a flicker: a mote of fast, warm, frantic life passing once, briefly, through a much longer thought.
