## What Fills the Reef

The herders of the high passes call them *coal-cattle* when they're feeling plain, and *the patient ones* when they're not, and they will tell you both names in the same breath without noticing the contradiction. No one has held one up to a lamp and made a drawing that another person agreed with afterward. This is not because they are formless. It is because they do not hold still long enough, in mind or under hand, to be fixed.

**The body.** Take a shape somewhere between a tortoise and a coal—a low domed back, a soft dark underside that never quite touches ground because the ground itself is always someone else's back. The dome is plated, not in scale or shell exactly, but in something like cooled slag fractured into a mosaic of facets, each facet rimmed in a thread of that banked inner glow. The light is not on the surface; it comes from beneath it, the way embers show through ash, the way a coal looks black until you breathe on it. There is no visible mouth that anyone has confirmed, though there are seams along the underside that some swear open during the brightening tides—not to eat, not to speak, just to vent, the way a kiln has a flue.

They have limbs, four or six depending on who you ask and how many you can see at once in a press that deep—short, splayed, suited only to the slow climbing-over that is their whole manner of movement. They were not built to walk a long way. They were built, if "built" is a word that applies, to be where the others already are, and to make of themselves one more surface for the next one to stand on.

**The glow as language, or weather, or both.** The friction-sound the basin makes is, herders agree, the sound of the coal-cattle's plates sliding against one another as they shift and resettle—not speech, but not quite silence either, the way a held breath is not silence. The sharper clicks that ripple outward in brightening waves are taken locally as a kind of *attention passing through*—not an alarm exactly, more like a name spoken into a crowded room, picked up and repeated by whoever's near enough to catch it, fading by the time it reaches the walls. Some old herders claim the brightening tides are the basin *thinking*, slow and total, the way a sleeping animal's whole side rises with one breath. Nobody has stayed long enough across enough cycles to say whether this is sentiment or simply correct.

**What going dark means.** A body that dims, stiffens, and is absorbed by the press is not, by the best local accounting, dying in the way a sheep dies. It is understood instead as *spending itself*—giving its banked heat back into the seam below, into the next body over, into whatever keeps the basin's total warmth constant. New shapes appearing at the walls are understood, just as readily, as the same heat finding a new shape to wear. Whether this makes the coal-cattle one creature wearing ten thousand bodies, or ten thousand creatures sharing one inheritance, is a distinction the herders consider a city person's problem. They simply say: *the reef does not lose count*. It has never needed to be fed because it does not, in any way they recognize, consume. It only redistributes what the dark rock beneath continues, for reasons no one living understands, to give it.

**The larger shapes at the edge.** These are rarely described twice the same way, which the herders take as proof rather than embarrassment—of course the elders look different to different eyes; they are old enough to have worn many of the small bodies before settling, decades or centuries back, into whatever they are now. Dog-sized, the report says, though "dog" undersells the wrongness of a creature whose every facet still carries that same banked ember-light, just slower, just deeper, the orange gone the dark red of a coal an hour from cold. They keep to the basin's far rim, near the scree, and are seen migrating toward the hollow chambers in the slope when they go dark for good—folding themselves into the wasp-nest interior of the wall the way a coal is raked back into the heart of the fire to keep. No one has followed one in. No one who has tried describes the chambers the same way twice, either.

**Regarding the ring, the platform, the post.** The coal-cattle show no behavior toward these that could be called reverence, exactly, but they show no behavior toward them that could be called indifference either. The ring of stacked black stone is never climbed; bodies part around its threshold even when the press elsewhere is climbing three deep. The platform is buried under them, yes, but never *displaced*—plate by plate, generation by generation of redistributed heat, the weight resting on it has stayed exactly three bodies deep for as long as anyone has measured, which suggests either an old habit too deep to break or an old instruction still being followed by something that no longer remembers giving it. The post at the center is the one place in the basin kept entirely clear, a single bare ring of cracked black ground a few feet across, with the coal-things piled close around its base in a ring that the herders, without irony, call *the offering*, though nothing is ever placed there and nothing is ever taken away.

Whoever built the ring and the platform and raised the post is gone past any reckoning the high-country people keep. What is left behind did not replace them, exactly—it simply moved in, the way heat moves into any space cold enough to hold it, and stayed, redistributing itself eternally among the available bodies, breathing its long red breath over the bones of someone else's purpose, patient, plural, uncountable, warm.
