They have no name for themselves that travels in any medium you could carry away from here, but if you spent long enough in the dark and let your ears do the work your eyes were built for elsewhere, you would start to sort the roar into populations, and the populations into something like a society.

Most numerous, and least like anything you'd call an animal, are the **Soles**—the legless gliders, the long pale flanks you glimpsed vanishing into the rock-folds. They are not soft-bodied in the way a slug is soft; their undersides are dense, muscular, ridged with bands that fire in sequence to drive them forward on the film of damp, and that same surface is studded with what you might mistake for pores but are in fact a fine fur of sensory filaments, reading the vibration of the stone the way your fingertips read braille. They are mostly mouth, proportionally—a long ventral seam that everts to graze a particular substance: a waxy, faintly luminous secretion that the colony-clusters leave smeared across the rock wherever they molt or feed or die. This secretion, this *tallow*, is the floor of everything here. It is what plants would be, if plants were possible. It is laid down by the dense, many-bodied things—the soft pale lumps that read at a glance like single fat animals and are in fact colonies, hundreds of small symbiotic units packed shoulder to shoulder, sweating tallow as a byproduct of their collective digestion, the way a hive sweats wax. The colonies eat almost anything that dies and is not too sharp; the Soles eat what the colonies sweat; smaller darting things eat what falls from both; and the whole arrangement closes its loop without ever touching soil, because soil here was never asked to provide.

The many-legged ones—the stutter-gaited shapes your eye kept trying and failing to smooth into a familiar gait—are predators of a particular, almost ceremonial kind. They do not stalk silently. They announce themselves constantly, a fast clicking percussion produced by specialized digits that strike the rock as they run, and this is not a failure of stealth but the entire point: they hunt by provoking response. The clicking triggers alarm-calls in whatever's nearby, and the alarm-calls reveal position, and position is all the many-legged ones need, because their actual kill is fast and silent and over before the echo of the alarm has finished crossing the basin. Their eyes—the ones that flash that flat coin-silver—are nearly useless as eyes; they are mostly there as a remnant, a vestige of some ancestor who once lived somewhere lit. What does the seeing now is a strip of naked nerve along the spine, sensitive to the pressure-wave of sound itself, so that the creature does not so much hear the roar of the Lowfields as *wear* it, the whole soundscape draped over its back like a second skin telling it everything it needs to know about where the food is and where the larger things are and how much time is left before the rain.

Because there is something larger. You did not see it, and you would not have wanted to, not at first—it does not announce itself in clicks or calls or coin-silver flashes. It is the deep arterial red, the slow inward glow that seemed to come from beneath a hide rather than off it, and it belongs to bodies that move so rarely and so slowly that even the dense traffic of smaller things simply flows around them, the way water finds its way around a stone too old to be called an obstacle anymore. These are the **Quiet**, and they are old in the way the land itself is old—finished with whatever process shaped them, settled into their final and enormous shape the way the rock settled into its swells and basins. They do not call. They do not need to. Everything else here calls *to* them, or rather calls *around* them, the entire roaring structure of the Lowfields' sound arranged, you begin to suspect, as a map whose fixed points are these vast red-glowing bodies, sunk into the deepest basins where the damp pools thickest, feeding on nothing so much as time, absorbing the tallow and the runoff and the slow chemical seep of the stone itself through skin that has long since stopped distinguishing between eating and simply existing in a nutrient bath. They are, perhaps, the reason the rain falls the way it does—some vast collective exhalation, the sky here keeping pace with breathing this old and this slow, the whole circulatory mist-system of the place tuned to lungs the size of valleys.

What you'd call *culture*, if you were generous with the word, lives entirely in the call-and-answer—the swells and lulls you noticed, the rhythms that resolve into nothing visual. The Soles trade location-information in long looping tones, marking which basin's tallow is fresh and which is stripped. The colonies hum a low collective drone that functions as both digestion-song and boundary-marker, warning off other colonies from territory already claimed. The many-legged hunters lie about their position constantly, clicking from one rock and striking from another, and the alarm-callers have, over some uncountable span, evolved counter-deceptions, false alarms, silences held just a half-beat too long to be trusted. It is, in its way, a conversation that has been running since before anything here had a body recognizable as such, an argument and negotiation over a finite quantity of damp and tallow and safety, conducted entirely in a medium that leaves no mark, no ruin, no architecture—because the architecture is the sound itself, built fresh every moment and demolished just as fast, a civilization that has never once needed a wall because it has never stopped talking long enough to need one.

And underneath all of it, beneath the clicking and droning and the false alarms and the true ones, there is the bass layer you felt in your teeth before you understood it—the Quiet, breathing, marking time in a register too slow for anything but stone to carry, patient the way only something that has stopped changing can be patient, waiting at the bottom of every basin for the mist to thicken, for the loop to turn over once more, for the tallow to rise and the small loud lives above it to go on, generation after generation, filling a barren floor with everything the floor itself refused to grow.
