The people who tend this place are easiest to mistake, at first glance, for foresters who got too good at their job. Look longer and the mistake corrects itself.

**The Wardens-of-Pitch**

They are called different things in different districts — pitchwrights, sapsmiths, the chalk-folk, depending on which census you trust — but they are one people, and they are built for exactly this terrain: long-limbed, deliberate in their walking, the kind of musculature that comes from decades of climbing graded slopes rather than stairs. Their skin, by middle age, takes on a faint sheen along the knuckles and forearms, almost varnished, from a lifetime of contact with resin and the mineral seep the wood gives off. It isn't disease. It's closer to what happens to a woodworker's hands, except the wood worked back.

They dress in layers that shed mist rather than block it — oiled bark-cloth, vine-fiber weaves left deliberately loose at the seams so the damp passes through instead of soaking in. Most carry, on a strap or a belt-loop, a stick of chalk worn down to a stub and a small mallet-and-tine tool whose only apparent purpose is striking certain bell-pods at certain hours. You will see this tool used constantly and never explained.

They speak in a register pitched to carry through wet air — slightly flattened vowels, slightly delayed consonants — and they have an unhurried, almost veterinary relationship with their town. They do not marvel at the door that's three times too tall. They measure it, note the measurement against last season's, and move on. When a causeway leads somewhere it didn't yesterday, the nearest adult treats it the way a sailor treats a tide: not a miracle, a *condition*, one you plan around.

**What the work does to a person**

Three trades, mostly, and most Wardens drift between all three across a lifetime:

The *root-walkers* maintain the causeways — not paving, but coaxing, encouraging growth along sanctioned lines with grafting knives and slow chemical persuasion, discouraging it elsewhere with the same. Their hands are the most resin-glossed of anyone's. They are quiet people, given to long silences mid-conversation, as if some part of their attention is always down in the substrate, listening to growth they can't yet see.

The *firekeepers* run the iron-and-resin machinery — the flues, the belt-mills, the fruit-bulb lighting grid. Louder than the root-walkers, soot-marked at the cuffs even though the soot here is greenish, some plant-derived fuel burning differently than wood ever should. They argue, a lot, cheerfully, about pressure and pitch and tolerances, the way engineers anywhere argue. They seem the most ordinary of the three trades, until you notice how carefully they avoid certain trunks after dusk.

The *correctors* — fewest in number, oldest on average, the ones who actually carry the chalk and ring the bells with intent rather than habit — are the closest thing to authority the town has, though no one would call it that. They don't fix the town's drift so much as *negotiate* with it: walking the disputed causeways at dusk, renewing marks, listening to that two-toned thump inside the walls with their ear pressed flat to bark like a doctor with a stethoscope. Ask one what they're listening for and you get a shrug that's clearly covering decades of unspoken theory. They know more than they say. They say less than they suspect.

**The bird, the thing in the walls, and what the Wardens think of them**

The stilt-legged grey bird that walks the rails at dawn is, to the Wardens, basically furniture — noted, ignored, occasionally fed. But the soft double-thump from inside the hollow trunk-walls gets a different reaction: a small pause in conversation, a glance toward the nearest wall, then a resumption like nothing happened. Ask a child what it is and they'll say, plainly, "the second heartbeat," as if this were a completed answer. Ask an adult and they'll change the subject with the practiced ease of people changing a subject they've changed a thousand times before.

There is a working theory, never spoken above a certain volume, that the corrector's bells, the root-walker's grafting, and the firekeeper's pressure-gauges are all, in the end, the same job: keeping company with something that grew too large and too old to have a single shape anymore, and that occasionally — at dusk, in a hallway, in a wall — remembers it used to.

**At the end of a working day**

They gather in low long-roofed halls grown wide and squat for exactly this purpose, drink something fermented from the same mist that beads the leaves, and don't talk about the day's anomalies unless pressed by an outsider — at which point they'll explain, patiently, kindly, the way you'd explain a quirky boiler to a new tenant: *yes, that hallway does that, no, we don't know why, yes, it's fine, no, don't go down it after the second bell.* Then they'll buy you a drink, ask where you're from, and steer the conversation gently, expertly, away from the wood — the same gentle steering you'll feel on your own arm at dusk, guiding you back before the town finishes deciding where you are.
