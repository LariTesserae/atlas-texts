They are called, by the people of the lower villages who trade up at terrible cost for that one iron blade, the *weir-folk*, though they call themselves something closer to "the ones who stayed put on the moving ground" — a phrase that doesn't translate cleanly because the verb for *stay* and the verb for *adapt* are, in their tongue, the same word inflected differently.

You'd know them on sight by their feet first. Childhood here means learning to read give before weight — the half-second lag between a footfall and the moss-mat's decision about whether to hold — and so the weir-folk grow up with a particular gait, weight kept low and back, a perpetual readiness in the ankle that outsiders mistake for caution and that is in fact closer to fluency. Old-timers can tell you, without looking down, whether a given stretch of floor is root over stone or root over hollow, just from the texture of the give. Children who haven't learned this yet are watched constantly and fall constantly and are not much fussed over when they do, because the moss-mass underfoot is also, usefully, a permanent cushion; the real fear isn't the fall, it's the sinkhole, and that fear is taught early and taught hard, with stories.

Their hands are rope-hands — calloused along specific ridges from the cane-and-vine work that never stops, fingers quick at knots the way other cultures are quick at letters, because a knot tied wrong is a bridge gone in the next storm and a bridge gone is, at minimum, a long detour through air thick enough to drown in. There's a whole vocabulary of knots here, dozens of them, each suited to a particular failure mode — knots that hold under sudden shock-load, knots that release on purpose if a section needs to be sacrificed to save the rest, knots tied loosely on principle because a rigid joint in a place this restless just transfers the strain somewhere worse. Watching an elder retie a hand-span of bridge-lashing, unhurried, half-listening to three conversations and a hawk-dispute overhead while their fingers do the actual work, is watching a kind of literacy.

They are, by necessity, a maintenance people before they are anything else — farmers, hunters, weavers second. The real profession, the one every adult does daily regardless of anything else they are, is *upkeep*: re-thatching, re-cutting, drainage-clearing, listening to the particular creak of a stilt-post and deciding whether it means this week or next. There's no separate caste for it because there can't be; the jungle doesn't wait for a guild meeting. So everyone carries a small fixed kit — bone awl, coil of fiber cord, a hooked cutting-tool worn smooth at the grip from a thousand hands before this one — slung at the hip the way other cultures carry a purse, because you genuinely might need it before you reach the next hut.

This produces a temperament you'd call, from outside, fatalistic, and they'd call, from inside, simply *accurate*. Loss here is not a tragedy, it's a Tuesday. A hut listing because its trellis grew off in the night gets shrugged at, propped, eventually re-set a few feet over, and nobody writes songs about the old spot. What they do hold onto, fiercely, almost religiously, are smaller and stranger things: a particular knife passed down, a particular knot-pattern that belonged to a particular grandmother, the names of springs (which surface, vanish, surface again decades later in the same place, and are greeted, when they return, like a relative come home). Their sense of permanence has migrated off the architecture entirely and settled on lineage, on names, on songs and knots and the exact pitch of a particular bell — things light enough to carry when the ground itself won't hold still long enough to be trusted with the job.

They are extraordinary listeners, by training and by necessity, because in a place this loud the only way to hear anything specific is to filter, and they filter constantly, instinctively: a parent mid-conversation will go silent and tilt half a degree at a changed drip-rhythm under the floor, or a goat-bell gone wrong, or wingbeats too heavy and too close, and resume talking three seconds later having decided it's nothing. Outsiders find this unnerving — the constant interruption, the way a weir-folk elder's attention seems to flicker off and back like a moth near a lamp — but it's just the cost of competent residence in a place this dense. Children are taught the layered soundscape almost as a second language: this bird means morning, this bird means a hawk is hunting, this particular silence — and they will tell you, dead serious, that silence is the one sound that should frighten you here, because nothing natural produces it, and on the rare occasions it happens, whole households go still and wait.

Physically they tend small and sinewy rather than broad — there isn't much call for raw strength when most of the labor is balance, dexterity, and repetition, and a heavy frame is, frankly, a liability on a suspended footbridge. Most carry old pale scars from rot — small infections from standing water that everyone gets eventually, that everyone treats with the same handful of known mosses and mostly survives, a low background cost of living here the way altitude sickness is the background cost of the climb up. Coughs are common in the wettest months. Lung complaints take the very old and very young the way storms take the bridges, expected, mourned briefly, absorbed.

Their dead, notably, aren't buried, because there's no ground to speak of, only mat — they're given to the canopy-edge platforms at the shelf's rim, wrapped, left, and the saturating jungle does in weeks what it would take a graveyard years to do, and the weir-folk seem to find this less morbid than other cultures find their own funerals: the place takes everything eventually, on a schedule that doesn't care about your name, and being taken cleanly and quickly by the same hungry green that fed you your whole life reads, to them, as a kind of fairness, almost a settling of accounts.

There's warmth in it too, plenty — communal, loud, constant proximity breeding a sociability that has no real off-switch. Doors don't fully close here (hinges are precious, kept for the few things worth locking, mostly the iron blade and the dry grain-stores), so households bleed into each other by default, children raised in a kind of loose collective custody, meals shared because cooking-fire is expensive in fuel and easier pooled. Disputes happen, plenty, in a place this crowded and this loud — but they tend to resolve fast, almost performatively, shouted across a footbridge and settled before nightfall, because grudges, like everything else here, are heavy things poorly suited to suspended architecture.

And at dusk, when the day-fauna and night-fauna overlap and the whole shelf screams for an hour straight, you'll find them doing the most ordinary thing imaginable in the middle of it: sitting in doorways with clay lamps lit, mending cord, half-listening to children, utterly unbothered by a wall of sound that would flatten a stranger — residents, by now, of an argument with the mountain so old it has started, on both sides, to feel almost like a conversation.
