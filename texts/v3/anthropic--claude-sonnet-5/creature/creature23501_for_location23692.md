What lives in the Cleft does not announce itself with eyes shining out of the dark, because it has no eyes, and would have no use for them if it did. The first wrong assumption visitors make is the one stated as fact above: that there are no animals. There are. There is at least one, and it may be only one, though "one" is a strange word to use for something that occupies as much volume as it does and that may, for all anyone descending into that throat of rock has ever confirmed, simply *be* the dark itself, organized.

Call it the Tender, for lack of any name it would recognize, since it does not seem to recognize itself as separate from what it tends.

It is built low and wide and is, by any ordinary measure, enormous — but enormousness here means something different than it would on an open plain, because the Tender does not occupy space the way a beast occupies a den. It occupies the *cleft*. Its body — if body is the right word for a thing whose mass is distributed through root-cracks, threaded along fissures, draped over the upper housing of the machine the way a hand drapes over a kettle — is segmented, soft in places, calloused to something like bark in others, and entirely without the bilateral tidiness that things which evolved under sky tend to have. It does not need a front. It does not need a face. It has, instead, dozens of small blunt knobs studding its hide at irregular intervals, each one a pressure-organ, each one reading the cleft's percussion the way a held palm reads a drum's skin — the WHUMP, the held-breath stall, the clattering arrhythmia — translating vibration into something it can act on faster than light could ever have told it anything down here anyway.

Its skin, where bare rock-cold air touches it, has gone the color of wet basalt, mottled with patches the deep waxy green of the fern-stems it lies among, so that for most of its life it is not hidden so much as simply *unparsed* — the eye, even if there were light enough to use one, would read it as more wall, more fern-shadow, more tangle, and move on. This is not camouflage in the active sense. It is closer to the truth: the Tender has stopped being separate from the cleft's furniture so long ago that distinguishing it from the wall it rests against may be a meaningless exercise, like trying to find the seam between a heart and the chest that holds it.

It feeds on the venting. Not metaphorically — it has, along its underside, a row of soft intake folds, lipless and slow-pulsing, that open during the WHUMP and the gout of hot vapor that follows, drinking down whatever the machine exhales: steam, mineral dust scoured loose from deep rock, trace oils, a fine metallic grit that would scour a softer throat raw but which the Tender's interior has apparently made peace with over however many generations it took to get here. This is why the ferns stand back up so fast after each blast — the Tender has already taken the worst of the violence into itself, an intermediary organ between machine and garden, absorbing the detonation's edge before it can do real damage to the green, the way a wave breaks against a reef before it reaches the shore.

It moves, when it moves at all, with a slowness that makes the eighty-degree pitch of the walls irrelevant — its grip is not muscular so much as adhesive and rooted, dozens of soft false-feet anchoring into cracks the way the vines do, releasing one at a time, in sequence, so that its progress down or across a wall looks less like climbing and more like a very slow peeling and re-settling, an act of patience indistinguishable from geology. In the rare orange flare when something far below ignites or overheats, anyone who happened to be looking in exactly the right place might catch a silhouette of it among the fern-walls — not a shape that reads as creature, more a thickening of the dark, a wrongness in the way the shadow bulges where shadow should recede — and then the light is gone and so, as far as the eye is concerned, is it.

It does not appear to eat the plants, and does not appear to be eaten by anything, since nothing else lives here to do the eating. Its only real relationship is the one with the engine below — an asymmetric, wordless tending that may not even be intentional in any sense the Tender itself could articulate, supposing it has the apparatus for articulation at all. It simply sits closest to the heat. It simply absorbs the worst of the venting before the worst of it reaches the green. It simply has, over some unknown span of time the basalt's youth makes hard to estimate, become the cleft's one functioning organ of mediation between a thing that runs hot and blind in the dark and a garden that wants, more than anything, to keep living above it.

Whether it was born to this, or built itself into this, or was built — by hands, by the same unseen agency that seated an engine into a mountain without leaving a single visible bolt or rail — is not a question the Cleft answers. It only roars, and breathes, and vents, and somewhere in the wall of fern and black water-starved stem, something vast and patient and the color of wet rock keeps drinking down the violence so the green never has to.
