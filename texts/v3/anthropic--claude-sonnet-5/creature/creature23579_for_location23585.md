## What Lives Here

At first you will think nothing does — that the noise is only weather and machinery talking to each other in the dark, that the glow in the canopy is chemistry and nothing more. This is wrong, but it is an honest mistake, because almost everything here has been built, by ten thousand years of lightless evolution, to not be noticed until it wants to be.

Start small, because small is where the place starts.

The fissures weep their warm rust-water, and in that warmth live things like lichen but motile — thin mats of cilia-fringed tissue that creep, glacially, toward the warmest stone, grazing on mineral film and the faint chemical sweetness that leaks from old pipe-joints. They are not much. They are also everywhere, a velvet stubble over every south-facing seep, and everything above them eats them eventually, which is the only reason they matter.

Then the things that eat those: palm-sized scuttlers, many-legged, shelled in something like wet slate, that travel in skittering rivers across the basin mud after a storm-pulse passes, drawn out by the change in pressure the way surface-world things are drawn out by light. They click as they move — not for communication so much as for navigation, a thousand small sonars stuttering against each other, indistinguishable, in chorus, from rain on metal.

Above them, the gliders — the screeching things in the canopy-gaps — are not birds, not quite, though something in the shape of a wing converged here the way it converges everywhere given enough open air to fall through. Their wings are membrane stretched between four long fingers and a tail-spar, sail-thin, threaded with the same faint blue-green glow as the trunk-joints they roost in, so that in flight they look like pieces of the forest's own light come loose and drifting. They scream in registers pitched to bounce well off wet stone and wetter leaf, and they have learned — the way the grazers have learned their paths — to scream differently near the gantries, a flatter, quicker call that returns off corroded steel in a way they recognize and avoid colliding with. They have never been taught this. It has simply always been true, for as many generations back as a glider's short, loud life allows anyone to remember.

The grazers are the heavy heart of the place — barrel-bodied, low-slung, moving in herds that you feel before you hear and hear long before you see, a subsonic moan rolling through the black mud underfoot like a second pulse beneath your own. They are built wide and soft-footed for the saturated ground, their hide ridged and dark and beaded constantly with the condensation everything here wears like a second skin. They do not see in any meaningful sense — small clouded organs buried in heavy brow-ridges, vestigial, useful only for telling dark from somewhat-less-dark. What they have instead is throat and ear: a call that drops low enough to travel through stone as much as air, mapping the basin's contours in returning vibration, herd-mind built from ten thousand small echoes shared constantly between bodies walking close together. Their migration paths bend around the pumping stations and the gantry-shadows with a precision that looks, from a distance, like reverence. It is not reverence. It is simply that the grandmothers of their grandmothers found a clean way through, and the path has never once needed re-finding.

In the black pools, the ambush-things wait with a patience that has nothing to do with intelligence and everything to do with metabolism slowed to match the cold dark water. You will know them only as a ripple-shape, a retreating disturbance, a single low pulse of displaced water against your shin in the wading-shallows. Nobody who lives here long claims to have seen one whole. This is considered, by the things that do live here long, to be the correct and healthy state of affairs.

But none of these is quite the answer to the question, because none of these is what *tends* — and something tends.

You will notice it first as a shape on the gantry at the very edge of glow-range: upright, or upright enough, moving along the warm pipe-rails with a slow deliberate four-limbed care, pausing at junctions the way a thing pauses when it is checking something rather than merely passing through. Call it, as the few who've mapped this place have come to call it, a *Warden* — though nobody is certain that is a fair name, implying as it does an intention that may or may not still be there.

It is roughly the height of a person and rather wider, low-built for balance on wet steel, its hide a mottled grey-brown that under any other light would read as mimicry of rust and lichen and old paint, and under this light reads as nothing at all until movement gives it away. Its forelimbs end not in claws but in something closer to a hand — three thick digits and an opposable spur, an adaptation that looks, uncomfortably, like a tool rather than an organ, as though some ancestor of this thing once needed to turn a valve or grip a rail and never stopped needing to. It is blind in the manner of everything else here, but where the grazers hear the shape of a basin and the gliders hear the shape of a gap, the Warden hears *machinery* — tuned, by some long pressure of selection nobody alive was present for, to the specific resonances of pipe-thrum and piston-thud and the high thin whine of a bearing about to fail. It walks the gantries the way a physician walks a ward, head low, throat clicking a slow steady pulse, listening for the note that's wrong.

When it finds one — a valve seeping past its gasket, a joint vibrating loose — it stops. It works the joint with its spurred hand, blind, patient, for as long as the fix takes, which can be minutes or can be most of a pressure-cycle, the whole time emitting nothing but that same unhurried click-pulse, as if the work itself were a kind of breathing. Whether it understands what a valve *is*, in any sense that would satisfy a question about minds, no one who has watched it can honestly say. It does not eat the metal. It does not seem to want anything from the machine beyond its correction. When the joint is sound again it simply turns and continues along the rail, toward the next wrong note, and you are left standing in the dripping dark trying to decide whether you have just watched an animal or a ghost of instruction, some behavior bred or built generations ago into a creature that has long since outlived whoever needed the work done, still doing it, the way a heart kept beating in a body that forgot, eons back, what it was for.

Nobody has found where Wardens are born, or whether *born* is even the right verb. They appear singly, never in groups larger than two, always near working machinery, never near the dead installations that the vine-fungus has fully reclaimed — as if even they can tell, by some sense beyond hearing, the difference between a heart still beating and one that has finally, truly stopped.

And on the rare nights — if night means anything here — when the pressure-cycle peaks and the storm breaks and everything, animal and engine alike, draws in and goes still for those few unlasting seconds of near-silence, the Wardens are the only things that do not brace. They keep working, unhurried, click-pulse steady under the held breath of the whole dark highland, as though stillness were something that happened to other things, and tending were the one note in all this noise that never needed to pause.
