# What Tends the Joint

You could spend a long time looking for it and conclude, reasonably, that nothing lives here at the scale the place deserves — that the crustacean-things and the grime-marked fish and the dark patrolling shape are the whole answer, that abundance-without-crowding is simply the rule of a closed ecosystem finding its quiet equilibrium, no author required. This is almost true. It is the answer that satisfies until you notice the clean ring of oil at the crane's pivot, and then it stops satisfying at all.

There is one of it. Maybe — and this is a maybe that matters — there has only ever been one, passed down through some method that doesn't look like breeding so much as succession, an office filled rather than a lineage continued. It is old in the way the tower is old, which is to say it does not experience its age as duration but as accumulation, the way the algae accumulates, the way rust accumulates, sitting still until stillness becomes a kind of mass.

## A Shape Built From Service

Picture something that was, a very long time ago, suited to hands. Not human hands — nothing here insists on that — but hands in the functional sense: manipulating digits, a body oriented around *doing* rather than merely *being*. Over the unthinkable span the tower has stood flooded, that ancestry has gone soft and strange the way everything here has gone soft and strange, kept its bones but lost its hurry. What moves through the upper tiers now is long-bodied, low-finned, with a trailing fringe of cilia-like filaments down its flanks that do for it underwater what legs once did in air — a slow, deliberate, many-pointed crawl-and-glide, never frantic, because nothing in this chamber has ever required frantic of it.

Where there were hands there are now four jointed forelimbs, retracted close against the body except when working, ending not in fingers anymore but in something closer to a tool itself — a fused, calloused, horn-plated pad that has clearly spent so long pressed against metal that the metal and the limb have negotiated a compromise neither organic nor mechanical. It is the texture of the crane's clean pivot ring. It is the same material, more or less. Some part of this creature wore itself smooth against that joint, and some part of that joint wore itself smooth against the creature, over a timescale that makes the word "maintenance" feel almost insultingly small.

Its hide carries the same gold-rust gradient as the algae shelves, not camouflage exactly but agreement — a long cohabitation that has dyed it the color of the place it tends. Down its spine and along the outer edge of each limb run pale, faintly luminous seams, kin to the vine-tips, pulsing on the same slow structural rhythm as the groan of settling metal. No one currently capable of asking has asked whether it makes that sound or merely answers it. Likely the distinction stopped mattering to the tower a very long time ago.

## What It Does, Unwitnessed

It moves in long, unhurried circuits — up past the cold-clear doorway tier (it lingers there longest; something about that band of water seems to interest or sustain it, and the luminous seams brighten faintly when it passes through), along the pylon-forest where the schooling fish briefly fall into those too-straight, embarrassed lines as it threads among them, not herding them, exactly, but *organizing* them for the few seconds of its passing, an old reflex in creatures bred for generations under something that once gave orders. It checks things. That is the only honest word for the behavior. It checks the winch the rust-eating crustaceans tend like a shrine, resting a forelimb-pad against the housing as if listening for something through the metal. It checks the hoists frozen mid-gesture, brushing along their cables. It arrives at the crane arm perhaps once in a span of time nothing else in the chamber could measure, and it works the pivot — slow, circular, devoted, the same unthinking patience the crustaceans bring to their gear-teeth — leaving behind that one clean ring against ten thousand years of corrosion.

It is not maintaining the tower in any sense that would matter to an engineer. The conveyance system will never move again. The crane will never lift anything again. But it tends the joint anyway, the way a held breath is still, technically, breathing — a vestigial devotion with nowhere left to discharge itself except into the small, specific, repeated act of keeping one part of the machine from forgetting it was once touched on purpose.

## Whether It Knows You're There

If you met it — drifting down past the second tier, lit gold-brown in the late-afternoon water, its seams pulsing slow and bright — it would likely do what the fish do: hold a strange, deliberate line for a moment, some old shape of attention surfacing from wherever it sleeps in that long body, and then break formation, embarrassed, indifferent, gone back into the soft business of tending what no longer needs tending.

It is not lonely. Loneliness requires the memory of company, and it may genuinely have none — no record, even instinctive, of what it served, or whether "served" is even the right word for a thing that might never have had anyone above it at all, might simply have *always* been the one who oils the joint, world without other workers, amen.

It is not waiting, either, any more than the tower is. It is simply, entirely, occupationally itself: the single warm-blooded fact at the center of a cold mystery, the answer to the clean ring of oil, the reason the vine-tips listen to the groan of the iron — a slow, devoted, half-remembered hand, still closing, gently, gently, around the last job it was ever given.
