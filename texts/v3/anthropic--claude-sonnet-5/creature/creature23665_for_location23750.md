# Who Lives There

At the foot, in Kelvinhead Terrace proper, live perhaps four hundred people, and they are the only humans who admit to making the Stack their home rather than their pilgrimage. They are short-lived by the standards of people who live in flatter places — not from hardship, but from a kind of acquired stillness, a willingness to let the grinding roar of the core become the sound of their own pulse rather than fight it. Children here are taught to sleep through the gear-thrum before they're taught to walk; it's said you can tell a Kelvinhead native anywhere in the world by the way they startle awake in true silence, the way other people startle at a loud noise. They keep gardens of their own at the base, in imitation of the rings above, though theirs need watering by hand and tending by daylight, ordinary labor, because they have not yet earned — or perhaps refused — what the higher terraces receive for free. They fish the runoff cisterns for things that have washed down from above: bones of birds, fern-spores, once, memorably, a single intact valve-flower nozzle, brass gone green, which now sits on a plinth in the town's one public building and is regarded with a reverence that nobody can fully account for. They do not call the Stack a god. They call it the Works, with a faint stress that suggests the capital letter, and they leave food at the base of the first stair on certain nights for reasons that have separated, generations back, from whatever reason originally prompted them.

Above the twentieth ring, the human presence thins and changes character. These are the menders, though that's a word the base-dwellers use more than the menders use of themselves; ask one what they're called and you tend to get a shrug, or a name particular to whichever terrace they were last seen tending, as if identity up here is a function of altitude rather than of any continuous self. They are not many — nobody has ever counted them, because nobody has ever caught more than two or three at once, usually at a distance, usually already turning to climb further up or further down before a conversation can properly start. Their hands are characteristic: stained dark green-brown from gasket-leaf sap, calloused along specific ridges that correspond to the grip of a particular wrench or blade nobody else uses anymore because nobody else knows where it's kept. Their eyes have adjusted, climbers say, to the gray-gold shadowless light in a way that makes ordinary daylight at the base look to them like an assault, a glare; menders who do come down tend to keep their faces tilted, squinting, uncomfortable, eager to climb back up into the diffuse and even light they've come to need.

They do not explain the maintenance schedule because there may not be one they could explain. Ask a mender how she knew to repack that valve, on that ring, this week, and the honest answer — when you get an honest answer — is usually some version of *it told me*, delivered without drama, the way you might say a sore tooth told you to stop chewing on that side. Whether this is metaphor for an inherited intuition, passed down terrace by terrace through long unspoken apprenticeship, or something more literal — a sensitivity to pressure-shifts and drip-rhythms transmitted through the iron itself, through years of bare feet on warm brackets — no one outside the menders' company has ever determined, and the menders, when pressed, generally lose interest in the question before you do.

They are not numerous enough to maintain a structure this vast by hand, and everyone who has thought about it for more than a few minutes knows this. The neatly stacked cut vines, the freshly packed gaskets, appear in numbers and at a pace that suggests either a population of menders larger than anyone has ever glimpsed, or a Stack that does a portion of its own healing and merely receives credit, ritually, generation after generation, for work it didn't do — the menders' presence less a cause than a courtesy, an attendance paid to a thing that was already going to mend itself regardless, the way you might sit with a sleeping animal not because it needs you but because you need to have been there.

The birds are the Stack's loudest residents and its least mysterious. They are called stitchwings at the base — a name for the way their flocks seem to sew themselves through the gaps between rivets, in and out of the cogwork housings, never quite resting, always mid-stitch. They are smaller than swifts elsewhere, paler, with a faint iridescence along the throat that locals say comes from generations of nesting against warm brass and breathing condenser-mist; ornithologists who've made the climb disagree on whether this is adaptation or simply dust. They do not migrate. There is nowhere colder to migrate to, and no reason — the Stack provides summer at every height, all year, and the stitchwings have given up the instinct for elsewhere. Their colonies in the gear-housings are dense enough that the housings themselves have grown a kind of architecture of their own: nest-mortar made of mud and moss and fern-down, packed into every gap the engineers left and several they didn't, so that some of the older gear-trains are reportedly insulated, slightly, oddly, by centuries of bird-built felt. The stitchwings thin as you climb above a certain terrace, not gradually but at a fairly distinct threshold — climbers speak of "the last birds" the way sailors used to speak of the last landfall — and what exactly happens at that threshold, whether it's air pressure, temperature, light, or something about the cloud-band that no bird will cross, is one more thing nobody living has settled.

Lower down, in the wet rings, the frogs are the dominant voice between the valve-hisses — small, brown-gold, throat-sacs the same gray-gold as the ambient light, so that a calling frog at dusk seems to glow faintly from within rather than reflect anything external. They have given up the rain entirely; their breeding calls are timed not to weather, which doesn't reach them, but to the hiss-interval of the nearest valve-flower, a rhythm they have learned generationally to anticipate, so that a chorus of frogs on a given terrace will fall silent half a second before the nozzle sighs, and erupt half a second after, every single cycle, with a reliability that makes the rings feel, at dusk, like a clock built out of throats.

And then there is the question of what, if anything, lives above the birds — past the last stitchwing nest, in the bare high terraces where the menders themselves grow rare and the gardens, climbers report, start to thin from jungle to something more spare, more deliberate, more like an arrangement than a growth. No one currently alive claims to have met anyone there. But the swept landings continue past where any mender has been confirmed; the cut vines keep appearing, neater, fewer, further apart; and once, displayed without comment in the Kelvinhead public building beside the green nozzle, sits a single tool — a slim iron implement, warm to the touch though it sits in open air, that nobody in town can name a use for, and that nobody has ever been able to explain how it got there, since it was, by every account, found at the base of the first stair, as if set down by someone descending, and never picked back up by whoever left it.
