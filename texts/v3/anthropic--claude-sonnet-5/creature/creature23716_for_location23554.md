# Who Lives in Maerhollow

## Bodies Made for the Wet

They are a compact people, broad-footed, low-balanced — the kind of build that comes from generations of bare or sandaled feet finding purchase on six inches of wet moss over old limestone, of legs that have learned to bend rather than catch when a step gives. Watch someone cross a terrace bridge here and you'll see it: a particular rolling, weight-forward walk, knees soft, hands trailing the rope rail without quite gripping it, eyes not on their feet but on the middle distance, because the feet already know.

Their skin runs a little soft and a little tough at once — perpetually damp, never chapped, with the deep-set wrinkling at the fingertips and palms that comes from a lifetime of wet rope and wet wood and wet stone, hands gone permanently water-worked the way a riverbed stone goes smooth. Hair is worn close-cropped or tightly bound; loose hair in this much standing damp grows heavy and sour, and the people have no patience for it. Eyes tend pale, or pale-adapted — wide-pupiled, comfortable in the shadowless gray-gold light, faintly unsettled by hard sun or sharp shadow when they travel down to drier country, where they're known to squint for the first several days like people startled awake.

They run a little deaf in one particular way — not poor hearing, but trained disbelief. A child here learns young that the ear lies about direction, that the bell at the east cistern will sound as if it hangs above your head, that a voice on the terrace below will seem to rise from underfoot. So they listen *through* a sound rather than *to* it — for the timbre, the rhythm, the specific clatter of a specific bell-keeper's hand — and have grown, as a consequence, remarkably good at telling people apart by sound alone, and remarkably bad at pointing toward anything reliably.

## Hands and Trades

Nearly everyone here can do the basic waterproofer's work — oil bark, set a pitch seam, weave a tight-bottomed basket — the way people elsewhere might all know how to mend a fence. But it is a culture with real specialists, quietly ranked, fiercely respected: the cistern-cutters, who know the stone well enough to carve a new tank without weakening the basin wall around it; the terrace-keepers, who can read a soil-bed's color and tell you how many more seasons of composting it needs before it'll hold a planting; the thatch-families, whose roof-knowledge passes down matrilineally and whose names are quite literally used as units of time — "born in the second Hollin re-thatch," people will say, instead of giving a year.

There is an entire caste of rope-and-bridge people, lean and disproportionately strong in the forearms, who spend their working lives suspended between terrace levels doing the patient, dangerous work of re-stringing the vine bridges before they rot through. They are teased gently by everyone else for their swagger and trusted absolutely with their lives.

Children begin working young — not from cruelty but from the simple unhurried logic of the place: a six-year-old can learn to weave a dry-basket badly for a decade before it matters, because nothing here is in that much of a rush, and the slow ruin of a clumsy first attempt is itself considered part of the teaching.

## Speech Pitched Against the Rain

Maerhollow voices are louder than they think they are. People raised here pitch their speaking register a notch above comfortable silence, lean in close even when there's no need to, touch a forearm or shoulder before speaking as a kind of announcement, the way you'd knock before entering a room. Conversations happen at close range, faces near, words clipped a little shorter than they'd be elsewhere — long sentences get eaten by the water-noise, so the language has compressed itself over generations into something economical, almost clipped, full of complete thoughts in four or five words.

For distance, or for the worst of the cataract-roar near the lower cisterns, there's the hand-shorthand — a compact, practical sign-vocabulary that every child learns alongside spoken words, used so constantly and unconsciously that Maerhollow people will sign small words to *each other* even mid-conversation, a kind of unconscious doubling, as if making sure the meaning arrives by two roads in case the rain eats one of them.

They are, as a result, a tactile people, comfortable with proximity, a little baffled by visitors who back away to talk. Personal space here is measured in arm's reach, not feet.

## Kinship Kept in Stone and Thatch

Memory in Maerhollow is not kept in writing — paper does not survive here, and they've made an uneasy peace with that, trusting instead to recitation, to the family-name calendar, to the practice of telling a child their lineage the way other places might teach a child to count. Genealogy is public, casual, constantly rehearsed: ask anyone their grandmother's grandmother's name and they'll have it ready, along with which terrace wall she helped re-set and roughly what season.

This gives the place a peculiar density of presence-of-the-dead. Walls are spoken of as "Old Maeve's wall," cisterns as "the one Ren's father dug." Nothing is anonymous. Every stacked stone has a hand attached to it, remembered, even generations on.

## Temperament

They are, on the whole, an unhurried, undramatic, dryly funny people — necessarily so, having made their peace with a world that will not be rushed regardless of preference. Patience here isn't a virtue performed; it's closer to a physical fact, like having two hands. Anger runs short and is let go quickly, because grudges, like everything else, are hard to carry through that much wet and that much daily proximity to the same sixty households.

They are wary of outsiders in a mild, curious way rather than a hostile one — Maerhollow gets few visitors and treats the ones who come with a kind of careful hospitality that assumes the visitor will be gone again before anything important needs to change. Those who stay long enough to slow down to the basin's pace are, eventually, folded in without much ceremony. There's no formal naturalization. You simply start being included in the bell-rounds and the thatch-counts, and one day someone tells you which cisterns you're allowed to fish, and that's that — you live here now.

## The Quiet Thing They Believe

It is considered, among Maerhollow's people, a small and real comfort — not quite superstition, not quite belief, occupying the soft territory between — that the dead go on speaking in the second voice of the place. When an old bell-keeper dies, when a thatch-mother is finally buried under the moss she spent her life repairing the roofs against, people will say, without much solemnity, that you'll hear her now from the wrong direction — a laugh that seems to come from over your shoulder when no one's there, a called name that arrives from the cliff face instead of the doorway it should have come from.

No one claims to fully believe this. Everyone, pressed, admits to listening for it anyway — and there is, in nearly every adult's quiet private accounting, at least one sound they've decided, on no evidence at all, to keep.
