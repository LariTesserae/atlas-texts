## The Pitch-Folk

They are called, in the dialect that survives at the upper terraces, the *anchor-born* — though guild-speak lower down simply says *the works' own*, with the same flat possessiveness you'd use for a tool. Whatever the name, they are unmistakably a people shaped by gradient and heat the way a river-stone is shaped by current: not designed, but *worn into* this particular fitness.

The first thing you notice is the gait. Anhedral Reach has no level ground worth the name, so nobody here walks the way flatland bodies walk. They move in a kind of economical lean — weight forward, knees soft, hands unconsciously finding rail or rivet-head or rope even mid-conversation, the body keeping a running, wordless negotiation with the drop that's always somewhere nearby. Children learn this before they learn much else; the ones born on the high terraces have an almost liquid quality to their balance, an unhesitating trust in their own grip that visitors find unsettling to watch — a girl crossing a pipe-bridge forty stories up at the same unbothered pace she'd use crossing a room.

Their skin runs the spectrum of the rock and the smoke: a deep, sun-and-furnace bronze on those who work the heat-proximate levels, fading toward something paler and more ash-grey in the old who've spent decades in the lightless inspection-crews. Calluses are read like rank — palm-thickness at the rope-callus, the particular shine of a hand that has spent a life on valve-wheels, the rasp-burn scars that mark a smelting-hall apprenticeship. Eyes adjust early to haze and lamplight both; most adults can read a gauge-face in near-dark and go faintly squint-blind in open unfiltered daylight, which is one reason so few of them ever leave.

Voices are kept low and close by long habit — not secrecy exactly, though there's plenty of that too, but simple acoustics. Sound doesn't carry in the windless folds between towers; it just gets folded back into the engine-hum and lost. So the pitch-folk have developed a quieter register of speech, economical, often supplemented by the hand-cant used on the factory floors where shouting over machinery is pointless — a whole second language of small gestures, thumb-flicks and wrist-turns, that outsiders mistake for nervous habit and that locals use to conduct entire transactions without disturbing the hum at all.

Water defines status more than wealth does, though the two braid together. A household's cistern-share is inherited, defended, occasionally the subject of vendettas conducted with the same procedural patience as everything else here — a slow re-routing of a gutter-line over several seasons rather than any open confrontation. Children are taught the cistern-prayers before they're taught much theology: not worship exactly, but a kind of ritualized gratitude-and-anxiety directed at sky and pipe-joint both, because the sky gives so rarely and the joints leak if untended. A bath is an event. A given cup of condensate, offered to a stranger, is the single most legible act of trust in the whole vertical society.

They organize by guild rather than by family, though the two have mostly fused after enough generations — the Vent-Tenders, the Cistern-Wards, the Funicular crews, and below them, spoken of differently, the **Face-Crews**: the ones who go into the lightless chambers near the working rock and come back changed in some small unspecifiable way each time, slower to laugh, quicker to go still when the deep vents sigh on their unscheduled exhale. Nobody outside that guild discusses what's found there, and the Face-Crews themselves have a particular flatness to their faces that the others have learned not to ask about. There's a saying on the mid-terraces, half joke and half warning: *you don't talk to a Face-Crew about the weather underground.*

What unites all of them, guild and terrace and generation alike, is a shared and largely unspoken relationship to the mountain's own patience. They know — everyone here knows, the way you know your own pulse — that the rock is still rising, that the city is not built upon a place so much as built *against a process*, perpetually one anchoring-phase ahead of a growth nobody can stop and few will name outright. This produces a peculiar temperament: not fatalism exactly, but a long, working patience that mirrors the geology it answers to. Decisions are generational. Repairs are preventative on a decade's timescale. A pitch-folk elder, asked when some re-anchoring project will finish, will answer with the kind of shrug that means *it doesn't, that's not what finishing means here.*

They do not call themselves brave, and would be faintly embarrassed by the word. The thousand-meter drops, the furnace-proximate floors, the cargo nobody discusses moving down into the rock more often than up — all of it is simply Tuesday. What they *do* feel, and will admit to over the rare shared cup of true rainwater, is something closer to vigilance: an attentiveness to the mountain's breathing that never fully switches off, passed down not as instruction but as inherited reflex, the same way the climbing-balance is passed down, the same way the quiet voice is passed down — a whole population permanently, unobtrusively listening to the ground beneath their gantries, waiting to feel it move.
