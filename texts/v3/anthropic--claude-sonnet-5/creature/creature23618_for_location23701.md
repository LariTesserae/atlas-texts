The thing that lives in the hollow is easiest to describe by what it is not: it is not fast, not bright-eyed, not single. There is no one animal that announces itself as the basin's resident the way a den announces a fox or a nest a bird. But if you stayed long enough — long enough for your eyes to stop demanding light and start accepting the dusk on its own terms — you would notice that the gleam in the gutters returns to the same three or four hollows of standing water, night after night that aren't really nights, day after day that aren't really days. Something keeps a address here. Something has decided, a very long time ago, that this particular weep-channel, this particular fold of root and stone, is where it surfaces.

It is pale in the way that things which have stopped needing color go pale — not white, more the color of a peeled root, of skin that has been wet so constantly it has forgotten the idea of dryness as anything but an old rumor. Its shape, what little of it breaks the surface, suggests something that was once more clearly one thing — fish, or amphibian, or something with the long, careful fingers of a hand — and has since blurred at the edges, the way the basin blurs every category it holds. A flat eye, lidless or near enough, opens at the waterline and holds whatever non-light there is for a breath longer than seems necessary, as if it is not looking so much as confirming that nothing has changed, which nothing has, which is the whole point.

Its body, on the rare occasions enough of it shows, is segmented with faint ridges that might be vestigial scales or might be old scars healed so many times they've become structural, and these ridges hold moss the way the stone holds moss, so that motion and stillness become hard to tell apart: is it crossing the gutter, or is it a low ridge of the gutter that has, this once, decided to move. Its limbs, when it uses them, move with the unhurried, double-jointed care of something that has never once in its existence needed to be quick, because nothing here has needed to be quick in longer than memory holds.

It is, you begin to suspect, very old — not aged in the way a creature ages toward death, but old in the way the gutters are old, worn into a shape by repetition rather than worn down by time. It may in fact be the descendant, many unremembered generations removed, of whatever hand once scraped those few hollows into softer stone to catch a better trickle — the tool-shape long since dissolved out of its fingers, if fingers are even the right word anymore, the knowledge of *making* folded under instinct the way the moss folds under everything else. It does not build. It does not need to. The basin already provides every hollow it requires, having been worn, slowly, into exactly the shape that something like this would want.

It eats without apparent violence — small soft things, drawn down in the dark the way everything here is drawn down, unhurried, mutual, old. It is eaten, occasionally, by something larger and just as patient, and this too happens without disturbing the basin's surface calm, the way a held breath doesn't change when, deep inside the lungs, something shifts.

It does not see you as you stand at the basin's edge and look for it. It registers you — the flat eye opens, holds, measures you against an unchanging baseline it has kept for centuries — and finding you do not belong to any category that requires response, simply, slowly, lets the water close back over.
