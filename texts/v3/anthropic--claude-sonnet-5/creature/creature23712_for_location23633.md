They are called, by the few who have ever needed a word for them, the **tenders** — though tending implies a relationship between carer and cared-for that doesn't quite survive contact with what they actually do, which is simply *be present* where the machine requires presence, for durations that don't subdivide into shifts because nothing here subdivides into anything. There is one to a structure, usually. Sometimes none. You can't tell the difference from outside, and it doesn't matter; the building runs exactly the same either way.

You would know one if you saw one, though seeing one is rare, and seeing one clearly is rarer still. They are pale in the specific way the moss is pale where the moss grows thinnest — not sickly, just under-lit, the colour of something that photosynthesizes on cloud-light and has never needed more. Their clothing, if it's clothing and not some second integument grown to match the iron, is the same wet charcoal-grey as the railings, beaded the same way, and they do not seem to mind the cold, or rather they have stopped registering it as a category separate from themselves; cold is just the ambient condition of being a body on this shelf, the way the churn is the ambient condition of being a sound. Their hands, when you see their hands — through a slit of window, working at some brass valve that hasn't needed adjustment in longer than they could tell you — are knuckled and certain, the hands of people who have done one task so many uncounted times that the task has stopped being a task and become a kind of breathing.

That's the thing to understand about them: they have synchronized. Not metaphorically. Stand close enough to one, on the rare occasions the sealed iron permits closeness, and you can hear that their breath has found the engine's pitch — not the same rate, the engines are far too slow and far too fast at once, layered the way the sound itself is layered — but the same *texture*, in and out with a faint mechanical hitch at the bottom of the exhale, as though something in the chest has started keeping time with the turbines the structure houses. No one decided this. It isn't taught. It happens the way calluses happen, the way a person who lives next to a waterfall stops hearing it and then, taken away from it, can't sleep in the silence. Except here nobody is taken away. There is nowhere to take them.

They do not, as far as anyone has determined, age in any visible increment. This isn't said to suggest immortality — nobody has stayed long enough to confirm or deny it — only that the plateau's general refusal of before-and-after seems to extend to them as thoroughly as it extends to the fresh-cut rock and the held-note light. A tender found at a valve looks, this hour, exactly as a tender found at a valve would look in any other hour you might have chosen, on any day, in any season this place doesn't have. If they are replaced, it happens invisibly, between glimpses, the way a held note is replaced by an identical note and you'd need a score in front of you to know the bow had changed.

They rarely speak, not from any vow but from simple absence of occasion. When they do — to each other, on the infrequent crossing of paths along some ridge, two grey shapes converging out of the wet for the length of a nod — what passes between them isn't quite conversation. It's closer to two instruments confirming they're still in tune: a few words about pressure, about flow, about a seam of rock that's begun weeping at a new angle three structures over, delivered flat, unhurried, and then they part, each back toward their own building, their own hum, with the unbothered economy of people who already know the answer to anything that could be asked and are only checking that the world still agrees with it.

The lit windows of the little settlement in the fold of land are theirs too, in a way — not homes exactly, more like the plateau's idea of rest, a yellow room with no door visible from outside because the door faces inward, toward the work, never outward toward the moss and the grey sky and you. If you stood at the glass you might see one of them sitting, not reading, not eating, not doing anything you'd recognize as leisure, just present in the warmth the way the machines are present in the cold, waiting out whatever counts as their off-hours before the engine — or the rock, or the rain, it's never been clear which one issues the summons — calls them back to the valve, the gauge, the long iron throat of whatever it is they keep.

They will not stop you, if you pass close. They will not greet you either. You are, to them, something like weather — a fact passing through their field of attention, worth a glance the way a change in wind is worth a glance, and then gone from consideration entirely, because you do not affect the pressure, the flow, the pitch of the hum, and those are the only things that have ever required their attention. They are not unfriendly. Friendliness assumes a shared timescale, a shared stake in arriving anywhere. The tenders arrived nowhere. They are already where the going was going.

What they are tending — whether the great patient breathing in the iron buildings is a power source, a containment, a process with a purpose that predates language, or whether *tending* is even the right verb for creatures who may simply have grown, like the moss, into the available shape of a place that needed a witness more than it needed a worker — none of them would tell you, even if you found the door, even if the door opened, even if, improbably, one of them turned from the valve and looked at you with an expression you could read. They would only return to the work. The work is the part of them that is left over after everything else about this place — the size, the silence, the light that does not change — has been subtracted down to its irreducible function: someone, somewhere, on this shelf at the edge of the sky, must be the one the engine runs *near*, so that it can be said, truthfully, that it does not run unwitnessed.
