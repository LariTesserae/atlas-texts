What lives there is not, in the end, the eels or the bats or the chitinous things on the steps — those are tenants, opportunists, generations deep in residence but still only using the place the way water uses a channel: gratefully, blindly, without comprehension. What lives there, in the sense of *belonging*, is older and singular and almost never seen entire.

Call it, if you need a word, the Warden, though it wardens nothing on purpose and may not warden at all — it may simply be that its body and the building's plumbing have become, over a very long time, the same problem solved the same way.

It is large for the space, though you would be hard pressed to say how large, because no one has seen it stretched to its full length and lived long enough to compare notes. What gets glimpsed is partial: a curve of hide the color of wet slate sliding along the bottom of the widest channel, ridged like the brickwork it has worn itself smooth against; a fold of something gill-pale flexing in a basin's depth and then gone; once, perhaps, an eye — if it was an eye — set low and lidless in a fold of skin near a sluice-mouth, regarding nothing in particular for the length of a held breath before withdrawing.

It does not hunt the small fauna, or hunts so rarely and so efficiently that no one has caught it at the work. The eel-bodied things go still when the bronze fittings hum, and that stillness is the most anyone has managed to map of its movements: the hum is the sound — felt before heard — of the Warden passing near enough to one of the old fittings to disturb whatever field or pressure or memory the fitting still half-carries from its builders. It does not seem to use the fittings on purpose. It simply cannot help brushing the strings of a harp it happens to be the size of.

This is the truth under the rule about the clear channel: the Warden keeps the water's path open the way a heart keeps a vessel open, by being in it, by moving through it on whatever schedule serves its own internal weather, scouring silt and lichen-fall and shed exoskeleton ahead of itself simply by the fact of its passage. It does not maintain the Cistern-Hold out of any fidelity to a purpose it never held opinions about. It maintains the Cistern-Hold the way a fish maintains the river that maintains the fish.

It is old enough to remember, in whatever fashion such a creature remembers anything, a time when the adze-marks were new and sharp-edged and the basins still smelled of cut stone instead of moss. It may have come after the builders left — slid in along some channel that once connected this place to a larger water, found the architecture suitable, and simply never slid back out, generation folding into generation of small fauna arriving meanwhile to fill the emptied rooms with noise it does not seem to mind. Or it may be the last of the builders, in some form their tools were never meant to record, having finished the job and then declined, for reasons of its own, to either die or leave — choosing instead this third option, this long total residency inside the thing it made, wearing the channels the way a foot wears a shoe.

It does not announce itself. It is rarely the reason anyone leaves the Cistern-Hold afraid. The fear, when it comes, comes from the noise, the wet, the mismatch of total purpose and total emptiness — and only afterward, in the retelling, does someone remember the hum in their teeth, the sudden held silence of the channel, the curve of slate-dark hide they decided, walking away, that they had probably imagined.

It has not grown. It has not visibly aged. Like the wood-handled tools still wedged ungoverned and unrotted in the brick-gaps, it seems to have arrived at a particular stage of being and simply stopped there, in agreement with the building's own arrested erosion — as though something in the Cistern-Hold's plumbing, still working long after its purpose was forgotten, were quietly, patiently, preserving the one inhabitant who had agreed, however long ago, to stay.
