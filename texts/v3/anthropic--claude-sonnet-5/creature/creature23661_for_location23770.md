# What Tends the Reach

Ask anyone who has crossed the Lowmere Reach and survived it whole, and they will disagree with each other on basic facts: how many legs it had, whether it had a face at all, whether they saw it once or many times, whether it was singular or whether "it" is even the right word for something that might be one creature repeated or one creature distributed. This disagreement is not failure of observation. It is the first true thing anyone can tell you about it.

## The Shape It Declines to Keep

It is not large, by the standards of the things you might imagine living somewhere this vast. Most who've glimpsed it put it at the size of a heron, or a wading dog, or a child crouched at the water's edge—the comparisons cluster around that scale and then diverge wildly on everything else. It moves low, belly-wet, favoring the channels over the open sward, and its silhouette reads first as bird: a long articulated neck, a narrow head. But the joints are wrong in the same small specific way the dragonflies are wrong—not grotesquely, not monstrously, just one hinge too many in the foreleg, a second knee where a knee has no business being, so that its gait resolves into something your eye keeps trying and failing to file as *familiar*. It does not have feathers or scales so much as a fine matted pelt the color of wet peat, beaded the same way the basalt is beaded, water tracking off it in the same continuous weep, as though it is not a separate object from the ground but a place where the ground has temporarily gathered itself upward and learned to walk.

Its eyes, on the rare occasions anyone has been close enough and steady enough to look, do not quite point the same direction at the same time—which would read as a deformity anywhere else, but here reads as consistent with everything else about the basin's light: two or three competing aims resolved, barely, into one creature.

## How It Moves Through Its Work

It is never seen idle. Whatever else is uncertain about it, this is agreed on by every account: it is always *doing something*, in the unhurried, undistractable way of a creature that has been doing the same something for a very long time and has stopped needing to think about whether it is the right something.

It moves along the fungal colonies first, nosing or touching each radiating shelf at its glowing edge, and the pulse-rhythm of that shelf changes minutely after contact—brightens, or slows, or shifts its violet-green half a register, the way the held bass note shifts half a step. Whether the creature causes this or merely notices it and moves on is not a distinction the basin seems to make. It pauses longest at the shelves growing off the most submerged logs, the ones nearly drowned, and seems, in some unverifiable way, to be *feeding* them, though no one has seen it carry anything to give.

It is also, more unnervingly, present at the pools when they go mirror-flat. Travelers who've witnessed the basin-wide stilling—every black pool locking glass-smooth in the same instant—report that the creature is reliably nearby in that moment, standing at the edge of one pool or another with its wrong-angled neck bent low, as if listening to something arrive, or checking that something has. Then the stillness breaks, the pools resume their micro-shimmer, and it moves on without any visible satisfaction or disappointment, the way a person checks a gauge they've checked ten thousand times before.

## The Reflection Problem

This is the detail every report converges on, however much they disagree about everything else: its reflection is wrong even by the Reach's standards. Where a human's reflection arrives a half-second early, lifting a hand before the choice to lift it, the creature's reflection in the black pools doesn't merely anticipate it—it sometimes continues moving after the creature itself has gone still, finishing some gesture the body declined to complete, or has already completed elsewhere. People who've watched this long enough describe a creeping conviction that the reflection is the senior partner in the arrangement—that what walks the sward is the echo, sent out to do the legwork, while the original goes on conducting its business in the water, underneath, where the rules are kept.

No one has tested this conviction by trying to touch the reflection instead of the creature. No one who has thought about testing it has stayed in the Reach long enough afterward to report on what they decided.

## What It Is, As Far As Anyone Can Say

It does not behave like a predator; nothing has been documented harmed by it, though nothing has been documented *helped* by it either, in any way a traveler could use. It does not behave like an animal that eats, sleeps, mates, or dies, though it must do at least one of these, somewhere, on some schedule the basin's slowness has rendered invisible to anyone passing through on a human clock.

The most useful way to think of it—offered hesitantly, by the rare few who've spent long enough at the Reach's edge to think about it at all—is as the last remaining hand on an instrument whose original operators are no longer anywhere to be found, and whose purpose it no longer needs to understand in order to keep performing its small, specific, endlessly repeated part: touching the lights, listening at the stillness, leaving its reflection to finish what it started. Not a guardian. Not a god. Something closer to a single working gear left turning in a clock that the rest of the works grew over, long ago, and forgot it needed.
