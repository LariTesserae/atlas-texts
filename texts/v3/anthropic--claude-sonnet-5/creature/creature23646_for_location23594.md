**The Tenders**

You would walk past one for an hour before you understood you were not looking at wreckage.

They do not announce themselves. There is no call, no rustle, none of the cues a body usually gives before it resolves itself out of a background of stalk and strut — because the Tenders are not really separate from that background, not in the way an animal is separate from a forest. They are the place's own slow attention, grown into something that can move.

Picture a shape roughly the bulk of a draft horse, though "shape" overstates the case — it is closer to a slow-motion event than an object, a tangle of cane and copper-wire root and corroded gear-plate that has, over some unfathomable span, organized itself into a working anatomy. Where a spine should be there is a fused rail of iron track, still gauge-true, still faintly warm with current; ribs of split bamboo curve from it like a hull's frame, lashed and grown through with the same phosphorescent fungal mat that lights the deep galleries, so that a Tender glows very faintly from within, a lantern built of its own viscera. It has no face. It has, instead, a forward cluster of valve-wheels, three or four, slowly turning of their own vegetable torque, venting small huffs of hot vapor in a rhythm that might be breath, might be nothing more than the same blind exhalation the walls themselves perform.

It moves the way the whole tableland moves: by groan and creak and the patient redistribution of enormous weight, root-clusters at its underside unspooling forward through the leaf-litter, gripping old rail and rotted brick and hauling the rest of the bulk after, an inch at a time, hours to cross a room a man could cross in ten strides. But it never stops. That is the thing to understand about a Tender — it has nowhere to arrive, and so it is never not moving, the way a heart is never not beating, the way the silos topple and reknit. It is simply the place's habit of repair given enough cohesion to relocate.

What does it do, this slow glowing wreck of a creature? It tends. Where a stalk has split a boiler-seam and the steam is venting wild and untended, a Tender will arrive — eventually, with the patience of geology — and lean its bulk into the wound, and the wound will, over days, close around it, vine and gear-plate and Tender-flesh knitting into a single patched joint, and when it pulls free weeks later it will have left some of itself behind, a graft, an offering, and grown a little more cane in the gap where it gave up the tissue. Where a flywheel has begun grinding bare against bare metal, screaming a thin metal note into the dark, a Tender will press a frond-mat into the joint until the friction is silenced, sap weeping into the gears as a kind of oil, the wheel slowly furring over with a black-green lubricant skin.

They do not seem to communicate, Tender to Tender, except in the way that two flames in the same draft might be said to communicate — by sharing the same current, the same temperature, the same hunger for continuance. Sometimes two of them will be found leaned root to root for years at a stretch, fused along one seam, sharing a phosphor-glow between them, before some signal — a pressure shift, a chemical tide in the sap-flood below the catwalks — sends them apart again to their separate, glacial errands.

They have no hunger you would recognize, no want, no fear: when a silo finally collapses on one mid-repair, it does not struggle, does not cry — it simply stops moving, and is, like everything else here, reclaimed, its rail-spine becoming someone's trellis, its valve-wheels rusting into ornament for new cane to climb. There is no funeral for a Tender because there is no real boundary between a Tender alive and a Tender composted; it was always borrowed material, on loan from the same recursive economy that grew the chimneys and the rails and the bramble-silos in the first place.

If you stood very still in one of the deep galleries — deep enough that the wire-veins thread thick as cable through the cane-wall, deep enough that the air sits hottest and stillest — you might feel, before you see it, a shift in temperature against your skin, a slow brightening of the amber under-glow off to your left. And then the valve-wheels would resolve out of the dark, turning without urgency, and the long rail-spine, and the huff of vented vapor, unmistakably aimed nowhere, unmistakably alive in whatever sense this place still permits the word — and it would pass you, eventually, the way weather passes, without acknowledging that you were ever there to be passed.

It is not hostile. It has no model of you to be hostile within. It is simply the Works' way of holding itself together one more century, made temporarily mobile, made temporarily singular — a wound, walking, in search of other wounds to become.
