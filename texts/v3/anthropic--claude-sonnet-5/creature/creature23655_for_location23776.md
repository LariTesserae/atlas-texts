# Who Keeps the Undercroft

They are easiest to describe by what doesn't apply to them: they do not hurry, they do not finish sentences if a sentence can be left provisional, and they do not seem bothered by the fact that you might walk through a room they're sitting in and not register them as separate from the wall behind them for a full ten seconds. That isn't camouflage. It's something more like agreement — an arrangement worked out between the residents and the architecture, ages ago, about how much of a person needs to be *resolved* at any given moment.

## Bodies and Becoming

They are built the way the court is built: in layers that don't always agree on a single silhouette. Underneath, there's something durable — bone-dense, slow-moving, settled — and laid over that, a kind of secondary anatomy that hasn't committed yet. Fingers sometimes end where you expect and sometimes end a knuckle short or a knuckle long, and nobody remarks on it, the way you wouldn't remark on a coastline shifting half a meter in a season. Children are the most unfinished, visibly so — their edges soft in the same way the granular ground is soft, their faces still deciding on a final set of proportions, and the adults handle them with a specific gentleness that isn't protective so much as *patient*, the way you'd handle wet plaster rather than a sleeping thing.

Aging here doesn't read as decline. It reads as resolution. The oldest residents are the sharpest-edged — faces settled into their final geometry the way the court's corners sharpen over a generation instead of a day — and there's a quiet status in having finished becoming whatever you're going to be. No one rushes toward that. There's no virtue assigned to speed. There's only a kind of relief, visible in the oldest ones, the relief of a line that has finally decided where to stop.

## Dress and the Laundry on the Rail

They wear layers too, of course — it would be strange if they didn't, given everything else — fabric that drapes in the conduit-glow with a faint sheen, not luminous itself but receptive to light the way the colorless stalks are receptive to it. The laundry that exists in two places simultaneously isn't a malfunction of the court; it's simply what happens when a garment is washed at a threshold where the geometry hasn't decided yet whether the rail is one rail or two. Residents consider this mildly inconvenient, the way you'd consider a dryer that runs long. They check both locations out of habit, the way you'd check both your pockets for keys.

## How They Move

They've learned — the way you learn a limp, unconsciously, body-first — to navigate by ratio rather than sightline. A resident crossing the court doesn't count steps or watch landmarks; they lean into the disagreement between the two measurements and let it carry them, the way a sailor leans into a current rather than fighting it. Visitors are conspicuous immediately, before they've said a word, because they walk like the geometry owes them something. Residents walk like it's still working it out, and they're willing to wait.

The central stair is treated with a specific, unspoken etiquette. You don't take it when you're in a hurry, because hurrying through eleven minutes that should be three produces a particular nausea, a grief almost, like being lied to gently. The old ones use it deliberately, sometimes, when they want to arrive somewhere slightly different — a different floor, a different color of laundry — the way someone elsewhere might take a long drive to think. It's understood as a kind of consultation. You go in with a question you haven't worded yet and come out one floor over, and sometimes the floor answers it.

## Kinship and the Loop

Family here isn't drawn in strict descending lines, because the architecture won't permit a strict descending line to mean anything for long — the room you were born in may not, structurally, still be "below" the room your parent was born in by the time you're old enough to ask. Instead kinship is tracked by proximity to conduit-glow, by who recharges near whom, by whose translucent garden-stalks lean toward whose threshold. A household is less a bloodline than a *cluster*, the way moisture clusters in a puddle-pocket — gathered by shape, not lineage, and just as liable to redistribute itself if the floor reshapes around it.

The worn path on the stair that loops back into itself belongs, residents will tell you if you ask carefully, to someone who is dead, or finished, or both — the path being the visible residue of a life that completed its geometry and stopped needing the rest of the building. They don't sweep it away. You don't erase a finished line. You just stop walking it, and let it stand as the only truly resolved edge in the whole undercroft, sharper than the corners, sharper than anything still becoming.

## The Ledge-Kin

The cat-sized thing on the high balconies is not a pet, not quite, and not wildlife either. Ask a resident and the closest translation is something like *cousin-not-yet*, or *what we were before we agreed to slow down* — a being that never accepted the deal the rest of them struck with the architecture, the deal to resolve gradually, to let edges sharpen on the court's schedule rather than its own. It changes shape between sightings because it never stopped negotiating its own geometry the way the residents did. It is regarded with a mixture of fondness and unease, the way you might regard a sibling who left for the city and came back strange. It is fed, sort of — bowls of condensation-water left out, more ritual than nourishment — and it is never touched, not out of fear but out of a sense that touching it might unsettle a question that's better left open.

The far-off bird-call that goes unanswered is, as far as anyone has determined, the ledge-kin too — calling, perhaps, to whatever it was before that, further up the chain of things that haven't finished deciding what they are.

## Language and Silence

They speak rarely and economically, partly from temperament and partly because sound behaves the way light does here — arriving from ambiguous sources, sometimes doubled, sometimes cancelling itself in transit between two points that should be close but aren't. A held silence is not withholding; it's practical. Important things are said once, plainly, in a doorway with good acoustics, and then left alone, the way you'd leave a wall alone once you'd hung something level on it.

Their humor, when it surfaces, is dry and architectural: jokes about a neighbor's room that's grown a third wall overnight, about a child's foot that came in a size larger than the shoe they'd been building for it, about the spiral stair being "in one of its moods." Grief is handled the same register-down way — quietly, structurally, folded into the maintenance of a finished path rather than spoken about directly, the same way the court itself doesn't discuss the bad pipe.

## What Holds Them Here

No one seems to consider leaving, and the few who have, residents say, found that the geometry followed them partway and then let go, abruptly, the way a hand releases something it was never quite holding. Coming back is described not as homecoming so much as *re-entering agreement* — the slow re-acceptance of a place that measures you in two contradictory ways at once and expects you to live inside both measurements without flinching.

What they have, more than any single trait, is the court's own patience worn into a population: an entire people who have made peace with being unfinished, who raise unfinished children and tend unfinished gardens and walk unfinished stairs, confident — not hopeful, *confident* — that the shape will arrive eventually, on no schedule anyone needs to track, and that in the meantime there is laundry to check in two places, water to leave out for the cousin on the ledge, and a floor, somewhere underfoot, quietly deciding the final position of its corners.
