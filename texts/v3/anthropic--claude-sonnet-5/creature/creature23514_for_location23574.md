They are called, by no one, the Held — because the only name that ever attaches to them is the gap in sound they leave behind: the minute of stillness after the organ-note exhale, when every grazer on the tier stops cropping the glow-moss and every too-jointed cliff-walker freezes mid-stride. That hush is the only signature they sign. You will likely never see one. You will feel, through the moss and the soles of your boots, the long low note arriving from some lower shelf, and you will understand, the way animals understand, that something has just breathed, and that you are smaller than it.

If you were to see one — and people have, rarely, at the very edge of a tier where the cliff goes vertical into cloud — you would have trouble deciding whether you were looking at an animal or a slow catastrophe of geology. The body is long, low, segmented the way the basalt is segmented, knuckle after knuckle of dark hide grown over with the same lichen that pads the rock, so that a resting Held is indistinguishable from a fold in the mountain until it moves, and then it moves the way fog moves — without apparent urgency, but covering more ground in a minute than seems possible. Its limbs, when it has need of limbs, fold out from beneath it in a number that doesn't resolve cleanly on a second count; mostly it travels low and braced, belly-down, more landslide than walk.

It has no eyes. It has never needed eyes. Along its flank, just under the lichen-crust, run lines of some pale, gill-like structure — not for breathing air, though it does that too, in those long held organ-notes — but for *listening* to the pistons. The heartbeat-vibration that visitors feel only as a faint unease through their boots is, to the Held, a font of information as rich as daylight: it reads the turning of the deep machinery the way a person reads a face. It can tell, from the rhythm bleeding up through forty meters of rock, whether a vein runs warm or has begun to cool, whether a turbine has slowed its century-long rotation, whether some far seam of the mountain's hidden engine has shifted in its sleep. It goes to the warm places the way a moth goes to flame, except slower, except over weeks, dragging itself shelf by shelf toward a pipe-vein's exhalation of vapor, and there it lies for a long time — months, maybe longer; nobody has watched one long enough to be sure — pressed against the warm rock with its gill-lines open, drinking heat the way the ferns around it drink humidity from the air.

It is not gentle, exactly, and it is not cruel; the distinction doesn't seem to apply to something that hunts the way it does. When it is hungry — which is rare, since the warmth alone seems to sustain most of its long stillnesses — it rises off the rock and becomes, for a while, the patient ceremonial stalker the grazing herds half-sense and never quite see: a humped shape at the edge of vision that the pale long-limbed grazers will not look toward, that takes hours to cross a meadow, that closes the last twenty meters in a silence so complete the moss doesn't even release its held water. The kill, when it comes, is small and final and over before the fog has finished registering that something happened. Then stillness again. Then, sometimes, the long exhale — that organ-note breath felt through stone for kilometers, source utterly unfindable, and every living thing on the tier goes quiet, because something in the body remembers, even without ever having been taught, that the note means *something here is large, and fed, and at rest.*

Nothing knows how old the Held are, individually or as a kind. They do not seem to breed often; no one has ever reported seeing two together, let alone a birth. There is a theory, unprovable and probably untestable, that there are not many of them at all — perhaps one to a great span of tiers, perhaps fewer — and that what people take for a species is really a scattering of individuals so long-lived that each one has simply outlasted the need for company. There is a second theory, murmured rather than argued, that the Held are not separate from the machinery at all: that somewhere far back, before there were eyes on this mountain to notice anything, the engines and the things that warmed themselves against the engines stopped being two kinds of thing and became one slow process wearing two faces — the turbines turning because the Held's long dormancy against them somehow keeps the pistons fed, the Held surviving because the engines never quite stop. Nobody has gotten close enough, or lived long enough watching, to settle it either way.

What is certain is only this: they do not build, and they do not tend, in any sense a person would recognize as building or tending. They simply lie where the warmth is, and breathe when they are full, and stalk when they are not, on a clock that makes the mountain's own glacial patience look almost hurried. They are not the engineers of Vond's Throat. They are its oldest tenants — the first thing, perhaps, to have understood that this place runs on something, and to have decided, with the whole slow weight of a body built like a landslide, that the correct response to that fact was not to ask why, but to lie down against the warm vein and stay.
