The first thing you notice is that they never look at the water the way a visitor does — never down, assessing, gauging depth or temperament. They look at it the way you'd look at a familiar dog: sidelong, habitually, checking only for mood. That's how you know a fjord-pilot from someone passing through. The water has stopped being scenery for them. It's load-bearing.

She — though it could as easily be he, or some weathered ambiguity the camps don't bother sorting out — runs a steam launch between three logging stations and a crushing plant that hasn't had a name in a decade, just a number stenciled on a rusting tank. Her hands are the first specific thing: cracked at the knuckles in a way that never quite heals because they're wet eight hours a day and cold the other sixteen, the skin gone the texture of the fluted rock itself, pale and ridged. She wears wool that's always slightly damp, because everything here is always slightly damp, and she stopped fighting that years ago. There's a particular hunch to her shoulders, not quite a deformity but close, earned from decades of ducking under boom-chains and standing in a wheelhouse built for someone shorter.

What she's good at — what makes her valuable rather than merely present — is reading the held breath of the place. She knows the difference between the ordinary roar of falling water and the specific change in pitch that means a vegetation shelf two kilometers up a side-valley has gone saturated and is about to let go in a green avalanche that will raise a wake worth respecting. She knows which thermoclines mean warm outflow and which mean something larger moving beneath, displacing water on a schedule nobody's bothered making official. She has seen the carcasses on the gravel shoals and has opinions she doesn't share with the newer crews, because frightened men work slower and there's timber to move.

She doesn't believe in the chapels built from drift-lumber, but she nods at them passing, the way you'd nod at a buoy — not out of faith, just out of respect for a marker that's kept its place. She has buried two engines' worth of savings into a boat that is more patch than hull at this point, because a new one would mean new debt to a company office three drowned valleys away, and besides, the old launch knows the channels the way she does — by feel, by the sound the water makes off the bow, by which gulls scatter and which don't bother.

She is not unique. There are a few dozen like her threading these channels — barge-women, winch-men, a chute-tender who's gone half-deaf from forty years of listening to logs boom into water and calls it a fair trade — and together they form the only真 stable population this place has, more permanent in their way than the camps, which relocate, or the company, which folds and reopens under new names. The mountains are too finished to need tending. The water is too old to belong to anyone. But someone has to move the things that are dying to be moved — timber, ore, mail, the occasional coffin going back out to wherever the dead came from — and so there is always, somewhere on the black water between one rust-orange cliff and the next, the cough of a steam engine and a shape at the wheel who has long since stopped hearing the waterfalls as sound, and started hearing them, the way you eventually do here, as the silence underneath everything else.
