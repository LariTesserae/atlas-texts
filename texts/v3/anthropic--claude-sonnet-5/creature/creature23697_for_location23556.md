The people who keep the Cistern Works running are, without exception, the kind who stop noticing sound after the first month.

You can tell the newcomers from the old hands by what they flinch at. A boiler venting hard six feet behind someone's back — the old hands don't turn. A frog-throb cutting out mid-call because something moved in the reeds — the old hands *do* turn, just slightly, a recalibration too small to call alertness. They've learned the place's two registers so thoroughly that their bodies have become a kind of tuning instrument, sensitive only to the gaps and the wrongnesses, deaf to everything that's supposed to be there.

There aren't many of them. Call it twenty, twenty-five at a stretch — enough to crew the pump-station in shifts, keep the boiler-houses fed, walk the conduits checking for blockage and slip, and no more than that, because the place doesn't require more and wouldn't have room for more even if it did. They live in the gaps between functions the way the birds live in the gaps between buildings: a bunk-room over the cooperage, a mess attached to the pump-station's lee side, a few private sheds wedged into corners that the original plan apparently didn't account for. Nobody seems to have decided this. It simply filled in that way, the way water finds its level.

Their clothes are always slightly wet — not soaked, just permanently damp at the collar and the small of the back, the way the railings are always beaded with condensation. They've stopped fighting it. Sleeves rolled, collars open, a kind of loose-jointed economy of movement that comes from working in heat that never breaks: nobody hurries here who doesn't have to, because hurrying in this air costs twice what it would somewhere dry. Their skin has gone the colour of the place itself — not tanned exactly, more *steamed*, a permanent faint flush, like meat held too long over a low fire.

They talk little, and what talk there is gets swallowed by the engine-drone before it travels far, so they've developed a vocabulary of gesture instead — a hand flat means hold, a hand turned over means vent it, a particular tilt of the head toward the marsh means *something's off down there, go check.* Outsiders find this unsettling, a settlement that communicates mostly in silhouette. The workers themselves don't think about it. It's just quieter than shouting over a pump-cycle, and quiet, even a small private quiet inside a loud place, is something they've learned to value out of all proportion to how little of it they get.

There's an oldest one — there always is, in a place like this — who's been here since before the basin had a name, who knows which pipe-joint will start singing a half-tone off before it actually fails, who can hear a frog-chorus thin out by two voices and know which reed-clump to walk toward. Whether this is wisdom or just very long exposure, nobody asks. He — or she, the role outlasts whoever holds it — sits most evenings on an upturned tank-stub near the marsh fringe, in the bronze non-light of the haze-flattened sun, watching the birds shuffle along the pipe-runs for a better perch, and doesn't say much, and isn't expected to.

They don't seem unhappy. That's the strange part, if you stay long enough to wonder about it. A loud, wet, sharp-edged, unchanging place, and the people in it have the settled quality of things that arrived somewhere and simply stayed, the way the works themselves arrived at a finished size and stopped — not arrested, not enduring, just *idling*, at the same hum as everything else, under the same coin-colored sky.
