# What Answers the Weight

You will hear it before you understand that you have heard it: a chord beneath the dripping, beneath the creak of growth-too-slow-to-be-movement, a bass note carried wet-wood-to-wet-wood across distances that should have swallowed it. The first time, you will mistake it for the reach itself settling. By the third time, you will start listening for the silence that follows, because the silence is how you will learn that something out there is also listening back.

It does not have a name that travels. The handful of cultures who have skirted Verdhollow Reach and returned to tell of it call it different things—the Slow Lord, the Root-Walker, the Sleeping Wall—names that all gesture at the same impossible category error: an animal that has been allowed, by sheer accumulated time, to become indistinguishable from the place that made it.

## The Shape of It, As Far As Anyone Has Confirmed

Imagine first the wrongness of scale. The fern-cliffs that stand three times the height of a person, the moss grown thick enough to be cliff-face in its own right—against this geography, the creature is not enormous. It is *proportionate*. That is the unsettling part. It moves through the upper canopy-terrain the way a creature of ordinary size moves through ordinary undergrowth, which means that everything about Verdhollow Reach has been quietly scaled to accommodate something this large as an unremarkable resident rather than an aberration.

Its body, where bodies can be said to end and canopy begin, is long beyond any single vantage point's ability to confirm—segmented not like an insect but like a fallen, articulated trunk, each section bearing its own accreted weather of moss and lichen and the small clinging ecosystems that have apparently decided, generations ago, that this moving ridge of flesh-and-bark was as permanent a foundation as any fused root-shoulder. There are things that live and die and are buried in the folds of its back. There are saplings, genuinely rooted, genuinely photosynthesizing, that have sprouted from accumulated soil-on-hide and grown to respectable size before some shift of the creature's slow migration shook them loose to fall and continue their lives on the ground proper, indistinguishable thereafter from anything else that grows there.

It has limbs, when it deigns to use them, fewer than the bulk would suggest necessary, thick as the trunks it threads itself between, ending in something that is neither hoof nor paw nor root but performs the function of all three: grips, presses, distributes weight across the soft sleeping substrate so evenly that even its passage barely qualifies as disturbance. This is why you find the bent-and-recovering vegetation rather than a true track. It does not so much walk across the canopy as negotiate with it, asking permission of each branch-ridge before committing weight, and being granted that permission, because the canopy has known it for longer than the canopy has known almost anything else still capable of motion.

Of a face, there is less to say with confidence than you would like. Reports disagree—some travelers swear to eyes, slow-blinking, set wide and deep in folds of bark-textured hide; others insist on no eyes at all, only a forward-facing density of sensory pits that read the saturated air the way the rest of the reach reads moisture: by accumulation rather than glance. What no one disputes is the mouth, when it opens, which is less a maw than a slow parting of growth, lined inside with the same bioluminescent fungal patience that pulses along the root-floor elsewhere in the reach—a mouth that glows faintly from within, on the same multi-day cycle as everything else here that glows, so that to see it open at all you must be patient in a way few visiting creatures remain capable of being.

## What It Does, Which Is Almost Nothing, Which Is Everything

It eats, if eating is the word, by pressing its bulk into a section of aging canopy-terrain and simply waiting—days, by some accounts weeks—while whatever has died and is dying in that fold is drawn slowly into and through it, processed at the same patient rate the reach processes everything, and deposited somewhere else, hours or days later, as the beginnings of new substrate. It is not predator. It is not quite scavenger. It performs a function closer to the slow churn of the canopy-mass itself: it is one of the mechanisms by which the reach keeps its central law, *nothing here ends*, operating at the scale of the individually dead leaf and the individually dead beast alike. It is digestion given the dignity of independent locomotion.

It sleeps in increments that may, from the outside, be indistinguishable from simply existing—a stillness so total that more than one traveler has built a fire against what they believed was a moss-grown outcrop, only to feel, hours later, the ground beneath them shift with the unmistakable, unhurried decision of something deciding to move on. It does not appear to resent this. It does not appear to register urgency of any kind, ever, about anything—not threat, not hunger, not the small parasitic ecosystems thriving and dying across its own back. Time, for it, is not survived. It is simply the medium it is suspended in, the way the perpetual green-gold dusk is the medium the whole reach is suspended in.

## The Old Marks, and What It Might Remember of Them

It is, depending on which account you credit and how much credit accounts deserve in a place that resists measurement on principle, old enough to have been alive when the wall-that-is-now-a-root-shoulder was still a wall, when the circular depression in the canopy-roof was still a dwelling with people moving through it. Whether it has any memory worth the name is unanswerable, but there is a behavior reported by exactly the kind of traveler whose word you'd ordinarily distrust and find yourself, in this place, inclined to credit anyway: that it pauses, sometimes, at those scattered absorbed ruins—lingers there longer than its usual unhurried passage would require, in a way that almost resembles attention. Whether this is memory, or only the accident of those spots retaining some chemical residue from their builders that its slow senses still register as worth dwelling on, no one has stayed long enough, or moved at a compatible enough pace, to determine.

## The End It Will Eventually Have, Which Will Not Be an End

It will die, someday—it is, after all, a living thing in a place that does distinguish between living and dead, even if it refuses to distinguish between dead and *gone*. And when it does, it will not be removed, will not be mourned in any sense a visitor would recognize as mourning, will simply settle, over a span of time too long for any witness to confirm beginning and end of the same observation, into the same fused mass that grew it. Some ridge of the canopy-terrain, some generations hence, will be subtly different in shape because of where it finally stopped moving—a little higher, a little richer with the kind of soil that only comes from something that large taking that long to finish becoming ground.

Walkers who cross that future ridge, whoever or whatever they will be, will feel it give slightly beneath them, like walking on the back of something sleeping, and they will be more right than they know.
