## What Answers

You came in expecting silence with maybe one thing living in it — a feral cat, a colony of bats, something with a name and an edge. That's not what this is. The Underchamber Works supports a population, stacked in strata like the leaf-litter itself, and no single creature in it is the *point*. The point is the arrangement. But if you wait long enough, and keep still long enough, certain shapes do resolve out of the aggregate.

### The Current Over the Water

The insect column above the trench is not one species behaving as a cloud — it's several, occupying different elevations of the same column like commuters on different floors of the same building. Near the water's black skin, something pale and many-winged skims in tight loops, laying eggs on the underside of floating leaf-fall; you never see them land, only the dimple they leave. Above them, a mid-air band of biting flies, drawn to the warmth that radiates off the pressure vessels, moves in a slow gyre that never quite breaks even when something passes through it — it parts and reknits like a curtain. And above *that*, near the gantry, something larger patrols the column on dragonfly wings the size of playing cards, picking off the rest at a rate that keeps the whole tower in equilibrium rather than plague. It has a flattened, almost reptilian head for an insect, mandibles that click audibly between strikes, and it is, as far as anyone has been positioned to determine, the closest thing this chamber has to a single dominant intelligence below knee height.

### The Argument in the Rafters

The three-note callers are not birds, not quite, though they do the job birds do elsewhere. They're built low and round, flightless or nearly so, their forelimbs vestigial paddles fringed with stiff plumage that's better for gripping bark than catching air. What they're extraordinary at is climbing and at *noise* — a resonating throat-sac, visible as a pale bladder under the chin when they call, that lets ten of them sound like thirty. They nest in the transformer housing because it's warm, and they've learned, generation over generation, to time their loudest calling to the moments the machinery vents steam, so that the warning — because it is a warning, a relay system passed bird to bird across the vault — rides under cover of a sound nothing predates on. They are calling about the climber. They have always been calling about the climber.

### The Climber

It is the size of a dog only in the way a closed fist is the size of an open hand — compact, but you sense the unfolding potential in it before you ever see it unfold. Knuckle-gaited, four-limbed but capable of an unsettling bipedal rear when reaching, its hide has gone the mottled green-brown of the vines it lives in so thoroughly that *camouflage* undersells it — it's less that the creature hides among the leaves and more that, after enough generations, the leaves and the creature stopped being distinguishable categories. It eats the fungal shelving, peeling dinner-plate tiers off the columns with hooked thumbs, and it eats whatever the bird-things fail to warn away in time. It does not seem to fear the machinery's hum or the current in the bus bar; if anything it beds down near the warmest conduits, curling there in a way that looks almost reverent, like a cat on a sunbeam, except the sunbeam here is voltage and the reverence might just be thermoregulation. You will hear it more than see it. The parting of leaves is the most you're owed.

### What's in the Trench

Nobody has put a hand into the standing water and gotten the hand back to confirm a theory, so this part stays a theory. But the black mirror ripples on a rhythm that doesn't match the condensation drip, doesn't match any draft, and is too regular to be the insects landing. Something long-bodied lives along the bottom of that trench, surfacing rarely, feeding — it's guessed — on whatever falls, drowns, or gets washed there from the chamber's slow accumulating rot. The fungal light along that wall pulses brighter on the same rhythm as the ripple, which either means the fungus is reacting to it, or means the two of them have been doing this dance so long they've synced.

### Underfoot

The leaf-litter's "small civilizations" are exactly that — civilizations, plural, layered: detritivores at the bottom breaking down a hundred years of compost into the black soil the vines are rooted in, predators of those detritivores one rung up, and at the very top of that miniature ladder something that builds — actual structures, small domed mounds of chewed pulp and mineral grit, dotted with unsettling regularity along the beaten paths, as if marking them rather than merely occupying them. Whether the mounds are nests, graves, or signs meant for the larger animals to read is not a question anyone underfoot is positioned to answer either.

### The One That Breathes

And then, down the corridor where the wet exhalation comes from at long, unhurried intervals — bigger than anything else accounted for, never seen, only heard and felt, a faint settling vibration in the conduit lines each time it shifts. The bird-things go quiet for several seconds after each breath, which is the only reliable evidence that it is, in fact, alive and not some failing piece of the machinery's last stubborn cycle. Everything else in the Works seems organized, however loosely, around staying out of its way, feeding on its leavings, or — in the case of the fungal light, which brightens perceptibly toward that corridor and dims everywhere else — *listening* for it.

No one has gone to check. The paths don't go that way. The paths, if you look closely at how they bend, go specifically *around* it — a wide, unbroken, leaf-littered avoidance worn into the ground by a hundred years of every other living thing in this chamber agreeing, without needing to discuss it, on the one rule that actually governs the place: the machine idles, the green grows, the small things eat each other in good order, and whatever sleeps at the end of the corridor is allowed to keep sleeping.
