They are easy to miss, and that is not an accident of size — it is closer to an accident of *tense*. You catch one only ever in the past: not "there is a pale shape on the root-stair" but "there was," the present already over by the time the eye finishes the report. A flicker the color of the underside of a leaf, low to the wet wood, gone into a seam before it resolves into a shape you could describe to someone else.

When they do hold still — and they do, sometimes, for whole minutes, mistaken for a knot or a graft-scar — you can see that they are not built like anything that ought to live in a forest. No fur, no shell, no clean animal economy of legs. They are soft the way new bark is soft: pale, faintly green-veined, damp without being wet, as if they were grown rather than born, in the same idiom as everything around them. A body like a folded leaf-bud, low and tapered, with too many small points of articulation along its underside — not legs exactly, more like a root system experimentally practicing at locomotion, each tendril-foot finding purchase and releasing it in a ripple rather than a step. They have no faces you could call faces. Where a face would be there is a cluster of soft retractable filaments, sensitive past any apparent need, that taste the air and the sap-moisture and withdraw.

What they do, in the rare unbroken stretches when you can watch, is small and exact: they press into the green-gold seam where one grown limb was fused to another and was nearly, but not perfectly, made to stay; they exude something clear from the underside of the body into the wound; they hold there, trembling slightly, until the seam has taken the offering and closed a fraction further toward seamlessness. Then they withdraw, fold close to the wood, and are gone again, indistinguishable from a fold of bark, before you can decide whether you actually saw a creature or a trick of wet light.

This is the whole of their visible labor: tending the last centimeters of joints that are, by any reasonable measure, already finished. They are not building. The building is over. What they are doing — what they seem to have always been doing, and seem destined to keep doing for as long as there is a them to do it — is the very last residue of the act of building, run on past its necessity, like a held muscle that has forgotten it was ever told to relax. They are the only things here that still, technically, change anything. Everything else — root, platform, canopy, stair — has been told to stop, and has stopped, and holds. They have not quite gotten the message, or got it and cannot comply, because compliance would mean ceasing to exist in any active sense at all.

There used to be more of them — you intuit this less from evidence than from architecture: there are vastly more grown joints than there are visible custodians to tend them, the ratio of finished structure to remaining attendant is wrong in a way that only makes sense as the tail end of a decline. Whatever they were when the growing was furious and the terraces were rising tier on tier — and they must have been many then, swarming the scaffolding of their own labor, threading limb into limb, training root downward at intervals — they are few now, and growing fewer, and quieter, each generation (if that's even the word — nothing has been seen mating, hatching, dying; they are simply, occasionally, present, and then less present) folding a little more permanently into the wood they tend, until the line between custodian and architecture blurs past any usefulness.

You begin to suspect, walking far enough along identical terraces, that this is in fact the destination, not an accident: that they are not separate from the building at all but its unfinished edge, the part of the growing-intelligence that has not yet agreed to stop, slowly, terrace by terrace, joint by joint, being persuaded into stillness the same way the limbs once were persuaded into walls. One day — not soon, not within any frame a visitor could wait out, but someday, the way every other tendril here was someday made to hold — they will press themselves into a final seam, exude the last of whatever they exude, and not withdraw. And the region will be, at last, entirely finished: no flicker at the edge of sight, no held-breath hand still moving the architecture into its rest, only the rest itself, complete, attended by no one, dripping forever into its own silence.
