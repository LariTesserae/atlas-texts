# Inhabitants of the Hollow Gardens

## The Canopy-Flyers

In the upper dark, where the fungal caps spread into rooftop-wide ceilings of their own, something like birds moves in flocks too dense to count. They are not birds — no feather has ever grown in this lightless air — but membrane-winged things, pale as the caps they roost on, with skin so thin the shape of their wing-bones shows through like the ribs of an old umbrella. They are called, by the few outsiders who have ever named them, *canopy-skimmers*, though they have no name for themselves that any visitor could hear as a name. They do not see in any meaningful sense; their faces have folded inward around clustered sensory pits that read the air itself — pressure, moisture-that-isn't, the faint thermal ghost of a body moving below. They flock by the hundreds, wheeling between the giant caps in patterns that look, from below, like weather. At dusk-that-never-ends they scream their territory all at once, a single compounding shriek that rolls down off the stone sky and back up again, and for a few minutes the whole cavern sounds like something tearing.

## The Ground-Herds

Below them, grazing the swelled stone in slow unhurried migrations, move the herd-beasts — squat, broad-bodied things, segmented along the back like old armor plate, moving on six or eight thick legs that never seem to hurry because nothing here has ever needed them to. They graze the bramble-curtains and the low fungal scrub with wide flat mouths built for tearing fibrous growth rather than chewing flesh, and their hides have taken on the exact grey-green of the moss they live among, until a resting herd looks like nothing more than another fold of ground. They have no eyes worth speaking of. What they have instead are long whip-sensitive antennae, constantly testing the air for the chemical breath of fresh spore-growth, and it's this faculty alone that steers their generational drift from one grazing ground to the next — a migration so slow that a single circuit of their feeding range can outlast the human reckoning of a lifetime. They are not dangerous, except in the way that anything large and indifferent is dangerous to stand in front of. They press through the old stone causeways without seeming to notice they are causeways at all, hooves — if they can be called hooves — wearing fresh grooves into the same fitted rock that something once shaped with deliberate, patient hands.

## The Tower-Nesters

The hollowed dwellings, the dark windowed shelves climbing the cavern walls, belong now to the nesting things — colonial, chittering, packed by the hundreds into chambers that once held families of some other kind entirely. They are small, quick (quick for this place, which is not very quick), clawed climbers with overlong digits suited to gripping worn stone, and they have learned the old architecture as thoroughly as any original tenant ever did: which ledges catch the updraft of warmer cave-air, which collapsed floors make the best refuse middens, which towers still hold their roofs against the slow centuries of falling grit. At dusk-that-never-ends they emerge in their hundreds to shriek territorial claims across the gaps between towers, a sound that bounces tower to tower until an entire district screams at once, a city arguing with itself in a language with no other use.

## The Deep-Callers

And below all of that, past the edge of the dim light, are the things only heard. Long, groaning calls roll up out of unlit fissures and lower chambers no one has mapped, calls too slow and too deep to belong to anything small. No one has seen what makes them. They might be a single old creature, ancient as the towers, moving with the same indifferent patience as everything else in this place. They might be many things, signaling to each other across distances too vast for any other sense to bridge. They surface a few times in a long dusk and then fall silent again for what might be days, and the not-knowing is, for everyone who has ever stood in the Hollow Gardens and listened, the single most unsettling fact about an unsettling place.

## The Stone-Kin

And then — maybe — there are the others. No one is certain the builders are gone. There is a presence sometimes felt rather than seen: stones restacked in small deliberate cairns at the heads of old causeways, with no animal motive a herd-beast or skimmer would have for doing it; a doorway that was collapsed last season and is, somehow, this season, cleared again; small arrangements of bone and dried fungal husk left in patterns too regular to be midden-waste and too crude to be obviously purposeful, like the muscle-memory of a hand that no longer remembers what building was for. If something like the builders survives, it survives diminished — smaller, quieter, folded back into the ecology as just another grazing, nesting, listening thing among the rest, indistinguishable at a glance from the fauna it shares the ruins with. It does not seem to build anymore. It seems, instead, to *tend* — absently, without plan, the way a hand might idly smooth a worn stone without intending anything by it. Travelers who have spent long enough in the deep towns report the same thing, always at the edges of vision, never confirmed: a watchfulness in the dark windows that the nesting-things don't have, a stillness among the rubble that isn't a herd-beast resting and isn't a trick of the gloaming.

Nothing here can confirm it. Nothing here denies it either. The Hollow Gardens keep that question the way they keep everything else — patiently, at the speed of stone, under a roar of living noise that will outlast anyone who comes looking for the answer.
