They call themselves Hollowfolk, when asked, though the name is more habit than identity — nobody decided it, it simply accreted the way everything else here accretes, layer over layer until it's load-bearing. What they are, more precisely, is the species of person this kind of building produces if you give it three or four generations: quiet-moving, narrow-shouldered, comfortable in spaces other bodies would call too small or too cold. They have the particular pallor of people who get their light filtered twice — through cloud, then through glass gone green with moss-stain — and a way of walking that favors the outer edge of the foot, learned without anyone teaching it, because the inner boards of half these floors have gone soft with old damp and a Hollowfolk child knows this in the bones before they know it in words.

Their hands are the tell. Most trades here don't separate cleanly into mechanic or gardener, so neither do the hands — you'll see calluses from valve-wheels sitting next to the particular green-black staining that comes from pinching dead growth off a heat-fern at the wrist joint, where the plant likes to root into itself. A Tender, which is the closest thing to a formal trade-word, is expected to know both the pressure gauge and the leaf: when a conservatory's vine cover is doing real structural work versus merely decorative work, when a stack's draft has gone wrong because something living in the flue is sick rather than because the firebox needs adjusting. Apprenticeships run long, ten, twelve years, not because the skills are individually hard but because the place itself won't sit still long enough to be learned quickly. You can't master a building that rearranges its own stairwells. You can only get good at noticing.

And noticing is the whole discipline. Hollowfolk are raised on a catechism of small attentions that nobody calls a catechism: watch the moss-glow, not the gauge, for whether a room's still breathable; never put weight on a stair you don't remember from the week before; if something's growing out of an engine housing, leave it, thank it if you're the sentimental sort, because it's doing a job nobody assigned it and doing a job is doing a job. Children absorb this the way children absorb anything load-bearing to survival — not as rules but as instinct, the way they instinctively duck under a dripping leaf-bell two streets from home without looking up, the way they go quiet, actually quiet, breath quiet, when they pass a stack that was cold yesterday and is humming today, because a stack that's changed state overnight is a stack still deciding what it's doing and you do not want its attention.

They live in households that are less family units than tenancy clusters — three generations, sometimes four, distributed across floors that may not, by spring, still belong to the same building. Permanence is not expected of walls here, so it isn't grieved when walls fail to provide it. An old woman's bedroom becomes, over eighteen months, the floor of someone else's greenhouse two structures over, on account of a partition wall getting reclaimed for pipe-fitting, and this is treated the way weather is treated: inconvenient, occasionally a genuine hardship, but not an offense against the order of things, because there is no order of things here to offend. The offense would be expecting one.

This produces a particular Hollowfolk temperament — unhurried about possessions, fiercely attentive about relationships, since people are the only infrastructure here that doesn't get cannibalized overnight for parts. They mark time less by calendar than by configuration: not "three years ago" but "back when the foundry on the third terrace was still running" or "before the root came through the east stair." Memory becomes a kind of cartography of a city that no longer matches its own old maps, and the elders are valued less for wisdom in the abstract than for being walking archives of where things used to connect — which is, in a town like this, very close to the same thing.

They are not alone in the buildings, and they don't pretend to be. The many-legged things that work the pipe-routes are spoken of the way you'd speak of a neighboring trade — "the line crew," half-joking, half not, because nobody's ever caught one being anything but useful, clearing condensate blockages and grooming moss-beds with a thoroughness no human schedule could match. The birds nesting in the flues are accounted for in the engineering — certain vent designs are shaped the way they are specifically to give a nesting pair purchase, an arrangement nobody remembers initiating but everybody now maintains. Whatever answers the birdcall that isn't quite a bird is not discussed much, not out of fear exactly, more the way you don't discuss a neighbor's private business: it's there, it's been there longer than most leases, it has never caused trouble that anyone will admit to.

What unites all of it — Hollowfolk, line crew, nesting things, the unidentified caller in the dark — is a shared and largely unspoken understanding that none of them are the owners here. They are tenants of a hollow that is itself still arriving at a decision, still sinking by inches into shale that hasn't finished its own argument about solidity, and the only sane way to live in a held breath like that is to stop waiting for the exhale and get on with the watering, the gauge-checking, the listening at stairwells for the half-second of sound that hasn't reached you yet. Ask an old Tender what they're doing, crouched at 3 a.m. with an ear against cold copper pipe, waiting on a click that hasn't come, and they'll tell you, without much drama, that they're listening to the house decide something. They'll tell you it's worth waiting up for. They'll tell you this like it's the most ordinary fact in the world, because down here, it is.
