The first thing you learn about the people of Undersill is that they don't call themselves miners, or settlers, or even cavers, though they are technically all three. They call themselves **growers' kin** — not because they grow anything in the agricultural sense, but because they've come to think of themselves as living in a relationship of mutual, slightly uneasy tenancy with something that is, itself, still growing into what it will become. You ask a grower's-kin what that means exactly and they'll shrug and gesture at the canopy, at the too-even branching intervals, and say *you'll see it eventually, give it time, everything here does.*

## The Body

They are compact people, deliberately so — narrow-shouldered, low-statured by the standards of surface populations, an adaptation (or just a culture-wide habit, nobody's sure which came first) suited to ducking under canopy-platforms and threading the gaps between trunk-columns where the rope-bridges are strung tight and low. Their skin runs sallow-pale, faintly waxy in texture, not unlike the chalky bark they live among — whether this is generations of vitamin-D starvation, or some slow ambient effect of the bioluminescent wash, or just the dust that gets into everything and never quite washes out (there being almost no water to wash with), is a matter they argue about themselves, usually over the last mug of something fermented from trunk-sap.

Their eyes are the most consistently remarked-upon feature: large relative to the face, pupils that stay generously dilated even in the arc-lamp work-zones, a trait you'd call nocturnal if there were any night down here to be adapted to. It gives them a look of perpetual mild startlement that doesn't match their temperament at all — they are, almost uniformly, unhurried people, slow-moving in the deliberate way of those who've learned that the compost-mat floor punishes haste and the gantries punish panic.

## Dress and Trade

They dress in fiber-cloth woven from the same loom-lines that process the trunk-fiber for rope — a coarse, warm-colored cloth, the brass-gold of the bioluminescence soaked somehow into the dye-lots, or maybe that's just what plant-fiber looks like grown without sun. Overlaid on this: leather aprons, iron-buckled, soot-stained at the foundry-workers, resin-stained amber at the tappers who run the sap-lines. Tool-belts hung not with surface-world implements but with things shaped for this specific terrain — root-axes for clearing deadfall, long-hooked poles for testing canopy-platforms before trusting weight to them, because platforms here go soft and treacherous at the edges where the wood hasn't finished hardening.

They trade in three economies simultaneously: the metal-economy of the foundry (ingots, tools, gear-stock), the resin-economy of the tap-lines (fuel, lubricant, the medicinal cuts that the older kin swear by and the younger ones are starting to doubt), and a third, less spoken-of economy of *information* — reports from the rope-bridge expeditions, news of which gallery's grown unstable, which trunk-column has started branching wrong. This third currency moves faster than the other two and is worth more, in the way that knowing where not to step is always worth more than the boot itself.

## Temperament

What defines them, more than trade or dress, is a specific patience — bred by the cavern itself, which punishes nothing so much as urgency. They've learned the cavern's acoustic geography, which gantry to shout across and which to walk; they've learned the compost floor's give and the canopy's groan-before-it-breaks; they've learned, above all, to live inside a structure that is not finished and may never be told that it's allowed to stop. There's a fatalism in them that doesn't read as despair so much as a kind of working acceptance — *the place is still becoming, so are we, get the foundry running anyway.*

They marry the practicality of an industrial people — gear-ratios, smelting temperatures, sap-yield calendars — to something closer to a folk reverence for the trunk-columns, never stated outright as religion but present in small customary gestures: a hand laid flat on bark before climbing a gear-lift, an offering of bruised fruit left in the root-hollows at certain unmarked intervals, a refusal, total and cheerful and never explained, to cut a trunk-column below a certain girth no matter how good the timber would be.

They do not, as a rule, go looking for the edges of the cavern. The expeditions are functionary, route-mapping, supply-logic — nobody goes out of curiosity. When pressed on why, the answer is never fear, exactly. It's closer to courtesy. You don't go poking at something that's still finding the shape of itself. You let it grow, the way it's let you live in the gaps of its growing, and you keep the furnace running, and you wait to see what it decides to become.
