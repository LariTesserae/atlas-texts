# The Tender

You will not see her arrive. You will only notice, afterward, that the moss-line is exact again, that a leaf you'd bent investigating has been coaxed back to its original angle, that a drip you'd been counting has resumed its rhythm at precisely the interval you remembered, as if it had only paused out of politeness while you were nearby.

She is small in the way that people become small when a place has been theirs long enough that they've stopped taking up more room than it needs them to. Gray coverall, once a uniform color, now the color of everything here — pipe-green at the knees, rust-orange at the cuffs, a permanent damp darkening the shoulders where she's leaned under weeping seams a thousand times to listen to something only she can diagnose. Her hands are the most legible thing about her: cracked at the knuckles from mineral water, stained at the nail-beds with a green that doesn't wash out, missing a fingertip on the left hand to some old mechanism she doesn't discuss. They move with the unthinking precision of someone who has trimmed the same six centimeters of moss so many times the motion has migrated out of decision and into reflex, like breathing, like the mist cycle she may or may not control.

No one assigned her this. Or someone did, once, decades or months ago depending on which worn patch of memory she trusts that day, and the assignment outlived its assigner the way the planting troughs have outlived whoever welded them. She arrived to relieve someone, or she arrived because the door was open and the warmth called to her the way it calls to the bird, and she simply never finished arriving. She thinks of herself less as a gardener than as a *setting* — one more dial the terrace hasn't locked yet, still finding her level the way the water still finds its channel, the vines their wire.

She knows the structure's moods by the tremor in the rail. A particular shiver means the compressors three levels down have started their slow cycle and the mist will follow in ninety seconds; she's usually already moving toward the rubbery dark-veined plant by the time it starts, not to protect it from anything, just to be present for it, the way you'd sit with someone you weren't sure was sleeping or only still. She has never told anyone that the leaves fold for her specifically, a half-second before the vent-warmth actually dips, as though they've learned her footstep as an early-warning system, or she's learned theirs. She doesn't examine this. There are many things on the terrace she has agreed not to examine, in exchange for the terrace agreeing not to examine her — how long she's been here, whether her knees still work the same as when she started, why the single bird seems to land nearer her than it does anyone else and yet she's never once fed it.

She talks to the moss sometimes, not in sentences, just a low continuous murmur pitched to match the pipe-hum, a sound meant less to communicate than to keep her own presence steady against the structure's, two low frequencies agreeing not to interfere with each other. She trims with a short curved blade kept on a cord at her hip, worn smooth from handling, and she trims by feel more than sight, running a thumb along the green stain until she finds where it wants to stop and helping it stop there, a millimeter at a time, every few days, forever.

She eats little, sleeps somewhere below that no door on the terrace admits to, surfaces at irregular hours that have nothing to do with daylight and everything to do with the clank from below — she's learned to read it the way you'd read weather, and certain clanks send her to certain corners with the unhurried readiness of someone responding to a call only she can hear. She does not seem to age the way the terrace does not seem to age. Whether that's because time moves strangely up here in the warm filtered light, or because she simply hasn't been here as long as it feels, or because some part of the system that grows the moss back exactly to its line has also quietly taken to maintaining her — she has stopped asking. It's one more pipe she's decided not to trace to its source.

If you stayed long enough, weeks, maybe a season, you would start to understand that she is not separate from the strangeness you cataloged — the responsive mist, the exact moss, the too-quick leaves — but its single visible expression, the one piece of the terrarium's quiet intention that happens to walk on two legs and carry a blade and hum along with the compressors. Ask her what she's tending and she'll look at you with the mild, unoffended confusion of someone asked to name the air she breathes, and say only: *it isn't finished yet* — meaning the garden, meaning the spire, meaning, though she'd never put it this way, herself.
