## What Lives There

You will not see it arrive. You will only notice, after some time in the deep root-folds, that the air-pocket you thought was empty has acquired a weight it didn't have before — a faint extra density to the dark, a presence that doesn't announce itself because announcing itself was never a survival strategy here.

Call it a hollow-dweller, though no one's stayed long enough to name it properly. It is pale in the specific way of things that gave up on light generations ago — not white, but the bruised, translucent grey of something seen through frosted glass, skin gone thin enough in patches that you could almost trace the slow dark movement of fluid beneath it if there were light enough to try. It is long — a meter, sometimes more in the older ones — and segmented in a loose, soft way, more like a string of damp vertebrae wrapped in cable-fungus than any animal with a clean taxonomic home. It moves on a fringe of short, root-pale limbs that aren't legs so much as graspers, suited to pulling itself hand-over-hand through tangle rather than walking on anything resembling open ground, because open ground is not where it lives. It lives in the seams. In the dead air between root and rust, where the engine-thrum drops to a hum you'd call almost peaceful if you didn't know better.

That hum is the whole of its world. It has no eyes worth the name — vestigial dark smudges beneath the skin, sensitive only to the crudest gradient of light and shadow — but it has, instead, a long dorsal ridge of fine cilial hairs, frost-stiffened at the tips, that read vibration the way your skin reads warmth. The compressor-thrum is not noise to it. It is structure. It is the difference between *safe* and *exposed*, between the felt presence of cover and the felt absence of it. The hollow-dweller has learned, over its short decades of existence as a kind of creature, to navigate by the architecture of sound the engines lay down — to know that a near-silence pocket between two thrum-zones means thin canopy, means risk, means cross it quickly; to know that a roar means cover, means safety, means you can stop here and not be found by anything that hunts by hearing, because nothing that hunts by hearing can hear anything in a roar.

It feeds on the fungal cable itself, mostly — the pale, slow-pulsing strands that thread the rootmass and seem, themselves, to carry some faint nutrient charge drawn off the condensation-damp and whatever leaches down from the rusting machinery above. It rasps at the cable with a soft, toothless mouth-disc, leaving the strand thinner but alive, the way you'd graze a thing rather than kill it, because in a system this young, this raw, killing your food source outright is not a mistake you get to make twice. In the lowest hollows, where the black mirror-pools sit unfrozen in their pockets of engine-warmed air, it will sometimes lower itself in entirely, submerged but for the cilial ridge breaking the surface like a single dark eyelash, drawing whatever drifts suspended in that unmoving water into the mouth-disc by a slow, patient current it generates with its own grasping limbs.

It does not flock. It barely tolerates its own kind, except in the deep cold of the dampest months, when two or three will knot themselves together in a fold of root for warmth, motionless for what might be weeks, indistinguishable in that state from the dead cable-fungus around them — and this, more than anything, is how it has survived being unseen: by being mistakable, by the rootmass itself, for more of itself. Its skin frosts the same way the lichen frosts. Its segments fold the same way exhausted vine-growth slumps over a frozen ridge. A survey team, if one ever came back down here, would walk past three of them in a hundred meters and see nothing but more hollow.

Young ones — smaller, more translucent, barely the length of a forearm — move faster and more carelessly, not yet having learned the sound-map, and it's this carelessness that the basin's only other notable predator exploits: a thing smaller still, blind and quick, more insect than anything, that lives in the threadwork between cable-fungus and rust and takes the unwary juveniles in the brief unguarded seconds when they cross a silence-pocket without checking it first. This is, as far as anyone's observed, the entire violence the hollow permits itself. Everything else here is grazing, holding still, waiting out the cold.

It will likely never leave the basin. There is nothing in it built for the world above — no tolerance for true light, no ear for silence that isn't engineered, no skin for wind that isn't already saturated with condensation. It belongs to the hollow exactly as much as the rootmass does, exactly as much as the rusting gantries do, an unfinished thing in an unfinished place, still becoming whatever it's going to be, keeping time — slow, patient, pale — with the engines nobody remembers switching on.
