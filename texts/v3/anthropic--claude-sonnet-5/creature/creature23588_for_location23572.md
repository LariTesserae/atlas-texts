It takes a while to see it clearly, because it doesn't want to be seen, and because the shadowless light flattens it against the stone until it could pass for a vein of mineral with ambitions of its own.

Call it pale. Paler than the walls, even, a bloodless white-grey the color of something that has never needed pigment because nothing here has eyes that hunt by color. Its body is a string of segments, eight or eleven or thirteen depending on how recently it's molted — the count isn't fixed, which tells you something about how it grows: not toward a final form, but toward whatever the chamber currently requires of it. Each segment carries a pair of legs, thin as wire, jointed in too many places, so that its walk looks less like walking and more like a rumor of walking, a suggestion passed segment to segment down the length of its body. It doesn't hurry. It has never had to. There is nothing in this room that wants to eat it, and nothing it wants to eat that runs.

The head, if it is a head, is barely differentiated from the segment behind it — a slight tapering, a slight darkening, two short filaments that aren't antennae so much as listening organs, built to take in vibration rather than light. It doesn't see the lattices pulse. It *feels* them, the way you'd feel a held note in your chest, and when it pauses near one of the black branching growths and lifts its filaments toward it, it isn't looking at anything. It's reading.

What does it eat? Not the lattice itself — it has never been seen to touch the black structure, not once, as though some old agreement keeps it at a respectful remove. It eats what the lattice leaves behind: the faint mineral residue left where condensation has been drawn down and metabolized, a kind of ash that isn't ash, a slag of trace elements too fine to see, that gathers in the hairline cracks like silt at a riverbed. It grazes. That's the right word, slow and incurious, the way a thing grazes when it has never once been hungry in the urgent sense, only hungry in the patient, scheduled sense, the way the room itself is patient.

It is, as far as the chamber knows, the only one of its kind in it — but "kind" might be the wrong frame. It may not be a species so much as a function: the lattice-organism's quiet way of cleaning its own floor, an outsourced janitorial impulse that grew legs because legs were the cheapest solution physics offered. If you cut it open — though nothing here would give you cause to — you might not find organs in the way you'd expect. You might find more lattice. Smaller, embryonic, dormant. A seed of the same black geometry, folded up and carried in a body that walks so the geometry doesn't have to.

It doesn't reproduce the way surface life does, in panic and abundance. There is, perhaps, one of it at a time, ever, in this room — and when this one dies (when its legs finally fail in their strange too-jointed way, when it folds down into the floor crack the way the water does), there will likely be a gap of stillness, a held breath within the held breath of the chamber, and then, weeks or years later, something will emerge from a different crack, slightly larger, slightly stranger, carrying the same listening filaments, continuing a lineage that isn't really a lineage so much as a single slow idea the lattice keeps having, over and over, about itself.

It has no name, because nothing here has ever needed to call to it. It has no fear, because fear requires alternatives, and it has only ever known one outcome for every hour of its life: wake, taste the pulse, graze the cracks, rest near the warmth, repeat. If you stood very still and watched it work its way across the bright wet stone, you might feel that you were watching not an animal so much as a single grazing thought belonging to the room itself — small, patient, untroubled, doing the one quiet job the deep, bright, buried world had ever asked of it, and asking nothing back.
