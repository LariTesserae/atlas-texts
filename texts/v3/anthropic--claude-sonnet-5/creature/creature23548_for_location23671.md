# The Tenants of the Spires

The easy answer is the small ones — the colonies. Pale, fat-bodied, clustered in the hundreds along every overhang dry enough to roost on, indistinguishable at a glance from the drape-moss they nest against because they have spent enough generations pressed into that moss that their hides have taken on its color, its texture, its faint sheen. They are the roar you hear before you see anything: a low, constant, multi-throated conversation, clicking and groaning and occasionally rising into something like song before subsiding again, no two voices ever quite synchronized, so that the sound never resolves into rhythm — it just continues, the way the dripping of the moss continues, the way the water continues to be exactly as deep as it is. They eat the insect-adjacent swarms that gather at the bioluminescent fern-fringes. They are eaten, rarely, by things from below that rise just far enough out of the black water to take one and vanish again, the door-closing sound, the long-traveling ripple. They have a thousand small lives going on at once in every roost, and none of it is the place's real secret. They are noise. The real inhabitant is quiet.

It takes a long time in these spires to understand that the apex of this place is not loud, and that this is itself the tell. Everything that calls and clicks and roosts is prey, or is small enough not to matter to whatever the towers actually belong to. What the towers belong to does not announce itself. You infer it from absence: the wide clean stretch of open water around the base of certain towers where even the heavy, half-swimming things give berth; the way the colonies fall quiet, all at once, for the length of a breath, at intervals that have no discoverable cause; the door-closing sound itself, which you eventually realize is not a hunting sound at all, but something closer to a punctuation mark — the only mark this place still makes on its own stillness.

Call it, for lack of any better word, a **Custodian**, though it tends nothing anymore and was never asked to.

One per tower. Never two. This seems to be the only law it obeys as strictly as the place obeys its own — vegetation leaves room around its monuments, and so, it turns out, does whatever this is. Enormous, certainly — the proportions only make sense at enormous scale, given how much water it must displace to make a closing-door sound audible from a kilometer off across dead-still black. Long-bodied, low in the water even when surfaced, more spine than shape, more weight than silhouette. Skin, where it can be glimpsed at all in the secondhand blue-green fringe-light, the same drowned-stone grey as the towers' submerged base, mottled with a paler underlay that might be scarring, might be growth, might be something closer to inscription — old enough, layered enough, that the line between the creature's hide and the moss-and-lichen crust that has taken hold of it has gone the way of every other boundary here: dissolved by time into something load-bearing instead.

It is blind, near enough as makes no difference. Eyes if any are vestigial, sealed, sunk under skin that healed over them centuries or millennia ago when there stopped being any light worth the maintenance cost of seeing it. What it has instead, in abundance, is the patience of something that locates the world by displacement and pressure and the very slow accumulation of disturbances in still water — and the water here is so still, has been still for so long, that the smallest events register at enormous range. A surfacing colony-creature a kilometer off. A visiting consciousness's boat, or breath, or heartbeat carried oddly through water that treats every vibration as news. It is less a hunter than a listener with teeth, and the teeth, when needed, are almost an afterthought — most of what it eats, it eats by simply being where slower things eventually, inevitably, drift.

It does not climb. It does not breach. The door-closing sound is the absolute limit of its relationship to the surface — a single, brief, vertical motion, jaw or skull or some armored dorsal feature breaking the black for the half-second necessary to take whatever has come too near the tower it has claimed, and then submergence again, total, immediate, the ripples spreading outward to carry the news to roosts a kilometer distant who fall silent at the sound the way smaller things everywhere fall silent at the sound of a door closing somewhere above them in a house they no longer think of as inhabited. It surfaces, by any outside accounting, rarely. By its own accounting it may surface constantly; time means something different to a thing that has occupied a single tower's base for longer than the moss above it has taken to grow load-bearing.

It does not appear to age in any externally visible way, which is consistent with everything else here — equilibrium reached, then simply persisted in. Whether it dies and is replaced, whether there is a single Custodian per tower across all the millennia of the flood or a long unbroken lineage so slow-turning that no visitor has ever caught the transition, is not knowable and may not be a meaningful question to ask of a place that has stopped distinguishing between *persisting* and *living*. There is no evidence of young. There is no evidence, either, of solitude troubling it; solitude appears to be the precise condition for which it is built, the way the fern-crowns are built for exactly the dry purchase a fern-crown gets and not an inch more.

What it is *for* — whether it once guarded something the towers held, back when the towers held anything, when hands carved the stairs it now circles the drowned foundations of forever — is the kind of question the place declines to answer, the same way it declines to explain why the towers stand at exactly the intervals they stand at, or why the water is exactly as high as it is and no higher. Perhaps it was built, the way the towers were built, by whatever carved this place with hands' intentions. Perhaps it was already here when the flood arrived, and the flood was no disaster to it at all, only a long-awaited completion — the world finally rearranging itself into the one shape a thing like this could be perfectly suited to. Perhaps "guard" was never accurate, and it has simply discovered, the way water discovers its own level, the one tower-base, the one stretch of black, that fits the exact shape and patience of its body, and has been occupying that fit the way the moss occupies its ledges: totally, finally, with nothing left to do but continue.

You will likely never see one. You will hear, if you stay long enough and the water stays as still as it has always stayed, a single sound like a door closing very far off — and then the colonies above you falling quiet for the length of one held breath — and then, after a while, because nothing here is in a hurry to do anything else, they will start their conversation up again, exactly where they left it, as though the door had never closed at all.
