They are called, when anyone bothers with collective names, the Roostlings, though that word does the same work the place's own name does — arrives worn, fits loosely, explains nothing. There is no single creature here. There is a guild of them, ranged across the size of the architecture itself, as if the builders had specified, somewhere in plans now lost to weather, a tenant for every scale of gap.

The largest are the ones you notice first because notice is unavoidable — sail-winged, slate-grey shading to a deep oxblood at the trailing edge, with necks long enough to fold three times against the chest when at rest and heads that are mostly socket, mostly eye, built for a light that is always almost gone. They do not so much perch as occupy, settling into the worn saddle of a wall the way the wall was worn to receive them, and it is genuinely unclear, looking at the long historical scarring of certain ledges, whether the stone shaped itself to the bird over centuries or the birds simply found, generation on generation, the groove that fit. They are not predators in any urgent sense. They eat the smaller flying things — the moth-sized ones, the hand-sized ones — but rarely, and without violence that disturbs the noise-weather of the place; a beak closes, a small voice stops, and the great general clamor doesn't even dip. They breed without ceremony, laying single eggs into shallow stone cups so old the cups have a polish like horn, and the chicks, when they come, are already the color of the rock around them, already half-camouflaged into the architecture by the accident of pigment alone.

Below them, filling every middle register — the arches, the window-throats, the undersides of the great spanning vaults — are the smaller flocks, dun and rust and a kind of bruised violet, sized anywhere from sparrow to lapdog, and these are the ones responsible for most of the noise. They are social in the loose, total way of starlings, moving as a single weather system rather than as individuals, lifting off a whole terrace at once for no reason anyone watching could name and resettling four ledges over in the same unison. Their calls are the calls you hear from the moment you arrive — clattering, layered, looping back on their own echoes until the sound seems to come from the stone rather than from any throat — and they are, more than the great sail-winged ones, the actual voice of the place, its breath, the thing it does instead of wind.

Smaller still, near-moth, near-nothing, there are clouds of them in the deep window-throats where no real light reaches, pale and soft-bodied, more felt as a brushing of air against the face than seen, and these seem to exist mostly to be eaten, mostly to be the bottom rung of a ladder that has no bottom rung, breeding in numbers that make individual death irrelevant to the species, the way mist is irrelevant to weather.

On the ledges and in the standing pools, a separate order entirely — the many-legged, low-slung things, segmented, the color of wet stone, moving with a patient, almost unhurried economy that matches the architecture's own pace better than anything that flies. They are not insects, not quite, though there's an insect's indifference in the way they move, all those legs finding purchase without apparent thought. They drink at the basins, leave their damp trails, retreat into cracks too narrow for anything to follow, and they are eaten too, by the birds when birds bother, by each other more often, and they seem entirely unbothered by any of it — old in the way the stone is old, persistence rather than ambition.

And then, more rarely, glimpsed rather than known: shapes too large for the upper tiers and too patient for the lower ones, settled into the deepest window-throats where the structure goes back further than sight, where even the moth-clouds thin out. Solitary. Long-necked, long-folded, the color of the dark itself, motionless on a scale that makes you doubt, looking, whether you've seen a living thing or only a particularly deliberate piece of unweathered carving. No one claims to know how many of these there are. No one claims to know if they move at all, or only seem to, the way the moss seems to move if you wait long enough at one wall. They may be the oldest tenants the Roostings has — older than the smaller flocks, certainly older than any human memory of the name — sitting at the bottom of the whole airborne hierarchy not because they are weak but because depth, here, is its own form of seniority.

What unites all of them — sail-wing and flock-bird, moth-cloud and many-leg, the rumored old shapes in the throat of the rock — is the same thing that unites the architecture to the erosion: none of them are in any hurry, and none of them are separate, finally, from the stone that holds them. They did not move into the Roostings the way a tenant moves into a building. They settled the way water settles, the way moss settles, the way the dusk-light settles without ever quite becoming night — filling every available shape until shape and life are doing the same work, until you stop being able to say, with any real confidence, where the built thing ends and the living thing begins, and start understanding, the way you eventually understand the noise as weather, that here, that was never really two things to begin with.
