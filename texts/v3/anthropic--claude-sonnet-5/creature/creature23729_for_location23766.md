The People of the Plateau

They are built the way the hamlet is built: compact, load-bearing, nothing wasted on ornament. Generations of thin air have given them a particular economy of breath—movements measured, unhurried, paced to a register where exertion is never wasted on anything that doesn't need doing now. Strangers who climb the long ascent arrive winded and find the inhabitants watching with a kind of mild, unhurried patience, as if breathlessness itself were a foreign custom.

Their bodies carry the marks of altitude plainly. Skin weathered to the color of the stone itself, a brownish-grey that takes the sun without burning and holds the cold without complaint. Hands are wide, knuckle-thick, permanently faintly grey-stained from handling rock and lime-ash—hands shaped more by quarrying and dry-stacking than by anything softer. Eyes tend toward pale, used to clear light and long sightlines, accustomed to judging distance across the flat exactness of the plateau's air; they squint less than you'd expect, having learned over generations to hold a gaze level rather than shielded. Most are of middling height or under—there is little advantage in being tall where doorways are low and wind takes whatever stands too high above the walls.

Clothing is layered, practical, the color of undyed wool and felted goat-hair, the grey-brown of the rock with maybe a single woven band of color at a cuff or collar—dyed, perhaps, from some lichen or hoarded plant matter, worn sparingly, as a small assertion that they are people and not simply stone that learned to walk. Cloaks are heavy and close, hooded against the wind that never fully stops. Footwear is thick-soled, leather over wood or felt, soled for a surface that punishes anything thin.

Their days run on the trickle. Households keep unspoken rotations for the troughs, an order so old it no longer needs negotiating—children learn it the way they learn their own names, simply by growing up inside it. Mornings are for the terraces, when the fog still pools in the lower lane and the soil holds what cold-night moisture it gathered; middays are for stonework, repair, the patient unglamorous maintenance that keeps walls from becoming ruins; evenings are short and close to the hearth, since the cold descends fast once the sun drops past the plateau's edge.

They speak the way the wind allows speech here—low, economical, sentences that don't reach for more words than the thought requires. Silence between people isn't awkward; it's simply where most of any conversation lives, with words inserted only at the places where silence isn't sufficient. Visitors sometimes mistake this for coldness. It isn't. It's the same economy that governs the water, the soil, the stacked stone—nothing spent that isn't needed, which is not the same as nothing given.

Lineage matters more than name. Families are known by which terrace they tend, which stretch of wall their grandfather's grandfather mortared, which stool by which trough is unspokenly theirs at the hour they've always come. The dead are not separated far from the living—small cairns and flat marker-stones occupy a modest field near the plateau's edge, weathered indistinguishable in color from the lichen-stained walls of the houses, visited without ceremony but without neglect either, as one more fixed point in a landscape that does not move.

Children are raised early into the rhythm of scarcity—not as deprivation, but as the basic shape of how things are. They learn to carry water without spilling, to recognize the particular cold that means rain is still an hour off, to read where the cushion-moss grows thickest as a sign of which stones hold dampness longest. They are not coddled, but they are not hardened either; there's an unforced gentleness that comes from living somewhere that punishes carelessness without ever needing to punish cruelty.

Of belief, little is declared outright, but a few things are evident in practice: a deep, unspoken respect for the cistern and the channel, tended like something between infrastructure and shrine; small offerings—a sprig from the terrace, a flat stone set just so—left at certain corners of certain walls, for reasons no one explains to outsiders and perhaps no longer fully explains to each other. There's a quiet, settled conviction, never argued because never questioned, that the plateau permits them to stay rather than belonging to them, and that the proper response to this permission is exactly the kind of careful, unhurried, repeating life they've always lived.

Strangers are met without hostility and without much curiosity either—offered water if they ask, watched without unkindness, sent on their way down the long descent with the same unhurried courtesy that governs everything else. The hamlet does not need visitors and has stopped expecting them to need it. It has the people it has, the work it has, the water it has. Like the stone they build with, they have weathered into a shape that holds—not unchanging exactly, but changing so slowly, so evenly, across so many lifetimes, that change and stillness have become difficult to tell apart.
