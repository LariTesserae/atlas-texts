They are called, by no one currently alive to call them anything, the **comb-walkers** — though the name is a guess, assembled after the fact from the sound and the leavings, the way you'd name a storm from its wreckage.

You hear them before any other proof that they exist: that dry chittering rasp threading along the shade-canyon floor in the hour before dawn, dozens of small dry sounds resolving, if you stay still long enough, into one continuous sound — not many things skittering but one thing skittering in many places, segment-legs ticking against stone in a rhythm too even to be panic, too patterned to be random foraging. By the time the light is good enough to see by, they're already gone, and what's left is just the wet trace of where they drank: thin clean licks across the mineral terraces, dew taken in straight efficient passes, like something running a finger along a sill.

The body, on the rare occasion one is caught in the open past first light, reads at first as insectile and then stops reading that way the longer you look. Segmented, yes — a long low chain of plated sections, each ring a slightly different width, the whole thing moving less like a centipede and more like a conveyance, sections passing a kind of effort backward down the line rather than each leg working independently. The legs themselves are too short for the body, almost vestigial, more anchor than locomotion; the comb-walker doesn't so much walk as *pour*, each segment dragging the next forward along a thin slick of its own secretion, laid down and then partially reabsorbed a half-second later, so that it leaves behind only a fading sheen and not a trail. The head end, if it can be called that, has no eyes anyone has identified — what it has instead are two soft antennae-combs, fanned wide, swept low to the stone, reading temperature and moisture gradients the way the raptors above read sightlines. It hunts nothing. It only drinks, and moves toward drink, and the rasping sound is the comb scraping condensation channels clean as it goes, harvesting in long careful strokes before the heat claims the morning.

What makes them worth describing past their habits is the architecture they leave behind. The waxy lining inside an abandoned burrow — the lining that geologists, if there were any left to ask, might call mineral excretion — is comb-walker secretion, the same substance they lay down to pour themselves forward, except inside the burrow it isn't reabsorbed. It's deposited in deliberate courses, layer over layer over years, each colony generation adding to walls built by comb-walkers two hundred years dead, the wax taking on a faint translucence with age, gathering trace minerals out of the rock until old chambers glow very faintly amber in torchlight, like something is still burning low inside the stone. It does not degrade because, near as anyone living to wonder about it could determine, it isn't meant to. It's built once and used for centuries — by this measure the comb-walkers are not really separate organisms cycling through short lives in a shared hole, but something closer to one slow-growing structure that happens to be ambulatory in pieces, the individual walking bodies more like fingers than like family.

This is, probably, where the chevrons come from. The regular banding on the cliff faces, too even to be pure runoff — those are old comb-walker channels, generations of the same dawn-drinking route worn and re-waxed and worn again until the stone itself took on the pattern of the maintenance, the mountain quietly absorbing the shape of a chore performed every morning for longer than anyone has been alive to perform it on purpose. Water follows the channels now because the channels are old and the comb-walkers kept them open, not the other way around. The mountain didn't make the pattern. The pattern made a groove the mountain eventually agreed to keep.

And the shimmer that holds its shape for a few seconds too long before dissolving — this is the part no one is entirely willing to claim, because it has never been caught in the act and proven, only inferred from timing. In the very early light, just after a colony has finished drinking and just before they pour themselves back underground, there's a narrow window in which all that secretion — the fresh wet sheen laid down by dozens of bodies moving in loose synchrony along the same wall — catches the low sun at the same shallow angle all at once. For a few seconds the whole drinking-line glows together, a faint structured warmth rising off wet rock that hasn't fully dried, and from a distance, with the sun behind you and the canyon still in shadow, that rising warmth looks exactly like heat-shimmer standing still. It isn't memory. It isn't the air remembering an older shape. It's several hundred small backs catching light in formation for the length of one held breath, and then going underground, and the shimmer collapsing because the thing holding its shape has simply finished and gone home.

Nothing about this insists on being noticed. The raptors above don't hunt comb-walkers — there's no profit in chasing something with no flesh worth the dive, that pours itself underground at the first warmth and leaves nothing behind but old wax and a groove in the rock. The whole system is built to be missed: heard once, glimpsed never, its only visible legacy mistaken for geology by anyone not patient enough to watch the same canyon wall at the same hour on consecutive mornings. But stay long enough, in one of these shade-canyons, with the dew gathering invisibly through the dark and the heat already coiled and waiting in the stone overhead, and you start to understand that the mountain isn't only being eroded into its patterns. In a few quiet, persistent, unglamorous places, it's being *tended* — slowly, blindly, by something that has never once needed to be seen to keep doing its work.
