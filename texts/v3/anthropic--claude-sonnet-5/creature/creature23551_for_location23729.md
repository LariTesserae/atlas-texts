They are quiet before they are anything else. Not silent—they speak, they call across terraces, they sing sometimes at dusk when the fog has burned off and the light goes long and grey—but quiet in the way of people who have never needed to compete with birdsong or insect-hum to be heard. Their voices have learned the plateau's particular acoustics: pitched low, unhurried, trusting that sound will carry itself across open rock without help. Strangers here talk too loudly at first, and then, within a day or two, stop.

Their hands are the first thing you'd notice up close—broad, calloused at specific points from specific tools, the kind of hands shaped by repetitive, patient labor rather than violent or varied work. Stone-fitters' hands, water-channel cutters' hands. Nails kept short out of necessity, not vanity; damp rock ruins long nails fast. Skin slightly weathered but not sun-cracked—there's no harsh sun here to crack it, only the constant low moisture that keeps their skin somehow both dry-feeling and faintly chapped, a permanent light roughness at the knuckles. Many wear a tightly-woven outer layer, oiled or waxed, over looser inner clothes—not for warmth, since the warmth is constant, but because that omnipresent wet will find its way through anything not built to shed it. Hooded, mostly. Hoods up even indoors sometimes, half out of habit.

They move carefully, but not slowly—a particular gait suited to wet fitted stone, weight kept low and centered, feet placed rather than swung. You can tell who has lived here their whole life by the way they cross a terrace edge without looking down at it. Children learn this early; falls here are not dramatic, mostly, but a wrenched ankle on wet basalt is a real and ordinary risk, so balance is taught before almost anything else, taught into the body before it's taught into words.

There is an unbothered quality to them that matches the land. Not passive—they work constantly, terracing, channel-clearing, paddy-tending, roof-repitching after the rare structural failure—but without urgency, because urgency has never been rewarded here. The water does not flood suddenly. The fog does not storm. Nothing in Anhuwen punishes hesitation, so haste was never selected for. Decisions get made at the pace of dripping water: eventually, definitely, without anxiety.

Family and work units cluster the way the buildings do—four or five households knotted together, separated by long walks of open terrace from the next knot of households, so that a person's daily world is made of frequent solitary walking interspersed with small, dense pockets of company. This shapes something in their temperament: comfortable alone, comfortable in small intense groups, slightly unpracticed at anything in between. Large gatherings—the central square fills perhaps a few times a year, for specific communal labor or specific communal rites—have a hushed, almost reverent quality, less like festival noise and more like many quiet conversations laid side by side.

They keep careful relationships with the water, the way river-people elsewhere keep relationships with rivers, except here the water is everywhere and nowhere, diffuse, ambient, requiring not navigation but channeling. Children are taught to read the seep-patterns on rock faces the way other children are taught to read weather. A certain dark mineral stain on basalt means a crack worth widening into a channel. A certain bead-and-trail pattern means leave it, it'll seal itself with moss in a season. This is inherited knowledge, passed hand to hand, eye to eye, more demonstrated than spoken.

Their food comes from the shallow paddies and the soil-pocket gardens—soft grains, broad-leafed greens, root vegetables coaxed out of thin caught soil with patient mineral feeding rather than abundance. Meals are simple, repeated, eaten in those small household knots, often in the covered walkways with the rain-sound or drip-sound for company, because there is no other ambient sound to compete with conversation, and so conversation here has a different texture—unhurried, threaded with long pauses that aren't awkward, just spacious, the way the terraces themselves are spacious.

There's no fear in them, particularly, of the place—no sense of the plateau as something to be endured. If anything there is a kind of unspoken gratitude worn so deep it no longer registers as gratitude, simply as how things are: the stone holds, the water comes, the channels carry it away, the roofs shed the damp, the fog comes and goes and never lingers long enough to rot anything through. Faces here, when at rest, default not to a smile and not to a frown but to something stiller than either—an expression you might mistake for blankness until you realize it's closer to the resting state of the rock itself: settled, undramatic, at peace with its own conditions, waiting without anxiety for whatever comes next, confident that whatever comes next will be, more or less, what came before.
