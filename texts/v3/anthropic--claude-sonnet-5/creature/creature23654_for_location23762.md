**The Silverfish**

It has no name, of course — no one has ever stayed long enough in the Sump Room to need one for it, and it would not answer regardless. But it is there, has been there, will be there: a long pale body, segmented, faintly luminous in the way that things which have never seen true light sometimes are, not glowing so much as *failing to be dark*, the way old paper is not quite white. Three long bristled tails trail behind it like an afterthought the body forgot to finish. Two antennae, longer than its body, sweep ahead of it constantly, reading the air the way a hand reads a wall in the black.

It does not see in any way a person would recognize as seeing. Its ancestors, dozens or hundreds of generations back, arrived as eggs in damp timber, or spores of a different kind of life riding the same ducts that brought the moss — nobody knows, least of all the silverfish — and the eyes that line still bears are vestigial now, dark flecks doing nothing in a room where light has never varied and so has never needed reading. What it has instead is taste and touch distributed across its whole hide: the temperature of a pipe, the mineral skin of the algae, the precise tackiness of a drop of condensation about to fall. It lives by surfaces. It has never known anything else.

It is small — a thumb's length, perhaps, antennae included — and it is, in the strict accounting of the room, almost nothing: a flicker of motion across a flange, there and then gone into a rivet-seam, gone for what might be an hour or a week before some sibling or cousin or perhaps the same individual, indistinguishable from the last, crosses the same pipe going the opposite way. It eats the room. Not metaphorically — it grazes the algae-skin in rasping passes too small to see as damage, browses the spore-felt at the base of the fern colonies, scavenges the soft rot where a dead moss-mat has gone to slime in some forgotten corner. It drinks by simply existing in air that is mostly water already. It has likely never been thirsty in its life. It has likely never been anything except exactly as wet as it is now.

There are more of them than the room shows. Inside the wall cavities — between the riveted outer plate and whatever older brick or older iron lies behind it, in spaces a human hand could not fit and a human eye has never entered — there is a population, not a colony exactly, since they do not cooperate or organize, simply a quantity of individuals sharing a habit of place the way the moss shares a habit of growing wherever silt collects. They breed without ceremony in the bilge-warmth behind the plates. They die without ceremony too, and what's left of them goes into the same slow recycling the room performs on everything: a husk in a seam, eventually indistinguishable from the rust-colored grit that gathers everywhere down here, eventually just more of the room's accumulated residue, the way the architects went past memory and the moss has no one to thank for arriving.

It has no enemies. Nothing hunts here; nothing could survive the trip down to hunt it and still have appetite for something this small and this hidden. Its only real danger is mechanical — a sudden surge of water through a channel it has misjudged, a drip falling at the one moment it has chosen to cross beneath the joint instead of around it — and even this danger is, by the room's own logic, not really a danger at all, because the drip-rate is constant, the channel's surge-pattern has not changed in longer than its kind has existed, and an animal that has evolved here for however many silent generations has had ample time to learn, in whatever wordless way a nervous system this small can learn, exactly when the room is safe to cross and when it is not.

It does not hear the pumps. Or rather: it has never *not* heard them, which amounts, for a creature with no other sound to compare them to, to not hearing them at all. The roar that deafens a visitor and arrives a corridor ahead of the light is, to the silverfish, simply the shape silence takes here. It was born into that roar the way a human is born into gravity, and would likely panic — if panic is a thing this animal is capable of — were the pumps ever to actually stop, the way a held breath becomes unbearable not while held but at the moment of unexpected release.

It is, in the fullest sense available to this room, the only thing that *lives* here, as opposed to merely growing, dripping, or rusting. It is small enough that the room's vast indifferent permanence has simply made room for it without noticing, the way a cathedral makes room for the dust motes turning in its one shaft of window-light. It will not be here in a thousand years, probably — its kind is not so patient with continuity as iron is — but its kind, or some descendant adequate enough to wear the name, almost certainly will be: still crossing the same pipes, still reading the air with antennae longer than its body, still finding, in the identical wet dark, exactly enough world to live in.
