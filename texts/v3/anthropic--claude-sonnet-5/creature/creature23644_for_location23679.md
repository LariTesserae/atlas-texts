At first you assume there are many of them, because the eye keeps cataloguing differences — pulsing globes near the surface, segmented arcs further out, clustered masses holding the rim like barnacles against a current that has no business existing. You start sorting them the way you'd sort a tide pool. Species. Genus. Habit.

It takes longer than it should to notice that none of them ever pass each other going the same direction at the same time, and longer still to notice why: they are not different animals. They are one animal, caught at four different moments, distributed across the basin so that all four moments are always, simultaneously, on display.

Near the rim, in the shallows where the water is warmest, are the intake bodies — soft-walled, almost gelatinous, no real shape to them yet, just translucent sacks the size of a forearm that open and close along one seam in time with the hiss that breaks the surface elsewhere. They are doing nothing you'd call living. They are doing something closer to filling. You can see straight through them to a faint internal churn, granular, like sediment deciding what to become.

Further out, where the slosh deepens, the compression-stage bodies hold position against that impossible current, packed close, their soft walls gone rigid, ridged now, plated, the translucence replaced by a dull basalt sheen that matches the rock they were never anchored to. They don't move. They are braced. Something in them is being squeezed smaller and denser, and you understand, without wanting to, that this is the most painful-looking part of the cycle and also the part the others seem to envy least and fear most — they hold still near these like apprentices near a forge, not touching.

Then the long segmented forms — the ones that surface in arcs and vanish without splash — these are the power stroke given a body. They move with a violence the water doesn't register; they leave rings sized for something twice their length because whatever made the ring already isn't there anymore, already mid-arc somewhere else, propulsion without apparent propulsor, going hard in one direction because for one brief phase of their existence, going hard in one direction is the entire purpose they were extruded to fulfill.

And in the cold dark below, lit by no light that should reach them, the pale shapes — spent, slowing, plates flaking off into a kind of fine grey rain that never seems to reach a floor — are the exhaust. They glow faintly because something in their decay still wants to be useful, still off-gasses warmth and a little radiance as it comes apart. They are not corpses, quite. They are still doing something, the way embers still do something. Eventually — months, the basin's idea of soon — what's left of them rises as nothing at all: dissolved into the held-temperature water, indistinguishable from cistern, waiting their turn to be drawn back into a sack near the rim and called an intake stage again.

None of them eat. None of them need to. They are not organisms maintaining themselves against entropy — they are stages of conversion, on a four-beat schedule a half-step out of phase with the engine's own four-stroke thrum, chasing a rhythm they can never quite land on because they are not the clock, they're what the clock sheds.

If you stay at the rim long enough, raising your voice over the machinery to whoever you came with, you'll start to track one individual sack through to arc to plate to glow to dissolution, a tenure of some months, and you'll feel the absurd parental tug of having watched something be born and unmade in a place that has no births and no deaths in the ordinary sense, only positions on a wheel.

That's the inhabitant, in the end — not a creature so much as a process wearing four bodies at once, looping through a crater that was never meant to hold water, manufactured continuously by machinery with no casing, indifferent to the fact that anything is watching, here since the rock was still warm enough to remember being lava, here long after you've stopped being able to tell, over the noise, whether the thing you're looking at is alive or simply still running.
