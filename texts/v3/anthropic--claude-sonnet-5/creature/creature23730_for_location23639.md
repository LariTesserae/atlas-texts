## What Lives There

You would be forgiven for thinking nothing does. That's the first and most durable impression, and it isn't wrong, exactly — it's just incomplete, the way "empty room" is incomplete if there's someone standing very still in the corner, waiting to see if you're the kind of person who looks twice.

There is one kind of inhabitant here, and it does not announce itself, because announcing is a kind of urgency, and urgency is not a resource this place has ever had reason to develop. Call it, for lack of a truer word, the **patient** — not as a name exactly, more as a description that became a name through long use, the way the structures became ruins through long erosion.

It is small. Smaller than you'd expect anything to be that has agreed to live this long. It resembles, if you catch it in profile against the grey coin of a pool, something between a stone and a held thought — a low, segmented thing, the colour of the clay it rests against, with a shell-like dorsal ridge that has the same cracked-mosaic texture as the ground itself, as though it grew its armor by watching the basin wet and dry a few thousand times and deciding to imitate the pattern rather than resist it. It has no hurry in its anatomy. Its legs, when it has reason to use them, move with the unbothered deliberation of mineral deposition — less locomotion than slow precipitation of the animal from one position into the next.

It feeds, if that's the word, on the mineral film that gathers at the edges of the pools where the water used to be larger — the pale crust marking old waterlines. It draws this film into itself the way the clay draws moisture: not eating so much as equilibrating, taking in just enough to remain itself, no more. It has never been seen at the center of a pool. It does not swim. It does not need to. Whatever it wants from the water arrives at the edges, eventually, the way everything here eventually arrives at its edges rather than its centers.

It does not breed in any season, because there are no seasons here worth the name, only the single sustained hour repeating. Instead, perhaps once in a span of time too long for anyone passing through to witness even the start of, it divides — not violently, not even noticeably, but the way a crack divides clay: a slow opening along an old line of weakness, until what was one patient is unambiguously two, each indistinguishable from what the first one had been the week before. Population, here, is not a count so much as a slow, recursive persistence of a single original patience, fractioning itself across the basin at a rate that makes "lifespan" the wrong unit to apply.

You will likely not see one. They are the color of the thing they sit beside, motionless for stretches that outlast a visitor's attention by orders of magnitude, and they have, over a very long time, perfected the art of being mistaken for a stone, a clod, a shadow that simply forgot to move when the light didn't. The few who have noticed them at all describe the same thing afterward: not surprise at finding life here, but a faint, settling recognition — *oh*, *of course*, *something like this would be the thing that stayed.*

Because that's the qualifying trait, the only one that matters: not speed, not cunning, not any of the traits that make a creature interesting to a hurried eye. Just the capacity to want nothing more than the basin already offers, at exactly the rate the basin offers it. The patient did not adapt to this place so much as it stopped being distinguishable from an adaptation at all — it is what's left over after a living thing subtracts every urge that the flats can't satisfy, until what remains fits the held silence perfectly, the way the pools fit their shallow beds, the way the ruined walls fit their final modest height.

It is not waiting for anything. That would imply an outcome it expects. It is simply continuing, in the same tense the rain falls in here, the same tense the light is lit in — present, ongoing, unresolved, and in no rush at all to resolve.
