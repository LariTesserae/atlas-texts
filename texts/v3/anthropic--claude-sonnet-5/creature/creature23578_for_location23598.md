# What Lives Here

You expect, climbing into this much elevation and this much cold, to find life thinning out — fewer species, simpler shapes, things hunkered and sparse the way they are above any normal treeline. The Anvil Highlands refuse you that. What you find instead, filling every terrace and crowding every engine-warmed hollow, is the *skarn-back*.

## The Skarn-Back

It is built like the rock it grazes: broad, plated, faceted rather than rounded. A skarn-back stands chest-high to a person at the shoulder, low-slung, immensely wide through the ribs, its weight distributed over four splayed feet that look less like hooves than like sets of stacked, horn-colored discs — designed not for speed but for purchase, for not slipping on wet basalt with a fifteen-degree pitch underfoot. The coat is the thing you notice first and longest: not fur so much as a layered shingle of stiff, oiled guard-hair over a dense undercoat, gray-green or rust-orange depending on which lichen colony the animal has been rubbing against, because skarn-backs anoint themselves deliberately, grinding their flanks into the acid-bright lichen sheets until they wear the plateau's own color. A herd at a distance is genuinely difficult to distinguish from the stone it stands on, which is, you come to suspect, the entire point.

Their heads are small for their bulk, blunt-muzzled, set low and forward — built for cropping cushion plants out of cracks rather than for grazing open ground, because open ground barely exists here. Behind small dark eyes, the skull flares into a heavy bony shield, not horned so much as *helmed*, a single fused plate that two animals will lower and grind together for hours during the autumn contests, producing a sound like millstones that carries for kilometers and adds its low percussive note to the wall of noise that never stops on the highland.

They do not graze in silence. Nothing here grazes in silence. Each skarn-back maintains a continuous, almost subvocal rumble — felt more than heard, a chest-resonance that you register first as a vibration in your own sternum if you stand close to a herd — and herd members modulate this constant hum to signal position, alarm, calving, the location of a fresh seep. It is a sound built to be heard through and underneath every other sound on the plateau: the shrieking flocks above, the insect fizz, the bellow-contests, the engines' hum. Evolution here did not produce silence as an option. It produced layering.

## Engine-Broody

Skarn-backs calve at the engines. Not near them — *at* them, pressed up against the warm alloy housings in numbers that from a distance look like a single shaggy drift piled against the base of each mechanism, mothers giving birth into the heated vapor that vents in slow continuous plumes from seams that have never been serviced and never seem to need it. A newborn skarn-back cannot regulate its own heat for the first several days of life; the engines do that work that the mother's body and the brutal exposed air cannot. Calves are born already faintly stained with the mineral residue that crusts engine housings, a pale chalky bloom that washes out of the coat within a season, so that you can age any calf at a glance by how much of that ghost-white dusting still clings to it.

No skarn-back has ever been seen to approach an engine warily, the way you'd expect an animal to approach an unfamiliar machine. They treat the engines exactly as the text of this place treats them — as geology, as a feature with the same standing as an outcrop or a seep, present since before any skarn-back alive was born and certain to remain after it dies. Whether the species shaped its calving instinct around the machines or whether the machines have, in some unprovable and patient way, been *selecting* for animals that would gather and warm and fertilize the ground around them with dung and bone over ten thousand generations — no one on the plateau is positioned to say. The lichen that grows nearest the engine housings, the unnaturally electric strains, is always thickest where skarn-back herds have calved for the longest unbroken span. Make of that what you will.

## The Tenants of the Engines Themselves

Smaller things have made closer arrangements. In the louvers and vent-grilles of every engine nest a creature the herders — if there were herders, if anyone here owned these animals rather than simply living alongside them — would call a *vent-finch*, though it is closer to a thing with fur than a thing with feathers: a fist-sized, big-eyed climber with a flattened ribcage built to wedge into a two-centimeter gap and a coat of fine quills that lie flat against alloy and trap the engine's leaking warmth against the skin. They breed in clutches inside the housings, fed on the insects that the engines' heat draws up out of the cold air in columns, and they have apparently never known a winter that wasn't survivable, because they have never known anything but engine-heat as the baseline condition of being alive.

Beneath the chassis, in the gap between machine and stone, something larger dens — seen rarely, mostly at the edge of vision, low and quick and toothed, living on what falls: dead vent-finches, skarn-back afterbirth, anything careless enough to die within reach of the warm dark space under an engine's belly. It is the one predator anyone has reliably described on these terraces, and it has organized its entire existence around a machine of unknown origin and purpose, denning in the lee of something it could not have built and does not appear to wonder about.

## The Shape of the Whole

Stand on any terrace at midday — flooded in that flat omnidirectional light, the cold pressing down like a hand, the bellow-hum-shriek-fizz of ten thousand things calling at once filling the air the way water fills a basin — and you understand the plateau's life as a single fact stated at every scale simultaneously: nothing here is alone, and nothing here is unattended. The skarn-backs warm their calves at the engines. The vent-finches warm themselves at the skarn-backs' breath. The denning things eat what both leave behind. The lichen eats what all three leave behind, in colors too bright to be entirely natural, and somewhere underneath all of it, on its own appointment, a flywheel completes another slow rotation, driving nothing anyone can name, asking nothing, having perhaps never once been asked to.
