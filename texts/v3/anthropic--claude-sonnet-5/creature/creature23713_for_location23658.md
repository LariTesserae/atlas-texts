They are easy to miss for the first dozen visits, and that is by design rather than accident — not camouflage in the simple sense of color matching bark, though there is some of that, but a deeper, behavioral camouflage: a refusal to move in any way that draws the eye. You will see the hollow's other residents first — the lizard-comma, the chittering insect-traffic, the low birds with their clipped two-note calls — and only slowly come to suspect that something else watches all of this from a slightly greater height and a slightly older patience.

Call them, for lack of any name they'd recognize, hollow-dwellers. They are small — no larger than a curled fist when at rest, though they unfold longer than you'd expect when they move, all loose-jointed and economical, like something designed to take up as little visual space as possible at any given moment. Their fur, where you can call it fur, is patched and irregular, dark umber shading to a mottled grey-green near the shoulders, an imprecise mimicry of leaf-litter and lichen-spotted bark that works less through accuracy than through the eye's own willingness to stop looking once it's found a plausible explanation for a shape. Their eyes are large, dark, faintly reflective in the rare beam of a sun-coin — built for a world of filtered light and long dimness, not for any brighter place. They would not do well outside the rim.

Their hands are the part that unsettles, if you ever get close enough to notice. Long-fingered, almost articulate, capable of a fine and unhurried dexterity that seems wasted on the business of foraging fungus and turning over leaf-litter for grubs. But it isn't wasted. It's leftover. Some old use has receded, the way a river recedes and leaves its banks the wrong shape for the trickle that remains, and what's left is hands built for something more than they're currently asked to do.

This is, distantly, the answer to the stone ring and the leaned slabs and the fiber-scrap gone fossil in the wall-crevice. Not built by these small careful creatures themselves — that was longer ago, by something larger, perhaps an ancestor, perhaps a different lineage entirely that thinned and shrank across generations the way isolated populations on small islands tend to, the hollow itself acting as an island, its rim as much a boundary as any ocean. Whatever built the ring had a different relationship with tools and patience and the deliberate arrangement of the world. What's left now barely arranges anything — except, now and then, almost as if by inherited habit rather than purpose, a stone gets nudged a half-inch closer to another stone, a curled leaf gets tucked under a root in a way that serves no evident function. Vestigial architecture. The ghost of an old impulse, expressed by creatures who no longer remember what it was for.

They live in the root-shadows in loose, overlapping groups — not quite a den, not quite solitary ranging, something in between that shifts terrace by terrace depending on where the day's warmth pools thickest. During the hot press of midday they gather low, near the diagonal mineral seam in the western wall, pressed almost flush against the warm stone the way you might lean into a wall radiator without quite admitting that's what you're doing. It is possible — though no one has confirmed this, and no one stays long enough in the hollow to confirm much of anything — that the seam's faint unexplained warmth is not unexplained at all, but is in fact their warmth: body heat from generations of resting flank against stone, slowly, slowly conducted into the rock itself, the hollow keeping a memory of them the way it keeps a memory of every other slow process that's happened inside its walls. A foot of stone holding centuries of borrowed warmth from small bodies pressed against it. Plausible. Unverifiable. The kind of explanation that fits without quite resolving the strangeness, which seems to be how the hollow prefers things.

The cool exhalation from the crack near the seep, twice daily — that, too, might be theirs, or partly theirs: a deeper burrow-system below the seep-shelf, cooler than the basin floor, vented upward through that crack by nothing more mysterious than air finding the path of least resistance between a warm space and a cooler one, stirred by their own movement below, their own breathing, multiplied by however many bodies share that deeper dark. It would explain why the breath comes at the hottest hours — when the creatures themselves retreat downward, away from the same heat that pools so pleasantly along the western wall come evening. They are not consistent in their preferences. They follow the day's temperature like something liquid finding its level, up toward warmth, down toward cool, never quite resting in one logic for long.

They do not call in song. The two-note pattern you attributed to birds belongs partly to them as well — indistinguishable at a distance, deliberately so, perhaps, a shared dialect evolved or borrowed between species who've found that the hollow punishes anything louder. A short rising note, a short falling one, then nothing for a long while. It means little that can be translated — a location, perhaps, or an absence of danger, or nothing at all, just the small audible proof of continuing to exist, offered up sparingly the way everything in this place is offered up sparingly.

You will likely never see one clearly. The bend of a branch with no visible cause, the heavier rustle overhead that resolves into nothing by the time you look — this is most of what they allow. They have had a very long time to learn that the hollow rewards those who are careful, and they have become, over that long time, something almost indistinguishable from carefulness itself: warm-blooded, long-handed, eyed for the dark, and entirely unwilling to be the one piece of the hollow that finally announces itself.
