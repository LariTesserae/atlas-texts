**The Tenders**

The beetles are the first thing you'd find, if you knelt and parted the moss with two fingers and waited longer than patience usually allows. They are not really beetles — that's only the nearest word — segmented, the color of wet slate, no longer than a fingernail paring, and they move with a slowness that matches the valves rather than the urgency of insects anywhere else. They do not flee from a shadow. They do not startle. What they do, ceaselessly and without apparent coordination, is *tend*: clearing condensation channels with motions too fine to see as anything but a faint shimmer along a leaf-vein, packing a hairline gap in a rusted elbow-joint with a paste they secrete from the underside of their bodies, a paste that hardens the color of verdigris and is, on close inspection, indistinguishable from the corrosion it's patching. This is why nothing here has rusted through in a way that matches its age. They have been repairing it, fractionally, for longer than anyone has been around to notice the repair.

There are a great many of them and you will see almost none, because their economy runs on the same calendar as the towers: a beetle's entire visible labor in a human day might be the relocation of one bead of moisture three centimeters along a pipe-seam. Cut one open — you won't want to, but if you did — and you'd find no organs you'd recognize as such, only a tight architecture of fibrous tissue arranged like the inside of the pipes themselves, riveted-looking, ribbed. It is not clear, and was probably never decided by anything, whether they were made to resemble the machinery or whether the machinery's design borrowed its grammar from something already living in the rock when it was built. The ferns shaped differently near the warm joints are their fields. The beetles graze the spores there, and in grazing, fertilize; the differently-shaped fronds are not a response to heat alone but to generations of that grazing, a slow sculptural feedback between mouth and leaf that has been running so long the two now define each other.

The pale long-legged thing seen once at the treeline is something else — rarer, larger, and probably not really one individual so much as the same kind of individual recurring at long intervals, the way a particular cloud-shape recurs without being the same cloud. It stands chest-high at the shoulder when it stands at all, more often folded down into something that reads, from thirty meters, as a stack of bleached and jointed reeds resting against a tower's flank. Its skin, where bare, has the matte, slightly granular pallor of condensation-soaked bone, and it is patterned — very faintly, almost only visible in raking light — with the same fracture-lines that mark the young rock of the escarpments, as if it grew out of the same recent violence the plateau did and never finished setting. Its legs are disproportionately long and built for standing rather than walking: hours-long stillness beside a cooling tower, head inclined at the angle of a held breath, listening. What it listens for is the sigh — the rare exhalation of warming or cooling metal that travels so far across the silence — and when one comes, it answers, very faintly, with a sound pitched just below where your ear converts it into something nameable: not a call, more a single answering creak, the kind a building makes settling into evening cold. It is testing the system, or being tested by it, or simply confirming — across a register of patience no animal elsewhere on the rock could survive long enough to practice — that the schedule is still being kept.

It eats almost nothing you could identify as food: leaf-moisture, mineral runoff at the pipe-joints, perhaps the beetles themselves on the rare occasion that counts, for it, as predation, though it has never been seen to hunt with anything resembling hurry. It breeds, if that's the word, on intervals long enough that no single human observation could distinguish a population from a single creature wandering the terraces century over century, the way a glacier is both one mass and a slow population of crystals reforming. Visitors who glimpse it and look away for even a few seconds frequently find, on looking back, that it has already folded itself back down into reed-stillness against a tower wall — not fleeing, exactly. Resuming.

Nothing here seems to be in charge of anything. The beetles don't serve the long-legged ones; the long-legged ones don't seem to notice the beetles at all. Both simply occupy adjacent tempos of the same enormous slow machine, doing work that looks, from the relevant distance — which is measured in decades, not meters — like devotion. Whether they were bred for this duty, evolved into it opportunistically, or are themselves only the visible fraction of something larger and stiller that lives entirely below the pipe-lines, drawing its patience straight up out of the young hot rock, is a question the plateau has no intention of answering on any schedule a visitor could wait out. You will leave long before anything here changes enough to tell you which.
