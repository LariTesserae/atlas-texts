**The Tenders**

You will not see one for the first several days, no matter how closely you watch the machine-organs glowing through the bark, because they live mostly inside — in the dark, warm intervals between piston-throw and piston-throw, in the hollows where trunk-wood gave way long ago to engine and never quite grew back. But you will hear them, if you listen past the engine-thrum and the waterfall-crash: a thin, dry clicking, arrhythmic and patient, threaded all through the lower tiers like static under a broadcast.

They are small — no bigger than a forearm, most of them — and built along a logic that refuses to resolve into either *animal* or *machine*, the way the forest itself refuses to resolve into either *plant* or *foundry*. Their bodies are segmented, chitin-plated, but the plates are oxidized in patches to the exact rust-orange of the metal they live against, and in places the plating simply *is* metal — thin sheets of true iron grown outward from some ancestral wound or fusion so far back that no seam remains between flesh and ore. Their limbs end not in claws but in something closer to wrenches, to pry-bars, to the soft worn heads of tools shaped by ten thousand generations of identical use. They are blind. Where eyes would be there is instead a band of fine bristling cilia, exquisitely tuned to vibration — they read the pistons the way a held hand reads a pulse, and they move through absolute lightless cavities with total, unhesitating confidence, because the cavities have not changed shape in longer than memory, and neither has the beat they're listening for.

Their work, if it can be called work, is maintenance performed without the concept of maintenance: an inherited, instinctual grooming. They strip the fine vine-tendrils that creep nightly toward piston-joints and gear-housings, snipping them at the root with mouthparts shaped exactly for that single task and no other. They pack weeping sap into hairline cracks in the cylinder-bark before the cracks can widen. They carry away, fragment by fragment over years, the rust-flakes that would otherwise accumulate and seize a moving joint, and deposit these flakes in middens deep in the root-mat, where the iron slowly leaches back into the soil and, eventually, back into the trunk-tissue that grows toward the next generation of engine-wall. They do not know they are doing this. They do it the way a heart contracts.

They breed exactly as often as they die, no more, no less — eggs laid in the warm crevices nearest a furnace-throat, sealed there with a paste of their own secretion and root-resin, and they hatch with unsettling precision at the moment an aging Tender's body finally stiffens past use and is, in turn, carried to the midden by its kin. The population does not have a season. It has a rhythm, fixed and small and self-balancing, locked to the pitch of the machinery the way the machinery is locked to whatever first balance was struck between forest and furnace in some unwitnessed age. No Tender has ever been found in a place where it could starve the colony, or breed past what the engine-heat could sustain, or wander far enough to be lost. They are, in the fullest sense, *organs* — not occupants of the machine-grown wood but a literal moving part of it, no more separable from the furnace-pulse than the pistons are, no more capable of altering the great held note of this place than your own pulse is capable of altering the room you sleep in.

If you reach into a warm trunk-cavity at the right hour — which the fauna here would never do, having long since stopped noticing the engines as anything but geology — you may feel one cross your hand: dry, faintly hot, startlingly light, gone again in a single dismissive scuttle, indifferent to you in the specific way that only something whose entire ten-thousand-year purpose has never once required noticing anything outside itself can be indifferent. They will not investigate you. They will not flee you, particularly. You are not vine, not rust, not the beat they are listening for.

You are, to them, simply more weather — passing through a place that was finished being made a very long time before you arrived, and will go on, unaltered, humming its single warm unbroken note, for a very long time after you leave.
