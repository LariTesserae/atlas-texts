They are a people scaled to gaps.

You notice this before you notice anything else about them — the way they move through spaces that seem, at first, too narrow to admit a body: between root-buttress and stilt-post, under vine-lattices trained low, along plank-walks no wider than a forearm's span slung between trunks. They are not small in any dramatic sense, not stunted or strange, but there is an economy to them, a folded compactness in how they carry their shoulders and place their feet, as though several hundred years of negotiating with a crowded green ceiling has bred itself into posture. Children learn to duck before they learn to walk upright. Old men still do it from memory, bowing slightly under archways that were cleared head-high a generation before they were born, the gesture outliving its necessity.

Their skin, in the perpetual underwater light of the canopy, takes on a faint greenish cast that washes out the moment they climb to the rim and stand for a while in full sun — which they do, sometimes, for no stated reason, the way a person might surface to breathe. There is a particular squint they share, a slow recalibration of the eyes, because most of their lives are spent in dimness pierced by motion rather than brightness pierced by stillness. They have learned to read shapes more than colors, movement more than form. A hunter here does not look for an animal's outline so much as for the wrongness in a pattern of twitching leaves — one tremble out of rhythm with the rest.

What strikes you most, once you've spent a day among them, is how little they seem bothered by the noise. To you, newly arrived, the basin's sound is a wall, an assault, something to brace against. To them it is simply the medium they think in. Conversations are pitched to carry under it without competing with it — a low, close-range register, words spoken almost mouth-to-ear, so that intimacy here is partly acoustic, forced into existence by the volume of everything nonhuman around it. You'll see two people lean their heads together to say something that elsewhere would be shouted across a room. Within a basin this loud, closeness is not a choice but an architecture.

They keep small fires not for warmth but as instruments — to thin the insect-bands, to mark thresholds, to signal across the tiers of the town when the walkways are too crowded with vine-growth to risk a shouted message. Smoke, here, is a kind of language: a thin grey thread rising from a particular house means something different than smoke rising from the firepit at the rim. Children are taught to read these signs before they're taught to read anything else, because in a place this dense, sightlines fail constantly, and smoke goes where voices can't.

Their relationship to the surrounding life is neither romantic nor adversarial; it is managerial, intimate, exhausted, and respectful in the manner of people who have negotiated the same terms with the same landlord for so many generations that affection and resentment have fused into something more like familiarity. They train the fruiting vines because the fruiting vines, untrained, would close every path within a season. They keep certain birds nesting close to the houses because those birds eat the insects that would otherwise drive the town's edges back toward the bare stone rim. They do not think of this as harmony, particularly — ask one, and they'll describe it the way you'd describe an old, difficult marriage: still going, still working, no longer questioned.

There is an unhurriedness to them that isn't laziness but pacing — the pacing of people who know the basin will outlast any single effort to subdue it, and so don't try. Roof-thatch gets replaced in its season, not before. Walkway lashings get checked after rain, not before. They've stopped fighting the green for territory and settled instead for a kind of permanent, low-grade negotiation, re-won every week, never finally settled, never expected to be.

The elders are the ones who carry the basin's memory of itself — not written, because nothing here would survive the damp for long, but spoken, threaded into the cadence of work-songs sung while re-lashing beams or husking the basin's small bitter fruit. They remember which trees their grandparents trained and which their own hands have continued training, and there's a kind of pride in this that has nothing to do with ownership and everything to do with continuity — the sense of being one more pair of hands in a very long relay, holding a shape that was old before they arrived and will be old after they're gone.

And yet for all that patience, there's an alertness under their calm that never fully switches off — a readiness in the eyes, a habit of listening even mid-sentence, because the basin's noise is also its warning system, and a sudden silence, a gap opening in the weave of calls and hums, means something is moving that the rest of the basin has decided to fear. They've learned to feel that gap before they consciously notice it, the way you might feel a held breath in a room before you realize no one is speaking.

They are, in the end, a people who have made their peace with being outnumbered — by insects, by birds, by the green itself, by every small loud form of life crowding the bowl they call home — and who have built, in the gaps that life left them, something durable enough to call a town: small, dim, damp, relentlessly alive, and entirely, stubbornly theirs.
