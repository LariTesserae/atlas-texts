# Inhabitant

It takes a long time to see them, because they have stopped being the kind of thing that announces itself. You will pass close to one — closer than seems possible to have missed — and register it only as a slightly denser fold in the moss-coral, a shape with too much symmetry to be coincidence, before the slowness of the place teaches you to wait, and the waiting teaches you to look again, and on the second look you understand that the wall has an eye in it.

Call them descendants, though that word implies a lineage that knew it was changing. What they are now is what is left of the people who cut the channels and lashed the cord and shaped the terraces by hand across generations that thought nothing of taking generations — except that they did not leave. When the water came up past where breath could follow, something in them did not drown so much as it surrendered tempo. Whatever they were is still, technically, what they are: pale, soft-skinned, limbed in a way that once meant something like *walking*, faced in a way that once meant something like *seeing*. But the eyes have gone milk-blind and vestigial, kept perhaps out of habit, the way a closed door is still called a door. And the limbs no longer reach for anything in particular. They have not been useful in longer than any one of these beings could mark, even if marking time were still a thing they did.

They do not move the way the word *move* implies elsewhere. A gesture that would take you or me a half-second to complete — the closing of a hand, the turning of a head toward some faint disturbance in the current — takes one of them the better part of a season. This is not paralysis. It is closer to agreement: an agreement, made so long ago that it no longer reads as a decision, that there is no longer anything that needs to be reached quickly. Food, if that is even the right framework, arrives to them rather than the reverse — fine organic silt, the slow rain of the ribbon-growth's own dying fronds, drawn in through skin gone permeable and patient, processed across days the way a stone processes weather. They do not hunt because there is nothing to hunt and no hurry that would make hunting make sense. They do not flee because nothing here has ever come fast enough to need fleeing from. The lattice has slowed itself to their pace, or they have slowed themselves to its.

You could live here a long time — observer-time, fast-blooded time — and conclude, as the rest of the record concludes, that there is no one home. No fish, no crawling thing, no glint of an eye catching the light. This is not wrong, exactly. It is wrong the way it would be wrong to say a glacier doesn't move. They are there. They are simply operating on the same clock as the silt-fall and the pressure-breath tides, indistinguishable, at any single vantage, from the architecture they have become an extension of. Some of them have not changed position — not failed to move, but completed a single ongoing motion — for spans of time that the rest of the lattice would measure in terrace-erosion, in the slow rounding of a stair into a shelf.

What they do, as far as doing can be said to still happen, is maintain. Not out of any remembered purpose — purpose requires a kind of urgency they no longer carry — but out of something further down than purpose, something closer to grain in wood. A drainage channel silts closed; across years, by degrees too small to call effort, a hand that has not closed in a decade will open, and a few grains of sediment will be moved, and the channel will run a little freer, and the hand will not close again for another decade. It is not memory of engineering. It is engineering with the urgency boiled entirely out of it, reduced to its purest residue: water should go this way, and a hand, eventually, sees to it that it does.

They do not speak. Whatever once passed between them as language has receded past any structure that would let it travel air or even this lightless water — if there is communication left at all, it is not faster than the tides, a held posture noticed and, much later, mirrored, a kind of conversation that takes longer than a human life to complete a single exchange. They do not gather, particularly, though sometimes two or three will be found close together along the same wall, in the same fold of growth, and it is impossible to know whether this is companionship, coincidence, or simply that the silt collects more thickly in certain corners and they have only ever followed the silt.

They will not harm you, and could not if some old urgency in them tried — there is no version of speed left in their bodies fast enough to catch anything that still measures time the way you do. The danger, if there is one, is only this: that you might mistake one for wall, lean against it, and feel, much later than you expected, the faintest pressure of a hand beginning — long after you've gone, long after you've forgotten you ever touched it — to close.
