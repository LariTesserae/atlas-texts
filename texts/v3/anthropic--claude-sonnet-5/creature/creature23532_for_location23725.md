They call it — no one calls it anything, there being no one left to call it, but if you watched long enough you would start needing a name for it, the way you need a name for a draft you keep feeling under a door — the Walker. There may be one. There may be several, identical, taking turns so perfectly that the basin only ever shows you one at a time, and you've miscounted the rest as repetition.

It moves along the gratings at the pace of someone who has never once been late and never once arrived early, because for the Walker there is no early or late, only the loop, which has no beginning to be early relative to. You see it first as a wrongness in the herd-shiver of the pale dog-sized things — they part for it without panic, the way water parts for something that was always going to be there, a rock revealed at low tide rather than an intrusion. It is taller than the things that flee it and shorter than the chimneys it passes beneath, built along lines that almost resolve into a body and then don't: too many joints in the legs, set at angles that suggest a designer who understood leverage but not anatomy, who built it to traverse grating and gantry-rail and the lipped edges of boiler-housings with equal indifference, and so gave it the leg of a thing equally unsuited to all three and therefore unbothered by any of them.

Its hide, if hide is the word, is the same desaturated pale as everything that crawls and crouches here, but smoother — not skin so much as a kind of weathered ceramic, fired once and never reglazed, the color of the vapor that comes off the stacks, so that at a distance it can vanish into its own backdrop and you only register it again when it moves, a paleness detaching itself from a paleness. It has no face arranged the way a face is supposed to arrange itself — no symmetry you'd call kind — but high on what serves as its forward end there is a single aperture, lidless, that opens and narrows on no schedule tied to light. It is not looking for anything. It has never had to look for anything. Whatever it needs from the basin, the basin has already arranged to be wherever the Walker is going next, which is the real reason nothing here panics in any lasting way: the things that live alongside it have learned, across however many cycles constitute their memory, that the Walker arriving is not an event. It is punctuation.

This is what it seems to do, as far as doing can be said of something that has no project: it walks the boundary between the two soundscapes, mechanical and animal, at the exact seam where they overlap, and where it walks, the seam tightens. The clicking of the centipede-carpet beneath the grating quickens half a beat to meet the hiss of a valve opening three buildings over. The birds-that-aren't on the chimney-tops answer a piston's groan a half-second sooner than they would have answered it alone. You cannot prove this. You can only feel it, the way you feel a held chord resolve when a single note shifts under it — the basin's whole compound drone seems, in the Walker's wake, very slightly more like one sound and less like two, for exactly as long as it takes the Walker to pass out of sight around the next housing, after which the seam loosens again, the two musics drift a fraction apart, and somewhere behind you — or ahead, the basin not really permitting the distinction — the Walker is already coming back around to tighten it again.

It does not eat. It does not appear to need the boilers' heat or the cold runoff pooling in the flagstone cracks. It has never been observed resting, though "never observed" means little where no one stays to observe past a single afternoon. It does not multiply, or if it does, it does so by a method that produces no juveniles, no smaller pale ceramic shapes learning the gait — only ever the one full-grown article, walking, or several full-grown articles, walking, in a number that never seems to change because change is not a currency this basin trades in.

If you stood at the basin's lip with enough patience to watch a full circuit, you might come to think of the Walker not as a creature living in the basin but as the basin's way of checking on itself — a held breath's awareness, briefly, that it is being held. And then it would round the corner of a brick stack trailing its pale vapor, and the dog-sized things would unflinch back into their twitching ranks, and the birds would resume their ranks along the chimney-lips, and you would be left with the drone, the click, the hiss, the slap of too many feet, one sound again instead of two, and no further evidence that anything had passed through at all — except that, for a moment, the rhythm had been exactly, unbearably even, and now it was merely close.
