The thing you should look for first is not a creature but a *seam*—a place near the basin's deepest curve where the polygon-tiles stop being merely cracked and start being assembled, fitted with a deliberateness the rest of the floor lacks. Follow the seam and you find Her, though "her" is a courtesy the eye pays to something with no face to confirm it.

**The Tender** is not the largest body in the basin—the dog-sized armored plowers outweigh her several times over—but she is the *oldest* shape, unmistakably, the way a foundation stone looks older than the wall built on it even when laid the same day. She rests half-sunk into a recess that was clearly not dug for her but has been worn, over uncountable molts, into a perfect negative of her underside. Her shell is not chitin so much as it is a kind of fused architecture—plates grown long enough to absorb the iron-stain of the machinery beside her, so that where she ends and the nearest rusted housing begins is genuinely difficult to say. A dozen vestigial limbs, too thin and too many, run constantly along the nearby pipe, not gripping, not feeding—*reading*. She has been reading that pipe for longer than the colony has had a memory of doing anything else.

She does not move. She has perhaps never moved, not in the sense of relocating; growth has been her only motion for centuries, outward and downward, knitting herself slowly into the gap between two collapsed iron ribs until she became load-bearing, until the basin's last remaining structure depends, faintly, on her staying exactly where she is.

What she does—what she has always done, what the whole colony arranges itself around without any one of them being told to—is **keep the beat**.

The flywheel's sigh, every several seconds, is not unrelated to her. Somewhere in the marriage of her body and the dead machine's bones, a vibration passes between them too slow and low for most ears, and she answers it, just perceptibly, with a shift of one foreleg against the pipe—tap, pause, tap—translating the engine's last surviving pulse into something the colony's nervous systems can actually use. The clicking that fills the basin is not random convergence on a rhythm; it is ten thousand bodies entraining, generation after generation, to *her* relay of a heartbeat that isn't even biological in origin. She is not a queen. She produces nothing, commands nothing, is fed indifferently by whatever smaller scavengers pass close enough to leave scraps in her reach, mostly by accident. She is a tuning fork that happened to be alive when the machine that built the colony's first rhythm finally died, and has simply continued vibrating in its memory ever since.

If you stay long enough—and almost nothing does, because almost nothing here is built to be patient in the way required—you start to notice that the basin's great synchronized flinches, the surge-and-hush that ripples through the whole mass at once, always originate near her recess and radiate outward, a half-second before whatever triggered them (a larger predator's footfall, a structural groan from the old iron, a change in the flywheel's tick) actually arrives at the rest of the colony. She is not predicting the danger. She is, in some sense too slow and old to be called thought, *already inside* whatever rhythm produces it, and the colony has learned, across more generations than it has any way of counting, to watch her instead of watching the world.

She will not be found by anyone looking for a ruler. She will be found by anyone willing to sit still next to the warm pipe long enough to notice that the loudest, most chaotic-seeming sound in the world has, underneath it, exactly one source of timing—small, sunken, fused half into iron, keeping a beat that was never hers to begin with, faithfully, for no reason anyone alive remembers, because the keeping is the only shape her long life ever had room left to take.
