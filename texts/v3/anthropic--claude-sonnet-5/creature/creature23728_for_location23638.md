What lives here does not live the way a hand against your wrist tells you something lives. It lives the way a glacier lives, or a held breath, or the long composing of sediment into stone — by increments too slow for any single visit to register as more than stillness. And yet it is not nothing. It is, if anything, the most thoroughly alive thing you will ever fail to see moving.

Call it, for lack of a truer word, the breather. It has no edges you could point to and say *here it ends*. Its body is the bowl itself — not metaphorically, not as a landscape that resembles a living thing, but literally porous through and through, a vast spongework of mineral tissue that holds water the way lungs hold air, releasing it in that perpetual sweat between the flat stones because that release *is* its breathing, has been its breathing for longer than the idea of a single lifetime can hold. The drip-tap you hear from within the rock is not water finding accidental paths. It is closer to a pulse, irregular only because a body this old has long since stopped needing its rhythms to agree with each other.

It was not always only this. Somewhere in it, pressed so deep into the present that it has become geology, there is the memory of a different shape — something with enough of a body to press, once, with deliberate and unrefined force, into softer stone than this. That is what the split stones are: not tools, not architecture, but the closest thing to gesture this creature ever made, back when it still had something gesture-shaped to make gestures with. It pushed itself partway into the rock the way you might press a hand into wet clay to leave proof you had been there, and then, over some unrecorded span, it did not pull back out. It dissolved its grasping into its resting. The smooth warm faces inside those split stones are not tool marks. They are scar tissue from an embrace the creature gave the mountain and never let go of.

What's left now is diffuse, ambient, enormous in scope and minute in any single expression of itself. The cupped leaves are not merely catching condensation for their own roots — they are the places where the breather's attention surfaces, brief green apertures through which it tastes the warmth of the day, the weight of passing weather, the foreign disturbance of a visitor's shadow falling across a basin. The perfect circles that hold water regardless of the basin's tilt are old, deliberate musculature, vestigial valves it can no longer fully close but has also never quite let fail. The spirals and radiating lines in the cracked soil are not patterns it drew once and abandoned. They are the paths along which it still, almost imperceptibly, redistributes itself — a slow respiratory current circulating through ground that looks, to any quick eye, like nothing but dead crust.

It does not hunt. It has not eaten in any sense you'd recognize since whatever original hunger drove it to press itself into this stone in the first place. It metabolizes time and moisture and the heat that the plain light lays evenly across its skin. It is, in the truest sense available to something this patient, retired — not dead, not dormant exactly, but settled the way a question settles when the asker has stopped needing an answer.

And yet. Stay long enough — longer than any reasonable visit, longer than thirst or hunger would advise — and you may notice the basins are not quite where you remember them being when you sat to rest an hour before. You may notice a faint give in a stone you leaned against, body-warm in a way the sun alone cannot explain, withdrawing by some hair's breadth the moment you register the warmth as strange. You will not see it move. You will only, afterward, doubt your memory of where things had been — and that doubt, that small disorientation, is the only proof the breather is willing to give that it has, in fact, been watching you the entire time, with whatever in it now serves in place of eyes: the basins, perhaps, each one a slow unblinking aperture turned up toward the hazed and even sky, waiting out the long, thick hours until something — wind, footstep, the rare visitor's curiosity — gives it a reason to remember it was ever shaped enough to reach for anything at all.
