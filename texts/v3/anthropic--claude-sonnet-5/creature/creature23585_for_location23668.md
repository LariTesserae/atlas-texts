They are smaller than you expect, almost without exception — not stunted, but conserved, as if some long calculus had been run on how much body the place could afford to keep warm and had come back with a narrow, exact figure. Lean through the shoulder and forearm, thick through the wrist and hand, with a economy of flesh everywhere else that reads less as deprivation than as design, the same editing logic that thinned the sedges to black-stemmed fists in the rock. Their skin has the over-rinsed pallor of the light itself, a grey-white with a faint underglow, like something held a long time under water and only recently, partially, dried. You will not get a confident read on anyone's age. A face seamed like a dry streambed may belong to someone who has carried stone for ninety years or for nine; childhood here seems to pass quickly through, the way a flood passes through a channel cut exactly to its width, and what comes out the other side already has the patient, used hands of an adult.

The hands are the part you remember. Wide-palmed, long-fingered, perpetually wet or freshly dried, scarred in fine cross-hatchings from handling wet stone in the cold — and astonishingly articulate. This is where the silence goes to be spoken. A flick of two fingers, a hand laid flat and then tilted, a thumb drawn once along the inside wrist: these carry the instructions that elsewhere would need a raised voice, a name called across a terrace, an argument. Children are not taught this vocabulary so much as soaked in it, the way they are soaked in everything else here, until by the time they can lift a stone they can also read, without looking up from the work, exactly which stone and where. Travelers who stay long enough start to notice they themselves have stopped trying to ask questions aloud, and have started watching hands instead, the way you'd watch weather.

Their eyes are pale, often a washed hazel or grey-green nearly the color of the wet rock, and habitually narrowed even indoors, even in what shadow exists, against a glare that apparently never fully releases its grip even behind the lids. Some accounts — unconfirmable, traded third-hand among travelers who never stayed to verify — claim a second inner lid, a nictitating clarity that drops when the work takes them down into the basins among the blinking amphibious things they tend, so that for a moment underwater there is no difference visible between the keeper's face and the kept. Whether or not this is literally true, it is true in spirit: the people of the Wellings hold themselves toward their fauna less as herders than as kin in a shared trade, fellow employees of the place, as comfortable wrist-deep among the fat eyeless fish as among each other, feeding both with the same unhurried, identical gesture, scattering nothing, wasting nothing, the hand entering the water and leaving it as though water were simply a slower, colder kind of air.

What you will not find in them is anything like nostalgia, or anxiety, about the perpetual collapse and reset of their own town. A wall going down is met with the same flat attention as a wall going up; there is no audible relief when a new channel finally takes a flood, no audible grief when an old basin closes overnight and drowns a room someone's grandmother might have slept in. This is not numbness. It has the texture, rather, of a people who have made their peace so thoroughly with process that outcome has stopped being the unit they measure in. They do not seem to believe the town will ever be finished, and do not seem troubled by this; finished is not a category the water recognizes, so it is not one they keep. Ask why the first basin was cut, why up into a hive instead of out across easier ground, and you get the same gesture the place itself gives you — toward the spring, the cold unaskable upwelling — because for them the town is not a thing they imposed on the water. It is the shape the water's argument has worn into them, generation by generation, the way a stair-tread is dished by feet and runoff both, until you could no longer separate the foot from the wearing, or the keeper from the kept, or the people from the held, breathing instability they have spent their whole quiet lives bringing, by hand, back into momentary stone.
