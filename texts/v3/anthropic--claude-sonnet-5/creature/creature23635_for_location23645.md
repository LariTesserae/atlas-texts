## What Surfaces

Call it the Tenant, because that is what the people who climb here call it, in the diffident way you name something you'd rather not address directly. There may be one. There may be several — the disputed pillar-count applies to the Tenant too, perhaps for the same reason, perhaps for a different one: it does not like to be seen twice from the same angle, or there is more than one of it and no one has ever been patient enough, or alive enough, to compare notes.

It is long — twelve, fourteen feet, hard to say, since no one has ever seen all of it in the same moment of light. What surfaces is a *section*: a stretch of pale flank rolling over near a pillar-top, the texture of old tallow, faintly ridged the way a snake's belly is ridged, or the way a fish's lateral line runs, except the ridges here are deeper, almost architectural, almost coursed, as if the thing took some early instruction from the masonry it has lived against for longer than any human lineage has lasted. There is no head that anyone describes the same way twice. Some say a blunt taper, eyeless, the skin gone smooth and poreless over where eyes used to mean something to its ancestors. Some say barbels, fine as cord-saw thread, trailing and tasting the warm dark water for the chemical signature of crustacean colonies thinning and thickening on the steps below. Some say nothing at all — say the front end simply isn't a thing you get to see, that it is always already turning away, that the Tenant presents itself to visitors strictly in retreat, the way a wave shows you its back.

It eats slowly and rarely, the way everything here does. The fur of pale shell on the submerged steps is not just colony, it is *pasture* — a renewable crop of crustacean bodies cropped low and left to regrow, generation on generation, the Tenant grazing a single stair clean over a span of years and then leaving it fallow, moving its long body to another stair, another pillar's shadow, on a rotation so unhurried that the same families of climbers who tell their children not to throw stones also tell them, more quietly, which stair the Tenant is "resting" this decade. The eyeless fish that hang motionless in the pillar-shadows are not the Tenant's prey, mostly. They are its company, or its commensals, or simply the other tenants of a system too poor in light to support competition — they take what drifts down off its body, the same way it takes what grows up out of the dark.

What it actually subsists on, beneath the grazing, is the warmth. The cistern's blood-near heat is not incidental to the Tenant; it may be the reason there is a Tenant at all rather than just an empty hollow the builders cut and abandoned to flood. Something in the deep mineral chemistry of the place — a hot seam the masons may not even have known they were quarrying toward, may have simply struck and capped and called finished — feeds a slow bacterial bloom along the cistern floor no one living has measured, and that bloom feeds the crustaceans, and the crustaceans, eventually, on a timescale that makes geology look impatient, feed the Tenant. It is the vent-economy the surface description gestures at, but carried all the way up to a single, immense, unhurried body at the top of it — the entire chamber's patience expressed as flesh.

Age is the wrong word for what the Tenant has, because age implies a clock running toward something, and nothing in this water is running toward anything. But if you had to guess — and the people who climb here do guess, in low voices, on the dry steps, before the light fails — you would guess it has been here close to as long as the cistern has held water at all. Not the same individual unbroken across two thousand years, probably; these things, whatever they are, must breed, must die, must hand the slow tenancy down the way the masons handed the terracing down, generation finishing what its grandparents started. But the population, the *line* — that may be original. May have come up the mountain in some founder handful, in a wooden bucket or a woven creel, carried by the same people who fitted the stone, released into the new-filled basin as the last act of construction, a consecration rather than an accident. A reservoir is not a useful structure if nothing keeps the water turning over below the still surface, if nothing aerates the sediment, if nothing closes the gap between fresh-cut stone and a self-sustaining system. Maybe the builders knew that. Maybe the Tenant was not a guardian or an offering but an *instrument* — the last piece of machinery they installed, alive instead of stone, because at some point a civilization with patient enough hands realizes the only material flexible enough to finish a project is something that can finish it after you.

It does not seem to mind being looked at, exactly, the way it does not seem to mind anything. It rolls into visibility with the unhurried economy of something that has never once needed to flee a predator, because nothing in the cistern is faster than it is and nothing above the waterline has ever gone in after it. The ripple it leaves takes a long time to die against the stairs. People watch the ripple longer than they watched the body. This may be the point, or the closest thing the Tenant has to a point: it is less a creature you observe than a creature you *infer*, the way you infer the shape of the cistern's blind floor from the seven or nine pillar-tops, the way you infer two thousand years of seep from joints you can no longer find with a blade. It is evidence of itself rather than a sighting of itself, and the people who climb here have made a kind of peace with that, the same peace they've made with not knowing what the cistern was for, what the builders called it, what they thought would happen here once it was done.

Something is being kept. That's as far as anyone gets. Something pale and old and in no hurry is being kept, by the mountain, by the stone, by its own grazing patience, in water that stopped wanting to be anything else a very long time ago — and it keeps itself, in turn, exactly the way the cistern keeps its stillness: not as an absence of life, but as life that has simply finished arguing with time, and gone on, underneath, at its own unbothered rate.
