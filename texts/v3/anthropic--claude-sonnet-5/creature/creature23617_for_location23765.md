## What Answers When You Knock

The small things come first, because they're the only ones that can be heard. Call them what you like — there is no name for them anywhere, no taxonomy waiting to claim them, and they would not answer to one if there were. They are segmented, low to whatever surface they move along, and built along lines that suggest insect ancestry the way a fossil suggests an animal: a resemblance worn down to its functional residue. Six points of contact, sometimes eight, sometimes a number that seems to change because what's actually changing is which limbs are touching metal and which are merely trailing. Chitin, if it is chitin, has gone the color of old rivets. They are warm to a degron no thermometer would register as warm, because they have spent however many millennia living in the three inches of ambient heat that leaks from a pipe joint, and they have stopped being separate from that heat the way the plateau has stopped being separate from its machines.

They eat almost nothing. This is the first thing that should seem impossible and stops seeming impossible quickly, once you understand the place. There is a residue in certain conduits — a fine dark dust, mineral, faintly oily, that collects where moving parts have wicked it free of themselves over geological time — and the small things graze it the way deer might graze a meadow, except the meadow regenerates at the speed of metal fatigue, which is to say: it will outlast them, their children, and whatever comes after their children. They do not seem to breed so much as *recur*. A scrabbling sound in a wall conduit might be the same individual that made a sound forty years before, or it might be the eleventh generation of something that simply never needed to look different from its ancestors, because nothing in the environment has asked it to.

They are not afraid of the dark, having never known anything else. They are not afraid of the machinery's sounds, having been born inside them. If they are afraid of anything, it would have to be silence — an actual stillness in the hum, an actual cooling of the pipe — because that would be the only event in their entire ancestral memory that had ever happened to them from outside. It has not happened yet. They have no plan for it.

## The Thing on the Girder

Larger, rarer, and never confirmed, is whatever moves along the highest gantries, glimpsed only as a displacement of the dim amber light — a shape blocking a bulb for half a second, there and then conspicuously not there. It may be a larger and lonelier version of the small things, grown big the slow way that island fauna grow big, in the absence of anything competing for the heat-conduits up high. It may be something else's predator, if predation is even the right frame for a creature whose food source is mineral dust and whose lifespan might be measured in centuries. Or it may not be an animal at all in the sense that word usually carries — it may be a *function*, an inspection-process the old systems still run, embodied for reasons that made sense to whoever embodied it and make sense to no one now. It tends to something. The pipes it walks above never seem to fail. That is either evidence of its diligence or evidence that nothing up there was ever going to fail in the first place, and the two explanations have become impossible to tell apart, the way every explanation on this plateau eventually folds into the machinery's own unbothered persistence.

## The Builders, Distributed

And underneath both of these, underneath the scrabbling and the high silent custodian on the girder, is the harder thing to describe, because it isn't seen at all, only inferred — the suspicion that the makers of this place did not leave it so much as *recede into* it, the way a voice recedes into an echo without technically vanishing. If they are here, they are not here as bodies. They are here as the steadiness of the gauge-readings, as the particular routing of a particular pipe that still, after all this time, carries something somewhere for a reason that was once urgent and is now simply habitual. If consciousness persists in the plateau at all, it persists distributed and slow, thinking thoughts on a timescale where a thought might take four hundred years to complete a single clause — and the hum that never quite stops, the pressure-sigh every minute, the patient unchanging note the whole apparatus holds, might not be ambient noise at all.

It might be breathing.

It might, in the very longest and most patient sense available to anything, be listening back.
