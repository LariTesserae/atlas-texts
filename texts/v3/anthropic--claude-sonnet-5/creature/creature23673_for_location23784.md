# Those Who Tend the Terraces

## The Small Ones — Conduit-Kin

You will hear them before you understand they are not part of the structure's settling and venting. The click of articulated legs on plate-steel, that sound like rain that isn't rain — that is the conduit-kin, and there are more of them than there is room for, by any sane accounting of the space between pipe and wall.

They are small, low, built close to the surfaces they travel — six- or eight-legged, though the count seems to vary even within what looks like a single kind, as though legs are added and shed the way the terraces add and shed plate. Their carapace is the gray of unfinished metal, faintly magnetic-looking, faintly oiled, and it is genuinely difficult — even at close range, even crouched and staring — to tell where rust-lichen ends and carapace begins, because the lichen *grows on them* as readily as it grows on the girders, and they do not seem to mind, or notice, or be anything other than improved by it.

They eat the condensate. This is the central fact of them: wherever moisture beads, conduit-kin gather, unhurried, patient, drinking with mouthparts so fine you mostly infer their presence from the small dry click afterward. They are not aggressive and barely seem aware of larger things moving through their spaces — they simply reroute, the way water reroutes, flowing around your boot and reconvening on the other side. In aggregate, gathered along a sweating pipe-run at the cool hour, they look like a slow-moving rust stain, like the metal itself deciding to migrate toward water.

No one has explained why they cluster, sometimes, in identical numbers on terraces a great distance apart — eleven here, eleven there, never twelve, never ten — as though counting matters more to them than location does.

## The Gliders

Higher up, where the air goes thin and sharp, the gliders launch from terrace-edges into currents that the terraces' enclosed geometry should not permit. They are the visible proof that something moves here besides the engine's slow turning.

Their bodies are narrow, ribbed, almost entirely wing-joint — four, six, sometimes what looks like an asymmetrical seven, folded and unfolded along hinge-points that gleam faintly metallic at the root and fade to something more like old paper at the tip. They are not heavy, not even when they look it; they ride the brassy light in long unhurried arcs from one terrace's lip to the next one's, sometimes covering distances that — given how the terraces themselves refuse to converge properly at range — should be impossible to judge, let alone cross.

They roost in clusters along the highest terrace edges, motionless for long stretches, wings drawn tight, faces (if that is the word) angled toward the shadowless light as though it fed them as surely as condensate feeds the small ones below. Occasionally one launches without apparent cause, glides a long unbroken arc, and lands on a terrace that — you would swear — is the same terrace it left from, just somehow on the far side of it. The gliders do not seem troubled by this. Nothing here that has lived long enough to be native to it seems troubled by the geometry. It is only the visitor who flinches at the repeated walkway, the looping sightline. The gliders bank through it like it's weather.

## The Tenders

Deeper in — past several thresholds, past terraces whose age you've stopped trying to read because the wear pattern stopped making sense around the fourth or fifth crossing — you may, if you are patient and quiet and lucky, see one of the Tenders.

They are larger than anything else that moves on the terraces, upright or near enough to upright, built along a frame that suggests purpose more than it suggests anatomy: too many points of articulation at the shoulder, hands — if they are hands — that resolve, on closer look, into something more like the actuated ends of tools than fingers in any familiar sense. Their surfaces show the same gray-metal, lichen-soft texture as the conduit-kin's carapace, but burnished in long-used patches, the way a handle gets burnished by a grip repeated ten thousand times.

They are seen, almost always, doing something to the structure — drawing a new seam tight, easing a runnel's slope by some increment too small to measure, laying a fresh plate at the indistinct edge of sight where the terraces keep extending themselves into whatever the place was before it was this. They do not look up when approached. They do not look up at all, much. Their attention, such as it is, stays on the joint, the seam, the next small correction.

No one knows if the Tenders are why the terraces don't finish, or whether they are simply the part of the not-finishing that happens to be shaped like a worker. It's possible — and this is the thought that follows you back down through the climbing, looping, repeating terraces, the thought that arrives in the quiet hours when the fog pools and burns away — that the Tenders did not build this place so much as the place is still in the process of building *them*, slowly, plate by plate, out of whatever raw, patient, accretive intention first laid the lowest, oldest, rust-softened floor and never once considered stopping.

## The Engine's Keeper, Maybe

Of whatever turns far below, far within, generating the sourceless light and the matchless cool and the pressure-hum that lives in the eardrum rather than the air — nothing is seen. Nothing is known. If it has a keeper, the keeper has never been observed, and the engine has run, by every available sign, far longer than the memory of anything that walks, glides, or tends among the terraces above it.

But sometimes, in the lowest reachable joints, where the fog pools longest and the rust-lichen grows thickest and oldest, the conduit-kin gather in unusual numbers, eleven and eleven and eleven, all facing the same unmarked direction, all still, for longer than stillness should hold an animal that otherwise never stops moving toward water.

They are not afraid. They do not look like a creature that has learned fear. But they gather like that, sometimes, and then they disperse, and the terraces grow another seam of plate at the edge of sight, and the engine keeps its long unhurried turning, and nothing — in this slow, patient, accretive place — is ever quite finished arriving.
