**The Wick-Swarm**

They are called, by what few outside chroniclers have catalogued them at all, the *thrum-kin*, though no one species name covers what moves through the Heights in such numbers. Better to think of them as one continuous population wearing several shapes, all descended — or convergent — from something that learned, long before any human eye saw this plateau, that the only food worth having here comes from water and stone and the leavings of the city itself.

Picture a body built for grip and damp and dark: a low barrel torso sheathed in something between chitin and wax, secreted in overlapping plates that catch the ambient wet and hold it as a second skin, the same translucent sheen as the mineral glaze on the rock below. This isn't decoration. It's camouflage by *material* rather than color — a thrum-kin pressed flat against a terrace of cooled mineral wax is, to any eye but another thrum-kin's, simply more terrace. They have made themselves out of the same stuff the plateau keeps almost-finishing.

Their eyes never developed past a useless pale dimple, lidless, sensing only the gross presence of the structural glow and nothing else. What they have instead are ears in the old sense and something past it: paired membranous vanes along the skull, fluted and ribbed like the wax-terraces, that catch returning sound and resolve it into shape, distance, density of crowd. They navigate the windowless dark of the city's interstices the way the histories say old sailors read swell and current — entirely by what bounces back. The chittering and clicking that fills the Heights isn't background noise. It's how every thrum-kin sees.

They move in three registers. Most of the time, in the great patient mass, they hang — clustered by the thousands in folds of stone and structural undercroft, wings mantled, barely stirring, feeding by slow capillary action on the mineral-laced condensation that beads everywhere, and on the warmer, richer runoff that bleeds from certain structural seams the way sap bleeds from a cut tree. Those seams are tended jealously; a good vent supports a colony for a generation, and territory along them is fought over in long, nearly silent wars of displacement that take years to resolve, exactly as glacial as the rock's own slow extrusions.

Then there are the surges — the wet slap and scrabble described by anyone who has stood too near a feeding-trough at the wrong hour, when some chemical or atmospheric trigger nobody has identified sends a colony pouring across bridges and down towers in a single coordinated mass, wings cracking open in a noise like a held breath finally let go, the air displaced in gusts strong enough to feel from yards off. These surges look like panic. They are not. They are closer to tide — entire colonies relocating to richer vents, or converging on a birthing-pool, or simply running the city's nightly circuit the way some flightless birds elsewhere walk a beach at the same hour every day, for reasons unrecorded.

And then the third register, the one that makes the place sound *populated* rather than merely infested: courtship and contest, conducted entirely in sound, because nothing here can show anything to anything else. Long low moans pitched to ride the stone for half a mile. Sharp clicking trills traded back and forth between rival males perched on opposite towers, each one a boast pitched in frequencies that make the bridge-cables hum sympathetically. Mothers calling broods through the dark by a particular stuttering pattern unique to each clutch, audible over the general roar the way a name is audible over a crowd.

They breed in the wax-pools at the base of the towers, where the warm mineral runoff collects and the young — blind, finned, more like something aquatic than what they'll become — spend their first months filtering the water for whatever organic trace the city's own metabolism sheds into it. Nobody has determined what, precisely, the city sheds. The old joke among the few who study this place is that the thrum-kin don't eat what the city makes — they eat what the city *is*, in trace amounts, the way a barnacle eats the sea, and that this makes them less wildlife living on the architecture and more something closer to its gut flora, scaled up past all reasonable expectation.

There is one more thing worth saying, told less often because fewer have seen it and lived to describe it well. Deep in the structural seams, where the faint internal glow is strongest, something larger than any thrum-kin sometimes moves — pale, slow, jointed in ways that suggest it spends its whole existence half-fused with the growing stone, tending it, or being it. Those who've glimpsed it call it a tender, or a root, or — older and stranger — an *engineer*, on no evidence but the coincidence of name. It does not call. It does not surge. It moves at the speed the towers themselves move, a few inches a season, and where it has passed, the half-finished basalt has finished a little more, taken a cleaner edge, grown a fraction nearer to whatever shape this whole plateau has been, for generations, slowly and wetly, trying to become.
