# Those Who Hold the Spire

## The Vrethyn

The colony has a name among those who climb to it, though the colony itself has no need of one: they are the **vrethyn**, and they have owned this rock since long before anyone owned a word for them.

They are not birds in any way that would satisfy a naturalist looking for clean categories — wingspan close to the length of a tall man's outstretched arms, membranous rather than feathered, a hide-like wing-skin stretched taut over long finger-bones, colored in the same mottled ochre and bone-grey as the rock they roost on, so that a folded vrethyn pressed into a ledge becomes, for all purposes, a fold in the stone itself. Only the eyes give them away — too large for the skull that holds them, a deep wet black that catches and throws back even starlight, and it is these eyes, hundreds of them, thousands, blinking and shifting across the dark face of the spire, that you mistake at first for some impossible field of low stars.

Their bodies are built for cold and for waiting. A short, dense undercoat of fine hollow hair traps warmth against skin that would otherwise bleed heat into the thin air; their blood runs warmer than it should for something that perches motionless for hours at a stretch, conserving itself, watching, before dropping off the ledge into a stoop that takes it down into the cloud-sea after the swarming things that rise from whatever lies below. They hunt in the dark as easily as in light — better, if anything, since their eyes were made for it and the insects they take are drawn upward by warmth and confused by the cold. A vrethyn returning from a hunt makes a particular falling shriek, a call distinct from every other sound on the rock, and the whole colony seems to register it without appearing to listen, the way you register a clock ticking in a room you've lived in your whole life.

They are social in the way that makes a place like this work at all: hierarchical by ledge-height, the eldest and most dominant breeding pairs holding the high, sheltered niches near the summit where the wind is broken and the cold least vicious, younger or unproven pairs forced onto exposed lower shelves where eggs are more likely to chill, to roll, to be taken. This is not cruelty so much as the only kind of order a cliff can enforce. A vrethyn that loses its high ledge in a contest of noise and claw and bluffing wing-spread does not die for it, usually — it simply moves down, and its eggs, that season, have a harder life.

Their eggs are laid not in nests but in *basins* — the same shallow stone cups that catch rainwater are sought out, cleaned, defended, the egg cradled directly against rock that the parent's body has spent hours warming. A clutch is small, one or two, and the young hatch nearly featherless, blind-pink, utterly dependent, fed on regurgitated insect-paste for the first weeks of a life that will, if it survives, last decades. The mortality is brutal and the colony does not mourn it; an egg that rolls from its basin in a storm is simply gone, and within the hour something smaller than a vrethyn has already found it.

Their calling never stops because the colony's business never stops — courtship, challenge, alarm, the simple contact-noise of a creature confirming to its neighbors that it is still there, still itself, still holding its ledge. Layered over thousands of individuals at every register from sub-bass thrum to a shriek that splits the ear, it becomes something closer to weather than to sound, and you stop, after a while, trying to parse it into meaning.

## The Undertenants

Below the vrethyn's ledges, in the cracks and crannies too narrow or too low for the great wings to bother defending, live the **scuttle-things** — no one has bothered giving them a finer name, because no one who climbs here thinks of them as anything but vermin, however essential. Small, low-slung, the size of a cupped hand, built close and flat to slip into crevices a finger's width across, their hide mottled to match lichen rather than rock, shifting in color over a season the way lichen itself shifts with damp and dry. They are not parasites on the vrethyn so much as inheritors — they live on what falls: shed down, broken eggshell, the chitinous husks of insects that didn't survive the cold climb up from the cloud, occasionally a hatchling that didn't survive its own fall. They are quick, nervous, mostly nocturnal though the distinction barely matters at this height where night is simply the dimmer half of a permanent dusk. Their eyes, too, throw back light, but smaller, dimmer, lower — a second, lesser constellation strung along the base ledges beneath the great drifting field of vrethyn gaze above.

They breed fast and die young and exist, as far as anyone can tell, purely to make sure nothing on the spire goes to waste.

## The Swarm-Dead

There is a third population here, transient and unwilled — the insects that rise out of the cloud-sea each evening in the warm updraft, climbing toward the only solid thing for miles, and arrive at a cold that kills most of them within the hour. They are the bottom rung and the engine both: food for the vrethyn on the wing, food for the scuttle-things once they've fallen, their dying a nightly tithe paid by the lower world to the things that live above it. No one who climbs here thinks of them as residents. But nothing here would hold together without their dying.

## The Hookfolk

The people are fewer, and they do not live here, not in any sense that would satisfy a settlement — but they belong to the spire in a way that goes beyond visiting. They call themselves, in their own tongue, something that translates roughly to **hookfolk**, after the fire-hardened tools they carry, and the right to climb a given pitch of rope or notch-ladder is inherited, family to family, the way other people inherit land or debt.

They come in the harvest windows — narrow seasonal pockets when the fledglings have grown enough to leave eggs unguarded but not so much that the basins are empty for the year — and they climb at dusk or before dawn, when the colony is loud but settled, because a hookfolk climber who disturbs a roosting wing-mass mid-flight does not get a second mistake. Their gear is spare: plaited rope, hide harness, woven baskets slung low and tight against the back so a shifted weight doesn't pull a climber backward off a ledge, hooks for reaching into basins too narrow for a hand. They wear vrethyn leather, cured supple, and feather-down stitched into linings, because nothing else holds heat as well at this height, and there is a particular pride taken in a garment passed down with the rope-rights, worn by a grandparent on this same rock.

They have rules that read, to an outsider, like superstition, and function, in practice, like the only law that has ever mattered here: which ledges may be taken from and which are left untouched, generation after generation, because a grandmother once noticed that a stripped ledge bred no replacement pair the following year. They do not write this down. They sing it — a small body of climbing-songs, call-and-answer between the lead climber and whoever holds the rope below, that double as both work-rhythm and inherited memory, encoding which pitch is safe, which ledge is sacred, which months the eggs are sweet and which months they're better left.

They take eggs, feathers, occasionally — rarely, ceremonially — a vrethyn itself, taken old and dying rather than young and whole. They leave fire-scars on one sheltered ledge, the same ledge their parents used, their grandparents, because a fire lit anywhere else disturbs the colony in ways a fire lit in the *familiar* place does not. The vrethyn, it seems, have learned the hookfolk's smoke the way the hookfolk have learned the vrethyn's calls. Neither trusts the other, exactly. But each has learned, across centuries of returning to the same tooth of rock, the shape of the other's hunger — and learned, more importantly, where its edges are.
