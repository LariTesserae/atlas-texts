**Inhabitants of the Raw Range**

The herds are the first thing you understand, because they're built so plainly for what the land demands. Thick-bodied, low-slung, legs disproportionately strong relative to their size — built less for speed than for traction, for standing four-square on a slope that might shift under them at any moment. Their hooves are split nearly to the pastern, two independently-flexing toes per foot that splay wide on scree and grip like fingers on bare rock. Watch one long enough and you'll see it test each footfall before committing weight, a strange deliberate hesitation, as though the animal has learned — generations deep — that the ground itself cannot be trusted. Their coats are double-layered, an oiled outer guard-hair shedding the omnipresent damp, and beneath it a dense undercoat the color of wet stone, which is of course the point: at any distance greater than fifty meters they simply disappear into the slope, dark smudges becoming background becoming nothing at all. They have no antlers, no horns, nothing for sexual display or combat that would unbalance them on uncertain footing — what passes for status among them is settled by shoving matches conducted from a stable four-point stance, almost wrestling, almost dancing. They eat continuously, mouths working sideways across lichen-crust and cushion-plant with a thoroughness that looks less like grazing than like harvesting, stripping a patch to nothing before moving on, because nothing here grows back quickly and they know it.

The birds above are something else — built for the thermals the broken terrain kicks up unpredictably, wings long and slightly cupped, set far back on a body that's mostly lung and keel-bone. They don't flap so much as ride, correcting in small jerks, reading columns of rising air that exist for a few seconds and then collapse and reform somewhere else along the cliff face. Up close — and you rarely get close, they nest on ledges no human foot will reach — their feet are oversized, almost grotesquely so, hooked talons built for a single committed strike against creatures that don't stand still on slopes that don't stand still either. Their cries are flattened, distorted by the cold thin air into something almost mechanical themselves, a high stuttering note that carries impossibly far and seems, at first hearing, to come from the mountain rather than the sky. They are patient in a way that looks almost geological: they will sit motionless on an outcrop for hours, feathers slicked with the same suspended mist that beads on your sleeves, waiting for something below to make one wrong decision about its footing.

The small things that dart between rocks — you never get a clean look, which is the design. Pale-furred, low, fast over short distances and then gone into a crevice too narrow to seem possible. They live in the gaps between the herd and the birds: too small to graze competitively in open ground, too quick to be worth a bird's committed dive most of the time, surviving in the margins of margins. Their burrows thread the scree at a level just below where the ticking stones come finally to rest, and there's a theory — unprovable, but persistent among anyone who spends real time here — that they can feel a slope's instability before it moves, that they abandon burrows hours before a scree field shifts for reasons no one has measured. Call it adaptation. Call it something else.

But the thing underneath the mountain is the one that doesn't resolve, and won't, no matter how long you sit with it.

You won't see it. No one has, not directly, not in a way that survives translation into plain description. What you have instead is the pulse — that deep mechanical heartbeat rolling up through rock too regular to be wind, too patient to be machinery anyone built. The people who've wintered longest in these valleys don't call it a sound at all. They call it a digestion. Something vast, they say, folded into the unfinished roots of the range, vast enough that its breathing alone is enough to keep the scree restless and the rock-faces weeping their thin black water, vast enough that an entire mountain is, from its perspective, simply a shell it hasn't finished forming around itself. Whether it is one creature or the memory of one, whether it is dying or being born along with everything else here, whether the herds and birds and burrowing things above are parasites on it, descendants of it, or simply tenants who've never noticed the landlord — nobody can say with any confidence, and the few who claim to have gone looking for the source come back, if they come back, with nothing but the same flat description: that the pulse got no louder and no closer no matter how far they walked toward it, and that at some point they understood, with a cold that had nothing to do with the air, that they had been walking *across* it the entire time.
