What lives here does not live the way that word usually means. There is no breath to find, no pulse to listen for, no warmth that isn't simply the mountain's own fever. But something is here, in the same sense that a fault line is *here* before it ever slips — present, patient, indistinguishable from the stone until it moves.

**The Mason**

Call it that for lack of anything better, though it was never a person and builds nothing now. It is the reason the wall has three courses and not two, the reason the archway faces nothing — because when it was raised, there was something to face, and that something is gone now, ground down to magma and memory by however many cycles of burial and birth this land has gone through.

It is shaped, when it has a shape at all, like a suggestion of uprightness — a vertical density in the dark, no more detailed than a standing stone, except that standing stones don't turn to track you, and this does. It is the size of a door, then the size of a silo, then gone, depending on whether it wants to be measured. It does not walk so much as *arrive*, the way the peaks arrive: not stepping across distance but simply being closer than it was, the intervening ground having apparently decided to cooperate.

It moves on the same clock as the mountains — which is to say, mostly not at all, for so long that you could mistake it for outcrop, and then all at once, in a lurch that matches the bass groan of the rock settling, as though its joints (if it has joints) are the same joints the range is finding for itself. When a ridge shoulders up through an older one, the Mason is sometimes the dark hump riding the new stone up out of the ground, not falling, not climbing — *included* in the upheaval, the way a fossil is included in the rock that holds it, except this fossil is still moving.

It does not seem to notice heat. It crosses the slag pools without melting them further, leaves no print, and if it ever pauses at the low wall or the lone archway, it does so the way you might pause at your own handwriting found in a house you don't remember living in — not affection, not grief, something colder and more careful than either. A checking. A count. As if it is keeping an inventory of what has resurfaced and what has not, and the mountains are simply the slow turning of pages it is waiting on.

It has never been heard to make a sound that wasn't already the mountain's sound — the groan, the bell-ring of splitting stone, the dry whisper of settling dust — and this is the most unsettling thing about it: not that it is alien to this violence, but that it is *fluent* in it, as though the upheaval is a language it spoke long before the range existed to speak it back.

No one has seen two of them at once. No one has seen it do anything resembling labor, despite the name it's been given. It does not build. It seems, instead, to be *waiting for the building to be uncovered again** — the older shape the ground is straining to remember — and until that happens, it does the only other thing available to something with geological patience: it endures the noise, drifts between the rare quiet marks that are its own, and watches the dark crack open with orange light, far below, gone again before anyone can be sure they saw it, or it.

Whether it is the last of something, or the first of something arriving early — whether the mountains are rising *to* it or *because of* it — is not a question this place answers. It only confirms that the answer, whatever it is, is being assembled very slowly, in the dark, under your feet, one collapsing ridge at a time.
