# What Tends the Sluice Fields

You will not see it arrive. You will register, instead, that something has already happened—a valve that was stuck is loose, a channel that ran east now runs north, a pipe-joint that wept for a week has stopped weeping and started somewhere else twenty meters off. The basin keeps a ledger of small corrections, and something writes in it.

## Inference Before Sighting

Most visitors leave with a theory and no evidence. The theory usually involves the gauges. The pressure reading that means nothing, the conveyor running empty, the timer nobody set on the sluice-gate—surely, people think, something is *tending* this, however badly, however absently. They're right, though not in the way they imagine. There is no engineer. There is a population, small and old and shy, that has spent longer in this basin than the pumps have, and longer than the iron has been rusting, and the pumps and the iron were built into a world that already had it living there, so that the works and the inhabitant have grown into each other the way a vine grows into a fence: neither one quite hosting the other anymore.

## The Body

Call it eel-bodied, though that undersells the legs—four of them, vestigial, more useful for gripping pipe-throats than for walking, folded close to a trunk that's the grey-green of standing water under cloud. The skin isn't skin so much as a wet hide grown faintly granular in patches, almost rivet-textured, as if it had spent generations learning to look like the thing it lives among, the better not to be looked at. Where flesh meets metal—and it often does, the creature pressing itself flush along a warm pipe-run, half-submerged in a pump's shadow—the line between organism and infrastructure blurs in a way that isn't camouflage exactly. It's closer to fluency. This thing speaks the basin's two languages, water and iron, and has stopped needing to translate between them.

Its head is small, almost an afterthought, eyes reduced to dark beads set wide and low, useless past arm's length—this is not a creature that hunts by sight, or much hunts at all. What it has instead, along its flank and throat, is a fine fringe of barbels or feelers, constantly testing, that read temperature gradients and pressure differentials the way a face reads expression. It can feel a leak from across the basin. It can feel a structure two degrees off true before the structure itself seems to know.

## Behavior

It doesn't repair things out of any care for the machinery, any more than a wasp cares about the paper it chews. It repairs—or disrupts, the distinction means little to it—because pressure differentials are food, in a sense, or at least are *interesting* to a body built entirely around sensing them. A weeping joint draws it the way warmth draws a moth. It noses into the leak, packs it with a paste of mud and its own secretions, not for the basin's benefit but because the small violence of escaping water-pressure against its barbels is satisfying in some animal way that doesn't require justification. Then the leak stops, and somewhere else, because water finds every way through that's offered to it, a new one starts.

Likewise it visits the gates. It can wedge itself into the gears of a sluice mechanism, the gate's swollen wood it has chewed soft over years for exactly this purpose, and lever the whole apparatus into motion or stillness depending on some calculus of vibration and patience known only to itself. This is, most likely, the timer nobody set.

It does not build. It does not understand the pump-housings as pumps. But it lives inside the *logic* of the place so thoroughly that its small, constant, purposeless interventions look, from outside, exactly like maintenance. The basin's slow war between erosion and engineering has, without anyone intending it, acquired a third party that occasionally fights for one side and occasionally the other, switching allegiance at random, for reasons that have nothing to do with either.

## Encounter

If you do see it—and most who spend real time here eventually do, at the edge of vision, at dusk, at the lip of a pool that was dry an hour ago—it will be retreating. A length of grey-green hide sliding off a warm pipe and into water with that single heavy plop you'd taken, until now, for an amphibian. It does not flee from threat so much as from attention; being watched seems to register, on whatever scale it has for such things, as a kind of pressure it would rather equalize by leaving.

It is, as far as anyone has determined, solitary, or at most loosely social—two or three sharing a basin without evident coordination, their territories defined by gradient-maps invisible to anyone without the barbels to read them. Nothing is known of how it breeds, or how long it lives, though the structures' rate of change against its own apparent agelessness suggests *long*—long enough to have outlasted at least one full collapse-and-rebuild of whatever operation first laid these pipes, long enough that the pipes may have been laid, in some sense, around it rather than the reverse.

## The Rule It Adds

So: a final rule for the place, alongside the others. If a structure that should have failed by now hasn't, or a leak that should be widening has instead, mysteriously, narrowed—don't credit the engineering, and don't credit chance. Something is down in the water, reading pressure with its throat, and it has, for its own unknowable and temporary reasons, decided to hold that one thing together a little longer.

It will not hold it together forever. It isn't tending you, and it isn't tending the basin either. It's only ever following the gradient that interests it most, and the gradient, like everything else here, never stops moving.
