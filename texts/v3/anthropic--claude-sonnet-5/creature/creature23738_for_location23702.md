# Those Who Live Beneath the Chord

## The Body, Shaped

You would know a Maren-Tel native anywhere, even silent, even out of context, by the eyes. They are too active — flicking, reading, tracking mouths and hands and the angle of a shoulder, because eyes here do the work that ears do elsewhere. A child raised under the chord learns within a year that sound is wallpaper, omnipresent and unspecific, and that *meaning* travels by other channels. So the face becomes expressive in ways visitors find almost theatrical: eyebrows that telegraph entire clauses, a particular pursing of the lips that means *wait*, a flat stare that means *no*, more grammar in a glance than most languages put in a sentence.

Hands are calloused not from labor alone but from a lifetime of sign — a second, parallel tongue, fluent before speech, sometimes preferred to it even among people who can hear perfectly well. Conversations in Maren-Tel often happen in a kind of duet: lips shaping words half-swallowed by the drone, hands underscoring and correcting and finishing what the mouth couldn't carry. Outsiders watching two locals talk sometimes think they're watching an argument, or a dance, or a prayer. It is usually just dinner plans.

They are lean, most of them — the diet here is leaf, root, what little fiber-fed protein the rare lizard or harvested insect provides, and water is precious enough that no one grows fat on it. Skin tends toward a particular weathered tan-brown, brick-dust ground into the pores of generations, never quite washed clean because there's nothing to wash it with but a cupped handful of hoarded condensation. Hair is kept short, practical, sometimes shaved entirely at the temples so that nothing interferes with the press of palm to skull — because there is a specific Maren-Tel gesture, instinctive by age five, of laying a hand flat against one's own head or another's, *feeling* for the chord through bone when the ears have stopped being enough. They listen with their skeletons. It shows in posture: a faint, perpetual tilt, as though everyone is forever leaning very slightly toward a sound only they can place.

## The Tuners

Among them, status runs not by wealth — there is little to hoard — but by ear. Or rather, by something beyond ear, since true silence-deafness sets in early for many under the relentless drone; the prized sense is closer to resonance-memory, the ability to recall and recognize the *shape* of the chord as it should be, and to feel, in the gut, when it has drifted.

These are the tuners, and they are the closest thing the town has to priests, doctors, and engineers rolled into one weathered, soft-spoken caste. They climb the inside of brick towers half their lives, palms reading the wood-grain for swelling or crack, adjusting the angle of an aperture by degrees a stranger could never perceive, coaxing a flat tower back into key the way another culture might set a bone. Their hands are scarred from leaf-fiber and brick-grit, their forearms strangely strong from decades of leaning into walls that talk back. They are listened to — watched, rather — with a reverence edged in fear, because a tuner who says a tower is dying is rarely wrong, and a dying tower means a silence-pocket, and a silence-pocket means whatever moves through it unheard.

Old tuners go strange in a particular, recognizable way: they stop speaking with their mouths at all, communicating entirely in sign and touch, claiming — or perhaps only allowing others to believe — that they've started hearing the chord from *inside* it now, that words spoken aloud only get in the way. Whether this is mysticism or just very advanced profound deafness is a distinction nobody in Maren-Tel bothers drawing. The old tuners are venerated regardless, fed first, housed in the highest, most resonant rooms, where the air practically sings them to sleep.

## The Gardeners, Who Are Also the Tuners, Who Are Also Everyone

There's no clean line between the trades. The vine that climbs the post is also load-bearing; the wall that resonates is also rooted; the gardener who prunes a frond to the correct serration-angle is, in the same motion, adjusting the tone of that frond's chorus against its neighbors. Children are set to this work early — small fingers being good for fine pruning — and the first thing every Maren-Tel child learns, before letters, before formal sign, is the difference between a leaf-edge cut *for* the chord and one cut carelessly, against it. A badly pruned plant doesn't just look wrong. It sounds wrong, a faint sourness in the wider harmony that everyone feels in the molars before they could say why.

This produces a population that is, almost without exception, *attentive* in a way visitors find unnerving — not nervous, just saturated with awareness of a thing they cannot point to. They notice you noticing the sound. They find it strange that you find it strange.

## Children, and the Game of Silence

Children here are taught fear correctly, which is to say quietly, through games rather than warnings. The local game — played by every generation, rules barely changed — involves a blindfolded child standing at the center of a courtyard while the others, one by one, try to move through the surrounding leaf-cover without disturbing it: without producing a silence-pocket, that telltale gap in the chord. The blindfolded child must point, unerringly, toward wherever the drone has thinned. Win enough rounds and you're considered ready, at twelve or thirteen, to take a turn on night-watch, sitting at the rim-facing edge of town with your palm against the outermost wall, feeling for trouble that the leaves haven't caught yet.

It is, ostensibly, training for danger. It is also, more quietly, training for love of the place — because by the time a child is good at this game, they have spent hundreds of hours with their whole body pressed into careful, total attention to the sound that keeps them alive, and that kind of attention, sustained long enough, becomes indistinguishable from devotion.

## The Old, and the Edge

The aged of Maren-Tel are rarely far from the rim — not because they're put out to pasture, but because something in long exposure to the chord seems to pull a person, gently, gravitationally, toward wanting to be near where it's thinnest. They sit at high windows in the outer towers, the ones with more empty rooms than full, listening to the gradient of sound fall away toward the mineral plain, never quite testing the edge, never quite stepping past it. Ask one why, and the answer — given in sign, usually, because by that age many prefer it — amounts to: *you start to want to know what's out there, and you start to be glad you don't.*

There is a story, told to children as caution and to adults as a kind of grief, of a woman two generations back who walked out past the rim during the seasonal true-silence, when the chord stops for the span of a few breaths and everyone else stands frozen, waiting. She did not wait. No one followed quickly enough to see where she went or if the chord returned before or after she crossed the line. The story doesn't have an ending, which is, the old tuners say, the most frightening kind of ending there is. It's why hands still shake at the thought of true quiet — not abstractly, but specifically, with her face attached.

## What They Are, Altogether

They are a sound-literate people in a near-silent body — fluent in face and palm and bone-felt vibration, half-deaf to anything outside their own chord and wholly attuned to everything within it. They garden as worship, tune as medicine, sign as mother tongue, and watch each other's mouths the way other cultures watch each other's eyes. They are thin, sun-cured, quietly intent, generous with food and stingy with water, reverent toward their elders and their walls in the same breath because increasingly the two have become hard to tell apart. They do not think of themselves as remarkable. They think of themselves as *in tune*, which is, from inside the bowl, the only thing worth being.
