## The Tenders

They call themselves Hollowers, mostly, though the older crews have other names depending on which district raised them — Furnace-born, Grate-walkers, Stack-children — names that function more like dialect than tribe, since everyone ends up working everyone else's machines eventually. There is no surface population to speak of anymore, not really; the fissure is a door, not a border, and most who live here were born several hundred meters under it, in barracks built into the cooling jackets of decommissioned foundries, where the residual warmth makes the walls bearable to sleep against.

You'd know a Hollower by the listening posture before anything else — a slight tilt of the head, habitual, unconscious, the body's way of triaging sound in a place where sound lies about where it comes from. They learn young to read the grinding-clang for direction and distance, the way sailors read swell. A child here can tell you, without thinking about it, whether that scraping cry from the gantries is close or just loud, whether the hum's wobble means the western turbines or just bad weather in the fog-banks. Outsiders find this unsettling — the way a whole population seems to be perpetually mid-listen, mid-flinch, never quite present in conversation because some fraction of them is always monitoring the building's pulse.

## Bodies Made for the Underworks

Generations of low light have done what generations of low light always do: pale skin, wide pupils, a squint that only deepens in true brightness, which is rare enough that work-light flares are met with reflexive hand-shade, an instinctive gesture passed down like a tic. The cold the engineers can't push back has selected for thick subcutaneous fat and a hardiness toward damp that surface stock apparently lacked — Hollowers run warm, sweat readily even in the chill, a trait useful for shedding the work-heat of the foundries but uncomfortable everywhere else, which is part of why so few of them ever leave for good even when they can.

Hands are the tell of trade and rank. Foundry crews have hands scarred bright pink-white from slag-burn; tank-farm workers keep theirs gloved permanently, even sleeping, because whatever's in the black liquid does something to skin that doesn't heal clean; gantry-riggers — the ones who go up into the heights after the nesting things, or just to grease joints no one's reached in years — have hands rope-strong and missing fingers in a pattern common enough that there's a hand-sign vocabulary built specifically around the gaps.

Hearing damage is universal and unremarked. Most Hollowers past forty have lost some range, usually the high end, which makes the scraping gantry-cries inaudible to elders and a constant low dread to children, who hear it long before anyone believes them.

## Trades and Tending

Society here organizes by sector-loyalty more than family, though the two overlap heavily since work passes parent to child by default — you tend what your mother tended, you live in the warren nearest your post, you marry someone from two sectors over because your own sector's all cousins by now. The foundries have their crews, cold and idle as the stacks are, kept on a strange permanent readiness no one questions; the active works further in draw the young and ambitious, where the noise is worst and the pay — paid in chits redeemable for the tank-farm's processed protein-paste, for warm billet-space, for the rare surface goods that filter down — is best. The tank-farms themselves are tended by a smaller, quieter guild who don't talk about what they do and whose gloves never come off in company.

Then there are the Readers — not a large group, never more than a few dozen across the whole Hollow, who specialize in nothing functional at all. Their work is the symbols: the stamped plate-signage that looks like instruction and resolves into prayer. They walk the older sections cataloguing variants, arguing about translation, maintaining a tradition of interpretation that has produced, over the unknown span of the Hollow's life, several competing scripture-like compilations, none of which agree, all of which are treated with a respect bordering on superstition by people who otherwise have no time for anything that doesn't keep the lights on. When the hum changes pitch, it's the Readers people quietly watch — not because the Readers know what it means, but because somebody should be looking like they might.

## What They Believe, Without Saying So

Nobody worships, exactly. But there is a working theology, unspoken, embedded in habit: that the Hollow is owed maintenance the way a body is owed food, that the machines you don't understand are not yours to fix and shouldn't be entered, that the cold is the place's true nature and the warmth borrowed, on loan, revocable. Children are taught the avoided sectors by name before they're taught to read. There's a phrase — *it isn't finished asking* — used about the excavation in general, about specific tunnels that keep growing past where last week's maps put their end, said with the same shrug surface people might use for weather. Nobody built a religion around the builders because nobody believes the builders are gone. They believe, in the diffuse unexamined way people believe things they'd be embarrassed to say aloud, that whoever dug the fissure and widened it is still down here somewhere, still working, and that the Hollow's whole population is essentially squatting productively in the margins of someone else's enormous, ongoing, incomprehensible job.

## The Lichen-Keepers

A small subset, often elderly, often those whose hearing or hands have given out for harder trades, tend the bracket-lichen colonies — not as agriculture exactly, since the growths are barely edible, but as a kind of folk-husbandry, encouraging the colonies into the better niches, thinning them when they threaten to crowd machinery, harvesting what's harvestable for the bitter tea it makes. These keepers are the closest thing the Hollow has to naturalists, and they're the ones who notice first when a sector's chemistry shifts — a runoff event, a new flood, a die-back — long before the engineers reading gauges catch the same change. Their knowledge is oral, passed in walked routes rather than written record, a slow inherited map of every good niche in a hundred kilometers of pipe and grating.

## The Ones Who Go Quiet

And there are always a few — every generation produces them — who start listening too well. Who spend too long near the avoided sectors, who start hearing pattern in the hum's pitch-changes that they can't explain to anyone else, who eventually stop talking about it because the listening posture has become permanent, total, and they've effectively gone elsewhere while their bodies stayed on the grating. The Hollow has a quiet, practical compassion for these people — they're fed, housed, not pressed to work — alongside a quiet, practical wariness, because every so often one of them walks into a sector that isn't on anyone's map and doesn't come back, and a week later the noise from that direction changes, just slightly, just long enough for everyone to notice, before settling back into the old familiar wrongness everyone has learned, by necessity, to call ordinary.
