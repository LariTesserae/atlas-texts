# Inhabitants of Vire Cassel

You will see them before you understand that you have seen them — which is to say, you will register motion, then absence, then the suspicion that you imagined it. This is not because they are hiding. It's that they don't move through the basin the way visiting eyes expect a person to move through a place: with destination, with urgency, with the friction of a stranger negotiating unfamiliar ground. They move like the second hand of a slow clock — present at every moment, perceptible at none of them.

## The Residents

Call them residents because nothing better presents itself. They are humanlike in proportion and not insistently more than that — two legs, a torso, a head, the architecture of a body you'd recognize on sight, but worn loosely, as if the shape were a garment chosen for convenience rather than a fixed condition. Their skin holds the dim light the way the buildings do, absorbing rather than reflecting, so that in the basin's perpetual late-afternoon grey they read as silhouettes with detail rather than as separately lit things. Up close — and you will rarely get close — there's a faint geometric patterning beneath the surface of them, not unlike the too-regular veining of the broad-leafed plants, as though something in the air here insists on the same lattice logic whether it's organizing chlorophyll or whatever organizes a person.

They dress, if dress is the word, in materials that seem to negotiate with the ambient temperature rather than insulate against it — draped, segmented, occasionally luminous along a seam in time with the same slow rotation that lights the towers. You get the sense that what they're wearing is less clothing than interface: a second skin tuned to whatever frequency the city itself operates on.

Their faces are the part that resists description longest. Not alien in the dramatic sense — eyes, a nose, a mouth, all present and where you'd expect them — but composed in a way that doesn't perform for an audience. No one here has practiced an expression for someone else's benefit in a very long time, maybe ever. When they do turn that composed attention toward you, it has the quality of the buildings' silence: not absence, but a held breath. Something is being calculated, or remembered, or simply witnessed, and you are not made privy to which.

## How They Move

They walk the way the paths assemble — which is to say, the ground decides to cooperate with them a half-second before it needs to, and you start to suspect the order of causation runs the other way from what you assumed. It isn't that the walkway resolves and then they step onto it. It's that they are already stepping, with the unhurried confidence of someone who has never once been wrong about where solid footing will be, and the material simply arrives in time to agree with them.

This confidence is the single most consistent thing about them. They don't hurry, because nothing here has ever required them to. They don't check their surroundings, because the surroundings appear to check on them instead — towers dimming or brightening half a beat after someone passes beneath, vines easing aside at ankle height without any visible mechanism, rain arriving in curtains that fall everywhere except where someone happens to be standing. Whether this is the city serving them or the two of them being, in some unclear sense, the same organism wearing two different grammars — flesh in one register, structure in the other — is not a question they seem to have any urgency about answering. It may not be a question to them at all.

## What Else Moves

The smaller things — glimpsed at leaf-edge, gone before resolving — are not animals in the simple sense, though they might once have been, or might yet become so. Soft-bodied, low to the ground, the size of a closed hand, they seem to graze at the interface between plant and structure: tending the fern-coral groves, perhaps, or feeding on whatever the buildings exhale in that soft mechanical sigh. They startle like prey animals but show no evidence of predators. Possibly they are gardeners. Possibly they are the city's own attention, distributed into something that can fit into a leaf-cluster and look out at you with whatever passes here for curiosity.

The long-legged shapes at dusk are something else again — taller, more deliberate, crossing the clearings on a schedule that seems tied to the bioluminescent rotation rather than to any need of their own. Residents speak of them, if residents can be said to speak at all in a way you'd recognize, with the same unbothered familiarity you'd use for weather. They are not feared. They are not explained. They are simply one more thing the basin does at a particular hour, like the rain, like the glow moving quarter to quarter, like gravity loosening its grip at shin height in certain clearings for reasons no one has bothered to make legible to a visitor.

## The Question of Population

It is tempting to call this place underpopulated, a city built for a density of life that never arrived or has since departed. But spend enough time in the held quiet and a different reading starts to surface: that the scattering is not absence but distribution — that what looks like a half-empty city is in fact a city operating at exactly the density it requires, because most of what a city needs to do here doesn't route through individual bodies at all. The towers tend themselves. The paths assemble themselves. The rain arrives where it's needed and withholds itself elsewhere with what almost reads as discretion. The residents, in this light, aren't the city's population so much as its remaining gesture toward company — present because presence is still, for now, one of the things this place has decided to keep doing, the way it has decided to keep growing leaves with veins like circuit-traces, the way it has decided to keep humming below the threshold of hearing.

They are new the way the land is new. Nothing about them has finished settling into what it will become. You get no sense, watching one cross a clearing in the grey late-afternoon light that never resolves into evening, that you are looking at a finished thing — only at a moment in a long, slow negotiation between flesh and ground and structure, still deciding, the way the bedrock itself is still deciding, what shape it will ultimately consent to hold.
