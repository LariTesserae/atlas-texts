# Those Who Listen Under

They call themselves, in the old tongue, something that translates badly — *the small loud-quiet*, or sometimes *the ones the trees haven't noticed yet*. Outsiders who bother to learn any of the canopy-tongues just say **Hollow-kin**, after the way every lineage names itself for whatever cavity in whatever trunk it has occupied for generations. There is no larger name for the people as a whole, because there has never been a reason to need one. Nobody here has ever stood far back enough to see themselves as a population. You only ever see your own hollow, your own root-bridge, your own stretch of attentive dark.

## The Body Made for This

A Hollow-kin adult is built narrow — not starved-thin, but compact, low-shouldered, with long flat feet that have lost most of their arch over generations of root-bridge walking, so they grip rather than step. Children are taught to walk on the outsides of their feet before they're taught to run, because the outside edge reads the give of old wood better than the heel does, and old wood lies about its strength. The skin tends dark and oiled with a self-secreted resin-sweat that isn't unlike the bark's own moisture-hoarding trick — nobody is certain anymore whether this is something the people brought with them generations back or something the forest has quietly bred into them, the way it seems to breed everything toward water-thrift eventually. Hair is kept close-cropped or bound tight in oiled cord; loose hair catches on thorn-vine and worse, catches the attention of things that nest in hair.

The hands are the most marked feature. Every Hollow-kin past the age of six has calluses across the first two knuckles from the sign-language — not a spoken pidgin but a true hand-grammar, fast and small, built to communicate at arm's length over a roar that swallows voices whole. Children are mute-trained before they are walking-trained. To speak aloud in the deep canopy, except in the specific safe hours, is considered something between bad manners and an actual hazard, and a Hollow-kin who talks too freely is teased, gently, as *thunder-mouth* — someone who hasn't yet learned that sound is a kind of food the forest eats.

## The Hollow and the Hold

A hold is rarely more than twenty or thirty people: an old hollow, a scatter of platforms wedged into the surrounding root-forks, a granary on stilts holding stored bark-starch and dried fungus-meat. Descent runs through the mother's line, because the hollow itself is inherited that way — a daughter takes the hollow when her mother can no longer climb to it, and her brothers and male cousins marry outward into other holds, carrying their labor and their knowledge of trail-signs to wherever they're needed. This produces, over centuries, a slow churn of men across the whole plateau and a strange intimacy between holds that might be three days' walk apart but share grandmothers.

Children are raised collectively and loosely until about seven, when the listening-training starts in earnest: hours spent sitting still in a clearing with an elder, learning to sort the layered noise into its components — *this clicking is the bark-grazers, that low throb is something using the Eastward Bole as a drum and is none of your business, that silence spreading from the left is the only sound that should ever make you move first and ask questions later.* A child who can name eleven distinct calls and explain which ones mean "harmless," which mean "leave," and which mean "we don't know, and that not-knowing is the dangerous part" is considered ready to walk trail alone.

## What They Believe, Which Is Mostly True

Hollow-kin cosmology doesn't separate the forest into "alive" and "aware" the way an outsider's might. The forest is not worshipped, exactly — there are no shrines to it, because building a shrine implies the forest is somewhere else, an audience to be addressed, rather than the medium you are already standing inside, like fish do not build shrines to water. But there is a working theology, practical rather than devotional, organized around the idea that *attention is a resource and so is being unattended*. The deep canopy notices in patches and pulses, never all at once, and a wise person's whole skill is in reading which patch of attention is currently slack and moving through it.

This produces a moral language oddly close to etiquette. Felling a living trunk isn't a sin in the sense of cosmic punishment — it's simply rude, the kind of rude that gets you and everyone near you noticed, and noticing, on Halvarrun, is rarely survivable for long. The pale, lichen-antlered standing-beasts that hold still in clearings for days are treated with the specific deference owed to something that is clearly *waiting on a larger attention than its own* — Hollow-kin give them wide berth and lowered hand-signs, the gesture equivalent of a hushed voice, on the theory that whatever eventually moves the beast is worth not being near when it does.

Death, when it comes, is handled with the same unsentimental practicality. A body is wrapped in frond-cloth and given to a specific known crevice in the home trunk's underside, the same one each generation, fed gradually into the rot that becomes the floor that becomes the home of whatever comes after. Nobody describes this as the deceased becoming one with the forest, because nobody here believes they were ever anything but already part of it. It is simply where the body goes, the way water goes downward and smoke goes up.

## The Quiet Negotiation

What makes the Hollow-kin distinctive, if anyone ever surveyed the whole plateau and tried to characterize them, is this: they have built no defenses, because the place cannot be defended against, only read correctly. Their whole culture is a transmitted, embodied literacy — a grammar of feet, hands, silences, and patient waiting, passed mother to daughter through the hollow, brother to cousin through marriage, elder to child through hours of sitting still and sorting noise. They are not masters of Halvarrun. They have simply, over a very long time, become very good at being a kind of life the forest's vast attention doesn't think it's worth turning all the way toward — small, quiet, easily missed, and for that reason, almost always still there.
