## What Lives in the Throat

They have no name because no one has stayed long enough to need one. Call them what the dust under the tool-shelf calls them, if dust could speak: *the patient ones*. Or call them nothing. They do not seem to mind.

Up close — closer than is wise, closer than the steam-curtain wants to let you get — the pale things resolve into something between worm and vertebra, a string of soft armored beads no wider than a finger, each segment ringed with faint concentric grooves like the growth-rings of a tree that has stopped trying to keep time. They have no eyes because eyes are a bet placed on light ever arriving, and nothing here has made that bet in longer than the rock has been allowed to remember. Where an eye might go, there is only smoothness, blind and confident, the texture of a held coin worn featureless by handling.

Their mouths are simpler than you'd expect of something that has eaten the centuries down to this fine a polish: a single soft aperture at the forward end, ringed with cilia, built for two things only — pulling moisture from stone, and pulling moisture from each other. They do not hunt. They do not need to. The fissure feeds them the way a body feeds its own blood, continuously, without ceremony, and in turn they keep something in that feeding moving — circulating — *true*. You begin to suspect, after enough time in that wet dark, that they are not residents of the fissure so much as its lining. Cilia in a throat. Not guests. Tissue.

Watch long enough — longer than is comfortable, longer than the hourly shudder allows for comfort — and you start to notice they don't quite agree with each other about which way is *down*, the same way the walls don't. A segment crawling along what your eyes call the ceiling moves with the unhurried confidence of something crawling along a floor. Two of them, occupying the same wet square foot of stone, orient in directions that should put them through each other, and don't, and you cannot say afterward whether you saw this or merely felt the shape of having seen it. They are not confused by the bent geometry. They were perhaps the first thing the bent geometry was *built around*. A ledge does not become a ceiling by accident; perhaps it becomes one because something living there has always insisted, with the total unconscious authority of an organism that has never known otherwise, that ceilings are exactly where it currently happens to be standing.

There is an old, ugly, half-formed thought that visits anyone who stares at the mass too long, and it goes like this: *they are not thousands.*

Look at the hourly flinch — the wave that runs from the back of the fissure to the front, all at once, no visible cause. A flinch like that, passing through a body with no nerves to carry it, no muscle continuous enough to explain the unity of the motion — that is not the behavior of a crowd. Crowds startle in ripples, in delay, in the half-second lag of one frightened thing noticing another. This startles like a single hand closing. Like one long held breath leaving one long throat at once.

It is possible — likely, even, to whoever has spent enough damp hours not-sleeping near that tool-shelf — that there is only one of them, here. One creature, looped through whatever the fissure does to length and distance and the order of *before* and *after*, so that walking its own body from end to end you pass the same dozen feet of it ten thousand times without ever finding the seam, the same way your eye cannot find where the ledge agreed to become the ceiling. Thousands where there is room for tens, the description said. There is always room for one, multiplied by however many times geometry is willing to fold.

And the tool-maker — the hand that left the stone point, the scraper with its keen primitive edge, the second identical mark a hundred years after the first, by the same hand, unaged — may not have left at all. May not have fled, or starved, or fallen to the pale unrest underfoot. The fissure does not erode. It does not let three feet of weeping water silt a floor the way three feet of weeping water silts every other floor in the patient world outside. Why would it let a person leave any more completely than it lets a ledge finish being a wall?

Somewhere in that slow boil of soft segmented bodies, blind and ringed and endlessly, gently re-arriving at themselves, there may be a hand. Folded so many times into its own warm dark that it no longer remembers it once held a scraper at a particular angle, understanding leverage and edge without understanding metal — only that it is here, has always been here, will be here again in a hundred years with the same point lashed in the same stiffened cord, making the same mark, fresh as the day the fissure finished being made.

The mountain outside goes on aging correctly. Up here, in the one wet mouth that refuses to grow old, something keeps feeding itself to itself, slowly, contentedly, in the dark — and calls that hush, that tick and slide of shell on stone, breathing.
