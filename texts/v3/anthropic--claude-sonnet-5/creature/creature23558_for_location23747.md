## What Lives in the Reef

It would be easy to say nothing does — and for years at a stretch, that would be nearly true. But there is one thing, and it is enough to explain the bone-fragments, the smoothed hand-holds, the patient, decades-spaced visits of whoever camped briefly and left.

Call it, for lack of any surviving name, the **sil-strider** — though "strider" overstates its pace, and "sil" is only the word the vanished people pressed onto the place itself, borrowed back for want of better.

It is not large. Fully grown, nose to tail-tip, it would span perhaps the length of an outstretched arm, though almost no one alive has measured one, because almost no one alive has seen one move. Its body is built low and long, ribs flexible enough to let it pour itself sideways through the same shoulder-width gaps a person must turn and squeeze through with held breath. Its skin is not skin so much as a kind of dense, granular hide, pale grey-white, faintly translucent at the thin places along its flanks the way the spires themselves are translucent at their edges — an old convergence, the same light-logic shaping stone and creature both. Seen still against a spire-face, even at a few paces, it does not look like an animal. It looks like a vein of mineral that has, impossibly, a shadow.

It does not eat, in any way a lowland creature would recognize. There is nothing here to eat. Instead it licks — slowly, at long intervals, sometimes only twice in a season — at the thin films of condensation that gather on shadowed stone in early light, and at the rare mineral seep where water has found a channel down through the rock and left behind a crust of salts. Its whole metabolism is built around scarcity: a heartbeat so slow that a held hand against its flank might not feel it at all; a body temperature that drifts down near the temperature of the stone it rests on, so that it loses almost nothing to the cold and gains almost nothing from the sun, content to simply *not lose*, for months, while it waits.

On its skull, two long, branching growths rise and curl back along its spine when collapsed flat for travel through narrow channels, then lift and spread when it is still, antler-like, porous, shed every several years and left behind in some crevice no wider than a hand. These are, very plainly, the source of the "bone-like fragments" found shaped to points near the old camp ledge — not bone at all, but discarded sil-strider horn, picked up by people who needed a point and an edge and found one already lying in the dust, pre-grown, pre-shed, asking only to be snapped at the haft and put to use. Whoever camped here did not hunt the creature. There would have been little point, and less need. They simply gathered what it left behind, the way one gathers driftwood, and moved on.

It moves mostly at the edges of the day, when the light comes low and raking through the gaps and the worst of the heat or cold has not yet set in, threading the maze of spires by a route that seems memorized rather than searched — and in a sense it is memorized, generation over generation, the same paths worn fractionally smoother by the same pressure of a body passing again and again over centuries, the reef's only true inhabitant tracing its only true paths through stone that otherwise belongs to nobody.

It makes almost no sound. What little it does make is lost entirely under the wind's layered moan and whistle, which may be why no one camping here ever wrote of hearing it, only of finding, now and then, a horn-shed in a crevice, or a faint groove in dust that hadn't been there the season before.

It is not rare because it is dying. It is rare because the reef can support, by its own arithmetic of cold and light and almost-absent water, very nearly nothing — and the sil-strider is the answer the place arrived at: a creature reduced to exactly the size and patience and need that Karak-Sil can sustain, and not one degree more.
