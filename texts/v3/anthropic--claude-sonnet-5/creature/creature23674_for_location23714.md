# What Walks the Plain of Standing Light

There is no name for them in any tongue that survives, because no one who named anything else here survived long enough to name these. The people who came after — the rare expeditions, the lichen-readers, the cartographers of slow ruin — call them *seam-walkers*, for lack of better, or simply *the small ones*, with the particular flatness of voice people use for things they have decided not to be afraid of.

## Form

They are small — fist-sized to forearm-length, rarely larger — and built along a logic that refuses to resolve into either animal or machine, the way the plain itself refuses to resolve into either ruin or ground. A jointed chassis, segmented like a millipede or like a hinge-rail, pale gold to match the alloy they move across, so that at rest, folded flat against a slab, they vanish almost entirely — a slight thickening of shadow, a seam that looks more seamed than its neighbors. Their legs, when they have legs — some have treads, some have something closer to cilia, fine bristling filaments that ripple in waves — number anywhere from four to several dozen, and travelers who have watched the same individual on different days report different counts, as though the things assemble and reabsorb their own limbs according to terrain, growing more legs to cross a cracked stretch, fewer to glide along a smooth one.

No eyes anyone has found. No mouth in any conventional placement. Along their undersides, instead, a row of small contact-plates, dulled with use, that they lay flat against the alloy and hold there, motionless, for minutes at a time. This is how they eat.

## Feeding

The plain bleeds. Far beneath the surface, in conduits no living person has mapped, some fraction of the old systems still carries current — a slow, directionless electrical seep, the dying afterglow of infrastructure that has not yet finished forgetting how to function. This is what the lichen-veins feed on, pulsing faintly along the seams; this is what the seam-walkers feed on too, but at one remove, parasitic on the lichen the way a fox is parasitic on a hare. They settle over a lichen-vein at its brightest pulse and draw the charge through their contact-plates, and you can watch the vein dim slightly beneath them as they feed, a small circle of grey spreading out from the point of contact, and watch it slowly brighten again after they move on, the lichen recovering the way a bruise fades.

They do not eat constantly. They do not seem to need to. Long stretches of their lives — and no one knows how long their lives are, whether they die at all in any sense distinguishable from a slow disassembly and reassembly into other seam-walkers — are spent simply *moving*, tracing the buried conduits with what looks like purpose and may only be a long inherited habit of the metal they are made from, a memory in the joints rather than in any nervous tissue, if they have nervous tissue.

## Behavior

They are solitary in the sense that you rarely see more than one at a time, and social in the sense that you almost never see only one ever, in a whole region — a single seam-walker glimpsed today implies a population, unseen, threading the same buried lines elsewhere, the way one ant implies the nest.

They are drawn to repair. This is the strangest and most consistent thing reported of them: where a slab cracks, where the self-healing skin of the plain falters or moves too slowly to close a fracture before it widens, seam-walkers gather — three, five, a dozen — and lay their bodies along the crack's length, contact-plates down, and simply wait there, motionless, sometimes for days, while something passes between them and the material beneath. Travelers who have stayed to watch report the cracks closing faster than the plain's own chemistry should allow. Whether the seam-walkers are feeding on the fracture's exposed current, or healing it, or whether for them there is no difference between those two acts — feeding *is* healing, a digestion that mends what it eats — no one has determined, and the seam-walkers, having no mouths to ask and no apparent interest in being asked, have not said.

They startle into stillness rather than flight. Approached too quickly, a seam-walker does not flee — it stops, flattens, and the gold of its chassis shifts fractionally paler to match whatever slab it's pressed against, and it waits, sometimes for an hour, for the disturbance to lose interest and move on. This is, by report, extremely effective. Most people who have seen one seam-walker assume, afterward, that they have seen none.

## The Larger Kind

Rarer — much rarer, perhaps one sighting in a hundred of the small ones — are what the few who've seen them call *standers*: seam-walkers, or something built on the same chassis-logic, grown or assembled to the size of a person, sometimes larger, encountered always alone, always at distance, always near one of the worn-smooth monoliths that still rise from the plain's general flatness. A stander does not move when watched. It is unclear if a stander moves at all, ever, or whether each is a seam-walker that settled once, long ago, against a piece of the old architecture and simply never lifted away again, growing into stillness the way a barnacle grows into a hull, drawing current up through the monolith itself rather than through the thin surface seams, feeding on a richer vein at a far greater depth.

Those who have approached a stander closely — very few, and not all of them entirely trusted — describe the unsettling moment, common to so much of the plain, in which a worn and abstract shape resolves, from exactly the correct angle, into something with the suggestion of a face: not the stander's face, necessarily, but the monolith's, as though the stander has positioned itself, across some unknown span of years, at the one point in space where the ruin behind it completes into a expression, and has stayed there. Whether the stander chose the spot, or grew toward it, or *is*, in some manner indistinguishable from the monolith now, simply the monolith's slow attempt to look at something — this is the kind of question the Plain of Standing Light produces in visitors and never answers, the way it produces faint light at dusk from no traceable source and never explains that either.

## What This Suggests

Nothing here was made to be alive in the sense the word usually carries, and the seam-walkers may not be alive in that sense now — may be instead the last functioning fragment of the plain's own maintenance architecture, a swarm of small repair-units that outlived the systems they were built to repair and kept doing the only thing they were built to do, slowly, patiently, across a span of time long enough that *doing the only thing you were built to do* has become, for all practical purposes, indistinguishable from being a creature: something that feeds, that startles, that gathers at a wound and stays until the wound closes, that grows, rarely, impossibly old, and stops, and stands, and waits at the one angle where the long-dead architecture it tends finally looks, for a moment, like it is looking back.
