The People Who Walk Between the Tides of Sound

They are called, in the one fragment of their own language that survives translation, something closer to *the ones who count the quiet* — though the quiet they count is relative, a matter of degree rather than kind, since true silence is not a thing they have ever known or perhaps could survive knowing. They are small people, compact and broad-footed, built low to the ground the way everything successful here is built low to the ground. Generations of walking on wet stone have given them feet that read texture rather than sight — broad-soled, calloused into something almost like hoof, with toes that splay and grip the polished rock through films of standing water. They do not walk so much as *negotiate* the ground, each step a small renegotiation with a surface that might be slick, might be cracked, might give way into a flooded hollow with something moving beneath it.

Their skin has gone the color of the haze — a grey-gold undertone, not pale so much as *unsaturated*, as if pigment were another resource too costly to spend in a place with no sun to protect against and no foliage to hide within. They keep their hair short or bind it tight; anything loose collects the damp and the cold and, worse, anything loose can catch on the chitin-thorns that drift up at the edges of the bone-drifts. Their eyes are notably large, round, adapted not for the dimness — the light here is, after all, ample if shadowless — but for *distance-judging* in a terrain that famously refuses to give them clean lines. They squint less than they tilt; you'll see them angling their heads, cross-referencing two eyes and a tilted horizon to decide whether that swell is forty paces off or four hundred.

But it is their ears, or what has happened to their ears, that marks them most. They have lived inside a permanent roar for longer than their oral histories reach back, and the roar has shaped them as surely as the water shaped the stone. Their hearing operates on a kind of triage — a learned, almost unconscious filtering that lets the great continuous drone fall away into background the way a fish stops noticing water, while reserving total, instant attention for the *break* in the pattern: a stutter in the click-trains, a missing throb, the specific overtone that means a stalking thing has gone quiet because it is close. Outsiders, dropped into the basin, are deafened and disoriented within hours, unable to parse threat from texture in the wall of noise. The People hear a *story* in it. They can tell, from across a swell, whether the swarm two hollows over is feeding, fleeing, or breeding, the same way a sailor reads weather in a sky that looks, to the unpracticed eye, uniformly grey.

They speak rarely, and when they do, in a register pitched to cut under the drone rather than over it — low, percussive, consonant-heavy, more felt through the chest and the stone underfoot than heard in any ordinary sense. A great deal of their actual communication is not vocal at all. They have developed a tactile and visual sign-language for use at close range, hands flashing precise small gestures, supplemented by a system of taps against rock or against each other's bodies that travels through the wet stone itself — a kind of percussion borrowed, consciously or not, from the very creatures whose noise drowns them. There's a particular irony the People themselves seem to appreciate: they have learned to talk in the language of the thing that makes it impossible to talk.

Their material culture is exactly as spare as the land allows and exactly as clever as survival demands. No metal, no fired clay — there is no fuel here for fire, nothing dry enough to burn for more than a sputtering minute, and so their toolkit is built from leverage, knot, and patience, as the cairns and frames suggest. Cordage is twisted from some unseen fibrous plant-analog harvested at the basin's distant, unglimpsed margins, or from the tough ligament-cords of the larger swarm-predators, dried and worked supple. Tools are hafted bone — the chitinous remains of the creatures, which hold an edge surprisingly well once shaped, and which they shape with stone-on-stone patience over days. Their shelters, those low collapsed frames, are not abandoned so much as *folded* — built to be raised quickly at dusk when the drone swells toward its worst, and struck again at the next lull, because nothing here rewards permanence. They are people who have made an art and a virtue of leaving no trace heavier than a cairn.

The cairns themselves are the closest thing they have to monument, archive, and scripture. Each is built by hand, flat stones gathered and stacked with a particular grammar of arrangement that encodes information for those who know how to read it: safe path, dangerous hollow, recent spawning ground, a death. They are not large — knee-high to shoulder-high, no taller than a person needs to stand on, because standing on a cairn is itself a practice: a way to lift your ears and eyes above the worst of the throbbing bass that pools low in the flooded crevices, a way to get one merciful half-step closer to seeing the shape of the basin rather than only hearing it. The People say, in the fragments that translate, that the cairns are *the bones of those who chose where to stop*. Whether this means literal burial or simple memorial is something even their own elders argue about, in low percussive disagreement, taps against stone.

They do not live *in* the basin so much as live *through* it, in slow seasonal circuits that track the tidal swell and recession of the noise itself, moving toward the duller roar, camping at its threshold, never quite at its center, never quite at its edge. This is not cowardice but ecology: the mid-tier predators that stalk the shallows on stilting legs are dangerous, but predictable, readable, avoidable by someone who has spent a lifetime learning the grammar of the drone. The vast shadow beneath the standing water is none of those things. The People have a word for it that translates roughly as *the listening one*, and a firm, absolute, generationally-enforced practice of never, under any circumstance, entering water deep enough to hide it. Their cairns near such pools are denser, more numerous, more emphatic — less path-marker here, more warning, more plea.

What they make of the unison-flicker — the basin-wide simultaneous flare of luminous seams that comes with no trigger and fades with no cause — is perhaps the truest measure of who they are. They do not flee it. They do not worship it, exactly, in any structured sense a missionary would recognize. They *stop* for it. Wherever they are, whatever they're doing, when the light passes through the swarm like a held breath being released, the People go briefly motionless and silent — not frightened silence, but a kind of attendance, a watching. Their oral fragments call it *the basin thinking*. They have never tried to interpret what it thinks. They have only, across however many generations it took to develop the habit, learned to notice when it does — which is itself a kind of theology: not to know the place, only to keep faithfully, attentively, quietly company with its strangeness, in the brief intervals the noise allows for quiet at all.
