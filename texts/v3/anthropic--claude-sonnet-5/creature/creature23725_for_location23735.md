They are called, when they're called anything, the Tenders — though that word implies a tending toward something, and what the Lowsteam's inhabitants do resists that kind of sentence. You don't see them at first. You see what they leave: a handprint's smudge of clean stone amid the mineral weeping, a valve wheel turned to a position no rust pattern would explain, a fern frond bent back from a catwalk joint and propped, almost tenderly, against the rail instead of trampled. The basin is full of small corrections like this, the way a sickroom is full of evidence of someone who was just here.

They are pale — not sickly pale, but the pale of something that has never needed to argue with the sun, skin the color of the condensation-haze itself, faintly translucent at the joints so that in the sodium light you can sometimes see the suggestion of works beneath: not bone exactly, more like the dark wet seams of pipe-work the basin itself is made of, echoed inward. They are built low and broad, knuckling along catwalks on four points of contact as often as two, comfortable in any orientation because the Lowsteam offers no ground that isn't also a ceiling to something else. Their eyes, when you find them — and you have to look for the wet glint of them, set deep under a fold of brow-skin slick as a leaf — are unhurried, the way a held breath is unhurried.

They don't run the machines. This is important, and it took the place itself a long time to make you understand it: the machines do not require running, only company. The Tenders move through the workshops the way you might walk through a room where someone is sleeping — quietly, attentively, adjusting a blanket without waking the sleeper. They turn a wheel because the wheel asked, in the small ways that worn metal asks, to be turned. They wipe a gauge because it had begun, in its slow glass way, to stop being legible to itself. There is no throughput because the machines were never about throughput; they are about continuance, and the Tenders are continuance's nervous system, diffuse, slow-twitched, distributed through the basin like nerve endings through a numb limb that hasn't yet decided whether to wake.

This is also, you eventually realize, where the wingbeats come from — not wings exactly, but the particular percussion the Tenders make when several of them cross a catwalk grate at once, a many-footed flutter against metal that resolves overhead into something with the rhythm, if not the shape, of flight. They gather like this in the rafters of the largest structure for reasons that don't translate to want or need — something closer to the way condensation gathers on the underside of a roof, finding the coldest available surface and pooling there because pooling is simply what the conditions permit. When you climb to look you find only damp stone, a smear of handprints, the ferns up there thicker and greener than anywhere else in the basin, fed by a closeness of breath you can't otherwise account for.

They do not eat, not in any way you'd recognize as eating; the broad waxy leaves that come up through the gutter-seams seem to feed them by some slow osmotic arrangement, skin to leaf, the way the leaves themselves feed off the basin's perpetual sweat. This is why nothing here hunts and nothing is hunted — the whole loop closes upstream of appetite. The Tenders are not predators because they were never given a reason to become any; the missing verb the place seems to lack is simply not missing for them. They have replaced it with another one, quieter, harder to name in any language built around want. Upkeep, maybe. Or company.

They are, as far as anyone passing through can tell, neither many nor few — the basin's sound-pooling acoustics make it impossible to count footsteps against echo, and they themselves don't seem to keep track, moving as parts of a single slow distributed attention rather than as individuals with separate errands. You might pass the same one three times in an afternoon, or three different ones who happen to tilt their heads at you with the same unhurried wet glint, and never know the difference, and they would not consider the question interesting enough to settle.

What they want from a visitor, if want is even available to them, seems to be only this: that you not interrupt the maintenance. They will let you walk the catwalks, let you stand in the heavy green light and listen to the engines that owe their continuance to no one in particular. They will glance at you the way they glance at a loose belt or an irregular valve-shriek — as one more thing the basin produces, to be noted, tolerated, and, if it doesn't settle into its place on its own, gently corrected. If you stay long enough, you may even feel yourself starting to itch with the same impulse — to straighten a railing, wipe a gauge, prop a bent frond back against its rail — the basin's hum settling into your hands before you've decided to let it. This, more than any sighting, is how you'll know they're real: not by seeing them clearly, but by noticing what your own hands have quietly begun to want to do.
