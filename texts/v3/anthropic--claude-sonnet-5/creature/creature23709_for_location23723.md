The people of the warren are built low and quick, and the first thing you notice — once your eyes adjust enough to notice anything beyond the lamp-haze — is the hands. Everyone's hands. Broad-palmed, thick at the knuckle, the skin of the fingers and the inner palm gone smooth and faintly pale in patches, like the handholds they grip a thousand times a day, because skin here is shaped by stone the way stone is shaped by skin: a slow mutual polishing. Salt-crust work leaves a particular mark, a fine white dust worked into the creases of certain families' hands that no washing fully lifts, and you learn to read lineage and trade in those creases before you learn to read it in faces.

The faces themselves are pale in a way that isn't sickness, isn't pallor exactly — it's underexposure, generations deep, skin that has simply never been asked to answer to a sun. It takes on the lamp-gold easily, sits well in that light, which may be no accident; what doesn't sit well is brightness itself. Children who scramble up near the fissure-mouth squint and turn back well before reaching it, not from rule but from instinct, the way you'd flinch from a too-loud noise. Eyes here run large, dark-pupiled, with a faint pale ring at the iris's edge in a noticeable minority, a trait some families claim and others don't, though no one can say it matters for anything except who you're more likely to marry.

Bodies are compact, broad through the shoulder, deceptively strong — built for ladders, for the narrow press between stacked dwellings, for the fissure itself, which everyone passes through sideways without thinking, the way you'd duck a low doorway. Backs bend early. There is a particular stoop, not quite a deformity, more a posture inherited and then earned, that marks anyone past forty who has spent a life moving through spaces built for people slightly smaller than themselves, in recesses cut by ancestors slightly smaller still.

But the body adapts least, here, compared to the ear. Sound is the weather, and the people of the warren read it the way surface-dwellers read sky. A change in the water-percussion under the floor-planks — a clearing channel, a new trickle — will turn heads two chambers away before anyone's said a word about it. Mothers locate children by voice through three layers of stacked dwelling without apparent effort. The unlocatable singer the warren is known for is not, in fact, unlocatable to everyone — there are a few, mostly old, mostly women, who can always tell you exactly which niche it's coming from, and who never say, because not-saying is itself a kind of inherited courtesy, a way of letting the warren keep one mystery for the newly arrived to wonder at. Hearing this acute has its costs. There's a particular flatness of expression you'll see on faces deep in conversation in a crowded chamber — not rudeness, but the practiced internal filtering of nine other conversations happening in the same cubic meter of air, a skill nobody is taught and everyone has by the age of six.

Socially, the warren sorts itself not by wealth in any portable sense — there is little to hoard, less to display — but by where you go and what you're trusted to touch. There are those who tend lamps, refilling and re-wicking on a rota so constant it has the rhythm of breathing, and they move through the warren with a kind of unbothered ownership, ducking into any dwelling unasked, because the lamp's needs outrank the household's privacy and everyone has agreed to this since before anyone agreed to anything. There are the wall-readers, who know which grey-black faces sweat the right salts and when to scrape them, who speak of certain stretches of stone with the proprietary fondness others might reserve for livestock. There are the few — always few, always regarded with a mix of respect and unease — who go into the unseen dark where the fungal-mats are tended, and who come back smelling of something the lamps don't smell of, something colder; they keep to themselves between trips, not from any rule, but because the rest of the warren has learned, over generations, not to ask them what it's like out there, and a question never asked becomes a habit, and a habit becomes a kind of manners.

And there are the ones — rarest of all, spoken of almost reverently and yet never quite trusted — who go out, properly out, along the routes that bring lamp-fat in from the surface and take whatever the warren can spare in trade. Children are told stories about what's up there, and the stories disagree wildly with each other in a way that suggests nobody currently alive has actually checked. The traders themselves, on return, tend to go quiet for a day or two, blinking too much, flinching from lamps that have never bothered them before. Nobody asks them what they saw either. Three silences, stacked: the dark-goers, the surface-goers, and the singer's location. The warren runs, in part, on what it has agreed collectively not to say out loud.

Children are raised loose and early into usefulness — there's no room, literally, for a long uselessness — and their chief sport is catching the pale thumb-length crack-dwellers with quick fingers and eating them roasted off a skewer, a pastime indistinguishable, to them, from chores, and indistinguishable, to an outsider, from joy. They're warned off the wingless blind nesters in the high recesses not with fear exactly but with a specific etiquette — don't reach in, don't make noise near the nests, leave the offered scrap of fiber-mat at the ledge if you take an egg — the kind of small ritual courtesy you'd extend to someone who'd been living somewhere longer than you and showed no particular sign of leaving. Nobody remembers deciding the birds were owed this. It is simply, like the planking, like the ladders, like the lamps, the shape the warren has always had.

What you'd struggle to find, among these people, is anything resembling ambition in the surface sense — not from any lack of will, but because the warren offers nowhere for ambition to point. You inherit a niche, a craft, a stretch of wall or a rota of lamps, and you tend it, and your children will tend something adjacent, and the warren will be exactly this size and exactly this loud and exactly this dim in their lifetime as it has been in every lifetime anyone's grandmother ever mentioned. This isn't resignation. It doesn't read as resignation from the inside — it reads, if you spend long enough among them, as something closer to competence: a whole people who have made an art of being completely, unhurriedly equal to the only world they have, who quarrel and barter and sing and raise children and scrape salt and tend lamps with the unselfconscious thoroughness of people who have never needed a horizon, because the warren, eleven meters down, dim as dusk and loud as a market square, has never once in any memory asked them to imagine one.
