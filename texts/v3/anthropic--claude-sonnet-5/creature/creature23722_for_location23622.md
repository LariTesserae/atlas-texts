# Who Tends It

## First Impression

You would expect them shouting, and they are not. That is the first wrong note. A place this loud should breed loud people, but the workers of the Cistern Works move through the noise like people moving through weather—heads down, shoulders set, mouths shut, communicating in a vocabulary of hand and glance that outsiders mistake for sullenness. It isn't sullenness. It's economy. Down here a wasted word is a wasted breath, and breath is for the lungs, which work harder than they should in air this warm and wet and metal-tasting.

They are not a uniform people, but the place has shaped them toward a type, the way a riverbed shapes the stones that pass through it long enough.

## The Body of the Place

Skin first: a particular pallor, not sickly so much as *cured*, like something kept from the sun long enough that the sun becomes irrelevant to it. Where skin meets steam day after day it goes soft and faintly swollen at the fingertips, the way skin does after too long in a bath, except here it never fully un-swells. Old hands down here have a permanently pruned, waterlogged look, padded and pale at the pads, hard as horn at the calluses where rope and chain and valve-wheel have worn their own geography into the palm.

Hearing goes early. Not all the way—nobody down here is fully deaf—but the fine edge of it dulls fast, the way a knife dulls against something it's used on constantly, and by middle age most workers carry a permanent ringing they've stopped mentioning because everyone has it and there is nothing to be done. Children are taught to read mouths and shoulders before they're taught to read anything written, because written language is a luxury of people who can hear each other well enough to need it less.

There's a stoop common to the long-tenured ones, not from age exactly but from a life spent ducking gantries, leaning into wind-shafts of steam, bending to shout into ears at the only volume that works—close, low, almost intimate, mouth nearly against the lobe. It gives the Cistern folk an odd tenderness in their body language, a culture of forced closeness, faces and bodies pressed near out of pure acoustic necessity, that reads to outsiders as either great warmth or great menace depending on who's doing the looking.

## Trades, Which Are Castes, Which Are Families

Nobody down here just "works." You are what your hands do, and what your hands do is usually what your parents' hands did, the trades running in families the way eye color does, because apprenticeship starts as soon as a child is strong enough to grip a wheel-spoke and young enough not to flinch at the heat.

**The Chain-Haulers** work the big pulleys, hauling the bucket-line up out of the black water hand over hand or wheel over wheel, depending on what's broken that week. They are built thick through the shoulder and back, bowlegged from a lifetime braced against deck-plate, and they have a reputation—earned, mostly—for a kind of grim humor, joking in grunts and shoves about what comes up the chain, because if you don't joke about it you start thinking about it, and thinking about it is bad for the work.

**The Furnace-Feeders** are leaner, quicker, and burned—every one of them carries old scar-glaze along some stretch of forearm or cheek where a flare caught them young and taught them respect. They tend to be talkative in the brief lulls between feedings, as if making up all at once for the silence the furnace-mouth demands of them the rest of the time. Children love them. They tell the best stories, mostly because they're the ones who've seen the furnace flare strange and lived to describe it.

**The Valve-Tenders** are a quieter, older trade, often the old chain-haulers and feeders aged into something more careful. They walk the gantries with a particular gait—a kind of constant minor adjustment, weight shifting always, anticipating the next surge before it arrives. They are the closest thing the Works has to clergy. Not because they preach anything, but because they're the ones who *listen* to the pipes for trouble, ear pressed flat against warm iron, eyes closed, perfectly still in a place that is never still, and there is something in the posture that everyone recognizes as prayer even though no one would call it that to a Tender's face.

**The Scrapers** clean the grates and intake screens of biofilm and tangle-eel and whatever else the water deposits, and they are universally regarded, with real affection, as a little odd—too much time alone at the waterline, too much time looking down into water that looks back. Scrapers tend to talk to the water. Everyone pretends not to notice.

## Children

Are born up top, mostly, in the stacked housing blocks where the air is marginally cooler and the noise marginally less total, and are brought down to the catwalks early, strapped to a parent's back or chest, because there is nowhere else for them to be. A Cistern child's first clear memory is almost always the same shape, regardless of who tells it: warmth, dark, a huge percussive sound felt through someone else's ribs, and lantern-light sliding past like something underwater seen from above. They learn to swim before they learn to walk properly, out of sheer necessity, in water that is not meant to be swum in and that they swim in anyway, expertly, contemptuously, the way children treat any danger they were born inside of.

They are warned about the deep platforms the way children elsewhere are warned about traffic or fire—a flat, unmysterious caution, repeated so often it loses all its weight. *Don't go below the fourth grate. Don't lean out past the last lantern.* Most obey, not from fear exactly, but because the warning has the textureless authority of something that has simply always been said.

## What They Believe, Without Saying So

Ask a Cistern worker what the furnaces are fed and you'll get a real answer, delivered without hesitation, because everyone genuinely knows their own piece of it—the bucket-men know what comes up the chain, the feeders know what goes in the mouth, the Tenders know what the pipes are saying. What nobody has is the *whole* of it, and—this is the important part—nobody is looking for the whole of it. Curiosity past your own trade is considered, mildly, bad manners. Not forbidden. Just impolite, the way it's impolite to ask a man his income. There is a tact down here built entirely around not assembling the picture.

This is not stupidity. It is closer to a kind of communal discipline, passed down the same way the trades are passed down: an inherited, unspoken agreement that the whole picture is not for holding, that the works have always run on partial knowledge distributed correctly among the right hands, and that this distribution is itself the safety mechanism. A Tender who starts asking a Feeder what's really in the furnace is a Tender who's lost the thread of what their own ear-to-the-pipe work is actually for.

And on the matter of the breathing in the water—the rhythm under the surge that isn't quite tidal, that comes up through the hull-iron at the worst possible moments, in the worst possible lulls—they have no theology, no name, no shrine. What they have is a habit: when it comes, conversation doesn't stop, hands don't stop, the work does not so much as flicker, but every person within earshot will, without seeming to decide to, briefly rest a palm flat against the nearest iron surface. Not gripping it. Just touching it, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal's flank to confirm, quietly, privately, that it is still only sleeping.

Then the hand comes away, and the work goes on, because the work has always gone on, and there is chain yet to haul, link over link, up out of the dark and into the small orange light that is the only light there is.
