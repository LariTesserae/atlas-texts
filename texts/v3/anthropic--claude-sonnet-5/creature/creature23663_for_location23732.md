# The Terrace-Tenders of Mirenastal

They are called, in the thick clipped consonants that carry best through warm water, something that translates roughly as *the Standing* — and the name is literal before it is metaphorical. Everything about their bodies argues against floating.

## Body

They are compact, low-built, heavier than water expects anything alive to be — bone-dense in a way that would crush a human ankle into a normal gait, but here only roots them. The skeleton runs thick through the long bones, riddled with a kind of internal ballast-marrow, mineral-packed, so that a Standing person sinks gently rather than bobbing, the way a stone sinks rather than the way driftwood does. Skin is grey-green, faintly ribbed along the shoulders and spine where colonies of the same leather-leafed kelp-analog that grows on the terraces take root directly in the flesh — not parasitic, not quite symbiotic either, more like a garden that happens to grow on a person instead of a slope. It sheds and renews itself slowly, the way moss sheds. No one finds this strange, because nothing here is dry enough to make bare skin meaningful.

The eyes are large, dark, set slightly forward, pupils permanently wide to drink in whatever the smoked-glass light overhead deigns to send down. Closed lids are rare — there is a nictitating inner membrane instead, near-transparent, that does the same work without ever quite shutting out the dim world. Hands are wide, four-fingered, the palms thick-calloused from adze-work and stone-fitting, the kind of hands built for gripping facing-stone against a slow current rather than for swimming.

They do not swim. This is the deepest fact about them, deeper than physiology. They walk, weighted at the ankle by fired-clay-and-stone soled sandals laced in fibrous cable, each step a small deliberate negotiation with buoyancy that wants to lift them and a will that refuses it. A Standing person who lost both sandals would drift up off the terrace like smoke — and this happens, rarely, to the very old or the careless, and is treated less as accident than as a kind of premature joining with whatever is or isn't above the grey ceiling of light. They do not speak of it as death exactly. They speak of it as *going early*.

## Voice

Sound is rationed here, the way fire is rationed by its absence. The Standing communicate close-up through a low, bone-carried humming — a vibration felt against the sternum more than heard, a private and intimate frequency reserved for kin, for terrace-mates, for the dying. For anything that must travel further — a warning, a summons, the marking of a season's turn — they use horns: hollowed shell, hollowed long-bone of the rare large pale fish, lashed and weighted to stay submerged and full-voiced. These horns are inherited, never made new if an old one survives; a horn is a lineage object, blown by only one mouth at a time across a generation, its low bloom recognizable terrace to terrace the way a name would be. To hear an unfamiliar horn-voice drift in from two ridges over is a small, rare event — answered, if answered at all, hours later, after due consideration of what reply is owed.

## Kinship and the Terraces

Property, if the word even applies, is vertical. A family does not hold a plot so much as a *flight* — a run of terraces handed down the slope from parent to child, each generation expected to extend the cutting one or two courses further down into the murk before they themselves go early. Children are shown, almost as a rite, the topmost worked stone of their flight: the place their own great-grandparent's hands stopped. They are told, without much ceremony, that they will not finish either. This is not delivered as tragedy. It is delivered the way weather is delivered — true, unremarkable, the shape the world has.

Marriage, kinship, inheritance — all of it is terrace-logic. To marry is to braid two flights together, root-cable literally lashed slope to slope. To be exiled (rare, reserved for those who damage a riser-face or let a flight's algae-tending lapse past saving) is to be cut loose from the cable and left, weighted and alone, on an unclaimed shoulder of rock with no flight to call a name from.

## What They Tend, and What Tends Them

Days — if the word means anything under a light that never brightens or fails — are spent in slow procession along the terrace-beds: gathering leaf-growth into woven creels with the unhurried, water-enforced economy that makes haste look almost obscene; resetting a slipped facing-stone with patient packed clay; clearing silt from between the fern-tree fronds so the rows stay regular, tended, clearly *meant*. They eat what the kelp-stands give, and occasionally the slow, thick-bodied pale fish, taken without ceremony, eaten without fire (this is the unspoken mystery even to themselves — *what is fire, what is the absence of it* — a question filed away with all the other things their hands know how to do without their minds knowing why).

The many-legged bottom-walkers that pick over the stone are neither hunted nor named. They are allowed to graze the algae off the riser-faces, which is useful, and so they are tolerated the way a held breath tolerates the diaphragm — present, working, beneath notice. Children play with them sometimes. No one stops this. No one encourages it either.

## Belief, or the Shape Patience Takes

There is a story — not agreed upon, told differently flight to flight — about the light overhead. Some hold it is the sun, reaching down through an impossibly deep and ancient sea. Some hold it is the sea, glimpsed from below, and that the Standing's ancestors are the ones who are *under*, while something else entirely walks dry and sunlit on a ceiling they will never reach. Both versions agree on the only point that seems to matter in practice: that the terraces should keep being cut downward and outward regardless of which story is true, because the work is not contingent on the destination. A flight finished would be strange — would in fact be slightly obscene, the way a held breath fully released is a kind of small death. The unfinished terrace at the bottom of every flight is not a failure of ambition. It is the point at which a person's portion of the patience runs out, and is handed, gently, weighted at the ankle, to the next pair of hands.
