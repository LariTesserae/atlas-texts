## What Built It

They were called — among the few who ever heard the name and lived to carry it elsewhere — the Ko-Vellan, though that may simply be the place's name worn backward onto its makers, the way a house sometimes lends its name to a family rather than the reverse. No one is certain anymore which came first.

What the walls tell you: they were small. Not childlike, not stunted — simply built close to the ground and close to each other in the way that creatures of cold, thin air tend to be, conserving warmth, conserving breath. The doorframes suggest shoulders no wider than a folded wing. The chest-height of the walls suggests they did not build to keep things *out* so much as to break the wind's worst edge before it reached the hearth — a wall as a held hand, not a held line.

And here is the thing the architecture insists on, over and over, in every spacing, every gap, every lone threshold with no neighbor close enough to borrow its fire: they did not live together. Not in the way that word usually means. The dwelling-clusters mirror the dwarf-tree groves outside the walls exactly — three, or seven, never more, spaced for privacy rather than defense. Whatever the Ko-Vellan were, they were solitary by temperament the way some animals are solitary: not lonely, not isolated, simply *built that way*, coming together for specific necessary things and otherwise keeping the wide stone between themselves and each other as a kind of courtesy.

They did not speak the way you and I mean speaking. Or — they may have, in close quarters, in whatever low private register suited a creature that size in air that thin. But across distance, across the shelf, they used the wind. The granary-pool building, the high whistle-structure fifty feet on, the hundred small ringing basins — these were not domestic accidents of acoustics. They were *built for it*. Each structure tuned, painstakingly, over generations, to hold a particular note when the prevailing gale passed through it. A Ko-Vellan standing alone at the eastern wall, a full ten minutes' walk from the nearest other living soul, could send a tone rolling across the shelf and be understood. *I am well. I am here. The fog is coming in.* Whole lives conducted at the distance a held breath can travel on a cold updraft, with the architecture doing the speaking so the throat never had to strain for it.

This is why the chord at dusk almost-resolves and then doesn't. It was never meant to be heard all at once. It was a hundred private sentences, overlapping, addressed to no single listener — and once in a long while, by the accident of wind direction, they briefly rhymed.

## Why It's Empty

Nothing violent took them. The valleys below still remember, in their oldest soil-layers, a slow downward seep of basalt-dwellers over centuries — not flight, just *drift*, the way a population thins when the high air gets thinner still and the lower air gets, by comparison, a little more forgiving. They didn't abandon the shelf so much as stop arguing with the wind's preference that they not be here. One generation walked down for a season. Their children walked down and didn't walk back up. The instrument kept tuning itself for an audience that simply, gradually, wasn't replaced.

## What's Left of Them

Almost nothing — and yet not quite nothing.

The thing in the basins, the parting of moss that closes again before you can call it a shape, is what's left of them in the oldest sense: a body that gave up most of what made it a person and kept only what made it *survive*. Small, low, soft-shouldered like the rock itself, it has gone soundless the way its ancestors went silent of speech generations before the last of them walked downhill — it doesn't sing, doesn't seem to hear the chord its forebears built an entire town to produce. It feeds on whatever the basins collect: drowned insects, wind-blown lichen-spore, the mineral skin that forms on standing water left too long. It is not sad. It is not anything so legible as sad. It is simply what is left when a culture built entirely on solitude finally achieves the most complete solitude available to a living thing — one without a name for itself, without a listener, without even the memory of wanting one.

The birds are not descendants. They're tenants — opportunists who moved into wall-gaps built for other bodies and found the wind-funnels, designed to carry a person's single low note across a whole plateau, equally good at holding a wing aloft for free. They did not inherit the Ko-Vellan's purpose. They inherited their leftover architecture, the way moss inherits a fallen wall, the way silt inherits a mountain.

And at dusk, when the tones almost-align — there is, if you wait long enough, in certain years, in certain weather, a stillness in the basin-creature's hollow that might be called listening, for the four or five seconds before the wind shifts and unbuilds the chord again. Whether anything is heard in there, in whatever's left of a mind that small, no one has stayed long enough up here, alone, in that thin air, to be sure.

That is the rule of the place, after all. It was never built for someone to stay and check.
