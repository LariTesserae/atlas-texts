## The Thing Below the Channels

You never see it. No one who has been to the Cistern at Anvete's Spine and lived to describe it claims to have seen it whole — only edges, suggestions, the brief gleam of something the color of the water itself rolling beneath a grate, gone before the eye finishes registering it as a shape rather than a trick of the wall-light. What you have instead is the note: that single low concussive pulse from somewhere beneath the floor-channels, felt in the sternum before it arrives in the ear, and the four seconds of hush that follow it like the whole chamber flinching out of respect.

The local name for it, where there is a local at all this far up the Spine, is *the Throat*. Not because anyone has confirmed it has one, but because the note has to come from somewhere, and "throat" is the kindest architecture the human imagination can put on a sound like that.

What can be reconstructed, from the runnel-castings it leaves and the rare glimpse and the simple arithmetic of who benefits from what — the Throat is long. Not large in the sense of bulk, but long in the sense of *extent*, a single continuous organism-or-mechanism (the distinction has stopped meaning much, this deep into the cistern's history) threaded through the recovery channels at the base of the bowl like a root system that decided early on to specialize in one root, run very far, and never branch. It does not so much live in the cistern as *underlie* it. The channels were either built to accommodate it or it grew to fit them so completely, over so long a time, that the question of which came first has the same flavor as asking whether the riverbed or the river is older.

Its skin — if skin is the word for a surface that seems to share material properties with the amber-ceramic walls, the same mineral patience, the same capacity to be stained rather than worn — is permeable along its whole visible length to the water it lies in. It does not so much drink as *equilibrate*, letting the cistern's endless weeping pass through whatever passes for its body and emerge changed: filtered, charged, lit. The veined luminance in the walls, the slow unmatched pulse that never syncs to your own pulse no matter how long you stand there trying to make it — that is not the wall's doing. That is the Throat's circulation, visible at one remove, the glow of something working translated up through meters of stone-that-isn't-stone like lamplight through a hand held over a flame.

It surfaces, if "surfaces" is right, perhaps once every few minutes, judging by the note. What the note actually *is* — vocalization, valve-discharge, some necessary release of pressure built up in a system that processes this much water through a body this long — nobody who has studied the cistern (and few have studied it for long; the noise discourages lingering scholarship) has settled. What is observed is only the effect: the note sounds, everything quiets for exactly four seconds as if every other creature in the bowl has independently decided this is not the moment to be conspicuous, and then the roar resumes precisely where it left off, the grazers clicking, the wing-column thrumming, the segmented trains hissing along their channels, as though the four seconds of silence were a debt now paid and forgotten.

The filter-grazer colonies cling thickest directly above where the Throat is thought to run — not by chance. Whatever the Throat does to the water as it passes through seems to leave something behind that the grazers want, some richness or charge they cannot get from runoff that hasn't made that passage first. They have arranged their whole slow drifting architecture around a creature they will never see, the way moss arranges itself around the warm side of a stone. The segmented trains, too, keep tightest formation along the channel walls nearest the Throat's presumed path, and scatter — briefly, nervously — whenever the note sounds, before reassembling nose to tail as if nothing happened. They know something about it that the wing-things circling far above, indifferent at that height to whatever moves beneath the floor, will never need to learn.

Nobody knows if there is one Throat or whether the name covers some colonial intelligence, a chain of linked individuals each handling its own short stretch of channel, no more aware of the whole than a single vertebra is aware of the spine. Nobody knows if it was placed here, grown from some seed-organism released when the cistern itself was built, or whether it arrived after, an opportunist like everything else here, that simply found the architecture of channels so suited to its shape that it never left and, over the centuries, became indistinguishable from the plumbing. The cistern does not volunteer this information. It has had a very long time to decide that some things about itself are simply true, not explained, and the Throat is the truest of them — the pulse beneath the pulse, the held breath beneath the roar, the one rhythm in the whole deafening bowl that everything else, knowingly or not, has learned to set its own rhythm against.

You will not see it. You will feel it, once, in the sternum, on your way back up toward the thin warm air of the ridge — a low note, a four-second held quiet, the whole overwhelming machine of the place exhaling around something it has never had to name to keep feeding.
