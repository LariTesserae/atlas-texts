It takes a long time to see her, and longer to be sure you have.

She is the color of the hollow — which is to say no color at all, but the suggestion of several: the grey-green of lichen, the wet black of stone, the pale fish-belly white of things that have not seen full sun in generations. What skin she has is mostly given over to moss, not in patches but as a kind of garment grown into the body itself, threaded through skin gone soft and porous as old bark, so that where she ends and the wall's fur of liverwort begins is a question with no clean answer. She is small — smaller than the space would let you expect, folded into a crevice between root and rock the way water finds the lowest seam — and still, so still that the eye slides past her three times before the mind admits that the unevenness in that shadow has a shape, and the shape has a face.

The face, when you find it, is the strangest part, because it has almost nothing left to do. The ears are sealed — not absent, but grown over, smoothed under skin the way the lake-stones are smoothed by patience, vestigial flaps under a film of the same pale moss that fur the rock veins. There was no use keeping them. A hollow that screams without pause at a single unbroken pitch teaches its inhabitants, in enough generations, that hearing is a wound, and she is what's left after that wound healed shut. What she has instead are eyes gone enormous and dark, twin wells adapted to the suggestion of light that comes down through the alder-and-yew ceiling, and a sensitivity along the skin — especially the long fingers, splayed wide and faintly webbed, and the bare soles of feet that never lift far from stone — that reads the world as vibration. She feels the fall before she would ever have heard it. She feels you, now, in the tremor your footstep sends through the shelf of moss-felted rock, long before you've noticed her noticing.

She does not startle. Startling costs more than this place affords. She unfolds slowly, with the unhurried geometry of something that has never needed to be fast, because nothing here has ever required speed of her — not weather, which doesn't change; not light, which doesn't either; not visitors, who are rare enough to be remembered individually across years. Her joints show the same mineral patience as the dripping vein in the rock: she moves in increments, decanting herself from the crevice the way the standing pools fill, a little at a time, with no impatience anywhere in it.

She tends the ring of flat stones. Not for any reason she could tell you, even if telling were a thing she still did — there's no one to talk to and the falling water would drown it if there were — but out of something older than reason, an inherited motion passed down the way moss passes down its way of growing on a particular vein. She straightens a stone when frost or root has nudged it. She has done this, or her mother did, or her mother's mother, for longer than the flint beside it has lain dulling in its root-crease, and the flint itself she touches sometimes, turns over in fingers gone soft and clever from decades of reading texture instead of sight, though what it once was for is a question that died with whoever knapped it.

She eats little. The hollow doesn't offer much — the occasional wren foolish enough to nest within reach, blind grubs under the fungus shelves, the slow soft mats of algae skimmed from the black standing pools with a patience that would unnerve anyone still operating on a hungrier clock. Mostly she seems to require less than a creature her size should: as if living this close to stone for this long has taught her body the same trick the rock has, the trick of needing almost nothing and getting almost nothing and calling that enough.

She will not follow you out. The light past the hollow's lip is not injury to her, exactly, but it is unfamiliar in a way that reads as wrongness — too thin, too even, lacking the particular quality of dark she's built her whole shape to answer. She'll watch you go, if watching is the word for what those eyes do, and then fold back into the crevice between root and stone, and within a few held breaths you will lose her again to the moss, and the dark, and the single endless roaring syllable of the fall, which has never once, in all the years she or her mothers have crouched here listening to it through their skin, said anything different.
