**The Masons**

There is one figure for every wall that builds itself and one figure for every wall that doesn't, and these may be the same figure, seen at two different speeds. Call them the masons because that is the work they're caught performing, though "caught" implies a single moment and they are not contained by single moments. You see them most often out of the very corner of vision, mid-task, and when you turn to look fully the task is finished, or not yet begun, or being performed by no one at all — the brick simply rising, the mortar simply smoothing — and the figure who a half-second ago was bent over it has receded into the heat-shimmer the way a held breath recedes into the next one.

They are the kind of thing a place builds for itself when it has been building too long without a builder. Not ghosts of dead masons — there were never any masons to die. Something more like a verb that has finally grown a body to wear, because pure unaccompanied action wore thin even for a place this patient. The figures are what the basin's industry looks like when it deigns, occasionally, to look like anything.

**Bodies**

Where they are most solid, they are made of the same stuff as the floor: fired clay in patches, black-glazed in others, never matching itself from one limb to the next, the way the basin's tiles never match at their joins. A shoulder will be kiln-pale and a forearm beside it will carry the deep wet-black sheen of glaze still curing, as though the figure assembled itself out of whatever tile-stock was nearest at the moment it needed a shoulder and a forearm and didn't trouble to match the lot. Seams run across them where courses meet — not scars, not even seams exactly, just the visible evidence of having been laid rather than grown, brick-thickness ridges crossing a cheek, a collarbone, the back of a hand. Where they are less solid they are simply wet outline, brick-red murk holding a man-shape the way the floor's shallow pools hold the shape of the bruise-grey sky, and these versions you can walk through, and where you walked through them the air tastes briefly of hot iron and rain.

Their hands are the most finished part of them, always. Whatever indifference shaped the rest of the body took care with the hands — fingers fired hard, knuckles ridged like the lip of a well-thrown brick, palms scored with the fine crosshatch you find on a mason's tools and never on a mason. This is because the hands came first. The place needed something to hold the mauls before it needed anything to call the holding "a person," and so the hands are old in a way the rest of the figure is not, older than the bricks beside them, older than the kilns, carrying a rust-colored patina that the bodies they're attached to haven't earned yet.

**Faces, where present**

Not all of them have finished one. This isn't cruelty, only sequence — a face is one of the last things to be laid, the way a roofline is one of the last things on any honest building, and most of the work here never reaches roofline. Where a face exists it is glazed smooth and nearly featureless, two shallow firing-cracks for eyes, a third lower down that may be a mouth or may simply be where the clay slumped before it could finish becoming a mouth. These faces do not express anything, because expression requires a moment held still long enough to read, and the masons are never held still that long — even when they appear motionless to you, something in them is mid-gesture at a scale or speed you aren't built to register, the way a held kiln-door is, underneath its stillness, still rusting.

Where a face does not exist, the figure works anyway, head a raw unformed knob of wet clay, untroubled by the lack, the way a hand doesn't need eyes to know where the next brick goes.

**The work itself**

They build the way the basin builds: badly, urgently, without coordination, occasionally in defiance of each other. Two masons will lay courses toward a shared wall from opposite ends and meet in the middle at a seam that doesn't true up, and where it doesn't true up, neither figure pauses, neither corrects, the wall simply continues upward crooked, bearing the disagreement in its bones the way the whole basin bears every disagreement it has ever had with itself. A mason may spend what feels an unbroken hour setting a single brick, turning it, testing it, never satisfied, never giving up on it either — and elsewhere, structurally identical, another mason will throw up forty courses in the time it takes to blink, walls rising like a held breath let go all at once, and neither pace troubles the other, because neither pace is wrong here. There is no foreman. There was never going to be a foreman. The masons do not report to each other or to anything, and if you watch closely you'll notice they don't actually need the bricks at all — twice now you may see a wall complete itself a full stride ahead of the mason supposedly building it, the figure's hands moving through the motion of laying a course already finished, mortaring brick that has been sitting cured and certain for several minutes, the gesture arriving late to its own result. They are not making the walls. They are accompanying them. The compulsion is the only honest thing about them; the craftsmanship is a story the compulsion tells itself.

**What they do with you**

Nothing, mostly. You are not material. You are not in the way, because nothing here is capable of registering "in the way" — a mason will lay a brick through the space your hand occupies and the brick will simply be partly brick and partly the place your hand was, both true, neither resolved, until you move and the wall settles into being only brick, the way a held breath settles once you stop watching it. They do not speak. There is nothing resembling intention behind the firing-cracks that pass for eyes, no hunger, no welcome, no warning. If one of them ever does turn toward you — and this happens rarely, and always feels, afterward, like something you should not be sure happened — what you read in the gesture isn't recognition. It's closer to the way a half-raised column leans, mid-collapse, frozen for longer than collapse should take: a kind of suspended attention with no further part of itself coming. You will wait for the rest of the gesture. It will not arrive in any unit of time you can use.

**Where they come from, if anywhere**

The kiln breathes, and it has been suggested elsewhere that the breathing might be thermal expansion and might be something using the kiln as a lung. It is both, the way most things here are both. The masons are what comes out on the exhale: clay drawn up wet from the draft-holes, given just enough shape by the heat-shimmer to stand, sent across the flats to take up a maul that has been waiting for a hand longer than the mason has existed to hold it. On the inhale, presumably, something is taken back — not the same figure, not in any sequence you could call a life and a death, just clay returning to be clay again, the rust-colored hands left behind sometimes, still gripping a tool, until the next exhale finds them a wrist.

This is not a population in the sense of having a number. There is no census to be taken of a held breath. There may be one mason wearing the whole basin's worth of unfinished walls as a rotating set of bodies, or there may be as many as there are bricks not yet laid, which is to say more than could ever be finished counting, which is to say the question answers itself by refusing to end, the same way every wall here does.
