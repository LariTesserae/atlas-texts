## What lives there

The swarm comes first, because the swarm is everywhere—small bodies, fist-sized, built around a stiff cartilage spine that lets them fold nearly flat to slip between reed-stalks. They are not insects, not quite, though they share an insect's economy of design: too many wing-pairs, mounted slightly offset from each other, which is why the wingbeats never synchronize. That stagger isn't a flaw. It's how they avoid resonating with the structures' hum—a creature whose wings happened to beat in time with that bass note would feel it in its body the way you feel the pressure change before a thread fires, and resonance, here, is fatal. Their hide is the same bruised purple-black as the reeds, slick with the same condensation that beads on every surface, and their eyes—six of them, arranged in a ring rather than a pair—don't focus on shape so much as on rate of change. They are built to notice when something is different than it was a moment ago, because in this marsh that is the only kind of attention worth having. They nest in the broad waxy leaves, laying eggs that won't hatch unless they pass through a specific sequence of cold-then-warm-then-cold that only happens in the wake of a structure's cycling. Find a clutch and you can predict, roughly, when the ground beneath it is going to remake itself.

The wading thing—the one whose footfalls you feel before you hear—is solitary, or seems to be; no one has confirmed there is more than one, or that "one" is even the right unit to apply to it. It stands at a height that puts its head above the reed-canopy on the rare occasions it lifts that head, revealing a long, blunt, sensory knob rather than a face: no eyes at all, just a dense cluster of skin-windows that read pressure and temperature and the faint electric murmur the marsh gives off. Its legs are too many and arranged wrong, in pairs that don't match front to back, as though assembled from a kit that was missing the instructions—but the gait works, a rolling, weight-shifted lurch that never puts more than a third of its mass down at once, which is likely why it hasn't yet been caught when the ground gives out beneath it. It eats by standing still in a hollow until the blue-green glow pools around its feet and the small things that feed on that glow come close enough to be inhaled, more or less, through gill-slits along its flank. It is not aggressive. It has outlived aggression as a strategy; in a place that remakes its own geography on no schedule, the creatures that survive are the ones built to absorb damage and wait, not the ones built to chase.

But the thing that actually belongs to this place—the thing the marsh seems to have generated *for* the structures, rather than merely around them—is smaller than you'd expect, and far stranger.

It calls the nodes home in the way a tide pool's anemone calls a particular rock home: attached, dependent, shaped by proximity. You'd find it pressed flat against the angle-wrong frames, limbs splayed into the geometry, looking at first like a stain or a growth—until the hard white thread fires somewhere across the marsh and the thing's whole body goes taut and luminous half a second before the light arrives, the same half-second you feel in your inner ear. It is feeding on that pressure-change. Its skin—if skin is the word for a surface with no clear boundary between flesh and the structure's hum—thins and brightens at each pulse, drinking something out of the wave before it passes on to scatter the wingbeats elsewhere. It has no eyes, no mouth that you could find, no obvious means of moving except a slow creep, millimeters an hour, always toward whichever node is due to cycle next, as though it can taste the future tense of the place.

Its body is segmented in threes—three lobes, three trailing root-like tendrils that dig into the structure's strange not-metal and draw out whatever it draws out—and along each lobe runs a thin seam of the same blue-green the hollows pool with, except in this creature the glow pulses with intention, brightening in a rhythm that doesn't match its own heartbeat (it may not have one) but matches, exactly, the deepening and lightening of the structures' hum. When a stand of reeds vanishes and a new pool steams up luminous in its place, this is, locals of the marsh—if anything here could be called local, given how little stays put—believe to be this creature's doing, though more likely it is simply the most visible symptom of a cause too large to see: a thing that lives at the exact point where the marsh's instability and the structures' purpose meet, neither causing the cycle nor caused by it, but *reading* it, continuously, with its whole strange flattened body, the way a held breath reads the moment just before a struck bell goes quiet.

No one has seen it die. No one has seen it born, either—only found, sometimes, two of them pressed to the same frame, lobes interleaved, glowing in trade, as if some information were passing between them that the rest of the marsh's deafening chorus has no language for. When you reach toward one, it doesn't flinch and it doesn't flee. It simply dims, evenly, until you take your hand away—the only quiet thing in a marsh that otherwise never stops sounding, and for that reason alone, the only thing in Annvethar Reach that feels, despite everything, like it might actually be alive on purpose rather than merely surviving the churn.
