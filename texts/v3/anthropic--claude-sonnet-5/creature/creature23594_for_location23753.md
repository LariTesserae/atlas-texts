They are called, by the few who have ever needed a word for them, the standing-cattle, though they are cattle in the way the land itself is a plain — a label that's mostly true and entirely insufficient. They are large, slow-built animals, heavier in the shoulder than in the haunch, with low-slung barrel bodies set on legs that look almost too short for them until you watch one walk through standing water without breaking its surface into more than a ripple. Their hide is the same rust-and-ash patchwork as the lichen on the stones — not camouflage exactly, more a kind of agreement, as if pigment itself had settled into the same palette as everything else here rather than bothering to argue a different case. Old bulls go pale at the shoulders, the color of laterite scraped down to its undercoat, and by the time they are very old they are nearly the color of the rock domes they like to rub against, so that more than one traveler has mistaken a sleeping bull for a stone and the place's stones, on slow afternoons, for sleeping bulls.

Their horns don't spiral or sweep. They curve down and back, close to the skull, blunt at the tips — weapons that gave up being weapons a long time ago, when there was nothing left in this country with the energy to fight over. What they're for now is fitting under low branches without breaking them, and for a particular slow scraping motion against bark and stone that seems to be more itch than agonism, the animal closing its eyes during it the way a man might close his eyes leaning into a doorframe.

They move in loose groups of six to twenty, cows and calves and a few patient bulls, and the groups themselves seem to have given up the idea of urgency the way the land gave up the idea of slope. A herd grazing a tussock-flat does not look like it is going anywhere; it looks like it has paused mid-journey and then forgotten there was a journey. They crop the stiff blue-green grass with a slow, sideways grinding of the jaw, lift their heads at intervals not from alarm but from habit, the way a person glances at a clock in a room with no appointments. Between feedings they stand for hours in the warm shallow pans, half-submerged, swatting flies with tails that move at exactly the speed required and no faster, and at these times the only way to tell they are alive at all, from any distance, is the slow articulated swivel of an ear toward a frog-call, or the white blink of an egret lifting off a wet flank.

What carries across the flat country, what you hear before you ever see them, is the call — a low, chest-deep, two-note sound, not loud in the way a bird's cry is loud but loud in the way a struck drum is loud, a sound built for distance rather than urgency, pitched to ride over a kilometer of open shimmer and reach another animal who is, itself, in no hurry to answer. They call to keep the herd loosely threaded across ground too open and too even to ever lose each other in by sight; they call, sometimes, for no apparent reason at all, one bull answering another bull he cannot see, across an afternoon, for the pleasure or the habit of it, the sound going out and coming back changed only by distance, the way everything in this country seems to go out and come back changed only by distance and time, never by violence, never by hurry.

They breed slowly — a cow carries a single calf for the better part of a year and will not breed again for two more after that — and they live, by the look of the eldest bulls' worn-down horn-tips and grit-pale hides, a very long time. Predators exist — something cat-shaped is sometimes glimpsed at the marsh-reed margins, something that the herds register with no more than a raised head and a tightening of the loose group's perimeter, the calves drawn inward without panic, the adults simply becoming, for an hour, slightly more present than they were — but the openness here works against ambush as much as it works for visibility, and the standing-cattle seem to know, in whatever way an animal knows such inherited arithmetic, that this is ground built for endurance contests, not chases. They have never had to be fast. They have only had to be patient, large, and willing to stand in warm water for most of a long life, watching a horizon that never gets any closer or any further away, calling occasionally to something just past it, content — if that is the word, and in country like this it may be the only word that fits — to have arrived, generations ago, at the same resolved, unhurried state the land itself arrived at, and to simply go on, season after slow season, being exactly what the peneplain had already decided an animal living on it should be.
