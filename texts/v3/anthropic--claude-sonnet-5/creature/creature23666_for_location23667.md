# The Pale Tenant

It does not have a name that travelers use twice, because travelers rarely see it twice, or even once cleanly enough to be sure of what they saw. Call it, if you must call it anything, by what the boot feels before the eye catches up: a give in the moss that shouldn't be there, a soft retreating weight, gone downward and away before your knee finishes bending to look. This is the only resident creature of any size on the whole plateau, and it has arranged its entire existence around not being found.

## Body

It is low — built for tunnels, not for standing — somewhere between the length of a forearm and the length of a grown person stretched flat, though no one has measured one end to end while it was both whole and still. The body is segmented in the loose, overlapping way of something that needs to flex through gaps narrower than itself: broad soft plates of skin shingled like the bark-scales of a young pine, each plate edged in a thread of deeper pigment so that in cross-section, in the rare specimen the rivulets surrender dead to the basins, the creature looks almost machine-tooled, gasketed, engineered for a wet world rather than merely surviving in one.

Color: none, or as close to none as a living thing can manage and still be called colored. Not white — white would show. A translucent, faintly green-grey, the exact hue of moss-water seen through moss-water, skin thin enough in places that the working of organs beneath shows as shadow rather than shape, a slow tidal darkening and lightening along the flank that might be breath, might be digestion, might be something with no human analogue at all. Lay it on wet sphagnum and it is not hidden so much as *continued* — the eye slides off it onto the moss beyond and does not naturally return, the same glare-and-shadow logic that makes the basins read as coins of light works on this body too, every wet surface here throwing back the same enormous shadowless light, the creature included.

Eyes: vestigial, paired black points no larger than seed-heads, set well back, of use only at the very mouth of a burrow where the engineered dark gives way to the engineered glare. Most of what it knows of the world arrives through the skin itself, and through a fringe of short, constantly testing filaments along the jaw and forelegs — not whiskers exactly, more like roots that have learned to retract — which read water chemistry, pressure, and most of all *vibration*, in extraordinary, layered detail. This animal does not hear the engines the way you hear them, as roar-laid-under-trickle. It reads them as a map. Each pump-housing has its own signature shudder, its own valve-cycle stutter, propagating for hundreds of meters through wet rock and wetter moss, and the creature orients its whole territory against that grid the way a sailor orients against named stars. Where the iron goes quiet — a stalled valve, a frozen wheel — it notices immediately, and avoids that quadrant of the plateau for days, the way you might avoid a house gone suddenly, wrongly silent.

## What it eats, and how slowly

It does not graze the living moss-surface; nothing here does, which is part of why the moss stands undisturbed into those ancient unbroken cushions. Its mouth is built instead for the interior — soft, blunt, ringed with a fine abrasive pad rather than teeth — and it feeds by working through the dead understructure beneath the living green, the slow brown felt of moss-that-was, drawing out whatever has decayed there into something digestible: the held bodies of midges fallen and sunk, threads of algae washed down from the basin-overflows and packed into the peat by years of seepage, mineral film leached out of the young, still-dissolving basalt. It eats, in other words, exactly at the rate the plateau produces food, which is to say barely, which is why it is rare, why it is slow, why a single individual may hold a territory of several terraces and a stretch of three or four rivulets and still go hungry weeks at a stretch without apparent distress. Its metabolism is matched to the moss's own unhurried accumulation — it does not chase the centimeter of new peat each year so much as keep pace with it, side by side, two slow processes converging on the same point from different directions.

## The iron, and what it does there

The engines are the one stable warmth on a plateau where everything else is glacial seep and shadowless cold light, and the creature has learned this the way water has learned the cracks in the rock — without intention, simply by what survives the trying. The housings run warm at their bases, where compressors labor against the cold; the orange stain of perpetual not-quite-rust bleeds down into the moss in long tongues, and beneath that stain the peat stays a few degrees above the terrace average, day and night, season after season, the one dependable thermal fact in an otherwise indifferent landscape. The creature dens there. Not inside the iron — the iron is loud past the point even this animal finds bearable, and it has been known to retreat from a station immediately after a valve-shriek, body flattened, filaments drawn in tight — but in the lee of it, in the warm wet hollow where housing meets moss meets rock, curled into a shape that reads, to a passing eye, as one more dome of cushion-moss with an odd faint sheen. This is almost certainly the "thing at the edge of vision" the plateau is known for: glimpsed near a pump-station, mistaken first for the moss itself, then for a trick of the spray-fog, and gone into the peat before the mistake can be corrected.

No one knows whether it benefits the engines in turn, whether some symbiosis runs the other way — whether the slow boring of its tunnels through deep peat does anything to channel water toward the intake mouths, whether its denning warms the base-plates enough on the coldest mornings to matter, whether the whole pump network was sited, ages ago, by whoever built it, with some early observation of these animals' habits in mind. It would be a satisfying thing to believe. The plateau offers no confirmation either way. The engines run; the creature dens near them; both continue, mutually indifferent or mutually essential, in the way that two old neighbors might be either after enough decades that the distinction has stopped mattering to anyone, including themselves.

## Solitude, mostly by necessity

They do not appear to congregate. The plateau's whole productivity — measured in midges, in algae, in leached mineral film, in whatever else the dead moss yields — is too thin to support more than one of these animals per several terraces, and so each individual's territory is vast relative to its size and almost entirely empty of others of its kind, crossed only at its furthest, wettest, most disputed edges, where two ranges of vibration-knowledge overlap along some rivulet and a brief, mostly silent negotiation occurs — a flank pressed against a flank in the dark beneath the moss, some exchange of pressure and chemistry that resolves the boundary without ever surfacing into light. Young are rare to encounter for the same reason mature ones are: born small and pale and already burrowing, in some moss-deep nest line whose location no description of this place has ever managed to record, because no one has stayed long enough, or quietly enough, near enough to a working engine, to be shown one.

## Its time, set against the plateau's time

It is, like everything up here, built for patience rather than speed, and like everything up here it is in a long, unhurried, faintly competitive relationship with the ground it lives on. The moss thickens by its few centimeters a year; the creature's tunnels lengthen by roughly that same advancing edge, always working at the boundary between living green and the dead understructure it actually eats, always just behind the moss's growth, never catching it, never falling far back either. Long after this visit, when peat has gone to proper soil and — if the plateau ever permits it — soil has gone to the first thin stand of trees, something descended from this animal, or its replacement, will likely still be down there, reading the vibration of whatever machinery is left running, denning in whatever residual warmth the iron, by then long collapsed, has left stamped into the ground beneath it like a fading handprint. It is not a creature built to be discovered. It is a creature built to outlast the discovering, by simply continuing, low and pale and unhurried, exactly as long as the moss does, exactly as long as the engines keep their stuttering, mapped, dependable noise.
