They are, before anything else, listeners.

You will not see them at first — the terraces swallow motion the way the drone swallows sound, and a person standing still in a doorway becomes indistinguishable from the doorway itself. But they are there, and when you finally see one clearly, the heat is written on them in ways that take a moment to parse as adaptation rather than affliction. Their skin runs dark, a deep burnt umber-red regardless of what color it started as generations back, thickened and faintly lacquered-looking, the pores small, the sweat glands long since evolved toward economy rather than excess. They do not perspire the way surface-dwellers do. They weep moisture the way the plants do — slowly, deliberately, mostly at night, a faint sheen on the brow and throat that they tend to wipe and drink themselves, unselfconsciously, the way you might blow your nose. To watch a Cistern native lick condensation from their own collarbone in the deep dim hours is to understand that here, the boundary between body and crop is thinner than you're used to.

Their eyes are the second thing you notice. Large, dark, slightly sunken beneath heavy brows that have thickened over centuries to shade against the one or two hours of real light the bowl receives — and yet those eyes are extraordinarily good in dimness, drinking ember-glow the way the leaves drink heat, dilated almost permanently, so that on the rare occasion one of them climbs the shaft into full daylight above, they shield their face and move like something nocturnal caught out of its hour. Children are taught early to lower their gaze on the rim. The old joke, told flatly, without much humor, is that a Cistern-born who looks straight at the sun is one who has decided to leave for good.

But it's the ears, if you could measure such a thing, that have changed the most — not in shape but in use. These are a people raised inside a chord. From birth, the wind-organs are the metronome beneath everything: lullabies pitched to harmonize with the day's drone, work-songs structured around its drift as the heat shifts hour to hour, arguments and courtships and grief conducted with half an ear cocked, always, to whether the bowl is singing true. They develop, as a matter of course, a pitch-sense most surface people would call inhuman — not perfect pitch in the abstract musical sense, but an intimate, bone-deep familiarity with the specific resonant voice of their own home, the particular stone-throats carved by the founders, so that a person born on the third terrace can tell you, eyes closed, whether the wind today is coming faster or slower than yesterday, whether rain is gathering somewhere far above on the surface they may never see, whether a wind-stone three terraces over has begun to crack from dry-rot, just by the way its note has gone sour. This sensitivity comes at a price: true silence — the kind found nowhere in the bowl, but rumored to exist somewhere on the flat hot scrubland above — unsettles them severely. Old stories describe surface-walkers, exiles or traders gone topside for long stretches, returning unable to sleep, plagued by an absence they can't name, until someone thinks to drag a clay jar back and forth across stone just to give the air something to hold.

Socially, they organize the way the terraces do — by layer, by craft, by inherited proximity to the work of survival rather than by anything as crude as wealth, since there is so little to hoard. The heat-succulent guilds form the spine of the place: families who have spent generations learning which leaves to press at which hour, which vines to let run wild and which to prune hard to force a sweeter exhalation, how to read the slow fattening of a fruiting body and know it's a day, maybe two, from bursting open into the only sweetness most of them will taste that season. Beside them sit the tuners — fewer in number, older on average, treated with a wary reverence usually reserved elsewhere for priests — who climb the upper rock with chisels and listen-sticks to recut the wind-throats when their voices drift, who can silence a screaming fissure or coax music from a previously mute stretch of stone, and who are, not coincidentally, usually the ones called on to officiate marriages, since a Cistern wedding is understood, at root, as a determination of whether two people's voices sit well together inside the town's larger chord.

Children are raised loosely but watched closely, allowed to range the terraces with a freedom that would alarm a surface parent, because there is nothing here to harm them except heat and falling, and falling is guarded against by handrail and instinct both. They are given small tasks early — a leaf to press, a clapper to strike at dusk, a trough to check for blockage — not as chores exactly but as a kind of enrollment in the town's continuous, unfinished work of staying alive. They learn the bowl's geography by ear before they learn it by eye, can often navigate a dark terrace blind by the changing pitch of the wind off the walls, a skill that looks like magic to outsiders and is, to them, simply paying attention to the obvious.

They do not fear the heat. They fear flatness — a day with no wind, a stretch of true quiet, a guild gone silent because its last elder died without passing down a tuning. Death itself, here, is spoken of in acoustic terms: a person doesn't pass away so much as go quiet, and the dead are not buried but laid in small stone alcoves cut into the upper rock near the wind-throats, so that the air still moving past their resting place is said, by those who want to believe it, to carry a thread of their voice on into the town's unbroken hum. It is, depending on how you hear it, either a comforting fiction or the literal truth — and the Cistern people, practical to the bone about everything else, leave this one question alone, content to let the wind answer it however it likes, every single day, forever.
