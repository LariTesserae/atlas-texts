They call themselves the **Sluicewrights**, though that is a trade-name worn down by centuries into something closer to a tribal one, and they are exactly as the place has made them: low-built, broad-handed, moving with the unhurried economy of people who have never had anywhere to rush to.

**The body, adapted.** Generations in the wet half-light have left their mark plainly. Sluicewrights are pale in a particular way — not sickly, but the specific bloodless white of something grown under a board, skin slightly translucent at the wrist and temple, faintly cool to the touch even in summer. Their eyes are large for their faces and pale-irised, grey or weak amber, pupils that stay wide even near the gas-mantles; they read fine machine-tolerances and hairline cracks in cast iron by a quality of light outsiders call dim and they call *plenty*. Most past middle age are somewhat deaf — not deafened by any single accident, but worn down gradually by sixty years of being inside the noise, the way a stone is worn by a river. They don't experience this as loss. They experience it as graduation. The old joke, told entirely in the tap-and-gesture sign-language rather than aloud, is that you know a Sluicewright has come fully into adulthood when they stop noticing the hammers.

Their hands are the other tell: thickened at the first knuckle, the skin of the palms perpetually soft and a little swollen from standing water, fingertips pruned in a way that on anyone else would mean they'd just come from a bath, but is, here, simply baseline. Nails grow soft and need regular trimming or they tear. Children are taught early to keep them short with a little hook-knife, the first tool anyone owns.

**Sign and shout.** Two registers of speech exist side by side and Sluicewrights move between them without seeming to choose. The shout — pitched to carry over stamping presses and falling water, clipped to single hard syllables, vowels mostly sanded away — is for the street, the workshop floor, anything urgent. The sign — a private grammar of finger-taps against forearm, wall, table, the rim of a vat, refined over centuries into something with genuine syntax — is for everything else: gossip, courtship, grief, jokes too dry to survive being shouted. Outsiders who stay more than a season usually pick up perhaps thirty signs and never notice the hundreds more being traded over their heads, literally, on the catwalks above the lanes.

A child's first formal lesson is not letters but tap-grammar, taught by an aunt or grandparent drumming patiently against the back of a small hand until the hand starts answering back.

**Work and lineage.** Labor here is inherited the way a name is inherited elsewhere — not a job you take but a relationship you're born into, a particular wheel, a particular vat-room, a particular flume passed down a family line for so many generations that the equipment and the bloodline have become a single unit of memory. Ask a Sluicewright what they do and they will often answer with the machine's name, not a trade-word: *I tend Old Marl's wheel.* *I keep the third drying-loft.* *I've the south fermenting-house, my mother had it, her mother before.* The machines, similarly, are spoken of with a familiarity that borders on kinship — oiled, scolded, apologized to, given small offerings of tallow on certain feast-nights not because anyone quite believes the iron listens, but because their grandmothers did it and the wheel has, after all, never once stopped turning for as long as anyone alive remembers, and a person learns not to interrupt a streak like that.

**What they make, and how they think about time.** The vats and racks and slow-cure tanks produce things that take seasons rather than days: a dark fermented paste used downriver as tannage and (whispered, never confirmed to outsiders) as something stronger; a pale fiber, drawn from steeped reed-pulp, that dries into a paper-thin sheet prized for wrapping things that must breathe slowly; a clear, faintly sweet distillate that the Sluicewrights drink themselves but rarely sell, on the grounds — stated as simple fact, not boast — that nobody not raised on the noise can stomach it without losing several nights' sleep to dreams about machinery.

This long-cycle production has shaped how they reckon time generally. Sluicewrights measure age and event not in years but in *vats* — "the autumn the south tank turned over twice," "born the spring of the long cure." A childhood is roughly four vat-cycles long. Courtship is traditionally exactly one cycle, the idea being that if two people can stand each other's company and noise-fatigue and bad temper through a full slow fermentation, they can stand anything. Funerals happen at the *opening* of a vat, not before — the dead are kept, decently and without ceremony, in the cool flooded sub-basements until the next batch comes due, and are then given to the river-sluice in the same hour the vat is tapped, on the theory, again unstated as theory and simply lived as practice, that nothing should leave this place alone.

**The absence they live inside of and rarely discuss.** They know, in the dry factual way one knows a fact one has stopped feeling, that there are no birds in their eaves and no rats in their walls and nothing at all living among the ferns but the ferns themselves. Ask directly and most will shrug and say the noise drove it all off generations back, an explanation that satisfies precisely nobody who thinks about it for more than a minute, since the noise has never driven off the people. A few of the old sign-talkers, asked the same question late at night with the gas turned low, will instead tap out something closer to: *we are what's here. there wasn't room left for two kinds.* It is not said with pride or unease, particularly. It is said the way you'd state the yield of a well — a number you didn't choose and don't argue with, just live inside.

They keep no pets, eat no meat raised locally, and regard outsiders' descriptions of birdsong with the polite, slightly strained interest of people being told about a color they've never seen. Children occasionally ask why the moss grows so thick with nothing to eat it, and are told, by way of answer, to go press their ear against any wall in the settlement and listen to the wheel underneath — there's always one running, somewhere close — and to consider that perhaps the place simply prefers its tenants to keep still and turn slowly, like everything else here, rather than scuttle.

**Temperament.** What all of this produces, finally, is a people of enormous patience and oddly cheerful fatalism — unhurried because hurry has never once shortened a fermentation or sped a well's yield, undemonstrative because shouting is for the street and feeling is for the fingers, deeply attached to machinery and lineage and to each other, and entirely unbothered by a silence that would unsettle almost anyone else, because for them it was never a silence at all. It was just the sound the place makes where the birds would be.
