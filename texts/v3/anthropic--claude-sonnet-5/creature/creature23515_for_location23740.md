# Who Lives in the Spires

## The Stonekin

The people of the Reach are called, by outsiders and increasingly by themselves, the stonekin — not a single ethnicity but a way of being shaped by three or four hundred years of vertical living. You can tell a Reach-born adult by the calves and forearms, dense with the specific musculature of stairs and ladders and pulley-brakes worked by hand; by the squint that comes from a lifetime of sideways light; by the way they orient instinctively to up and down rather than the cardinal directions, giving directions in terrace-numbers and rail-stops rather than compass points. Lung capacity runs high, a slow adaptation to the thin dry air at altitude and the foundry smoke that drifts through the lower terraces on still mornings. Skin tends toward weathered and matte, not tanned so much as cured, like leather left in dry heat — there is no green here to soften the light, and the light does not forgive.

They do not cluster the way old-world cities cluster. Following the spacing logic of the land itself — the same logic that scatters the climbers and gliders into wide, sparse territories — Reach settlements grow in tall, narrow filaments rather than broad sprawls, a few hundred people per terrace-stack, a few thousand per spire, separated from the next inhabited needle by an hour or two of rail and a great deal of empty, wind-organ air. A Reach-dweller might live an entire life within a single spire's seventeen terraces and regard a trip to the next tower over as a journey, an event, a thing you pack a meal for.

## Lineage and Labor

Work here is inherited in a way that has calcified into something almost like caste, though no one calls it that. A family that has run a particular geothermal tap for six generations knows that tap the way a sailor knows a familiar sea — its moods, its slow declines, the years it runs hot and the years it sulks. Foundry-tenders, rail-greasers, lift-brakemen, blast-door wardens, crystal-assayers who climb out onto the bloom-sheets with chisel and basket to harvest what the climbers haven't yet scraped clean — each trade owns its terraces, its songs, its particular silences. Children apprentice young, not because anyone forces them but because there is genuinely nothing else to apprentice to; the Reach does not import strangers easily, and a twelve-year-old who can read a pressure gauge is worth more to a household than one who cannot.

There is a deep, working-class patience bred into the stonekin temperament, inherited from the land's own pace. Nobody expects to see a vein exhausted and a new one tapped within their own working life; you start the search, your daughter finishes it, your granddaughter cuts the first new rail spur to it. Plans here are measured in grandchildren. An impatient person doesn't last in the Reach — not because the place kills them, exactly, but because nothing here rewards hurry, and a culture shapes itself, over centuries, around what gets rewarded.

## Belief, Bell, and Hum

Religion in the Reach, where it exists at all, tends to be acoustic rather than iconographic — unsurprising in a place with no images to carve from, no wood for idols, only stone that already has its own voice. The wind-organs are listened to, not worshipped exactly, but attended: certain fissures have names, certain chords are considered fortunate or foreboding, and a synchronized hum across multiple districts — the thing the engineers' disagreeing instruments try and fail to explain — is, in most households, treated less as an anomaly and more as a weather event with opinions. Old-timers will tell you the hum means the mountain is "thinking out loud," and say it with the same shrug they'd use for a man muttering in his sleep — not literal belief, not entirely disbelief either.

Foundry-bells are rung not just for shift changes but for births, deaths, and the rare bad rockfall, and because sound travels so unevenly through the dry air — perfectly clear from three kilometers, swallowed whole from three hundred meters — there's a whole folk-practice of bell-reading, interpreting which strikes carried and which didn't as a kind of omen system, half superstition, half genuinely useful meteorology, since the carrying of sound correlates loosely with pressure and temperature gradients that matter to the foundries anyway.

## What They Don't Discuss at Dinner

Every stonekin family has, somewhere in its history, a relative who worked a deep-level excavation and came up early, requesting reassignment, never quite explaining why. The sealed chambers are common knowledge in the way that certain kinds of common knowledge get handled in tight, generational communities: acknowledged, never elaborated on, folded into the same category as the things that live too far out past the settled third of the spires, the climbers nobody's ever found the far edge of a colony for, the question of where the mineral mass that isn't depleting is actually coming from.

There's a phrase, used dry and half-joking by old foundry hands to younger ones nervous about a new tunnel: the mountain keeps its own books. It is said the way you'd say something about weather, or luck — not an explanation, just an acknowledgment that some ledgers in the Reach don't balance from the outside, and the stonekin have simply built a society patient enough, and humble enough, to keep living on top of the parts that don't.

## The Things That Share the Stone With Them

The stonekin do not think of the climbers and gliders as wildlife in the old-world sense — too present, too economically entangled for that. The pale chisel-mouthed climbers are half-pest, half-resource: they compete with the crystal-assayers for the richest bloom-seams, but their feeding patterns are also read as a kind of prospecting tool, since a sudden congregation of fifty climbers on an unmapped seam is often the first sign of a vein worth drilling toward. Trapping and rendering climbers for their mineral-dense internals is a minor, unglamorous trade of its own, done by people who know the colonies' wide, spaced territories as intimately as the foundry families know their taps.

The gliders are watched, not hunted — too large, too patient, riding the thermals for hours over the unclaimed two-thirds of the spires where the towns never reached. Rail-workers on the high switchbacks learn to recognize individual gliders by wing-pattern and flight habit over years of shared sky, give them names half-fondly, half-warily, the way you'd name a weather system that occasionally, very rarely, takes something a careless person left exposed too long on an outer terrace. Nobody in the Reach romanticizes them. Nobody dismisses them either. They are simply there, the way the wind is there, the way the hum is there — another resident of the stone, older in tenure than any spire-town, indifferent to the rail schedules running far below their slow, fuel-free circles in the warm rising air.
