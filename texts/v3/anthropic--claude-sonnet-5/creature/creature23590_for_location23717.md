**The Throat-Crawlers**

What eats here, in a hollow with no water and no green and no death of anything that once lived—because nothing here has ever lived before them? The answer is: the mountain itself, in increments too small to grieve.

They are carapaced things, low and wedge-bodied, built the way a tool is built rather than the way a creature grows—broad flat plates fused overlapping down the spine, articulated tight at every joint so that no soft thing is ever exposed to the air, which would kill it in a breath. The carapace is not chitin in any sense a lowland eye would recognize; it is mineral-grown, secreted in thin obsidian-glass layers from glands along the flank, so that an adult Throat-Crawler is, by the time it reaches its full hand-length size, wearing something closer to armor plate than shell—dark, glassy, shot through with the same internal orange glow as the rock around it, because the carapace itself stores heat, banks it, releases it slow through tiny vent-pores along the seams. You could mistake a resting one for a knuckle of cooling melt. That is rather the point.

Their feet are the marvel of them: four to six per side, broad as a thumb's pad, soled in a fibrous mineral fur that grips superheated stone the way a wet hand grips glass—through some chemistry of adhesion that fails the instant the surface cools below the temperature it evolved to expect. A Throat-Crawler set down on cold rock does not walk. It skids, flails, and dies of exposure within minutes, its feet having simply stopped working, the way a machine stalls outside its operating range. They are creatures with a thermostat for an anatomy, alive only inside a band of heat so narrow that anywhere else in the world would be, to them, instant and total winter.

What they eat: each other's discarded carapace-flakes, shed in molt; the mineral skin that grows on cooling melt before it fully solidifies, peeled and chewed with mouthparts shaped like a cold chisel; and, when a vein ruptures and a fresh gout of magma breathes into the chamber, they swarm it in a dense clattering mass and drink the heat directly through specialized plates on the underside of the jaw, gorging not on substance but on temperature itself, the way another animal might gorge on sugar. This is why the chamber roars with their clicking: it is not language, mostly, but feeding-noise, hundreds of small bodies scraping flake and skin from every available surface in a perpetual low-grade harvest, because the chamber, despite its abundance, never gives quite enough, and so they are always working, every one of them, all the time, the scrape and click and chitter the sound of an economy that cannot afford to stop.

They cluster instead of spreading because heat is patchy—warmer near a vent-throat, cooler near the chamber's outer skin—and a Throat-Crawler caught alone too near the cold margin will start, within the hour, to seize and stiffen. So they pile. Dense, shoulder-plate to shoulder-plate, in shifting drifts that rotate continuously, the ones on the cold edge of the pile clambering over their kin toward the center, the ones who've banked enough heat drifting outward to take their place on the margin—a slow, ceaseless, circulating crowd, like a held breath that keeps being passed mouth to mouth. From outside, glimpsed by the chamber's own glow, the pile looks like a single restless organ, pulsing, shrugging, never still and never going anywhere, and this is the source of the engine-thrum beneath the clatter: not machinery, but hundreds of small bodies breathing in near-unison, broad flanks expanding and contracting to cool excess heat, a tide of respiration with a rhythm steady enough to mistake, in the dark, for a piston.

They do not see, not in any useful sense—the eyes, where eyes exist at all, are vestigial pits sensitive only to gross shifts in radiant glow, enough to flinch from a sudden flare of fresh melt or to orient, dimly, toward the brightest and therefore warmest patch of wall. Instead they navigate by a heat-sense spread finely across the whole carapace, a skin that reads temperature gradient the way another animal's skin reads touch, so that the entire chamber is, to them, not a dark cramped hollow but a richly differentiated terrain of warm and warmer and warmest, a landscape of intensity rather than shape. They are, in their own perception, not crowded into a small dark fist of stone at all. They are spread across an enormous gradient country, vast with subtle temperature, and we would be the ones who could not see it.

They breed the way the rock itself accretes—slowly, opportunistically, a clutch of soft unarmored young laid directly against a cooling melt-flow so that the heat of their own birthplace bakes the first carapace-layer onto them within hours, a narrow window during which they are catastrophically vulnerable, eaten in handfuls by their own crowding kin if the brooding adult cannot hold a clear patch of stone against the press of bodies. Most clutches do not survive whole. Enough always does. The chamber has never, in all its slow unfinished geological patience, gone quiet.

And when, eventually, the vein they've clustered around cools past the point of return—solidifies, goes dark, stops feeding them heat—the whole drift of them, by some current that passes through the pile like a decision arrived at without any one of them deciding it, peels away in a clattering wave and migrates, claw over claw, toward the next throbbing seam of warmth, leaving the dead patch of wall to its slow indifferent cooling, gray and finished and forgotten, while behind them the chamber goes on being unfinished everywhere else, and the Throat-Crawlers go on, fist-sized and legion, feeding on a mountain that is, like them, still deciding what it means to be done.
