## The Roothollow Folk

They are smaller than you'd expect, and they move faster than that smallness suggests it should allow — a compact, economical way of walking that never wastes a step crossing the tuber-mats, because they learned young that hesitation in this place gets you trampled by a grazer-herd, scalded by a flash-fungus, or simply lost in the noise. Their bodies are wiry, ropey with a kind of lean strength that comes from constant labor rather than constant feeding; they eat what the bazaar eats, which is to say opportunistically, whatever the cavern offers up that hour — tuber-flesh, the meat of the blind grazers, the strange sweet pulp inside the fungal shelves, insects roasted whole in the fire-pit's small clean emptiness.

Their skin runs pale, almost the color of the rhizome-mat itself, a bloodless underground pallor with a faint waxy sheen — not sickly, just unsunned, generations deep. Where it's exposed to the green mineral light for long stretches, some develop faint mottling, blotches that glow the barest amount in true darkness, like a residue of the cavern's own bioluminescence absorbed and kept. The old ones have more of it than the young. No one's sure if it means anything, or if it's just what happens to skin that's lived a long life soaking in that pulse.

Their eyes are wide, light-hungry, set a little large for their faces — adapted to a world with no steady sun, only dapple and flare and tidal green breathing. They've learned to read motion in flicker, to tell a predator's approach from the pattern of shadows shifting wrong rather than from any clear sighting, because clear sighting is rare here and reaction time is everything. Their hearing has gone the opposite way, oddly blunted at the edges — not deaf, but selectively deaf, a learned ability to let the roar of the place become background, to fish one shout, one warning-click, one structural groan worth heeding out of a wall of continuous noise. A Roothollow child is taught this before they're taught to weave: how to *not* hear, so that the one sound that matters doesn't drown.

Their hands are their real inheritance — broad-palmed, strong-fingered, callused along the same ridges generation after generation from the same three tasks: cutting vine-rope, weaving stem-bark, working bone hooks into points. They have not smelted metal and may never need to, because the cavern provides tools faster than it provides reasons to improve them; stone and bone and fiber do the work, and the work is constant, and there's no leisure built into a life spent matching pace with something that outgrows its own ceiling.

They dress in woven stem-bark and harvested hide from the grazer-herds, simple wraps that breathe in the thick body-warm air, dyed unevenly in whatever the mineral veins or fungal saps will stain — sulfur yellow, pulse-green, the rare deep black that doesn't wash out and is treated, quietly, as significant, almost ceremonial, when it appears.

They live in extended, overlapping kin-clusters rather than households — the lean-tos wedged into root-buttresses shift their occupants as the buttresses themselves shift, so identity here is bound less to *place* than to *task and group*: you are who you harvest with, who you stand fire-watch with, who pulled you out of the grazer-stampede that one time the path changed without warning. Loyalty is functional and fierce. Strangers are folded in quickly if they can pull weight, because the bazaar always needs more hands and never stops generating work.

And they are, every one of them, faintly, permanently uneasy — not afraid, exactly; fear would imply something specific to flinch from, and they've long since stopped flinching at the scuttling and the roar and the screech of distant kills. Theirs is a deeper, structural unease, the kind you'd feel living inside something that hasn't finished deciding what it is. It shows in small customs: the rings of unharvested tubers nobody will explain except with a hand-flick and a look away; the habit of sweeping dust over a footprint that's lingered too long, just in case sweeping it helps; the old ones' insistence on building fires a little brighter than necessary near the slow-pulsing vines, as if competing for the cavern's attention, or appeasing it. They don't worship the place, not in any organized way — there's no temple, no priesthood, nothing so settled — but there is a working theology of gesture and avoidance, passed mother to child, harvester to apprentice: *don't take from that ring. Don't walk there after the third groan. Bank the fire higher when the green slows down.* No one asks why anymore. The ones who asked loudest, the stories say, aren't around to keep asking.

What holds them together, more than kinship or custom, is the bazaar itself — the constant transactional hum of harvest and trade and rebuilding, which functions less like an economy and more like a shared nervous system. Everyone always knows, roughly, where the danger is moving and where the food is thickest, because that knowledge is shouted, relayed, passed basket to basket across the whole scattered settlement in something close to real time. It is loud because it has to be loud. Quiet, here, is what happens right before something goes wrong.

They don't call themselves anything grand. Among outsiders — if outsiders existed, if anyone from elsewhere ever found their way down through whatever surface looms forgotten above — they might be the Underroot people, the Bazaar-folk, the ones who live in the throat of the still-growing thing. Among themselves, mostly, they just call themselves *us*, and the cavern *this*, and they get on with the work of living provisionally, stubbornly, inside a place that has not yet finished arriving — betting, the way all of them seem to without ever quite saying it aloud, that when it does finish, they'll still be standing on it.
