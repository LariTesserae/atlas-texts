They are smaller than the doorframes suggest a builder ought to be, which is the first wrongness you notice once you've found one — and you will find one only by touch, hand brushing a shoulder in a corridor you thought was empty, the shoulder going rigid and then, after a long pause, simply not being there anymore. They do not flee loudly. They unmake their presence the way the rest of the town does: by going still, by letting their breathing drop below the threshold of the architecture's own groaning, until you cannot tell their stillness from a wall's.

Skin like the inside of a husk — pale, dry, faintly grained, cool to the touch rather than warm, because warmth is a thing that gives you away here and they have bred it down to almost nothing over more generations than their own memory reaches. The eyes did not vanish so much as concede; small, lidless, set wide, good for nothing but distinguishing the deep black from a slightly less deep black, which is all the gradient this town has ever offered anyone. What took the eyes' place is everything else: ears held slightly forward and faintly translucent at the rim, fine hairs at wrist and jaw and along the long sensitive ridge of the spine, reading pressure and draft and the particular timbre of a footstep two corridors off. Hands hardened at the pads, almost hooflike, shaped by lifetimes of adze-work and thorn-needle work into something closer to tool than flesh. They move on these hands as much as on their feet, low, close to the swept floors, fitted to a town built at the scale of a people who never stood up straight enough to need a doorway taller than a hip.

They do not eat what you would call food, or not much of it, or not often. What there is to live on is the town itself — the slow, endless, centuries-long exhalation of the dead plant breaking down by some chemistry that isn't rot, releasing a fine sweetish dust into the stale air, settling into the corners the swept floors are kept clear of. That dust, gathered, pressed, sometimes wetted with what little condenses on the coolest stalks at the lowest hours, is the whole of the larder. It asks very little of a body to live on it, and so very little is what their bodies have become — slow hearts, shallow lungs, child-rearing spaced out across years the way the wall settles a finger's width per generation, because there is rarely enough surplus dust in a season to spend on growing something new. A child here is not common. An old one, by contrast, can be very old indeed, indistinguishable in the dark from any other shape until it speaks, if it ever does.

Speech, when it happens, doesn't sound like speech. They have learned — bred into themselves, or simply practiced into themselves over enough centuries that the line stopped mattering — to pitch their voices into the same low, creaking register as the dying thicket's own settling, so that a word and a groan of cooling fiber are, to anyone not raised here, the same sound. This is not decoration. It is camouflage, and also, perhaps, courtesy: a way of putting oneself inside the town's voice rather than against it, of never being the one sound that doesn't belong. Whole conversations may pass between two of them at a distance of two streets, mistaken by anyone else for the building merely doing what it always does, and that, more than the swept floors or the axes left ready in their alcoves, is the real architecture of this place — not stalk and pith and lashed fiber, but a discipline of stillness so total it has become a second skeleton, worn over the first, holding up everything that's left to hold.

They keep doors because the town taught them to think in thresholds, long before they had anything to put behind one. They sweep floors because dust left ungathered is dust uneaten, not because of any love of order, though by now it would be hard to tell the difference even for them. And whether there are many or few, spread thin through districts whose names only they still know, or whether what's left is a scatter of solitary, ancient, patient bodies each keeping a different quarter of the dark — that, like everything else here, you will not learn by looking, because there is nothing here to see. You will learn it, if at all, the way the town itself is read: by the dust someone else has already pushed into a corner, by the tool set down at exactly the angle a hand would want it, by the held breath behind a wall that is, for just a moment longer than a wall should, too quiet to be only settling.
