They are, first, economical — not poor exactly, though by surface measures they might read that way, but economical in the deeper sense: people who have learned to spend nothing they don't have to. Motion is conserved. A person crossing a catwalk here doesn't swing their arms; gesture has been trimmed down to what's needed in a space where every swung elbow might catch a vine, a pipe-joint, a neighbor's shoulder. You learn this within a season of arriving, or you bruise constantly until you do.

Their bodies run a little soft at the edges, in the way of anything raised in sustained heat — looser-muscled than surface folk, sweat-glazed most of the day, skin gone sallow-pale in the dim light but flushed dark at cheek and throat from the climbing warmth. Older Undergladers move with a particular stooped economy, shoulders rounded from decades of ducking scaffold and stair, and there's a recognizable Underglade gait — short-stepped, flat-footed, weight kept low, the gait of people who have internalized that the footing is always slightly wet and the ceiling is always slightly closer than you think.

Voices match the place. Not whispering — that takes its own kind of effort — but a flattened, close-pitched way of speaking, words kept near the body, because everyone has had the experience of saying something at ordinary volume and hearing it travel three tiers down clean as a bell, and having a stranger answer a question they weren't asked. Privacy here is achieved through register, not walls. Families develop a kind of shorthand, half-words and held looks, because full sentences carry too far and cost too much breath in air this thick. Visitors are often mistaken for rude or cold; they are only loud.

What they do, mostly, is tend. The line between engineer and gardener dissolved here a generation or two back, and nobody talks about it as a notable thing anymore — it's just the job. A crew might spend a morning rethreading a belt-drive and the afternoon training a vine off the same belt-drive's housing, and consider both tasks the same kind of work: keeping something alive and moving in conditions that want to either rust it or root it. There's a particular kind of person who's good at this, who can tell by smell or by the pitch of a creak whether a given groan is the wood settling or the iron failing, and these people are valued the way healers are valued elsewhere — quietly, constantly, without ceremony, paid in favors and rationed water and the kind of social credit that means someone will remember your name when you're old and slow and no longer climbing the scaffold yourself.

Water governs everything beneath the surface of daily life. Not violently — there's no hoarding-and-raiding here, the place is too small and too interdependent for that — but everyone knows whose basin is whose, who owes whom a morning's collection, which condensation-drip runs slow this week and which family's roof-catch needs reinforcing before the dry spell that isn't really dry, just drier. Children learn the basin-courtesies before they learn to read. It produces a culture of small, constant, almost invisible debts — favors logged and called in, water shared today against water owed next week — that knits the whole bowl together into something that functions less like a town and more like a single household grown enormous and stratified across four stories of rock.

The children themselves are a particular kind of strange to outsiders. Most have seen the high pale wash of daylight at the sinkhole's mouth and almost none have stood in direct sun, and so the surface occupies in their imagination roughly the place that the deep unlit tier occupies in an adult's — a known-but-unvisited country, talked about in stories, invoked as threat or promise depending on the teller. *Behave or I'll send you up top* is not, here, the joke it would be elsewhere. There's real ambivalence in it. Some children dream of climbing out. Most, by the time they're old enough to actually attempt the stair in earnest, have absorbed enough of the bowl's gravity — its warmth, its low ceiling of family and water-debt and known machinery — that the climb starts to feel less like escape and more like exposure, like stepping out of water into thin, cold, unbearable air.

There's an older generation, fewer of them every year, who remember when the walls were rawer than they are now — who remember, or claim to, the early seasons of settling, before the lattice of pipe and scaffold filled in the volume, when the Underglade was mostly bare rock and a few brave structures clinging to the slope like burrs. These elders are listened to with a particular reverence that has little to do with wisdom and everything to do with continuity — they are living proof that the bowl can be survived across a whole life, that the heat doesn't kill you even if it wears you slowly soft, like the green-furred housings of the oldest machines.

And there is, running under all of it, a low watchfulness directed downward rather than up — toward the unlit tier where the machines give out and the original cave resumes, where the rat-analogue is heard threading the wall-panels and the larger rumored things are never quite seen. Nobody speaks of this with real fear. It has the quality, instead, of a held boundary, an edge of the known world kept respectfully unexamined — the Undergladers have made their peace with the surface they can't quite reach above them, and made a second, quieter peace with the depth they don't quite want to reach below. They live, by both necessity and habit now indistinguishable from temperament, in the warm thick middle distance between the two — tending their root-bound engines, rationing their dripping water, speaking low, moving little, breathing as one slow held breath with the machines they have stopped, after enough years, bothering to tell apart from themselves.
