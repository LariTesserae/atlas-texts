# The Listeners of the Reach

They are quiet people, and it costs them effort.

You notice this first in the home-spaces, the interiors carved into the towers' lower levels or dug into the lee side of a ridge where the rock's youth means the walls still smell faintly of fracture and mineral. Indoors, in the rooms reserved for sleep and food and the raising of children, they speak in near-whispers, a habit so deep it reads as constitutional rather than chosen. Ask why and they'll look at you as if the question answers itself: outside, the world will not let you hear yourself think, so inside is the only place thinking gets to happen out loud. Children are taught to whisper before they're taught to walk steadily. A raised voice indoors is a small social violence, the kind that makes elders go still.

Outside, they don't bother trying. The sound-stack of the lowfields defeats the human voice at any distance past arm's reach, and so they've built a second language atop the spoken one — a grammar of light and gesture, refined over generations into something fast and exact. Hand-lanterns, wrist-mounted and palm-cupped, throw quick sequences of color and pulse-length across distances that would swallow shouting whole; a person standing at one tower can hold a real conversation, argument, joke, warning, with someone a kilometer off at another, the two of them blinking meaning back and forth through the pearl-grey haze like something between semaphore and conversation between lighthouses. Children learn this before they learn to read. Lovers court in it. The old keep using it long after their voices have gone, because it never depended on the voice to begin with. Visitors find it eerie — whole settlements gone, to the ear, silent of speech, while two or six or twenty people are in fact mid-conversation, hands and lanterns flickering, faces lit from below in passing colors that mean things the visitor cannot follow.

Their eyes give them away as Reach-born: a permanent slight squint, an economy of blinking, pupils that have learned to do most of their work in a light that never sharpens to a true shadow and never quite settles into a single color. Skin tends pale and matte, suited to a sun that's more rumor than presence; what color they carry, they carry in the cheeks and palms, a sort of wind-and-damp flush that doesn't fade even indoors. They dress in layers that shed easily, because you cross from dust-dry stone-flat to ankle-deep condensation hollow inside the length of a single errand, and clothing that can't adapt mid-stride marks you as someone who hasn't lived here long.

They do not, by and large, claim the towers. This is the thing a stranger has to be told twice before it sinks in: the architecture predates them, or predates their comprehension of it, by enough that "ownership" has curdled into something closer to tenancy. There are people — not many, a handful per settlement, called by a word that translates roughly to *listeners* or *those who stay near* — whose whole vocation is proximity to a given tower or dome: tending what can be tended, recording what changes, occasionally receiving from the structure some adjustment, some intervention, that arrives without explanation and is obeyed without complaint, because three generations of obeying it have kept the dome from venting wrong, or the tower's resonance from drifting into a frequency that makes infants scream and animals bolt. The listeners are respected the way you respect someone standing closer to a cliff edge than you'd care to. They don't claim to understand the machines either. They claim only to have learned their moods.

Most people, though, live by the fauna, not the architecture. The membrane-flocks that gyre over the breathing domes are watched, timed, in places lightly herded — driven with long flagged poles and patient walking toward grazing-thermals where their warm bronze undersides can be harvested, gently, for a waxy secretion used in nearly everything: sealant, fuel, medicine, the slick coating that keeps causeway-built shoes from soaking through in the damp hollows. The ground-things, the many-eyed browsers, are tracked across their slow circuits through the fern-mat and culled with a kind of formal reluctance, the way you'd cull something you suspect is smarter than it lets on. The pack-shapes that follow at a respectful distance are not hunted at all — there's a working theory, never confirmed, never abandoned, that they are not predators so much as a kind of ambient population audit, and killing one invites a season of bad luck that nobody can define more precisely than that, but everyone avoids testing.

Families and the kin-groups built around them tend to be small and mobile, because the aquifers move and so must they. A homestead built into a dry hollow this generation may find itself, two seasons on, the newest wetland on the map, the moss-ripple rerouted straight through where the hearth used to be; there is no shame in this, only the practical work of disassembling what can be carried and re-engineering around what can't. Houses are built to be left. Memory of place is kept less in structures than in story and light-signal-relay — the route a family has walked for six generations gets passed down as a sequence of remembered signals as much as a remembered path, so that even after the land has rearranged itself underfoot, the *telling* of the way still holds.

What they share, child to elder, listener to herder, is a kind of patient deference that looks from outside like passivity and isn't. They have made a life in a place that is still actively becoming something, where the ground is sharp because it hasn't had time to be anything else, where the machines that shelter them are older and stranger than any inherited memory can account for, where the life around them outnumbers the shelter built for it ten to one and shows no sign of slowing. They have not tried to finish the place. They have built themselves to fit its unfinishedness instead — quiet rooms inside a roaring world, light-talk instead of shouting across distances too vast for the throat, small movable homes instead of permanence staked against a ground that hasn't decided yet what it's going to be.

Ask a Reach-born what they're waiting for, and most will tell you, without much weight on it, that they aren't waiting. The place is not *almost* finished arriving. It is, like them, simply still arriving, and they have learned to live well inside the arriving rather than the arrival.
