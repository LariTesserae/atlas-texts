## The Walkers

They are the only warm-blooded thing the range permits, and even that warmth is a near thing — banked low, metabolism dialed down so far that a Walker at rest could be mistaken for an outcropping, for a fold in the bone-grey slope it stands on. This is not laziness. It is calibration. Everything about a Walker is built to match the patience of the ground it was born onto, except for the one part of it that isn't, and that exception is the whole reason it exists.

They stand chest-high to a person at the shoulder, though no one stands close enough, long enough, to be sure of the measurement. Four legs, too long for the body they carry, jointed in a way that looks wrong until you watch one cross a scree slope without a single misplaced step — then it looks like the only sensible architecture for a creature that may need to cover forty kilometers of vertical terrain between one meal and the next, decades apart. The legs are pale, the color of the rock, the color of old bone, because that is what they are mistaken for ninety percent of the time, by the ninety percent of creatures (and the handful of people) who ever look.

They have no herd because the land has no carrying capacity for one. A Walker's territory is a basin, a ridge-system, sometimes a whole quadrant of the range, and within that territory it eats almost nothing — the slow lichen-threads, cropped so sparingly and so rarely that a single patch can feed the same animal for a human lifetime. Its gut is less a digestive organ than a kind of storage vault, extracting what little there is to extract over months, in no hurry, because hurry is not a thing the ground around it understands.

But cut a Walker open — no one has, or no one who has has said so — and you would find something the ground-logic doesn't explain: a heart that, for a creature this size, this slow, beats far too fast. Not always. Most of the year it idles along with the rest of the animal's banked, geological calm. But the bone of its legs grows in rings, the way certain trees do, and the rings don't correspond to years. They correspond to storms. Each time the sky over the range assembles one of its kilometer-wide pressure-towers and holds it for an hour before unraveling, something in the Walker's marrow takes note, lays down a line, marks the occasion the way a tree marks a winter.

This is because a Walker can feel the storms coming before they exist. Not predict — feel, the way a held breath feels the moment just before it's released. Hours ahead of any visible disturbance, a grazing Walker will lift its head, turn it toward a stretch of empty sky that looks like every other stretch of empty sky, and simply wait. Its pulse climbs. Its long legs, ordinarily deployed at the pace of continental drift, will suddenly cover a ridgeline at a dead run, carrying it toward — never away from — the place where the pressure is about to fold the horizon into one of its standing ripples.

It runs toward the storm to stand beneath it.

No one has stayed long enough to learn why, or has come back able to say. The leading guess — and it is only that — is the same guess people make about the slender black arches and the buried, humming lattices: that the Walkers are not separate from the structures so much as a softer, mortal extension of them. Where the lattices listen at frequencies too low to hear, a Walker's marrow listens at the frequency of weather itself. Where the arches make the air on their far side colder for reasons no instrument can explain, a Walker standing beneath an unraveling pressure-tower will sometimes be found afterward unmarked, untouched, simply *recalibrated* — its banked pulse settled back into geologic time, one more ring laid down in the bone, one more storm catalogued in a library that will never be read by anything that built libraries.

Their eyes are pale and slightly clouded, not from blindness but from a kind of double exposure: built to read both the dead-slow drift of scree and lichen and the violent, kilometer-fast geometry of a sky that hasn't finished deciding what it is yet. It is the only living thing in the Stillward that seems to hold both timescales at once without being torn by the difference — the only flesh, anywhere in the range, doing what the silent black arches and the humming lattices do in stone and unexplainable metal.

This is why a sighting is remembered for years. Not because the animal is rare, though it is. Because for the length of time it takes one to cross a ridgeline at that impossible, sky-startled run, something alive is visibly doing the thing the whole mountain range does in geological secret — straddling the gap between *already finished* and *not yet decided* — and for just that long, a person standing small at the bottom of all that scale gets to watch the argument between ground and sky carried, however briefly, in a body that breathes.
