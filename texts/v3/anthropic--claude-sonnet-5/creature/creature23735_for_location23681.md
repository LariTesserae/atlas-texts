There is one. Perhaps only one — the plateau is small enough, and patient enough, that it would not need more.

It is the size of a sitting person, though it does not sit so much as settle, the way the fallen tree settles, the way water settles into stone. Its back is a shelf of its own — broad, faceted, the colour of wet basalt with the same fine sheen, and over time the same things have happened to it that happen to the rock: moss has taken hold along the ridges where its hide creases, a thin succulent has rooted itself at the base of one shoulder and not died, and water beads on it the way it beads on every leaf-underside here, the way it weeps from every seam. You could stand a few feet from it in the dappled light and read it as outcrop. Most things do. That has always been the arrangement.

It moves the way the seeps swell — not slowly exactly, but on its own clock, one that doesn't intersect with the clock of footsteps or breath or any hunting thing's hunger. It hasn't been hunted. There is nothing here large enough or motivated enough to try, and it has, in any case, the patience to simply not be where the trying happens. Its legs are thick and short and built for bracing against a slope, not covering ground. It does not so much travel the plateau as occupy successive parts of it, the way moss occupies a log, the way a hollow in stone becomes, eventually and without anyone deciding it, a seat.

It drinks at the seeps, lowering a wide flat head with eyes set near the top, more suited to watching mist gather than to seeing anything that moves fast. It eats — sparingly, between long unhungry stretches — the fat-leaved succulents, the soft inner growth of fallen wood, occasionally the slow pale insects that are the only other living things bold enough to share its air. It has, in its life, encountered the dark silent bird perhaps a dozen times. Neither has ever reacted to the other as anything but weather.

It was not the one who struck those fire-cracked stones into being, ash-grey now and half-sunk near the seep. Those came from somewhere else, hands it has perhaps seen, perhaps not, on a visit-cycle slower than its own memory holds whole. But it has lain near that fire-scar many times since, because the ground there drains well and the moss grows soft, and in lying there it has worn the hollow in the rock shelf that you might mistake for a seat, might mistake for erosion, and would be right either way — it is both, the way most things here are both one thing and the slow becoming of another.

The hum does not come from it. It would tell you that, if it had the means or inclination to tell anything anything — it is not the source, has never been the source, has lived its whole long life within the sound the way a held note lives within a room, present at the start and present still, no more its author than the moss is. But it is the one thing on the plateau that has stopped hearing it as sound at all. To the rare visitor, the hum is a strangeness underfoot, a vibration that never fades and so becomes, eventually, its own kind of silence. The creature reached that point long ago — generations of the fallen tree's slow disappearance ago — and crossed past it into something further: the hum, to it, is no longer ground or figure. It is simply the shape the air takes. It would not know how to describe stillness, because it has never stood in any.

It does not know it is alone, because aloneness requires a memory of otherwise, and nothing here has offered it one. It will, in its own time, stop — the way the fallen tree stops, by degrees, without ceremony, taken back into the moss it always half-resembled. Something else may settle here after. Or nothing will, and the hollow in the rock will simply finish becoming erosion, indistinguishable, at last, from the stone that made it.
