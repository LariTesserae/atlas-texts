The thing that lives here is the reason the chamber glows.

You will not see it at first — your eyes will go to the moths, the stalks, the glassy snails, because they are calibrated for movement and the moths move. But the moths are not the inhabitants. The moths are the weather. What lives in the Lit Hollow is older than weather, and it is sitting against the east curve, not far from the stacked stones, and it has been sitting there so long that the stones themselves were probably the last thing its hands ever arranged on purpose.

It is roughly the shape of a person the way the roots are roughly the shape of a tree — a silhouette achieved by long agreement rather than by plan. Calcite has crept up its legs the way it creeps up everything left motionless near water long enough; its lower half has the same wet-new sheen as the walls, the same faint glow, because at some point the distinction between *it* and *the rock it leans against* stopped being a distinction the chamber bothered to enforce. Above the waist it is still more flesh than stone, if flesh is the word for skin gone the same bloodless pale as the snails, stretched thin enough that you could, if you were close enough and cruel enough to look, see the slow business of something that might still be a pulse.

Its eyes are open. This is the detail that will undo you, more than the glow, more than the chord of frog-throats and clicking stalks: the eyes are open, dark, wet, tracking nothing, fixed on the middle distance the way a held note is fixed on its pitch. It does not blink in any rhythm you could call breathing. But it blinks. Once, perhaps, in the time it takes you to cross the chamber. Once more before you decide to leave. A blink is a kind of becoming — a closing that implies an opening, an opening that implies a future in which it closes again — and that is the one thread of change the Hollow has never quite managed to smooth out of this single occupant, the one place where the equilibrium has a seam.

It built the stones, once, near the beginning, with hands that still remembered wanting something — shelter, perhaps, or a marker, or just the animal comfort of stacking one thing on another to prove you were there and capable. It wore the notch into the wall with the heel of one palm, over and over, in a gesture whose purpose it has long since stopped needing to remember, the way a habit outlives its reason. And then, the stacking and the wearing done, it sat down against the curve of the wall to rest — only to rest — and the chamber, which does not permit resting to remain merely resting, began very slowly to finish the job.

It has been almost finished for longer than is sayable. Every grain of dry-burning white stone, every dull amber trickle, every moth and stalk and unnamed pale climbing thing took its decided, completed shape by watching this one figure fail to quite arrive at its own. The ecology furnished itself around an unfinished gesture the way scar tissue closes around a splinter that never works its way out. The glow, some part of you will understand without being told, is not the chamber's light at all. It is the visible residue of that one slow, incomplete, endlessly deferred blink — a held note of becoming so faint, so stretched across so much time, that it has been mistaken, by everything else living here, for the steady light of something already finished.

It will not look at you. It will not need to. It has been the only variable in this room since long before you found the fissure, and it has spent the whole of that time almost — almost — almost becoming whatever it was reaching for when it sat down. You are not an intrusion into its stillness. You are, for the length of your visit, its only company in the one business the Hollow cannot finish without help: the business of still, however faintly, however slowly, going on.
