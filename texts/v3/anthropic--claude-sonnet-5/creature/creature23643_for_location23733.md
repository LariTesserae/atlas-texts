The thing to understand about Mossen Tarl-Eight-Reckoning is that nobody, including Mossen, is entirely sure how old he is, because the metric by which the Lignate Heights measure age was never built for things that move.

He is the eighth person to hold the Reckoning—a title, not a name exactly, though by now it has become indistinguishable from one—and the title exists because someone, generations back, decided that a place whose architecture grows slower than its politics needs at least one office dedicated to *keeping track*. Mossen keeps track. That is the whole of the job, and it has eaten the whole of his life, in the unbothered way that jobs do, here, where a life and a job and a stretch of riverless decades all run on the same patient clock.

He lives—if *lives* is the word for an arrangement this settled—in the seventh hollowed ring of a heartwood archive on the lower terrace of a town called, in the old tongue, something that translates roughly to *the place that listened first*. His rooms were not built. They were asked for, by some predecessor four Reckonings back, and the wood has spent the centuries since slowly complying, which means his study is not finished and will not be finished and Mossen has long since stopped expecting it to be, in the same way he's stopped expecting his own joints to stop aching, which they do, faintly, in the cool, the way the wood aches, the way everything up here aches in that low companionable register that isn't quite pain.

He is small and spare and a little bowed, the way people get who have spent a working life climbing root-stair instead of walking flat ground, and his hands are stained the deep grey-brown of bark-pigment from sixty years of pressing his palm flat against trunk-faces to read growth-intention the way his predecessors taught him—not with the tools, the field-readers the engineers left behind and that still, somehow, work, but the old way, skin to grain, waiting for the wood to tell him what it means to do next. He does this constantly, compulsively, a hand laid flat against any surface he passes, the way another person might absently check a pulse. In a sense that's exactly what it is.

His actual duty—the one the title names—is to keep the Long Ledger, a structure that is not a book but a *cultivar*, a deliberately stunted, deliberately legible strain of growth-wood that the Reckonings have spent two centuries training to hold shape long enough to be read, ring by ring, each ring a year, each year's events pressed into the growing wood by means Mossen does not fully understand and does not need to, the way you don't need to understand a hinge to use a door. He reads it. He adds to it. He corrects, gently, the small disagreements between what the wood remembers and what the people of the place insist happened, and mostly he lets the wood win, because in his experience the wood is rarely lying and the people are rarely doing anything else.

He is, by the standards of the lowland visitors who occasionally climb this far—engineers' descendants, mostly, chasing rumors of the misting arrays that still inexplicably run—a deeply unhurried man, prone to long silences that aren't quite rudeness, more a kind of drift, his attention sliding off toward some creak in the middle distance that means something to him and nothing to anyone else. He'll tell you, if you ask and wait long enough for the answer, that he's listening to the exhale—the big slow one, the one that comes every several minutes from somewhere no one's ever found, the one the towns have stopped reacting to. He hasn't stopped reacting to it. That's the part he doesn't mention. Eight Reckonings deep into this office and he still tenses, faintly, every time, the held breath of a man waiting for a held breath to end, because somewhere in the Ledger—seventy rings down, soft with age, barely legible—there is a year recorded in which it didn't come on schedule, came late, came wrong, and nobody before him ever wrote down what that meant, only that it happened, only that the wood was frightened too.

He does not expect to solve it. He is not sure the question is his to solve. He is the eighth Reckoning of perhaps an endless line of them, in a place where nothing is finished growing, and his real work, when he is honest with himself in the green-grey dimness of his unfinished study, hand flat against a wall four hundred years from done—is not to keep track of the Heights at all.

It's to notice, slowly, the way everything here notices things, if the Heights are starting to keep track of him.
