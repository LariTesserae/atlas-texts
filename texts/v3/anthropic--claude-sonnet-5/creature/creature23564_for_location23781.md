What lives there learned the rhythm before it learned anything else. That comes first, before eyes, before legs even — the forty-second pulse is in the egg, in the larva, in whatever passes here for infancy, a count kept by something more like instinct than memory. You can see it in the thing that matters most in that hollow, the one that has claimed the ring between scalding and freezing as its whole world: a low, segmented creature, knee-high at most, the size and rough silhouette of something between a salamander and a boar, moving on six limbs that splay wide for purchase on wet stone. Its skin is the same wet-leather grey as the amphibians at the pool edge but plated faintly along the spine, not armor exactly, more like the rock itself has started to grow back onto it in thin overlapping scales, picked up from years — no, months — of lying in mineral-rich steam until the residue crusted and the body simply incorporated it. It is, in a small way, made partly of the place.

It does not hunt by sight. The fog forbids that, and besides, sight is a poor sense thirty meters deep in dishwater light. It hunts by the vent-clock. It positions itself at the threshold of a crack just before the discharge, when the warmth starts climbing but the steam hasn't yet broken the surface, and it waits there — utterly still, plated back blending into the boulders — for the insects that flee upward ahead of the heat, for the bare-skinned rodent-things that bolt their tunnels a half-second too late, for anything that has misjudged the count. Then it strikes low and fast, jaw first, a hinge wider than its head should allow, and is gone again into a fern-pocket before the actual vapor breaks the rock. It eats in the eye of the cycle the way gulls work a wave just ahead of the breaking foam.

There are not many of them — six, eight, in a hollow this size, no more, because the ring of habitable warmth is too narrow to hold more mouths than that without the smaller life collapsing under the pressure of being eaten. They don't fight each other for it. There's a loose order, something almost companionable, vent-claims passed without much violence, because the engine below produces more cracks than there are creatures to claim them, at least for now, at least this season. They cross paths at the black pools sometimes, lower their heads to the iridescent skin of the water, drink without ceremony, and part. There is no nest. The young — small, soft, unplated, alarmingly vulnerable in a place built on narrow margins — are simply produced in clutches tucked into crevices warm enough to incubate but shielded from direct vapor, and a startling number of them die in the first weeks, boiled or frozen by getting the count wrong, and this is treated, if treated is the word for something with no apparent grief, as unremarkable. The ones who keep the rhythm survive to grow the plates. The ones who don't, don't.

What's strange, if you watch long enough, is the lack of urgency in any of it despite the violence. The bird-things squabbling in the high crevices, the amphibians at the pool lip gulping mist, the rodent-things ducking down their fern-tunnels, the plated hunters timing their strikes to the second — none of it has the panic you'd expect from animals living this close to scalding death on one side and exposure on the other. It reads, instead, as administrative. Busy. Everyone going about a shift on a factory floor that nobody supervises, clocking in and out of the vent-cycle with the unbothered competence of creatures who have simply never known a world that worked any other way, because for them there has never been any other world — this hollow, this rhythm, this band of habitable warmth between hot below and cold above, is the entire and total history of their kind.

None of them know — have any way of knowing, any organ for knowing — that the rock under their feet is younger than some of their own grandparents would have been, had grandparents lived that long. None of them know the vent might seal itself next year, or crack wider and swallow the basin in superheated stone, or simply cool, the way the heat-body below eventually exhausts whatever it's burning and goes quiet, and the moss dies back, and the fog lifts for the first time in anyone's memory, and the cold finally wins all the way down to the rock. They will not see it coming. They were never built to. They are creatures of a forty-second cycle, dense and busy and brief, thriving with everything they have in a ring of warmth that was never promised to last, and they have organized the whole of their loud, plated, hungry little lives around a machine with no operator, in a room with no walls, that could go quiet between one breath and the next — and not one of them, plated hunter or soft-skinned young, has any reason to stop and listen for it.
