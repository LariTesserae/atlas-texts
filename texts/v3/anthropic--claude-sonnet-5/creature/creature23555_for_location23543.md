# What Lives in the Drowned Terraces

## The Tenders

You will see one before you understand what you're seeing—a shape on a middle terrace, motionless in a posture that looks at first like erosion, like a particularly suggestive slump of the pale gray-green stone. Then it moves, and the movement is the wrongness that tells you it was never stone at all.

They are tall in the way driftwood is tall—long-limbed past any proportion that would suit walking, and indeed they do not quite walk so much as redistribute themselves across a surface, four or six or sometimes what looks like eight points of contact touching down in a rolling sequence too smooth to count. Their skin, if it is skin, has the same matte, weathered quality as the architecture itself, a pale gray-green with the faintest sheen when wet, which is always. It is not clear where Tender ends and terrace begins until one of them peels itself upright, and even then the boundary feels provisional, like a decision rather than a fact.

They have no faces you would recognize as faces. Where a face would be there is a soft concavity ringed with small dark openings, never less than seven, never arranged the same way twice between individuals—not eyes, not quite, but something that orients toward instability the way a sunflower orients toward light. A Tender will stand on a platform that seems, to you, perfectly sound, and then turn its concavity toward some far terrace and simply wait, unhurried, until minutes or hours later that terrace exhales and folds into the lake below. They know before the architecture knows. Or perhaps they are how the architecture knows—a nervous system grown loose and motile and shed from the stone it once served, still listening for the building's pain on its behalf long after whoever required that listening has gone.

They do not seem to build. They do not seem to repair. What they do, as far as you can tell across long patient observation, is *witness*—arriving at a site of imminent collapse with no urgency, settling into stillness nearby, and remaining through the fall, after which they sometimes reach into the new rubble with limbs that lengthen disconcertingly past their resting proportions, drawing out something pale and limp and still—one of the many-jointed clinging things, or an eel too slow to flee the turbulence—and consuming it without any motion you'd call eating, a kind of absorption more than a bite. Then they move on, toward the next terrace whose failure they have already, somehow, anticipated.

You never see them in groups larger than two. You never see them communicate, though sometimes two Tenders will stand near each other for a long while, concavities angled in parallel toward the same distant instability, before separating again without any signal you can name. Whether they are the diminished remnant of whoever raised these terraces, or a species that arrived after, parasitic on the architecture's slow dying, or something stranger—a kind of immune response the place generates for itself, grown flesh out of stressed stone—is not a question anyone has answered. They do not seem to mind not being understood. They do not seem to register you at all, in fact, except as one more unstable thing among many, worth a glance from the dark openings, not worth changing course for.

## The Small Lives

Beneath the Tenders' slow patient witnessing, the rest of the ecology runs its more frantic business.

The eel-ropes in the flooded lower terraces are not one species but at least three, distinguishable mainly by the particular quality of disturbance they make in still water—some a long unbroken ripple, some a series of small distinct flexions, like a held thought interrupting itself. They are blind, colorless, longer than seems structurally reasonable, and they feed upward into the collapse zones with an instinct for timing that rivals the Tenders'—you can tell a terrace is about to fail by watching the water at its base go suddenly, attentively still, ropes gathering just beneath the surface, waiting for what the fall will deliver to them.

The flock-things overhead—soft-bodied, membrane-winged, riding the downdrafts that collapsing terraces throw into the air—travel in numbers that shift constantly, splitting and rejoining the way smoke does, never resolving into anything you'd call a formation. Up close, on the rare occasions one rests on a railing-stub or wedged stone lip, you can see that their "wings" are not wings at all but a single continuous fold of pale tissue, almost translucent, threaded through with a faint branching darkness that might be vessels or might be something else entirely—nerve, maybe, given how immediately the whole fold twitches at the sound of distant stone giving way, the entire creature flinching toward a collapse it cannot yet have any sensory evidence for.

The many-jointed clingers under the overhangs are the least understood of all the visible fauna, because they are almost never visible doing anything. You find them by their absence as much as their presence—a smooth patch of overhang stone where one was, hours ago, when you happened to glance up; the place vacated by the time you look back. Up close they resemble nothing so much as a closed hand made of too many small hard plates, dark, glistening, utterly still, and the few times one has been seen in motion it has been at terrible speed, crossing meters of wet stone in less time than it takes to register, vanishing into a crevice or under the black surface of a flood-pool before the mind finishes the sentence *that moved.*

## What Is Below

There is something else, deeper, that almost no one claims to have truly seen—only its evidence. A vast slow disturbance beneath the surface of the deep flooded terraces, a displacement of water too large and too patient to belong to any eel, a pressure-change felt in the ears before any visual confirmation arrives, gone by the time you look properly. The Tenders, on the rare occasions they stand at the very edge of the black water rather than on the dry stepped ground, seem to orient their concavities downward, into it, with the same unhurried attentiveness they give a failing terrace—as though whatever is down there is simply one more slow collapse, occurring on a scale of centuries instead of hours, and they are, as ever, only here to witness it.

What lives in the drowned terraces of Ven-Kor, in the end, is a single patient ecology built entirely around falling: things that sense the fall before it happens, things that feed on what the fall delivers, things that watch the fall without ever once attempting to stop it. Nothing here resists the place's slow undoing. Everything here has simply learned, across some unimaginable span, to live *inside* it—to treat the architecture's perpetual quiet collapse not as catastrophe but as weather, as season, as the single dependable rhythm in a place that otherwise offers none.
