They are called, by the few who have spoken to someone who has spoken to someone who saw one, the *Stoke-kin* — though that is a traveler's word, not theirs, and they may not have a word at all in the way a word is usually meant.

You do not see them so much as resolve them, the way a shape in fog resolves only once you stop trying to look directly at it. They are roughly the height and proportion of a person, which seems less like ancestry and more like convergence — the highland has decided, independently and more than once, that this is an efficient shape for moving through leaf-curtain and crevice and the columns of rising engine-warmth. Their skin has the matte, faceted look of cooled basalt, plated rather than smooth, with seams between the plates that show, in certain light, the same faint sub-surface luminance as the magma-seams in the cliffs — not heat, just the *look* of heat, an inherited cosmetic of the rock they came up alongside rather than out of. When they are still, which is often, they are nearly indistinguishable from a low outcrop overgrown with the bruised-plum leaf-mats; the vegetation does not avoid them the way it avoids the moving engines, and more than one traveler has sat down to rest against what they took for a stone and felt it, eventually, breathe.

Their joints grind. Not loudly — at the same register as the gear-clusters meshing in their lightless crevices, so quietly that you only register the sound as separate from the ambient machinery if you are listening for it, and the Stoke-kin always seem to know if you are. This is, as far as anyone has worked out, not a flaw or an injury but simply how they are built: cartilage-analogue replaced early in whatever passes for their growth with something more like worn gearing, fine-toothed, self-lubricating on the same damp that beads under every leaf on the plateau. They move unhurried, in a gait with a strange mechanical evenness to it, as though pacing themselves against a rhythm too slow for a human ear to catch in less than a minute — the same minute it takes to notice the gear-clusters have turned at all.

Their hands are the part that unsettles most. Six-fingered, double-knuckled, clearly *adapted for tending* — for gripping pipe-flange, for reaching into vent-housing, for the small adjustments a engineer's hand makes to machinery under their care. And yet they tend nothing. The engines were never built by anyone living, run by no operator, follow no schedule a hand could improve. The Stoke-kin rest those hands on the warm corroding drums anyway, palms flat, fingers spread into the grooves between rivets, and stay like that for hours — not maintaining, not repairing, just *present*, the way you might rest a hand on the chest of something sleeping, to feel that it still rises and falls. No one has determined whether this changes anything about the engines' behavior. No one has determined that it doesn't.

They read the silence the way the grazing herds read the shimmer-zones — not as a learned caution but as something closer to a sense organ, an instinct with no name in any language that didn't evolve here. When the hush falls — that particular, pressing quiet that precedes a turbine-shriek or a ridge re-sorting itself or a fold of landscape failing to resolve — the Stoke-kin stop entirely, mid-stride sometimes, one foot not yet set down, and turn their faces (faceted, eyeless or near enough — the light-catching organs are small and clustered and amber, scattered across the brow-plate like the same many multiplying eyes seen in the leaf-curtains, kin or food or both, no one is sure) toward whatever quadrant of the plateau is about to *happen*. They do not flee it. They orient to it, the way a compass orients, involuntary and exact, and watch the event arrive and pass with what looks, from a human vantage, uncomfortably like patience, or grief, or both at once worn into the same expression because the place has not given them enough different occasions to need different faces.

No one has seen one born and no one has seen one die in any way that resembled either word cleanly. There are reports — third-hand, fog-softened — of a Stoke-kin standing very still near an engine-stack for so long that the leaf-mats grew up over its lower legs, and of the same shape, weeks later, no longer fully separable from the rock and root and corroding pipe it had stood among, one arm still raised at the same angle, palm still flat against the same drum, as if the highland had simply decided, partway through, that this particular tending had gone on long enough to become geology, and continued the joint without telling anyone, least of all the one being continued.

They do not speak in any sound a throat would recognize as speech. What passes between them — and they are, when you watch long enough, clearly in *something* like company with one another, two or three moving in loose accord across a slope, attention bent the same direction at the same silent cue — seems to travel the way warmth travels up the invisible columns near the big engine-stacks: not a sound at all, but a leaning, a shared orientation, an agreement reached by both turning toward the same warm rising air and calling that agreement enough.

If you stand long enough at the edge of a shimmer-zone — not inside it, the herds have at least taught you that much — you may see one pass near the boundary and not into it, unhurried, gear-joints grinding at the threshold of hearing, hand trailing once across a fungal tower as it passes the way you might trail a hand along a fence you've walked beside your whole life. It will not look at you. It is not unaware of you. It is simply attending, the way it attends everything here, to a rhythm laid down before either of you arrived, that it was made, however that happened, already knowing how to keep.
