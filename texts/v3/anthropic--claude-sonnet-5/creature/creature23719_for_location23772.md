The curved-billed bird is called, by the handful of people who've needed a word for it, a seep-thrush, though it isn't a thrush and the name is just what stuck. It is small enough to disappear against wet stone — mottled grey-brown, the exact tonal range of charcoal-stained rock under cloud-light — and built almost entirely around that one curved tool of a bill, long and thin and slightly down-hooked, evolved for one purpose: working the black seep-lines that run down every tower face, probing the algal film and the threadworms and midge-larvae that live nowhere else in this country but in that permanent skin of moving water. It walks more than it flies. Flight, here, is expensive — there's little thermal lift off cold wet stone, and the gaps between towers are wind-funnels that punish anything trying to cross them — so the bird picks its way along ledges and seep-channels on legs a little too long for its body, head ticking side to side, bill dipping into the film every few steps like a stitching needle. Its call, the single dry note that takes so long to die in the open air, isn't song. It's a spacing call, a way of telling the next seep-thrush over — and there's rarely more than one per district, the food too thin to support density — that this drainage is taken. They pair only briefly, in the wet season's heaviest weeks, and nest in old drainage cavities worn smooth by decades of water, lining them with lichen pulled in sheets from the lower walls. The chicks fledge fast, hard, and scatter immediately; there isn't enough here to hold a family together past necessity.

The pale animal is harder to know, and that's by design rather than accident. It's a small predator, fox-shaped because that shape — long body, low to the ground, broad soft feet — is what works on wet stone and grit, but it isn't kin to any fox; call it what crosses the plateau calls it, a mist-fox, and leave the taxonomy to people with more time than the place affords them. Its coat is pale not for camouflage against snow, because there isn't any, but because the towers themselves run pale in the flat grey light, bone-white and pearl, and a coat that color simply stops registering against that backdrop at any distance past twenty meters. Its feet are wide, padded, faintly webbed at the toe — not for swimming, but for purchase on the constant film of water that sits on every stone surface, the same film that would have any harder-footed animal sliding. It hunts the same seep-lines the bird works, lower down, where the threadworms thicken and the moss-seams hide things with shells. It is mostly nocturnal, not from any fear of light but because the cold rain that falls in the dark hours brings the lichen's small tenants — slow mites, pale woodlice-analogues, things with no good common name — up to the surface to feed, and the mist-fox simply follows the food's schedule. It dens in the hollows beneath fallen slabs, in the rare dry pockets the rockfall leaves, and it is solitary except in the deep wet season, when for a few weeks two will travel together before separating again, the female denning alone afterward in whatever cavity is driest. Nobody has ever gotten a clean look at one for longer than a few seconds. This isn't elusiveness so much as economy — there is so little of anything out here that every encounter is brief by necessity, predator and prey and accidental witness alike spending as little time as possible exposed on the open stone before the rain finds them.

Beneath both of them, holding the whole sparse structure up, is the unglamorous mass of small life that never gets named at all: the threadworms living in the seep-film itself, translucent, barely a centimeter, feeding on the bacterial mat that grows wherever water moves slow enough to warm half a degree above the surrounding stone; the mites and springtails working the lichen sheets from underneath, eating the soft growing edge where the lichen meets bare rock; small crustacean-like things, almost shrimp, that have found a way to live out their whole life cycle in pools that never quite dry — laying eggs that can survive a hard freeze, hatching out again the moment the water softens. None of this is abundant. All of it is enough, just barely, stacked thin layer on thin layer the way the towers themselves are stacked, mineral sheet on mineral sheet, until something — a bird, a fox-shaped shadow, a film of crustaceans no bigger than a fingernail — manages to stand on top of it and call itself alive.

What lives in the Standing Districts, in other words, lives the way the Districts themselves were built: by patience, by working a thin resource for a very long time, by asking very little of a place that has very little to give and has never pretended otherwise. There is no abundance here, no teeming. There is persistence, which is a different thing, and harder to see, and the only thing this country has ever rewarded.
