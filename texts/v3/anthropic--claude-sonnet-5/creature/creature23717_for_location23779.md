## What Lives in the Standing Thickets

Start small, because the place insists on it, then let your eye climb.

At ground level, where the herds pass, the air smells of crushed bromeliad and warm clay. The grazers are not subtle animals but they are not crude ones either — long-bodied, low-slung things, somewhere between tapir and tortoise in the logic of their build, armored along the spine with overlapping plates of keratin gone soft and leaf-green with age, host to their own small ecosystems of moss and biting fly. They move in family-herds of thirty to eighty, matriarch-led, and they have never once needed to look up, because nothing that matters to them lives in the canopy. Their eyes sit low and wide-set, built for reading the ground six feet ahead, for finding the soft mat of vine-fall that means easy footing. They are patient browsers and incurious migrants, walking the same avenues their grandmothers walked, and what passes for herd memory here is really just worn clay — they go where the path already is, and the path is there because they went.

Up at the threshold, in the dim mid-canopy where light drops by half, is where the place gets a mind.

Call them — because someone eventually will, in whatever language gets spoken here first — the **threadwalkers**. They are the ones who scatter upward in silent waves when something serious happens thirty meters off the path, and they are also, often, the something. Built narrow and long-limbed, with a membrane running wrist to ankle that they fold tight against the body when climbing and snap open when crossing — gliders, but unhurried ones, more interested in distance than speed, covering an avenue-gap in one long unhurried arc with the wings barely cupping air. Their hands are the real architecture of their lives: four fingers and an opposable thumb on each, but also a second, smaller opposable digit on the heel of the palm, evolved for exactly one purpose, which is gripping wet bark without looking. They almost never look at their hands. They look at where they're going.

They are not loud because the place doesn't reward loud. Their calls — when they call at all — are low frictive clicks pitched to travel through wood rather than air, a vibration sent into a branch and felt by the next threadwalker's feet rather than heard by ears, so that an entire crown-district can pass warning or welcome to its neighbors without making a single sound a grazer thirty meters below would ever notice. This is not language exactly. It is closer to the structural groan of the root-towers themselves settling — the threadwalkers learned their voice from the building they live in, or maybe it's the other way around, and after enough centuries the distinction stops mattering.

They live in family-knots of six to twenty, denned in the hollow crowns where aerial roots have curled into natural chambers, and they keep gardens — actual gardens, tended with hands and patience rather than tools, since there are no tools. A threadwalker den will strip back competing vine growth from a chosen patch of trunk to favor a particular fungal shelf, will carry overripe fruiting bodies from one tree to seed another, will, over generations, shape which bromeliad-analogues thicken around their own entrance and which thin out, until the den's threshold becomes recognizably *theirs* without a single mark made by claw or tooth — only by selective neglect and selective care, applied for so long it reads as architecture. It is the same logic that built the towers, run one size class down, on purpose instead of by accident, and the threadwalkers may be the only thing in the basin that knows it's doing this.

They do not hunt the grazers below — too big, too slow-witted to be worth the risk, and besides, the grazers are not their register. What hunts the threadwalkers, when something does, comes from the same mid-canopy dimness they keep to: a thing rarely seen whole, fast in short bursts and otherwise nearly stationary, colored exactly like the lichen-and-shadow pattern of an old trunk, which is why no one sees the kills, only the scattering. The threadwalkers have a word-that-isn't-a-word for this — a particular four-click pattern sent foot-deep into a branch — and the whole denned crown goes still when it's felt, the way the text already noticed: a stillness that follows wherever something serious has just happened thirty meters off the path.

Higher still, in the canopy-top districts where the towers punch through into open sky and grow their own private weather, almost nothing lives that you'd call an animal at all in the usual sense, and this is where the place stops being legible to a ground-eyed visitor. There are things up there that may simply be very old, very slow threadwalkers who stopped coming down — denned so long in one high crown, fed by such a stable fog-condensation drip, that their membranes have gone stiff and leaf-mottled and fused half-shut, more lichen now than animal, sitting in the mist at the top of a ninety-meter tower the way a gargoyle sits on a real cathedral: once a participant, now a fixture, still faintly, glacially alive.

And underneath all of it, on the raw bone-pale stone the roots have only begun to colonize, there is one more inhabitant worth naming, though it isn't an animal and barely qualifies as a plant: a slow-spreading lichen-and-microbial crust, gray-gold, that is the actual leading edge of the forest's conquest of the ground — the first thing to land on virgin stone, the thing that makes the first skin of soil that lets the first root take hold that becomes, four hundred years later, the base of a tower eighty meters tall. It has no name anyone living up in the crowns would think to give it. It is simply what arrives first, every time, on every fresh plate of cracked stone, doing on the scale of centimeters exactly what the towers do on the scale of kilometers: pressing outward, patiently, against a neighbor, until pressing is indistinguishable from building.

That, really, is the whole population of Murn Hollow, top to bottom: grazers who don't look up, threadwalkers who garden in the dark with their hands, something unseen that hunts at the threshold, old denned things gone half to lichen at the misted crowns, and a gray-gold crust doing, in miniature and at the very start of everything, the same patient pressing that built the rest. No rulers. No center. Just every register of the same one habit, repeated at whatever scale the local light and food and time allow — which is, on reflection, also a fair description of how the forest itself came to be a city in the first place.
