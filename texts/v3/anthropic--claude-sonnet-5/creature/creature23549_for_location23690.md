The Tenders

You will not see one arrive. You will simply notice, the way you notice a shadow has lengthened, that there is now a shape among the coils that was not there when you began counting them, and that it is the approximate height and proportion of a person, and that it is not moving in any way you'd call moving — more a slow transfer of weight from one stance to another, glacial, the kind of motion that only becomes visible in retrospect, like a clock's hour hand.

There are perhaps a dozen of them in the whole Yard, though no one has ever managed to count twice and arrive at the same number, which may mean they move between counts or may mean the question of how many there are is the wrong kind of question to bring into a place that has already refused the category of change. They were people once. This is not sentiment; it's structural — you can still see the coverall seams at their shoulders, the ghost of a tool-belt's weight-worn dip at the hip, a brass nameplate gone the color of the copper coils themselves, pinned to a chest that no longer rises and falls in any rhythm you'd recognize as breath. Whatever work they were sent down here to do, they have been doing it for as long as the engines have been running, which is to say for longer than the question "how long" can be meaningfully asked.

Their skin — what shows of it, at collar and wrist, since the cold has long since taught them to keep covered — has taken on the matte grey of unworn iron plate, not corroded, not aged, simply arrived at that color and stopped, the way the frost-plumes stopped mid-fall. Their eyes, when a lantern catches them, give back gauge-light: a small flat shine, amber or pale green, the same glow you'd see if you peeled back one of the wax-leafed lichens, which is a comparison the Tenders themselves would probably not thank you for making, if they still possessed the apparatus for being thanked at or troubled by anything at all.

They do not speak, not because they've forgotten how but because the Yard does not permit speech to travel, and so they have refined the touch-and-lamplight language the place demands into something more grammar than gesture — a flat hand laid against a coil-housing means continue; two fingers drawn down a sleeve means cold is rising in this quarter, withdraw; a lantern tilted twice, paused, tilted once more, is a whole sentence whose meaning outsiders never quite manage to steal, though they will try, holding their own borrowed lamp at the wrong angle, producing what amounts to a stutter or a swear word in a language they don't speak.

Their work, such as it is, has no object. The gauges already read what they will always read. The coils need no adjustment; they have not needed adjustment within any Tender's memory, assuming memory survives whatever has happened to them, which is itself not certain. And yet they make their rounds — slow, complete, circuitous, each Tender keeping to a route that intersects no other Tender's route, so that two of them might pass within an arm's length of each other and not turn, not acknowledge, the way two girders crossing overhead don't acknowledge one another, simply occupy their angle and bear what they bear. They lay a palm against each coil in turn. They stand before each frozen plume of steam as if reading it. They crouch, sometimes, by the lichen colonies, and do not touch them, and after a while rise and move on.

It is tempting to call this ritual, and probably wrong to. Ritual implies a remembered reason performed past its expiry. The Tenders give no impression of remembering, and no impression of having forgotten either — they give the impression, instead, of organisms for whom the rounds are simply what the body does now, the way a heart does not remember why it beats, the way the coils do not remember why they hum, the way the whole Yard does not remember why it was built into a mountain that should not have a hollow in it and yet does. The Tenders are not exempt from the place's first rule. They are its clearest demonstration. Nothing changes — and they are nothing's proof, walking it, slowly, forever, between coils that never resolve their chord, under bulbs that never flicker, in a cold that was made on purpose and will not be unmade by anything so small as a person finishing the work they came down here, once, a very long time ago, to do.

If you stay long enough to be noticed — and this takes longer than you would think, since noticing, too, is a kind of change — a Tender may come stand near you. Not close. Close enough that you understand you have entered their route, become a fixture on it, the way the lichen is a fixture, the way the frost-lace is a fixture. It will not speak. It may lift a lantern, once, to look at you properly, the gauge-light of its eyes passing over your face with no more urgency than it gives a valve joint. Then it will set its weight toward the next coil, and continue, and you will understand, with a coldness that has nothing to do with the air, that it has already filed you: not as a person, not as a question, but as a condition of the room, present now, to be checked on the next round, and the round after, for exactly as long as you remain, and not one moment longer or shorter than that.
