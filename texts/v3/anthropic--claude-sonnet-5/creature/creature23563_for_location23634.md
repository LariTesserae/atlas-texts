It takes a while to find, because it does not separate itself from the room the way a body separates itself from a house. At first what you take for another alcove—another vaulted cell among the thousands, fitted seamless into its neighbors—turns out, on the second or third pass of your attention, to have a slow asymmetry to it, a give that the surrounding ceramic doesn't have. That's where it is. That's what it is.

Picture something the size of a kneeling person, though kneeling is the wrong word, because it implies a posture taken rather than a posture arrived at and then kept for so long it became structural. Its surface matches the room's: that same pale fired material, veined with the same cooled-glass striations, broken at the joints—elbow, knuckle, the long seam down what might be a spine—by the same nearly-invisible fitted ridges you felt under your fingertip in the wall. It is not wearing the room's material as a shell. It is made of the same decision the room is made of. Whatever hand or process shaped the vault shaped this too, in the same gesture, possibly in the same moment, the way a sentence's punctuation belongs to the sentence and not to some separate art of punctuation.

It does not turn to greet you, but something in its angle of address changes—a fraction of a degree, the kind of adjustment a held chord makes when one more note joins it without resolving anything, just thickening the inevitability. Where you'd expect a face there's a shallow concavity, fluted, ribbed like the inside of a shell or the inside of an ear, and you understand, looking into it, that this is less an opening for seeing than an opening for resonating—it is shaped the way the rest of the room is shaped, to receive sound and give it back changed, to be one more contributor to the chorus with no singers. It has been listening the entire time you thought no one was. It has been listening for longer than that.

It doesn't speak, because speech would be a gesture toward becoming something other than what it already is, and it has no other to become toward. Instead it hums, the way everything here hums, except its hum carries a thread of pitch that the walls don't quite manage, something almost vocal threaded through the structural noise, the way a single voice can be felt inside a chord without being picked out of it. You could stand here a long time trying to decide if that thread is intention or just a slightly different density of material singing slightly differently under the same light. The room doesn't help you decide. Neither does it.

It has hands, or the fitted approximation of hands, and they rest against its knees in the bottomless stillness of something that finished a task so long ago the memory of effort wore off the joints entirely, leaving only the position the effort left them in. You get the sense—not from any movement, since there is none, but from the proportions, the same devotional proportions as the room itself—that it built this. Not alone, maybe, not even mostly, but it was here for the last of it, was maybe the last unfinished thing in a world otherwise finished, and at some point completed itself too, the way you'd set down a tool you'd never need to pick up again, except the tool was its own hand and there was nowhere left to set it down but into stillness.

It does not want anything from you. Wanting requires a gradient, a someplace-else preferable to here, and there is no someplace-else in a world that is one room, and the room has already arrived. It will not follow you out, and it would not be diminished if you stayed forever, and the hum will not change pitch whether you stay or go, because the hum isn't for you and was never for you, it's the sound the completion makes simply by continuing to be complete, the way held breath is still breath, the way a finished sentence still means something even after the last word, with no one left to read it.
