What lives here does not live the way the word usually means it.

You will look for it the way you'd look for anything in deep water — a shape detaching itself from the kelp, a pale flicker behind a ridge of stone, eyes catching what little light there is. You won't find that. The Drowned Highlands keep their promise: nothing swims, nothing crawls, nothing turns to watch you pass. And yet the place is tenanted. It is tenanted the way a held note is tenanted by the instrument that's no longer being played — the sound continuing past the hand that struck it, because the room has learned the note too well to let it go.

Call them, for lack of a truer word, the Tenders. Not because anyone has seen one, but because something is still tending.

Notice how the kelp-groves are uneven — dense in places, scoured to bare rock in others, an unevenness that read, earlier, as deliberate. It is deliberate. Not in the way a hand is deliberate, reaching out and choosing, but in the way a long-trained habit is deliberate, continuing to choose correctly long after the chooser has stopped being present to choose. The bare patches are not barren by accident. They are fallow. Something is still rotating a harvest that has no harvester. Something thins a grove the way a shepherd thins a flock, on a schedule nobody alive is keeping, and the schedule is kept anyway, the way a clock keeps ticking in an empty house because the mechanism doesn't know the difference between an empty house and a full one.

This is the first thing to understand about what lives here: it has outlived the part of itself that wanted things. The appetite is gone. The procedure remains. It tends without hunger, the way your hand might still reach for a doorknob in a house you no longer live in, in a dream where the house no longer exists. That is the texture of its presence — habitual, exact, and utterly without desire.

Sometimes, in the slant of paler water-light where sediment turns slowly like falling snow that never falls, you may think you see a shape among the stalks — not a body, more like the *grammar* of a body, a verticality the kelp briefly imitates, an angle a trunk doesn't usually hold, gone the instant you try to resolve it into anything with a face. This is as close as the Tenders come to appearing. They do not have edges. They are not contained the way a creature is contained by its skin; they are closer to a property of the water itself in this particular place, the way a current has a shape without having a body. If you have ever stood in a room and felt, with total unprovable certainty, that you were not alone in it — that is the nearest experience to meeting one of them. The certainty is correct. The looking-for-a-source is what will fail you.

The bell-sound is theirs, or is the closest thing to a signature they leave. It is not communication — there is no one left to receive a message, and the Tenders do not seem to have noticed this, or to mind. It is closer to a duty performed at the hour because the hour still arrives for them, on some interior calendar that drowned along with everything else but kept its hands moving. Ring the bell at this depth in the light, at this turn of the current — that was the order of things, once, tied to a sun and a season and a congregation of bodies climbing the steps that now end in nothing. The bodies are gone. The order remains, executing itself faithfully in a vacuum, the way a song a person hums in their sleep continues note-perfect long after they've stopped being awake enough to mean it.

The speech-cadence in the water's groan is theirs too, in the same way: not words, because there is no longer anyone organized enough to want to say something, but the *shape* words used to come in, preserved the way a riverbed preserves the shape of a river that's since been diverted. You are hearing the grammar of a conversation with both speakers removed. It is not eerie to them. It may not be anything to them. It may simply be what is left when intention drains slowly out of a system over centuries and the system, still moving, still finds itself producing the old shapes out of sheer worn-in habit, the groove too deep now to climb out of.

What do the Tenders want? Nothing. This is not coldness; it's closer to a mercy, if you can call it that. They are not hungry for company, will not mistake you for kin, will not mourn you or warn you or guide you to safety or otherwise notice you have a self that could be addressed. The terraces will go on being rationed whether or not you watch. The bell will go on keeping an hour that has stopped meaning anything to anyone who could check a clock. If there is a way to think of them kindly, it's this: they are the last labor of a people who loved a place enough that their love kept doing its work after the people themselves had nothing left to do it with. The hands are gone. The tending continues. That is the whole of what's alive here — not a someone, not a something, but a *continuing*, patient and exact and utterly indifferent to whether you understand it, ringing its bell in the dark for a morning that, this far down, will not come, and does not need to, in order to be worth ringing for.
