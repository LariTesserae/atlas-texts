They call themselves the *Hollowed*, though not in any tongue you'd recognize as a name — more a description, offered with a tilt of the head and a flat hand pressed to the sternum, as if indicating an absence rather than claiming an identity. There are perhaps forty of them spread across the dozen structures, though the terrace never holds them all in view at once. They move in the gaps between fern-stands the way the birds move between roosts: low, unhurried, conserving some quality of stillness that seems to matter more to them than speed or comfort.

They are unmistakably human in frame — two arms, two legs, a face built for expression — but altered in ways that resist a single word. The shoulders run too wide and too high, blading up toward the neck in a way that suggests, without confirming, something folded beneath the skin along the spine. Their collarbones sit prominent and slightly forward, the ribcage barreled, built for a kind of breath you don't use for walking. Their eyes are large for their faces, pale-irised, lidded in a way that closes sideways as much as down, and they don't blink so much as *flicker*, a fast nictitating motion that makes prolonged eye contact with them feel like being looked at by something mid-thought, calculating an updraft.

Their skin carries a faint stippling at the forearms and along the collar — not feathers, not yet, but the place where feathers would set if given another few hundred years to commit to the idea. Older members of the settlement, the ones who sit motionless on the stilted porches for hours at a time, have more of it: a fine vellus down visible only when the grey light catches it edge-on, silvering their jawlines and the backs of their hands. The children have none. This is the detail that unsettles most, once noticed — the down does not appear at birth. It *arrives*, slowly, as if earned, or as if something is deciding, generation by generation, whether to finish the work it started.

They do not speak in the way you'd expect speech to fill a place this populated. Words exist — short, clicked, glottal, exchanged in bursts of two or three syllables and long silences — but the bulk of their communication runs through gesture and through the birds. A raised palm, fingers spread and trembling slightly, sends a ripple through the nearest roosting line that passes itself down the terrace faster than any voice could carry. A specific click of the tongue brings three sparrow-sized birds wheeling down to a shoulder, where they sit and *receive* something — a touch of the beak to an earlobe, a moment of stillness — before lifting off again, carrying it elsewhere. The Hollowed don't own these birds. They don't feed them, cage them, or call them by name. The birds are not pets. They are closer to a circulatory system, ferrying some kind of information through the settlement in a constant, silent traffic that the people seem to read the way you'd read the tension in a room.

The oversized doorframes are not built for a future visitor or an absent god. They are built for what the eldest Hollowed are *becoming*. In the deepest structures — the ones set furthest back into the stone, half-grown over with vine — live the ones who no longer walk the paths. You glimpse them only as a shifting bulk in a doorway too dim to resolve, a long limb drawn back into shadow, a sound like canvas pulled taut. These are not separate from the Hollowed. They are what the down was promising all along, the terminal shape of whatever slow transformation the bloodline carries: bodies grown too heavy and strange for the ground, wings developed past any use for flight in a space this cramped, kept instead folded and enormous and waiting, fed by hand through doorways built three times human height, tended by children who will, eventually, take their place inside.

The thing that moves along the outer wall at dusk is one of these, restless, testing a wingspan against air that has at last grown wide enough to hold it. It does not yet fly. It paces the wall the way a fledgling paces a branch, gathering courage or strength or whatever final piece of itself still needs arriving. The bare poles across the open ground are not decoration and never were — they are perches sized for exactly this, set at intervals matching a wingspan no one currently flying possesses yet, built in patient anticipation by people who have apparently seen this happen before, elsewhere, generations back, and know the shape the waiting takes.

The silence, then, is not reverence and not fear. It is technique. Noise disturbs a fledgling's nerve. So the birds murmur, the mammals rustle, the Hollowed click and gesture and hold their breath collectively, generation over generation, narrowing the world down to a held quiet large enough to let something enormous finally trust the air — while the doors stay oversized, the poles stay bare, and the whole terrace leans, faces outward, toward the day one of them finally steps off the wall into the open sky it was built to release them into.
