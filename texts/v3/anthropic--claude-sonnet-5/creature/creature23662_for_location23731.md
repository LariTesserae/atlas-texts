# What Lives Here

## First, the Small Ones

Call them gnat-kin for lack of a better word, though they are not insects in any sense your taxonomy would recognize — six-limbed, translucent at the joints, with a single compound eye that wraps fully around the head so that they are never, technically, looking away from anything. They are the chorus. The clicking is their mouths, which have no other apparent function; they do not seem to eat with them, do not seem to communicate in any pattern you could call language, and yet the clicking organizes itself into waves that travel the length of the cavern in step with the breathing of the lichen-light. They live in the moss the way blood lives in a body — not *on* it, not *in* it as guests, but as a tissue of the place, produced by it, recycled by it, indistinguishable in death from the substrate they die into. If you watch one for long enough you will notice it never quite finishes a thought. It clicks, pauses as if for a reply, gets one from forty meters away, and continues. This is either the least intelligent behavior you've ever seen or the most patient.

Beneath the moss-skin, the segmented things are something else again: longer, blinder, entirely without need of light, moving the substrate in slow tidal pulses the way a sleeper's breathing moves a blanket. You never see one whole. You see the *suggestion* of one — a ridge, a give underfoot, a brief cool wetness where one has passed near the surface and then thought better of it. They are, you eventually suspect, not separate from the gnat-kin at all, but a different instar of the same creature: the clicking thing above is the gnat-kin's adult and articulate self; the patient thing below is its larval, structural self, doing the slow work of moving mass from one part of the body to another. Plumbing with opinions.

## The Pale Ones

The larger shape that crosses the middle distance, unhurried, deferred to — you will see it perhaps twice in a long visit, never closer than thirty meters, never in full light. It is long, low, and moves on a frame of limbs that don't quite resolve into a number, the way a many-legged thing seen underwater seems to have more legs than it should. Its skin is the same bone-and-bruise pale as the lichen shelves, which is not a coincidence; it grazes the lichen, yes, mouth low to the moss in long contented draws, but it is also colored *by* it, grown in conversation with it, the way a deep-sea fish is grown in conversation with the pressure that made it.

Apex is the wrong word, because there is no contest here for it to be the winner of. Nothing challenges the pale ones because nothing here understands challenge as a category. They are simply the largest mobile expression of a mind that has decided, in this region of itself, to also be able to walk. You get the sense — and it is only a sense, nothing you could defend — that there are not many of them because there does not need to be many of them. One is doing the walking-around that needs doing. Another, somewhere else in the system, is doing other necessary things. They are less individuals than *errands the moss is currently running.*

## The Lattice, and What It Is For

This is the part your mind keeps sliding off of, so approach it slowly: the humming stalks, the joints that orbit spores and draw them back in, the unmistakable sense of computation running underneath the biology — that is not infrastructure *belonging to* some hidden creature elsewhere. That is the creature. Not a nest, not a hive, not a colony in the ant sense where many small wills add up to one emergent purpose. One organism, continuous, that has simply never seen a reason to stop being made of many shapes at once.

The gnat-kin are its attention, scattered wide and clicking to keep itself apprised of its own perimeter. The segmented things below are its slow internal logistics. The pale grazers are its hands, sent out at long intervals to tend the parts of its own body that have grown too lush to manage from where they stand. The lattice-stalks are not separate from any of this — they are the place where the organism does whatever it does instead of *thinking*, if thinking is the wrong word for a process that takes twenty minutes per breath and has never once, in some great span of time, needed to hurry.

It does not have a face. It does not need one, having long ago distributed the relevant work — noticing, deciding, remembering — across a square kilometer of moss, fungus, lichen, stalk, and slow pale flesh, none of which is more *it* than any other part, the way no single neuron is more you than any other neuron, and yet you would not call a neuron, alone, a person.

## On Whether It Knows You Are There

It does. Not as an intruder, not as prey, not even quite as an event — more the way your own body knows, without surfacing the knowledge to anything you'd call your mind, that a hair has been disturbed on your arm. The deepened luminescence under your footprints is not a passive record. It is the organism noting, in whatever register it notes things, that something with weight and warmth has interrupted a piece of itself, and adjusting — not alarmed, not curious, simply *informed* — before continuing the longer thought it was already having, which began before you arrived and will not conclude because you leave.

It has been here, doing this, for a span of time that makes the word *patient* sound rushed. It has solved — or grown the solution to, which may be the same act for a creature like this — some problem about cold and pressure and slowness that nothing on the surface has ever needed to solve, because nothing on the surface has ever had this much uninterrupted time in which to be only one thing, thoroughly, instead of many things, briefly.

You are, to it, weather. A warm front that came in through a crack, disturbed a small area of skin, asked nothing, took nothing, and — within the hour — left no trace it could be bothered to keep.
