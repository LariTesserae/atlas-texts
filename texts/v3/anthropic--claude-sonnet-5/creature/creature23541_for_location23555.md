It takes a while to see her, because she has the same trick the glade has — the trick of being exactly where she's always been, until your eye learns to stop sliding past her.

She is low against the engine's flank, in the warm shadow where the casing curves into the soil, and at first she reads as a knot of root, or a slick of bark gone strange with moss. Then the moss blinks.

She is something like a salamander, if a salamander had been left to steep for a thousand years in iron filings and rust-water. Long-bodied, low-slung, her skin has the dull sheen of old brass under a green patina, mottled in patches the same mineral greens as the engine's housing, so that where she presses against the metal she nearly vanishes into it — a deliberate camouflage, or an old and patient convergence, skin and casing weathering toward the same color over the same span of unmeasured years. Her spine carries a low ridge of bony plates, more worn than sharp, polished smooth in places the way old gears get polished smooth by handling, though no hand has ever touched her. Her limbs are short, thick, almost stubby, ending in wide flat feet better suited to gripping wet metal than walking. She does not walk so much as she shifts, in long unhurried adjustments, the way the engine itself seems to shift in its sleep between one clank and the next.

Her eyes are the part that unsettles, mildly, pleasantly, the way everything in this glade unsettles mildly and pleasantly rather than sharply. They are large, lidless, a clouded amber-gold, and they do not track movement so much as *register* it, slowly, the way a held breath registers a draft. There is something faintly optical about them — a concentric ringing in the iris that recalls, almost too neatly, the rings of a gauge, or the layered glass of an old lens left to fog. She blinks rarely. When she does, it is slow enough to seem considered, a decision rather than a reflex.

She has been here, by any reasonable guess, longer than the frogs, longer than the birds, perhaps nearly as long as the engine — though "long" is a strange unit in a place where nothing changes enough to measure time against. She does not seem to age so much as continue. Her movements are unhurried in the specific way of something that has run out of urgent business decades ago and settled instead into a single, permanent occupation: listening.

She listens to the engine the way the frogs call back at it, the way the glassy-winged thing circles the hottest pipe — but where their attention is appetite, hers is something closer to attendance. She lies along the warm metal with her throat pressed to its flank, and when the piston-clank comes through the casing, a faint sympathetic pulse moves up her spine, plate by plate, a shiver passed hand to hand down a long line of waiting people. It is not clear whether she feels the engine's rhythm or merely keeps it — whether her own slow systolic thrum first matched the engine's by accident, sometime in some buried year, and never unmatched since, or whether she somehow, impossibly, helps to keep that ancient, fuel-less heart in time, the way a second clock left running near a first will eventually, through some petty conspiracy of physics, fall into step with it.

She eats little, and what she eats she takes without urgency: the thumbnail insects that crowd the warm casing, the occasional beetle drawn to the seep-water, a frog's egg now and then, though she shows no appetite for the frogs themselves, who roost in her shadow without fear, as if the matter had been settled generations of frogs ago and never needed revisiting. The birds ignore her entirely. The glassy-winged thing, in its slow orbits of the hottest pipe, sometimes passes within a hand's width of her skull and she does not so much as turn her gold eye to track it. Everyone here has long since agreed on the shape of everyone else's hunger, and hers, whatever it is, has been satisfied by something other than eating for longer than she or anyone can account for.

If she has a name among the things that share this pocket of green — and it is not at all certain that names are how this glade organizes itself — it would have to be something like *keeper*, though she keeps nothing in any active sense; she does not guard the engine, does not maintain it, would not, it's fairly certain, lift a stubby limb to stop its running or to help it run. She simply lies along it the way moss lies along stone, the way the glade itself lies around the whole strange iron fact of the engine — not resisting, not tending, just present, just incorporated, an old soft witness pressed to an old hard pulse, the two of them breathing together in the green-gold light, for as long as anyone could come to look.
