There is a woman who lives at the edge of the switchbacks, in the last fold of rock before the climb gives out onto the flats — not on the plateau itself, never quite on it, but in the place where the plateau's authority begins and the lower world's last claim ends. She has been there long enough that no one climbing up for the first time can quite picture the trail without her, though no one climbing up for the first time expects her either.

She is old in the same unperformed way the rock is old. Not bent, not frail — weathered, the way the rounded shoulders of stone are weathered, down to whatever durable thing was underneath the original shape. Her hands are wide and cracked at the knuckle, her face the color and texture of the lichen-patched slabs, rust and grey-green under a hood she wears less for warmth than out of long habit, the way the cushion-plants wedge into their cracks not because the wind asks them to but because it's simply where they've always been. You could not say what she eats. There is a fire-ring near her shelter — the one fire-ring within a day's walk of the standing heights, kept small, kept low, fed with the same lower-elevation wood the staffs are cut from — and beside it, a few stone basins not unlike the meltwater pools above, except these are tended, swept clear of grit by hand, kept ready.

She does not live on the plateau. She lives at its door. This distinction matters to her in a way she has never had to explain to anyone, because everyone who reaches her already understands it without being told: that the flats above are not a place for staying, that her whole purpose is to be the last thing made of human warmth before the cold and the roar and the indifferent light take over, and the first thing made of human warmth when they hand you back.

She says little. What she says is mostly logistics — how much water to carry, which of the basins up there are deep enough to trust, how the herds tend to cross at certain hours and how a sensible person times their walk around the thunder rather than through it. She has watched the great flocks for so many decades that she can read, in the pitch of the standing roar, whether the day above is calm or restless, the way a person who has lived by the sea can read weather in the particular quality of the surf without needing to look. She knows the burrow-things by their chitter alone, knows which sound means a fox-shaped predator she's never managed to see, knows the difference between rain-seep and true storm from the smell that comes down the rock-face an hour before either arrives.

She has climbed to the top herself, of course, more times than she could count and fewer times than she once did. She does not describe what she finds there in the way visitors expect — no reverence, no fear, none of the things the plateau seems built to inspire in people seeing it new. To her it is simply a place that does its work, the way the trail does its work, the way her fire and her swept basins and her quiet recitation of warnings do theirs. She is not a guardian in the sense of someone who forbids or protects. She is more like a hinge — the small, necessary, unglamorous part where two very different kinds of world are allowed to meet without tearing.

People leave her things sometimes, descending — a coin, a strip of dried meat, a word of thanks she receives without much comment, as if thanks were a kind of weather too, arriving and passing the way rain does. She has no name that travelers ever learn, or else she has many, a different one assigned by every party that passes through, none of which seem to bother her. She will be there for the next climber, and the one after, doing what she has always done: tending the threshold, keeping the small low fire, telling people what the wind and the herds and the light are likely to do, and letting them go up to meet a place that does not need her, and was never, in any way that matters, hers to keep.
