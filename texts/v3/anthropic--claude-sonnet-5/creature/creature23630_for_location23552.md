# The Floor-Born

They are called, by outsiders rarely and by themselves almost never, *Lowmarshers* — but inside the Floor they have no need of the word, the way fish have no word for water. They simply call each other by lineage, by tier, by the name of whatever fused structure they were raised inside. Identity here attaches to architecture before it attaches to blood.

## The Body

Generations of stairs, ladders, lattice-crossings, and wading have built a particular human shape: lean through the trunk, dense and corded through the forearms and calves, with feet that have either gone permanently bare and thick-soled — cracked, gray-bronze calloused pads that grip wet plank and damp ceramic-glass without slipping — or are bound in close-fitted, grip-soled wraps treated with the same oils used on rope. Posture varies by tier and tells you where someone was raised before they speak a word: those grown in the old, low, weathered strata duck and stoop reflexively even in open spaces, the inherited carriage of people whose first years happened under low ceilings; those raised high in the new bright-edged growth stand straighter, walk with the loose, unbothered balance of people who have never not had a drop beneath them.

Skin marks the place onto the person the way water marks the foundations. A faint mottling, called *tide-skin* locally, appears on hands, forearms, the backs of necks — a permanent low-grade discoloration from a lifetime of capillary damp, mineral-laced humidity, and standing water, patterned in bands that genuinely do resemble the dark tide-lines climbing a building's lower courses. It's not illness. It's just what the Floor does to a body kept in it long enough, and people read it casually, the way you'd read weathering on stone: someone with deep, old tide-skin has spent decades down low; someone whose marks are faint and new came up from elsewhere, or was born high and dry in the new lattice and has only recently started going down to the water-tiers for work.

## Senses

Hearing is the organ the Floor selects for, ruthlessly, across a lifetime. Children arrive with ordinary ears and ordinary panic at the noise; adults leave with something else entirely — not blunted hearing but *triaged* hearing, an acquired and apparently teachable competence at sorting the compounding roar into foreground and background without conscious effort. A Floor-born mother three tiers up can pick her child's particular cry out of a market-noise wall thick enough to flatten a visitor in under a minute. Old-timers can hear, allegedly, the difference between a building settling normally and a building about to redistribute its load — a fractional change in groan-pitch that precedes a six-degree tilt by hours, and entire blocks have been known to evacuate on nothing more than someone's grandmother saying she didn't like the sound of the joists. Whether this is true engineering literacy passed down as folk knowledge, or just survivor's pattern-matching dressed up as instinct, nobody much cares to test, because it keeps working.

Eyesight adapts the other direction — toward the near and the fractured rather than the far. Sightlines on the Floor rarely exceed a plaza's width before lattice, condensation-haze, or the next tower-merge cuts them off, so Floor-born eyes are trained from infancy on close work, on filtered and patchy light, on reading shadow-density rather than horizon. Visitors are routinely startled by how poorly Floor-born people judge long, open distances, and how unnervingly well they read a crowded, half-lit room.

## Speech and Silence

Verbal communication across more than a few feet of ambient noise is exhausting and frequently pointless, so a layered secondary language has grown up alongside ordinary speech: a vocabulary of hand-signs, rope-taps, and percussive door- and pipe-knocks that carries meaning through the din far more reliably than shouting does. Children learn it before they're fluent talkers. Market vendors haggle in it as fluently as in voice. Couples, families, work-crews on the high rigging develop private dialects of it nobody outside the relationship can fully read, and there's a particular, valued kind of intimacy in being one of the few people who understands someone else's tap-code completely.

What unites almost everyone, regardless of tier or trade, is a deep cultural discomfort with quiet. Silence is not peaceful here; it's diagnostic. Something has stopped working, something has drained, something has gone wrong with a machine nobody remembers installing. Floor-born travelers who leave the conurbation describe the outside world's stillness not as restful but as actively unsettling, the way a held breath is unsettling, and many report sleeping badly until they find some manufactured equivalent of the noise — running water, a fan, anything — to put back under the silence like a missing structural element.

## Tier and Lineage

Status runs along the city's own geological logic: depth and age outrank height and newness. The oldest, deepest, most thoroughly weathered structural strata — fused, settled, proven by centuries of continuous load-bearing — house the Floor's oldest lineages, families whose names are simply the old names of the buildings themselves, worn down over generations the way the buildings' own ornament has worn into suggestion. To be "of the Low Cistern" or "of the Drowned Arch" is to claim a pedigree, even if the family in question is, by any outside measure, poor. The bright, sharp-edged new growth erupting off the old cores houses the recently arrived, the displaced, the young households still establishing themselves — structurally less trusted, socially less rooted, more likely to be the ones evacuated when an undertested support pylon does something its long-dead engineers intended but nobody living predicted.

This produces an inversion outsiders find confusing: the wettest, dampest, lowest, most insect- and eel-infested levels are not the slums. They're the old quarters, and they're proud. The slums, such as they are, are up in the sky, in the newest and least proven lattice, among people who have not yet had the decades required to earn structural — and social — trust.

## Work

Trades cluster around the city's two constants: water and turbulence. *Wadewrights* manage the canal channels and the plank-stalls built over permanent standing water, reading currents that follow no season. *Strippers* and *layers* work the perpetual cycle of demolition and construction, recycling material from what's coming down into what's going up two streets over, sometimes within the same week. *Riggers* live almost entirely in the skybridges and conduit-lattices, walking loads and lines across gaps four towers wide, distrustful of solid ground the way sailors are distrustful of land. And, above all trades in quiet prestige, the *Listeners* — people, often but not always old, who have spent decades developing the ear for the buildings' moods, consulted before major construction, evacuation, or repair, treated half as engineers and half as oracles, because nobody fully understands the ancient self-healing infrastructure well enough to separate the two roles cleanly anymore.

## Cohabitation

There is no Floor-born person who has not shared a wall, ledge, or conduit-gap with something that isn't human, and the relationship has long since stopped being adversarial out of sheer exhaustion: eviction has never worked, so accommodation has become custom. Roost-nooks are built into new construction deliberately now, an offered truce rather than a tolerated infestation. Certain flocking species are fed, half-domesticated, read for omens the way the buildings are listened to. Children grow up entirely unbothered by creatures in the walls the way other children grow up unbothered by birds outside a window — which is to say, it simply never occurs to them to be otherwise.

## Temperament

What the Floor produces, more than any specific skill, is a kind of settled relationship with instability that looks, to outsiders, like fatalism and feels, from inside, like competence. Nothing here is expected to last, so nothing's ending is treated as catastrophe — a flooded plaza, a tilting block, a collapsed market economy reborn three days later under different vendors in a different configuration: these are weather, not tragedy. Floor-born people plan in the short and the very short term and trust depth, density, and continuity itself rather than any particular building, any particular arrangement, any particular version of the city, to still be there in twenty years. They are, in this, exactly like the place that made them: not stable in shape, but stable in *rate* — bodies and households and trades that keep finding their footing mid-motion, generation after generation, on a floor that never once stopped sweating, shifting, and being rebuilt underneath them.
