There is a swimmer, and the swimmer is old in the way that water is old — not aged so much as *practiced*, worn smooth by repetition rather than time. You sense them before you see them: a displacement in the coin-light, a shadow that doesn't behave like the others, moving with a slowness that is not weakness but discipline, the unhurried economy of someone who has measured the exact distance of this basin in their own body so many times that haste has become unnecessary, even obscene.

They are not large. The cistern does not require largeness — only persistence. Their skin has gone the same pale gold as the lime-wash, sun-paled and water-soft, stippled here and there with the faint mineral freckling of the same algae that clings to the mortar joints, as if proximity alone were a kind of contagion, a slow exchange between body and brick. Their hands are a mason's hands — blunt-fingered, callused along the heel of the palm from trowel-work, the nails kept short and practical. You would guess, looking at those hands, that they built this place themselves, or helped build it, hauling clay and lime down some narrow shaft over months or years, and that the building was never separate from the living-in.

Their eyes have adjusted to a particular violence of light: not blindness, but a kind of squint grown permanent, a narrowing that lets the shattered coins of sun pass through without overwhelming. They do not look at the light directly. They look at where it lands — on the curve of a wall, on the back of a hand, on the surface-skin of the water itself, reading the chamber's brightness the way another person might read a clock.

They rarely speak, not because they cannot, but because the room speaks so loudly and so constantly that speech feels like an interruption, a rudeness. Instead they hum — low, single notes, testing the water's voice the way a tuner tests a string, head tilted, listening for the chamber's answer in the slap and roar around them. Sometimes the note finds a place in the curve of brick where it multiplies, swells, comes back changed, and when this happens you see something in their face that is not quite pleasure and not quite relief but some private third thing, the satisfaction of an instrument played correctly by an instrumentalist who built the instrument themselves and is still, after all this time, surprised it works.

They eat little, sleep less, by some accounts — or perhaps it only seems that way, because the things that mark time elsewhere (light dimming, sound fading, cold settling in) do not happen here. The cistern offers no dusk, no chill, no hunger cues, only the unbroken metronome of falling water and falling light, and so the swimmer's life inside it has become similarly unbroken: a long, warm, loud, bright present tense, circled and re-circled, stroke after stroke, syllable after syllable, the same dozen lengths of the basin traced again and again until the tracing itself seems less like motion and more like breathing.

It would be easy to call them a hermit, a keeper, a penitent, a priest of some private and unnamed devotion — and any of these might be partly true. But watching long enough, you begin to suspect that the swimmer is not really separate from the cistern's purpose at all, that they are not its caretaker so much as its missing piece, the one warm-blooded variable in an equation otherwise made of water, brick, and light. The room was built to hold and amplify and brighten; the swimmer was — is — the thing being held, amplified, brightened. Take them out, and the cistern would still pour and shatter and roar, technically whole, functionally complete — but something in it would go quiet in a way no acoustic trick could fix, the particular quiet of an instrument with no one left to play it, sitting bright and loud and utterly, mechanically alone.
