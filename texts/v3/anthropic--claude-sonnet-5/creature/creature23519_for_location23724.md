They are not numerous, and this is the third wrongness, once you've absorbed the first two: in a place teeming with grazers and gliders and the slow boil of burrow-things at dusk, the ones who actually *belong* to the plateau — who seem made by it rather than merely tolerated by it — can be counted, on a given horizon, on one hand. You might walk four days and see none. You might wake from an exhausted sleep to find one standing forty feet off, motionless, watching you with the patience of stone.

They are tall in the way the ridgelines are tall — not towering, but elongated, drawn out, as if something had taken a more compact creature and stretched it gently over a very long time until it matched the proportions of the land it stood on. The legs are disproportionately long and thin, jointed wrong by any standard you'd bring with you, bending in places and directions that make the walking gait unsettling to watch: a slow, rolling, almost liquid stride, each foot finding stone with a deliberation that looks less like caution and more like conversation. The hide, if it's hide, is the exact dun-grey of the scree, mottled in patches with the same rust and ash-green as the lichen, and it's never entirely clear where the texture of the creature ends and the texture of the rock begins — you'll lose one against a boulder field for whole minutes at a time, only to have it resolve again when it shifts its weight.

The head is small for the body, set low and forward, with no obvious eyes that you can find on the ones that let you get close enough to look — or eyes so recessed, so adapted to function in this perpetual dim haze-light, that they read as shadowed hollows instead of organs. What it has instead, what it seems to *lead* with, is a broad flat plate along the lower face and throat, ridged faintly like the surface of a dried lakebed, and you understand without anyone telling you that this is an ear, or the nearest analogue this creature has to one — built for catching exactly the kind of sound the plateau rations out. The low calls of the herds. The click of hoof on stone three ridges over. And the throb. Always, underneath everything else it might be listening for, the throb.

They move toward the moisture pockets before the moisture arrives. This is the detail that, once you notice it, you can't stop noticing: a creature standing motionless over a dry, frost-burned hollow for an hour, two hours, before the condensation begins its slow bead and slide down the stone shelf above. Not waiting on hunger. Waiting on a schedule it can feel and you can't — the same schedule, you start to suspect, that governs the spacing of the throb-pulses beneath the rock, the same long patient arithmetic that decides when the herds will drift toward water and when they'll drift away. The creature kneels — folds, really, all that wrong-jointed leg collapsing into a posture that looks like a held breath — and lays the flat plate of its throat against the bare stone. It stays like that long after the dew has gone. You get the impression, watching, that it isn't drinking. It's listening to something the rock is telling it, something with a rhythm too slow for the moisture itself, and the moisture is only the part of the message you happen to be able to see.

They do not interact with the herds, and the herds do not startle from them, which is its own kind of statement. They do not interact with each other, as far as you ever witness — two of them might stand within sight of one another for the better part of a day, motionless, attentive each to their own patch of ground, and never close the distance. There's no aggression in this, no territory being defended. It reads instead like specialization, like each one has been assigned, or has assigned itself, a different stretch of the engine's vast and buried circulatory system, and the work of standing watch over one's own length of pipe doesn't require company, only diligence.

Once — if you're patient, if you're lucky, if the haze thins for the hour it takes — you may see one stretched flat against the rock at the moment the throb comes due, the whole long body laid out like something fallen, the throat-plate pressed to stone, the wrong-jointed legs splayed loose and slack. And you'll feel the pulse rise up through your own boot soles the way you have a dozen times already, that deep slow mechanical heave from somewhere under the dead mountains' bones — and you'll see the creature's body answer it. Not flinch. Not startle. *Answer* — a long, slow ripple traveling from throat to haunch to the trailing tips of those impossible legs, in time with the engine's beat, like a held note finally getting to resolve.

Then it rises, unfolds, and walks on, toward whatever the next quiet appointment is, leaving you with the absolute certainty that you have just watched something pay a debt, or keep a promise, or simply do — unhurried, unbothered, exactly as the plateau requires — the one piece of work it was shaped to do beneath a sky that has agreed never to hurry it.
