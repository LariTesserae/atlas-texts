# The Tenants

You will likely never see one. This is not because they are rare in the way a leopard is rare — though they are that too — but because they have made of rarity itself a kind of practice, a discipline as deliberate as the plateau's own discipline of spacing. They call themselves, in the old tongue that the wind has mostly worn off them, something that translates badly but lands somewhere near *the ones who keep the distance.* Travelers who have spent enough nights up there, watching the towers breathe their slow borrowed light into the dark, have other names for them. None of the names agree, which is itself consistent with how the tenants prefer to be known.

## Body

They are tall in the way the standing outcrops are tall — built for a horizon, not a room. Lean past the point where leanness reads as hunger, with long limbs adapted less for speed than for patience: the patience of standing very still in the lee of a boulder for the better part of a day, the patience of walking for a week without arriving anywhere that looks, to an outsider's eye, any different from where they started. Their skin is a dull rust-and-bone color by daylight, banded faintly the way the rock is banded, so that a tenant standing motionless against a tablelands escarpment is not hidden exactly — they don't trouble themselves to hide — but simply ceases to be the thing your eye sorts for. It is only at dusk, and more clearly at night, that the wrongness shows: a faint iridescence along the jaw, the inner forearm, the ridge of the collarbone, the same wrong-spectrum shimmer the towers throw back from the dark. It brightens, very slightly, near the structures. It is most active, most clearly *alive*, in the old who are nearing the end of something.

Their eyes are large, pale, and unblinking for long stretches — eyes built for the same enormous light the soaring hunters ride, though the tenants do not soar. They watch the sky the way the hunters do, but for different signs.

## How They Live

They do not live together, not in any sense a village or even a family would recognize. A tenant is born — rarely, perhaps once in a generation per several hundred kilometers of plateau — to a mother who will leave it within a season at a stone ring she has chosen with apparent care, among others of her kind who happen, by the slow drift of their solitary circuits, to be passing near. The child is raised in the gaps between adults' comings and goings, fed on what the land feeds everything else, taught a handful of practices and almost no stories, and then it, too, begins to walk.

What follows for the rest of a tenant's life — and they live very long, long enough that none currently walking the tablelands remembers a time before the towers, though none of them believe the towers were *built* by anyone, themselves included — is a circuit. Each tenant has one, an enormous looping path across the plateau that may take years to complete, that brings them, with the unhurried inevitability of a slow comet, past certain towers and certain stone rings and never others. They meet other tenants perhaps a few dozen times in a long life, almost always near a tower, almost always briefly. There is courtesy between them, something that might be affection grown so patient it no longer needs frequent expression to remain true. There is almost never urgency. Urgency, to a tenant, would look like a kind of illness.

## What They Do at the Towers

This is the part no outside witness has ever fully understood, though several have watched it happen and described enough of it, independently, that the accounts agree on shape if not on meaning.

A tenant arrives at a tower — usually alone, sometimes in pairs if the circuits have aligned — and stands close enough to touch it, and does, palm flat against the seamless surface, for a span of time that can run from minutes to the better part of a day. Nothing visible occurs during most of this. The tenant's faint inner light seems, to careful observation, to brighten very slightly and then settle, as a held breath settles. Afterward they sit, sometimes for days, in the stone rings nearby — those waist-high circular foundations that look so much like the remains of a simple pastoral people, and which the tenants treat with unmistakable tenderness, clearing windblown dust from them with slow unhurried hands, though they build nothing new, repair nothing, add nothing. The rings are not used. They are *visited.*

The old among the tenants — and you would know an old one by the depth of the shimmer under the skin, by a kind of stillness that has stopped pretending to be patience and has simply become the tenant's whole remaining nature — eventually stop walking their circuits altogether. They go to a tower of their own apparent choosing, sit in the nearest stone ring, and wait. What happens next has been witnessed exactly enough times to be believed and not enough to be explained: the vertical lance of light that the towers release, rarely, at no fixed hour — it happens, on these occasions, very near where the old tenant sits, very shortly after they have gone still in a way stillness does not usually go. Those who have seen it describe the body as simply *lessened* afterward — not removed, not consumed, but somehow thinner, lighter, as if some essential fraction of it had already gone up with the light and what remained no longer needed tending. The plateau's small scavengers take care of the rest, unhurried, the way they take care of everything.

Whether the towers are ancestors, or archives, or simply the patient mechanism by which something in this place converts a long quiet life into one more pulse of light against the dark — the tenants have never said, in all the words that travelers have managed to extract from their long silences. Asked, the answer that recurs most often, badly translated, comes to something like: *it isn't a question we walk toward.*

## Why So Few, Why So Wide

It is sometimes said, by those who have lived near the tablelands' edge long enough to have opinions about it, that the tenants are simply the plateau's way of being thoughtful — one more grazer in the wide thin ecology, except this one grazes on time instead of the silver-leafed shrubs, ranges across decades instead of seasons, and leaves behind not dung or footprint but the occasional held silence near a tower that wasn't quite as held the day before. It is not a flattering theory. It is also, near as anyone has ever been able to tell, not far wrong.

What can be said with more confidence is this: there are not many of them, there have perhaps never been many of them, and there is no sign that there are meant to be more. They do not cluster because clustering is not what this land rewards, in tenants or shrubs or soaring hunters or the towers themselves. They are spaced the way everything here is spaced — by some old wide law the plateau enforces without needing to explain itself — walking long slow circuits between half-remembered ruins and structures that argue with physics, keeping a kind of company with both that asks nothing in return except to be occasionally touched, occasionally sat beside, and, in the very end, to be the last warm thing a long life leans against before going up, once, as light.
