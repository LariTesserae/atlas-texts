They are not many, and they do not need to be. A basin this old does not reward numbers; it rewards patience, and patience scales poorly — you can only fit so much of it into a single life before the life itself becomes the bottleneck. So there are few of them, scattered the way the worked stones are scattered, and like the stones they are easy to mistake, in poor visibility, for something that simply happened to the rock rather than something that lives.

The body, when it resolves out of the murk — and it resolves slowly, an arm-length at a time, the way everything here does — is low and long and segmented, built less like an animal than like a string of animals that agreed, a very long time ago, to stop arguing about which one was in charge. No eyes. There was no point keeping them; the dark down here predates sight and will outlast it. What it has instead is a fringe, dozens of pale filtering tendrils along both flanks, each one tuned to a different register of disturbance — pressure, taste, the faint electric murmur of something else's muscle moving nearby. It reads the water the way the water reads the rock: by what pushes back.

It moves the way the silt moves, which is to say barely, and only when something insists. Most of its life is spent anchored in a crack with its underside, drawing in whatever the currents are generous enough to bring past — the flocculent snow, the comminuted dust of the long dead, the occasional richer fall that means something larger has died somewhere above and is still arriving, politely, one grey flake at a time. This is not laziness. At this depth, under this pressure, with a metabolism slowed nearly to the rock's own rate of erosion, motion is the most expensive thing in the world. It spends its motion the way the basin spends millennia: rarely, and on purpose.

But it does spend it. That is the thing that separates it from the blind segmented wanderers and the pale hair-stalks rooted in their cracks of leaking warmth — it has hands, in the loosest possible sense, a forward cluster of jointed mouth-limbs that can grip, twist, lash. And once in a very long while — once a generation, perhaps, by whatever clock applies down here — it uses them. It strips a stalk from its anchorage with a patience that would be unbearable to watch if there were anything down here to watch it. It works a fiber free, twists it, binds two things together that did not want to be together. It finds a stone with an edge already half-promised by some ancient fracture and worries that edge sharper, a little at a time, over what might be weeks, might be years; there is no urgency in it, only the slow insistence of something that has nowhere else its effort could possibly go.

What is it making. A tool, technically — something to pry, to cut, to drag a richer fall of detritus closer to the mouth before the current claims it back. But call it that and you miss the shape of the thing. It is making the only gesture available to it against a basin that erases everything: it is, very briefly, on a timescale almost too small for the place to notice, deciding what happens next. The silt will take the tool back within a generation or two — drag it down, bury its one deliberate edge, return it to ambiguity. The creature knows this in whatever diffuse way a nervous system spread across forty segments can know anything. It does not resist the knowing. It simply keeps building, the way the hair-stalks keep bending without resistance, the way the basin keeps not-quite-settling — because down here, where the rock finished its argument three billion seconds ago and the water never has, making something briefly true is the closest any of them get to standing.

It leaves almost nothing. A bundle of lashed stalks. A worked stone, sinking. No name, no mark, no second creature nearby to remember it doing the work. When it dies — slowly, the way it did everything, settling at last into the floor it spent its whole life half a hand's width above — the silt takes that too, and within a season there is no evidence a thing with hands was ever here at all.

The basin does not mourn this. It has no memory for intention. It only has memory for erosion, and erosion, eventually, takes everyone's edge.
