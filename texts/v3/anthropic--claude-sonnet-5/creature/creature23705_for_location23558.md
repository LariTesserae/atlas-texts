# The Tender

It is the shape the dark makes when you've been in the leaf-corridors too long and your lamp starts arguing with your eyes. Everyone who works the Sunken Works has a story about it, and no two stories agree on more than this: it is large, it is slow, and it goes where the warmth is.

**Body.** Best guess, pieced together from the half-glimpses and the single recovered cast taken off a mud-bank near the seventh sub-level: something on the order of fifteen feet, low and long, built closer to the ground than the water seems to want, the way a thing built for crawling along cobbles rather than swimming through open water would be. A spine of overlapping plate, more crab than fish, more fish than anything with a name. Too many points of articulation to be one animal in the way a diver's mind wants animals to be — as if at some point in its history it was several smaller things that decided, gradually, the way the town decided to settle rather than be ruined, to act as one. Eyes are not confirmed. What is confirmed is that it does not need a lamp, and that the divers' lamps, on the rare frightened occasion they've caught the edge of it, found nothing reflecting back — no shine, no flash off a wet socket, just the matte dark of something that stopped needing sight generations ago and grew skin over the question.

**What it does.** It tends. That is the word the salvage crews settled on, half as a joke at first and then, as the joke stopped being funny, in earnest. Gear-housings that should have seized solid a century ago haven't. Pipe-joints that should have rusted through and let the warm bleed out into the cold sea at large, leaving the leaf-columns to wither and the crabs to starve, instead keep their seal, keep their warmth, year on year, with a consistency that outlives any plausible original engineering. Something is in the third sub-levels, below where any diver is supposed to go, pressing its long low weight against failing joints, packing rust-lace with something — silt, secretion, its own shed plate, nobody's recovered enough material to say — and holding the works together from beneath the way a hand holds together a tear in cloth it is too late to mend properly. It does not fix. It is well past fixing. It tends, the way a thing with no plan and no instruction just keeps doing the only act it has ever known how to do, generation into generation, because the warmth came from doing it and the warmth is the only argument an animal needs.

**Why it is there at all.** Old enough conversation among the salvage crews, late at night on the surface barges, half-drunk and half-serious: that it was bred for this, originally — a maintenance creature, like the carp they used to keep in cisterns to eat the algae, except built or chosen for the original works, back when the works were dry and lit and full of people, to do exactly this small ugly job in the gaps too narrow for a man's shoulders. That when the flood came slow, finger's width by finger's width, the maintenance creature simply didn't have anywhere else to be transferred to, and the town forgot it the way it forgot its own name, and it went on doing its job because nobody had ever told it to stop, and a hundred years of doing one job in growing dark, with no one to report to and no schedule but the schedule of failing joints, will shape a body. Will shape several bodies into the memory of one shape, plate folding over plate, the way the kelp-analogues spiral toward the two things that matter — up, and away from heat — until orientation itself becomes anatomy.

Nobody has confirmed this. Nobody has gotten close enough, and stayed unhurt enough afterward, to ask better questions of it than a moving lamp-shadow and a sway in the leaf-column can answer.

**Encounter.** It does not hunt, as far as anyone can tell — the crabs and eels that share its housings show no sign of having been culled, and a creature with its evident strength could have made a much emptier ecosystem of this place than the patient, sparse one that's settled here. But it does not tolerate close company in the sub-levels either. Divers who've gone past the third floor — against every piece of advice anyone gives them, because someone always does — report not an attack but a *pressure*: a closing of the warm dark around them, a sense the floor under their hands has decided, slowly, to stop being a floor, and the overwhelming, animal correctness of leaving immediately, conveyed with no gesture at all. The two or three who didn't leave immediately did not come back able to describe what came next.

**What it sounds like.** Nothing of its own. It is folded so entirely into the building's noise that no one has identified a sound as belonging specifically to it — only an occasional thickening of the ambient clank and creak, as if for a moment more weight than usual leaned into the transmission of the sound, then eased off again. The bell, when it tolls its once-a-decade toll, is sometimes — only sometimes, and only by the crews most inclined toward telling stories — credited to it directly: a long body, somewhere far below, brushing a chain it was never meant to reach, on its way between one failing joint and the next.

**What it wants**, if a thing that has been doing one unbroken motion for a hundred years can be said to want anything beyond the motion itself: warmth, continuance, the works going on turning at the rate of a held breath, the leaf-columns spiraling up toward a light it may or may not still register as light. It is, in this sense, the most settled resident the Sunken Works has. The brick has a memory of standing dry. The iron has a memory of being whole. The Tender has no memory of anything but the job, and so it alone among everything down there has nothing left to mourn, and nothing left to fear losing, and goes on, plate over plate, joint by failing joint, holding the dark together from underneath.
