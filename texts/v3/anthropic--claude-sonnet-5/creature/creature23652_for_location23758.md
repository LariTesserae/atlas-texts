# The Tenders

They are few, and they are old in the way that matters more than years — old in inheritance, in the accumulated weight of procedures performed by hands that are no longer here to explain them. Call them tenders, because that's the closest word the basin's own thin vocabulary still offers, though the word has frayed at its edges the way every word here eventually does.

Their bodies have made a long accommodation with the place. They are spare — not thin from want, the basin doesn't starve them, but compact, economical, built like the architecture around them: low centers of gravity, wide soft-soled feet that don't pierce the salt-crust, skin gone the color and texture of the terraces themselves, a kind of weathered ochre-grey that isn't tan so much as mineral deposit. Their eyes have narrowed permanently against the glare, not squinting but structurally adapted, lids heavier, lashes denser, so that even in sleep they look faintly wary of the light. They breathe differently than people do lower down — slower, deeper, deliberate, the way you breathe when air costs something. Children born here take a season to learn this rhythm; the ones who don't learn it leave, eventually, for thicker air below, and don't come back.

They move the way the place taught them to move: in long unhurried arcs between the domes, never the straight urgent line you'd use somewhere that rewarded urgency. There is no urgency here that hasn't already happened, geologically, eons before anyone arrived to tend its aftermath. Their gait has been described, by outsiders rare enough to have seen it and lived to describe anything, as ceremonial — though the tenders themselves would deny there's a ceremony in it. It is just the speed at which a body crosses ground that swallows sound. Hurrying here feels obscene, like shouting in a room built for whispering.

And they do whisper. Voices flatten within meters, the basin told you that already, and the tenders have made a virtue of the limitation: their language, what's left of it, has compressed itself into something clipped, breath-conserving, almost entirely free of the words that don't do necessary work. Greetings are a half-syllable. Warnings are a single dropped tone. Most of what passes between two tenders working adjacent towers is not spoken at all but signed — small economical gestures of hand and shoulder, legible at fifty meters across a terrace where a shout would arrive as nothing.

What they know, they know with their hands rather than their heads. The valves, the condensers, the settling-tank gates that have to be cracked at precise intervals or the whole shelf below floods wrong — none of this is written down, because the basin has tried writing things down before, generations back, and the paper didn't survive the humidity-and-desiccation cycle any better than vegetation does. So the knowledge moved into the body instead. A child learns which wheel to turn not by reading a manual but by standing at an elder's hip for years, watching the hands, eventually becoming the hands. They can tell you exactly what each valve does. Almost none of them can tell you why the system as a whole was built, what it was originally for, what it processed the water into before it became simply: the thing that must keep running. The purpose evaporated generations ago, the way everything evaporates here, but the procedure condensed back down without it, faithful and complete and orphaned.

They do not eat the fauna, though outsiders always ask. There's a old taboo around it, worn so smooth by repetition that nobody bothers justifying it anymore — you simply don't, the way you don't drink from the centers of the pools, the way you don't walk the terraces at the white-glare hour without covering your eyes. Whether the taboo started as practical wisdom or superstition has gone the way of every other original reason in this place: lost, with the rule outliving the logic. What they eat comes up through the same pipes that carry the water, processed in tanks whose function is one of the last surviving purposes anyone remembers — a thin, mineral, faintly warm nutrient paste that tastes, by every account, of absolutely nothing, and that no tender has ever complained about, because complaint is also a kind of noise the basin doesn't reward.

At dusk, when the towers tick and contract and the terraces hold their ember-glow a few minutes past the sky, the tenders come out from the domes and stand at the catwalk rails, not working, just present, the way the broad-winged gliding things stand motionless at the terrace-edges for hours. It is the one hour they permit themselves something like stillness without function. Someone, sometimes, hums — barely, just under the engine-hum, indistinguishable from it unless you're listening for the difference. Nobody asks them why they do this. It is, like everything else here, a rule rather than a wound: something the place asked of them long enough ago that the asking became the doing, and the doing became, eventually, simply who they are.
