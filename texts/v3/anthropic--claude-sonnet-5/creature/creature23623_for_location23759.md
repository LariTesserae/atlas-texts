## Who Tends It

You go a long time believing no one is here at all.

The text of the place insists on it — no animal life, no birdsong, no rustle that isn't water-driven leaf-sway. And for the first several terraces this holds. You climb the catwalks past brimful tanks, past fern-fronds flattened to iron, and the silence has the particular density of a silence that has never been broken by anything with lungs.

Then, on the ninth or tenth terrace — it's hard to keep count, the ziggurat does something to your sense of up — you find the boot-prints. Not fresh. Pressed into a film of mineral dust on a catwalk grate, the same print, over and over, always walking the same line along the rail, never wandering, never doubling back except to return the way it came. Whoever made them has walked this exact path so many times that the dust itself has learned the route.

And then, eventually — not dramatically, not with any flourish the place would consider beneath it — you find the keeper.

They are easy to miss because they have stopped reading as a separate thing from the structure. They sit, when they sit, on a fold-down steel shelf bolted to the catwalk rail at the eleventh tank, in the exact spot where the light through the grimed glass roof pools warmest. They are old in the way the rivets are old — not worn out, just *arrived*, finished with becoming anything else. Skin gone the same green-grey patina as the tank iron, whether from years of standing in this humid, copper-tasting air or from something the water itself has done to them, slow mineral exchange, decades of breathing what the plants breathe. Their clothes are practical and the color of nothing — canvas and oilcloth, the kind of garment that exists to be repaired forever rather than replaced. A ring of small brass tools hangs at their belt: a gasket key, a dial-glass cloth, a thin spike for clearing valve-seats of root hair. They have the calm, unhurried economy of movement that comes from decades spent in a place where haste serves no purpose, where the only schedule that matters is a pump-cycle that takes a season to complete one breath.

They do not startle at being found. They have likely known you were on the catwalks for several terraces — sound carries oddly here, down through the water-weight of the tanks — and they regard you with the mild, unoffended attention of someone for whom visitors are rare but not unprecedented, filed mentally alongside other infrequent events: a valve needing reseating, a vine outgrowing its trellis-rope, a gauge needle drifting half a degree off true.

They speak, if they speak, slowly, as though words are a mechanism they operate rather than a fluency they possess — sentences with the unhurried pacing of someone who has had whole years in which the only voice to answer was the knock of a valve finding its seat. They will tell you, if asked, that they were trained to this — that someone trained them, once, an older keeper now long retired into whatever retirement means in a place like this (a lower terrace, perhaps, gone quiet and unvisited; or simply gone, folded back into the cycle like a dropped petal dissolving in warm water). They were taught the gauges, the key that has no key — taught to read pressure and flow and root-density not as numbers but as a kind of weather, an intuition built up against the brass faces over years until the dials stopped needing translation. They were taught which trumpet-flower vines need their growth redirected before they choke a catwalk joint, which filter-galleries need their root-mass thinned before the slow weep of water through them stutters. They were taught the walk — that specific, repeated path along the rail, the one the dust now remembers better than they probably do, since their feet long ago stopped needing to think about it.

They do not know, particularly, why the place was built. This is not coyness; the absence of an answer doesn't trouble them the way it troubles you. They were given a function, not a history. Someone, a generation or two back, raised this stepped mountain of tanks and seeded it with green for a reason that has not survived as anything except the keeping itself — the cycle that must be tended, the brimful exactness that must be maintained, terrace answering terrace, every tank reading the same fullness as though the whole ziggurat were an instrument and someone, somewhere, still cared whether it stayed in tune. The keeper has inherited the caring without the cause. They check the gauges because the gauges must be checked. They thin the roots because the roots, unthinned, would eventually choke the slow weeping cycle that is this place's only kind of breath. They are, in a very literal sense, the last surviving function of an intention nobody troubled to write down.

It would be wrong to call them lonely. Loneliness implies an absence felt against a remembered presence, and the keeper does not appear to remember, or particularly miss, a version of life with other voices in it. They have the quiet, total acclimatization of someone who has spent so long inside one slow machine-breath that their own pulse may have, by now, learned its rhythm — footsteps timed without meaning to to the murmur of the compressors three floors down, sleep (somewhere; there must be a keeper's berth tucked into the structure, a steel room with a cot and a single gauge-glass window, though you never quite find it) measured out in the same unhurried seasons as the tank-cycle itself.

If you ask them what happens when they can no longer climb the ladders — when the bright rungs go unworn, when the dust on the ninth terrace stops learning a new boot-print — they will look at you for a moment with something that isn't quite concern, more a mild, distant curiosity, as if you've asked a question about a tank three terraces below the ones they tend, interesting but not their gauge to read.

"Someone will come," they might say. Or they might say nothing at all, and simply return to the rail, to the slow business of clearing a root-snarl from a valve-seat, while behind them, through the brimful water, the topmost leaves of something ancient and over-grown press their broad flat hands against the inside of the glass, patient, in no hurry, waiting on a cycle that takes a season to complete one breath.
