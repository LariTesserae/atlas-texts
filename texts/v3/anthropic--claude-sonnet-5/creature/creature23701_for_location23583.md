# Who Holds the Terraces

They are, at first glance, unremarkable — broad-shouldered hill people in undyed wool and weathered leather, faces seamed by wind rather than worry, hands thick-knuckled from stone-work and looms. You could set them down in any highland village in any unremembered country and they would not look out of place. It is only after a day or two among them that the wrongness of their ordinariness begins to surface, the way a held note starts to sound strange the longer it continues without resolving.

## Bearing

They move at the pace of the mist — unhurried, unworried, with the particular economy of people who have never once needed to rush toward an unanswered question. Their eyes, disproportionately often, carry a paleness that echoes the grazing herds threading the staircases: a light grey-gold, faintly luminous in low light, as if some of the same blood runs under both kinds of skin. They speak in the market's habitual murmur not from politeness but because raised voices feel, to them, like a category error — noise pitched for a world that changes, used in a world that doesn't.

Ask a child what they want to be and they will answer with a trade, never a place, never an ambition to leave. Not because leaving is forbidden — no one forbids anything here — but because the thought arrives in them already foreclosed, the way you don't *decide* not to walk off the edge of a terrace. The geometry of their wanting matches the geometry of the stone.

## Work

Everyone here is, eventually, exactly one thing. The smith's hammer-arm is the smith's hammer-arm from boyhood; there is no apprenticeship in the sense of trial and uncertainty, only a slow recognition, as a child grows, of the shape they were apparently always going to be. Looms are inherited along with the precise tension of their threads. The council house is staffed by men and women who seem to step into their seats already knowing the customary phrasing for grain disputes and water-rights, as though the role taught itself to them in sleep before they ever sat down.

They tend gardens that yield the same harvest at the same hour of the same season without being told to, and they receive this consistency without surprise — the surprise, if it exists at all, would be in a harvest arriving early, or short, or strange. None ever has.

## Birth, Age, and the Long Patience

They are born in the ordinary way, age in the ordinary way, die in the ordinary way — wrinkled hands, white hair, a last quiet breath in a stone room with turf overhead, mourned with the same unhurried murmur as everything else. This is the one mercy or one cruelty of the place, depending on who you ask: the people are not frozen, only the town is. Grief moves through Vethnir Quoth exactly as grief should, rising and softening over a season, while the house the mourners sit in does not change a single stone in sympathy or indifference.

What unsettles, if anything does, is the replacement. When the old smith dies, a young one is already there with the right calluses. When a council elder is buried, an empty chair does not stay empty past the season — someone simply moves into it, already speaking the cadence the seat requires, and no one remarks on how quickly or completely the knowledge transferred, because to them it isn't transfer. It's recognition. The town has a finite number of shapes a person can be, and death only ever opens a shape back up for the next person already growing into it.

## What They Believe, If "Believe" Is the Word

They have no creation myth worth the name — ask after one and you get the same shrug an elder gives about the town's age. Not evasion. Genuine absence of the question's relevance. If pressed, the oldest among them might say something like: *the terraces were always going to be terraces, and we were always going to be the ones standing on them.* It is not fatalism, exactly — fatalism implies a fate imposed from outside, and they do not experience their permanence as imposed. It is closer to the calm of someone standing in a room whose dimensions they have simply never needed to question, because the room has never once given them cause.

The keepers of the council-house flame — there is always one, a quiet, unremarked vocation, never advertised, never sought, simply settled into by whoever turns out to have been meant for it — will tell you, if you ask gently enough, that the flame doesn't need tending because it was *lit correctly the first time*. They say this the way you might say a knot was tied correctly: not as miracle, but as competence, on a scale large enough that "the first time" and "always" have stopped being different words.

## To a Stranger's Eye

You will be fed, housed, spoken to in that low courteous register, and never once made to feel unwelcome. But you will also notice, if you stay more than a few days, that no one asks where you're from with any real hunger for the answer, and no one asks when you're leaving with any real curiosity about it either. You are weather to them — mist, passing light, a thing that moves through the held breath of the place without being able to change its pressure.

They are not unkind. They are simply calibrated, the way the lamps are calibrated, the way the harvest is calibrated — to a town that finished becoming itself before anyone alive can remember, and folded its people, gently and completely, into the same finished shape.
