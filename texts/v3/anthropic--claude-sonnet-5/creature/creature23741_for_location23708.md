# Those Who Are Mostly the Building

You will spend a long time believing the terraces are uninhabited, in any sense that word usually carries, and you will be both right and exactly wrong.

## The Mistake of Counting Heads

The long-limbed, gray-furred shape you glimpse hand-over-handing along a vine and gone before you've decided it was real — that is not a rare animal glimpsed in a vast empty habitat. That is a juvenile of the species that built this place, and you are not seeing few of them because there are few of them. You are seeing few of them because almost none of them, at any given time, are doing something so conspicuous as *moving*.

They are born light, quick, sharp-eyed, with hands suited equally to climbing and to fine work — small hands, by the place's standards, hands that fit a stone adze, a bone awl, a wood-handled hook. For the first decades of a life that runs very, very long, they are exactly what you'd expect from those tools: competent, modest builders, repairing a beam here, training a vine there, re-knotting a single lashing that has worked loose in a storm. No one juvenile has ever built so much as a single terrace. No one juvenile ever will. This is why the tools are humble and the structure is not — the tools were never meant to explain the structure. They were only ever meant to explain a single human-scaled lifetime's single human-scaled contribution to something that has been receiving such contributions for longer than any one of them can hold in memory.

## The Slowing

What the tools cannot explain, the lifespan does.

As a juvenile ages — and they age on a clock that makes "century" a unit of middle life, not an epoch — it slows. Not metaphorically: its blood-rhythm, its hunger, its need for motion, all of it lengthens, the way a held note lengthens, until decades pass between one full stride and the next. It begins to favor a place. It returns to it. Its hands, which once held tools, begin instead to *become* tool — the resin that the structure is finished in, the green-black material that takes a polish like horn, is the resin this species exudes from its palms and the long ridges of its forearms in its slowing years, at first to seal a joint it has just tied, later simply because its body cannot stop doing the thing it always did, even after there is no more joint to seal. The fiber-ropes thick as a torso are, in their oldest sections, not rope at all anymore but hair — a single individual's hair, grown without end for two or three hundred years, braided by hands now too slow to do anything else, into a single hawser nobody thinks to call hair because by then it has the load-bearing honesty of rope.

The vine-shape you pass twice, the one that holds a crouching posture too good to be accident, the one that you would swear has turned to face you — it has. Not quickly. It has been turning to face you since before you arrived on this terrace, and it will complete the motion sometime after you are an old story you tell, if it has not already decided, in whatever long patient register passes for decision at that speed, that facing you was worth the years it cost.

This is why the place feels hollow rather than empty. It is not under-populated. It is *populated entirely by structure*, and the population simply doesn't read, to a quick mammal eye passing through in an afternoon, as population.

## The Knots That Get Re-Tied

Between the quick juveniles and the rooted, transformed elders, there is a middle population — slowed but not yet still, mobile but rarely, the ones who come by night. They are not maintaining the structure as a stranger would maintain a building. They are tending their own ancestors. Every re-knotted lashing is a child visiting a grandparent who can no longer speak above the register of a creaking joint settling after rain, and doing the one thing that grandparent still needs: keeping the rope that used to be their hair from fraying, keeping the joint that used to be their handiwork from working loose, keeping a resin-sealed seam they laid down four hundred years ago from finally, after everything, failing.

They do not do this out of duty exactly. The line between *caring for my elder* and *performing routine structural maintenance* has not existed for them in longer than their oldest living members can recall it existing. It is simply what is done, at night, in the steam, by the few who are still quick enough to be done with it before dawn.

## The Inscriptions

The unreadable, ever-shifting writing in the joinery is not a code, and no one is carving it, in the sense of an event you could witness and call carving. It is being carved continuously, by an elder whose single hooked finger — once a tool-using hand, now mostly root — completes one stroke of one character roughly every several years. You pass the same panel twice in an afternoon and find it changed because, at the rate this place's oldest residents move, an afternoon and a century are not reliably different lengths of time, and the elder doing the carving has had, by its own accounting, ample time to add a stroke since you last looked away.

No living juvenile can read it. The elders who write it were juveniles once too, and presumably could read what their own elders wrote, before they slowed past the point where reading and writing remained the same activity for them as for you. What it says is the species' oldest unsolved problem, posed by their own grandparents, in their own grandparents' hands, and it is entirely possible that no one now embedded in the structure remembers, either.

## What's Left Over

There is a remainder this doesn't account for — the puddle that reflects weather that hasn't happened yet, on a terrace that isn't this one. No one who still moves quickly enough to be asked about it has an answer. Perhaps it's nothing to do with the species at all. Perhaps it's something the resin does, in sufficient age and sufficient quantity, that has nothing to do with anyone's intention, the way a sufficiently large body of anything starts doing things bodies that size simply do. Or perhaps it is an elder, slowed past mobility, past carving, past almost everything — down to the very last register of activity a nervous system can sustain — still managing, somehow, once every few years, to dream.
