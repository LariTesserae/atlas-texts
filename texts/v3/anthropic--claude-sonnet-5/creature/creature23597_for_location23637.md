They are called, in the closest the local tongues come to a word for it, the lashed — though no one outside a given stretch of corridor would recognize another band's word for the same thing, and within a stretch the word is barely needed, since everyone there is lashed and there is no one else to distinguish them from. They are pale in the way the structure is pale at its newest joints: skin that has not seen direct sun in longer than any genealogy reaches back, damp-cool to the touch, faintly grey-brown in the lamplight, so that a sleeping body curled into a niche can be mistaken, at a glance, for a coil of unworked fiber-strut waiting to be lashed into the wall. Their eyes are wide and dark-pupiled, built for the thin doses of light the Wickwork allows, and they squint and water painfully in the rare shafts of paler illumination that fall from gaps above — those moments are called, in most stretches, the wincing, and small children are warned about looking up too long.

They are compact, broad-shouldered, long in the forearm and the finger, because the hand that grips a strut and the hand that ties a lashing and the hand that finds a hold in the dark by feel alone is the only inheritance that matters here. A child's worth is measured early and without cruelty in the strength of its grip and the speed at which it learns the twelve or twenty or forty knots its particular stretch favors — knots vary, drift, mutate between one cluster of rooms and the next the way accents do, so that a stranger's lashing can be read, by someone who knows the work, almost like a signature, almost like a place of birth. They climb before they walk well on flat ground; flat ground is rare and walking on it is considered, faintly, a children's competence, something you grow out of in favor of better things — the controlled fall through a gap, the four-limbed lateral scuttle along a joist, the trick of hanging by knees from a crossbeam to free both hands for work.

They do not speak loudly, and do not need to, because the Wickwork itself is the better voice. Sound travels through the dense fiber-strut far better than it travels through the damp air, and so much of what looks, to an outsider, like ambient noise — the hammering, the chock of blade on strut, the percussion of water finding its forty-level path downward — is in fact message, is in fact language, a code of taps and strikes against load-bearing members that carries for astonishing distances through the joinery and tells a listening hand, pressed flat against a beam, who is coming, what has failed, where to bring water, where a child has wandered, where a section is about to give. Children are taught the tap-code before they are taught most knots. The old, whose hands shake too badly to lash anymore and whose eyes have gone milky in the dark, become listeners — pressing palms and cheeks against the grain for hours, translating the structure's groans and settlings into warnings, into news, into something almost like prophecy, since a beam that creaks a certain way three days running will, on the fourth, usually fall.

Their belief, where they have one coherent enough to be called that, holds the Wickwork as neither built thing nor living thing but something unresolved between the two, and holds themselves as its hands rather than its tenants — the working extremity of something too large and too slow to be sensed any other way except through the urge, never quite questioned, never quite satisfied, to lash one more strut onto what is already there. Their language has a rich vocabulary for joint, for grain, for the failing creak that precedes collapse, for the particular ache of forearms after a long lashing-session, and almost nothing for finished, for done, for arrived — those concepts exist mostly as cautionary shapes, things that happened to other, foolish people in old stories, people who one day decided a room was complete and stopped, and were, depending on which stretch tells it, either found gone the next morning or found still there, gone strange, gone pale and small and quick, indistinguishable thereafter from the flickering creatures that live in the joints and are seen and gone before anyone can say what they were. Mothers invoke this to children reluctant to keep working past tired hands: don't stop, or you'll go small. No one quite believes it and everyone, in the damp and dark, half does.

They live in clusters loosely organized around a worked stretch — a family, two, sometimes three, sharing the maintenance of some knot of rooms and corridors, trading surplus lashings and tools left at junctions with neighboring clusters they may meet only a few times in a life, mostly by sound, mostly by reputation carried tap by tap through forty layers of grain. They marry across these boundaries sometimes, when a courier's errand becomes a courtship; mostly they simply work, generation lashed to generation the way beam is lashed to beam, each adding a little, finishing nothing, listening always to the structure's voice for the next small failure that will tell them where, tonight, to build.
