## The Tender

You will meet it only once, and only if you walk far enough that the idea of turning back has stopped occurring to you as an option.

It does not appear so much as it is suddenly the thing your eye was already resting on — a figure among the shelf-grass, upright, unhurried, the same patient grey-green as the waxy shrubs around it, so that for a long moment you cannot say whether you are looking at a person or at a stand of vegetation that has merely adopted, provisionally, the rough proportions of one. It has limbs in the right number. It has the suggestion of a head, held at the attentive angle of something listening for a sound it does not expect to hear. Beyond that, description begins to slide off it the way water slides off the waxy leaves — not because it resists looking, but because looking at it tells you less than you expect, the way looking too long at the machines tells you less the longer you look.

It is always found near one of the machines, though never touching it. It does not turn the toothed wheels. It does not climb the riveted towers or crack the frosted gauge-faces to read what they no longer measure. Its relationship to the machinery is something closer to companionship than custodianship — the bearing of someone keeping an old colleague company through a shift that ended decades ago and that neither of them has thought to mention is over. Sometimes it rests a hand flat against the cold iron, not to warm it, not to listen for a heartbeat inside it, but simply in the manner of a hand laid on a shoulder. The iron does not respond. The Tender does not seem to expect it to. This appears to be, as far as you can tell, the whole of its labor: to be present at the unfinished thing without finishing it, without disturbing the particular grace of its unfinishedness, the same grace that keeps the basalt sharp-lipped and the grass forever the color of unripe wheat.

It does not speak, though you will feel, standing near it, the distinct impression of a conversation that is already underway and that you have walked into the middle of — as if it has been mid-sentence for longer than language has existed, addressing the machine, or the rock, or the whole plateau, in a register pitched too low and too slow for a single human encounter to catch more than a syllable of. The syllable you catch will not translate. You will carry the shape of it home anyway, the way you carry the shape of a name spoken once in a dream.

Up close — and you are permitted close, it does not retreat, does not startle, regards your arrival with the mild unsurprise of a thing that has always known the exact day you would come, though it had no calendar to know it on — up close you will notice that its skin, if it is skin, holds the same patina the gauge-faces hold: not rust, not age, but a fine even matting, as though it has been standing in this clean ordinary light for so long that the light has begun, very gently, to settle into it as residue. Its eyes, if they are eyes, do not track you so much as include you, the way the horizon includes the next shelf rising blue-grey into haze, without urgency, without particular interest in whether you stay.

It will not stop you from touching the machines. It will not warn you about the grass. It does not seem to feel responsible for the few unspoken laws the plateau keeps — it did not write them, did not enforce them, perhaps did not even notice they existed before you arrived to test them. Its only evident function is fidelity: to remain, exactly here, exactly like this, for the tower or the boiler-drum or the long horizontal frame of interlocked wheels, in case the labor they were built for is ever assigned after all, in case the single hand that never came finally arrives to turn the first gear — and in that eventuality, which it does not appear to anticipate any more than it discounts, to be the thing standing nearby that remembers, in whatever way a creature this patient remembers anything, what the machine was for.

You will not see it leave. There is no moment of departure to witness, only the gradual, retrospective realization, some shelves later, that you have been walking alone for a while now, and that you cannot say when that started. The grass where it stood will show no path worn into it, no flattened blades, no proof. The plateau does not keep records of its own visitors, including, it seems, the one who never really left to begin with.

If you ask the place — and there is no one to ask, but if you stand still long enough the asking happens anyway, in the muffled quality the air gives your own thoughts — what the Tender is *for*, the plateau will not answer in words. It will only continue to show you one more shelf, rising slightly higher, holding one more distant tower, for as far as you are willing to walk: the same patient unfinished motion, held level, forever very nearly about to begin.
