They call themselves, when they bother to call themselves anything to outsiders, the Tenders, though among each other the word they use translates closer to *the ones who stayed.* Nobody remembers exactly when that staying began — three generations, four, long enough that the surface world is a half-believed rumor passed down with the same weight as a fairy tale, a place with too much sky that their grandmothers' grandmothers walked away from when the works flooded and nobody official came back down to fix it. They fixed it themselves. They have been fixing it ever since.

You'd know them first by how they move before you'd know them by how they look — a particular economy of motion, low and sure, shoulders rolled forward, built across years of ducking under cable-vine and stepping wide of channels without looking down, the body having long since memorized the hollow better than the eye could read it fresh. They are not a tall people. The ceilings taught them that, or maybe just selected for it; either way, in a space where the canopy presses low and the pipework runs at exactly the height to crack an inattentive skull, getting big was never an advantage. What they have instead is a deep, settled compactness, strong through the forearms and back from a life of hauling, pruning, hand-cranking the older valve-wheels that the belt-drives never got linked to. Their skin runs pale-green-grey in the bottle-light, not from anything strange in the blood but simply because that's the only color of light most of them have ever stood in for more than an hour at a stretch; on the rare climbs up toward a grate-shaft where real daylight leaks thin and gold, you can watch them flinch and squint like something nocturnal caught out, and then watch the pleasure spread over their faces anyway, helpless, the way anyone reacts to warmth they don't get often.

Their clothes are practical to the point of camouflage — close-woven, oiled against the wet, mottled green-brown-rust not by dye but by years of the same stains setting in over and over until the cloth simply gave up being any other color. Tool-belts instead of jewelry. Where decoration exists at all it's functional first and beautiful second: a pruning-hook handle wound in colored cord to mark whose it is, a boiler-tender's gloves embroidered at the cuff by someone who loved her enough to spend an evening's firelight on it. Hair worn short or bound tight, always — anything else, the vines apparently take a notable interest in.

They work in a rhythm that has nothing to do with day or night, since the hollow gives them neither, and everything to do with the machines' own patient demands and the plants' slower ones. There is a roster, unwritten but absolutely kept, of who walks the channel-lines checking for blockage, who oils which gear-train, who climbs the bamboo-stalk lattice to re-route a pipe before a root finally wins its decades-long argument with the iron. Children learn the rules before they learn much else — *don't block a channel, don't starve a light* — recited like nursery rhyme because that's exactly what it is, survival instruction worn smooth into song so it sticks. By ten they can tell a load-bearing vine from a decorative one by feel alone, in the dark, which they need to be able to do because half the hollow *is* dark, electric bulb-light being a resource budgeted as carefully as anything else, strung where it's needed and dimmed or dark where memory and touch will do instead.

They are not gardeners exactly, and not engineers exactly, and they would probably tell you — if they trusted you enough to talk, which takes time, strangers down here being rare and not historically a good sign — that the distinction doesn't mean much when your boiler is wrapped in moss that's the only thing keeping its old cracked housing from venting steam into your face, or when your best irrigation channel was originally a drainage line for a textile press that hasn't run in sixty years but still, somehow, structurally, needs to be kept from silting. They've stopped asking which parts of the Greenworks are nature and which are machine. Both keep them warm. Both will kill you if you stop paying attention. That's the whole of the theology down here, if you want to call it that: attention is owed, and the hollow collects on the debt either way, so you may as well pay it gladly.

There's a particular sound they make that you wouldn't predict — they sing, often, low and to no apparent audience, working-songs pitched to carry through the muffling green the way ordinary speech doesn't, so that a boiler-tender three chambers off can hear the line of a song and know that whoever's singing it is fine, is working, is exactly where they're supposed to be. It is, you eventually realize, the closest thing the Greenworks has to birdsong. They made it themselves, on purpose or not, to fill the silence the real birds left when the city built over them. It carries up through the grates sometimes, thin, on the warm wet air rising toward streets that don't know to listen for it — a sound with nowhere further up to go, returning instead, the way everything down here eventually does, back into the green.
