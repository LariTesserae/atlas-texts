They are called, by the few outside the basin who have reason to name them at all, the **pump-tenders**, though that word does no justice to what tending means here — not maintenance in the ordinary sense, but something closer to nursing, to midwifery, performed on machines whose actual workings passed out of living memory generations before the current tenders were born.

You would not see them at first. They are the same grey-on-grey as the stones, dressed in waxed canvas and oiled hide gone the color of the lichen they crouch among, and they have the trick — learned, not chosen — of going still when something unfamiliar moves through the basin. Crouched on a plank, motionless, a tender can pass for one more knuckle of weathered rock until you are nearly on top of them, and then they unfold, slow, joints first, the way the many-legged things in the shallows unfold from beneath their stones.

Pale, yes — paler than people who live under open sky, skin gone soft and slightly puckered at the fingertips from a life spent in perpetual damp, the way skin puckers after too long in a bath. Their eyes are open but underused; sight here is a secondary sense, good for picking a dry stone to step on and not much past that, and so the eyes of the older tenders have a milky, unfocused quality, not blind but simply *retired*, while the rest of the face has sharpened to compensate — wide nostrils for the mineral smell of healthy water against the sourness of water gone wrong, a faint permanent cock of the head, listening. Their hands have gone strange too: broad, slightly webbed between the first knuckles, skilled at the particular grip needed to walk a wet plank or right a stone gone loose in the channel. Stooped, most of them, from a life bent forward — over pipes, over the gaps between stones, over each other, checking.

They live in the pump houses themselves, or in the lean structures built against them, sharing the warmth that leaks from the iron casings. Families — if that's the right word, and it may not be, given how loosely the basin's people seem to attach to one another — tend clusters of three or four engines each, inherited the way a name is inherited, without ceremony, simply by being the one still standing nearby when the previous tender's hands gave out. There is no larger settlement, no center to the basin's human geography; just these small constellations of kin spread among the pump houses, meeting rarely, trading across the planks in a kind of unhurried barter — a jar of the glowing algae-mat for light, a clutch of the pale jointed creatures cleaned and skewered, a length of cured gut for binding pipe-joints where the iron has worn thin.

What they do, mostly, is *listen*. Each tender knows their engines' breathing the way a parent knows a sleeping child's — knows the register of a good piston-stroke from a bad one, the particular cough that means a valve wants oiling, the deeper, worse silence that means something has stopped that should not stop. They have no schematics, no inherited diagrams; the knowledge lives entirely in the ear and the hand, passed down as a kind of apprenticeship in sound. When an engine sickens they treat it as a tender treats illness — observation first, then small interventions, oil and patience and a tap-code knocked along the pipe to some other tender further down the basin, a request for a second pair of hands relayed engine to engine faster than any voice could carry it through the damp.

They eat what the basin gives them: the eyeless fish-things from the seep-channels, split and dried over engine-heat; the many-legged creatures, which they regard with the mild affection of people who've eaten the same thing their whole lives and stopped finding it strange; mats of the glow-edged algae scraped from the stones, which they say — though none of them are quite sure why they say it — sweetens the water if you boil it. They do not eat what they find embedded in the stones, the half-drowned machined fragments, the seams and corners too regular for geology. Those they keep. Every pump house has, somewhere in its dim brick interior, a small accumulated shelf of such things — a smooth curved plate of unidentifiable alloy, a ring of metal too perfectly round, a fragment etched with marks no tender has ever been able to read as language or as anything else. They are not worshipped, exactly. But they are not discarded either, and a tender moving house — which happens, rarely, when an engine finally and permanently dies — will carry their shelf's contents with the same care as their tools.

They do not, on the whole, wonder very hard about the larger questions. Ask a tender what the engines are *for*, where the water goes once pumped, what the basin is feeding — and you will get a shrug, unbothered, the shrug of someone who has made peace with maintaining a thing larger than their understanding of it, the way anyone makes peace with their own heartbeat. The engines breathe. The stones hold their shape. The basin has not flooded in anyone's memory, nor in their parents', and that is, as far as the tenders are concerned, evidence enough that the tending is working, and that the working is the point.

They are not unhappy. There is a particular, settled quietness to them, the quietness of people whose whole world is a held breath they have learned to breathe inside of — small, damp, dim, endlessly the same, and theirs.
