They are few, and they have made an art of being few.

You will meet them in ones and twos — a woman re-pegging a shutter, a boy hauling a bucket up from the flume with the unhurried economy of someone who has done it ten thousand times, an old man sitting cross-legged in a doorway with his eyes shut, apparently listening to something. They move quietly, almost apologetically, against the enormous sound — small gestures, economical footsteps, the particular hush of people who have spent their whole lives inside noise and have learned to make none of their own. Ask them a question and they answer in a voice pitched low and close, leaning toward you, because shouting over the racket has never once occurred to them as the solution; the racket is simply weather. You shelter your voice the way you'd shelter a candle.

There are not many of them — perhaps the population of three or four large households, spread thin across a settlement built for ten times that number. But the work of the place is not done by hands alone. Generations of patient tinkering have given them water: channeled, dropped, weighted, leveraged, so that the mountain's own endless seep does the labor that bodies would otherwise have to do. Looms knock because a trip-hammer fed by a flume knocks them. The gong sounds at its interval because a counterweight, filled drop by drop from a runnel cut precisely for the purpose, tips on its own schedule and has done so for longer than anyone currently alive can account for. A dozen mechanisms run all day untended, each one doing the work, and making the noise, of a person who isn't there. The terrace sounds inhabited by a hundred because a hundred years of someone's grandfather's patience built the hundred small machines that now do the haunting for them.

They will not explain this to you readily, not because it is secret but because it does not occur to them as remarkable. It is simply how the work gets done, here, where there are not enough hands and never were. A child here learns the flumes before she learns her letters — which gate to lift for the eastern loom, how long the western hammer-trough takes to fill, how to read the pitch of dripping water to know if a channel has silted. Old age, here, is measured less in years than in how much of the mountain's plumbing a person carries in their head; the oldest among them are revered not for wisdom exactly but for memory, for being the last living index of which pipe feeds which silence.

About the green they are unbothered to the point of reverence. They cut a path when a path becomes truly impassable and they accept, without complaint or surprise, that the ferns will have it back within two seasons. There's a way they talk about the mountain — rarely, and sidelong, the way you'd talk about a relation whose temper you respect — that suggests they understand themselves as guests on ground still settling its own opinion of itself. The cracks in the foundation stones are watched, not feared: someone goes out after the gong's slow interval and runs a fingertip along the same hairline fracture they ran a fingertip along last season, feeling for whether it has widened a grain's width, and says nothing about it to anyone, only nods, or doesn't, and goes back inside.

They do not hunt, much, and do not seem to wonder why so little moves on four legs or two wings up here; when you ask, you get a shrug, or a glance toward the mist at the terrace's open edge, as if the absence of fauna is itself a kind of company they've gotten used to, the way you get used to a person who doesn't talk much but is reliably present. The single dark bird working the updrafts at the cliff has, more than one of them will tell you without irony, a name.

At night the sound changes register but does not stop — the gong rarer, the looms stilled, but the water never quiet, and somewhere a single mechanism left wound will knock on through the dark hours doing some small chore for nobody in particular. People sleep close to that sound the way you'd sleep close to a hearth-fire's tick: it is the proof that the place is being tended, even when every visible hand has gone still. They will tell you, if you stay long enough and ask gently enough, that this is the whole of their work and their inheritance both — not to finish the mountain, not to outpace its slow argument with itself about what it means to become solid, but to keep it company through the becoming, with enough noise that it never has to settle alone.
