They are called nothing here, because there is no one to call them anything, but if you watched long enough you would start needing a name for them anyway — the long-necked grazers, the pale ones, the herd that is never quite a herd.

They stand chest-high to a person at the shoulder and taller at the head, which they carry not proudly but practically, lifted on a neck that lets them browse the cushion-plants without bending the enormous, slow architecture of their legs. The coat is pale to the point of being faintly luminous in the steppe's strange directionless light — not white, more the color of dry grass gone through one winter, individual hairs hollow and packed dense as the cushion-plants themselves, an insulating mat grown for air this thin and cold and for nothing else. Their chests are absurdly deep, built around lungs that have learned the same lesson the whole plateau has learned: that there is less here to work with, and so everything must work harder, slower, with less waste. They breathe audibly, a long low working sound, like a bellows worked by someone who is in absolutely no hurry.

They move the way the description warned you to expect — patient, ground-covering, a walk that could continue for days and probably does. A "herd" might be three animals or it might be forty spread so far across a swell of land that you only ever see three at a time, the rest implied rather than witnessed, the way the rest of a cloud-formation is implied by the piece of it overhead. They graze in passing bites, taking a mouthful from a cushion-plant and moving twenty feet on before taking another, so that no single mat of ancient growth is ever struck twice in a day, sometimes not twice in a season. It is less feeding than visiting. The plants, for their part, seem to have arranged their whole slow strategy of growth around being visited this way — gently, glancingly, like something being kept rather than consumed.

Their eyes are set high and wide for a horizon that never stops, dark and large enough to gather what light there is at dusk, when the cold drops fast and the grazers turn, as one, unhurried, toward the lee of whatever stone is nearest, and stand through the night facing into where the wind will come from, necks low, breathing audible, patient with the cold the way they are patient with everything. They do not flee from much. There is not much here to flee from, and a very great deal of land to do it across if they had to, so mostly they simply continue, the way the wind continues, the way the rock continues being worn — at a pace that assumes there will always be enough time, because so far there always has been.

They are old when they die, by any reckoning, and their bones — when you do, rarely, find one — are bleached and smoothed and have usually been pulled half into the turf already, accepted by the same slow processes that smooth the boulders, becoming one more pale curve in a landscape made of pale curves, indistinguishable in a generation from the stone.

Above them, all day, the bird-shape works the updraft along the escarpment, and it is tempting to think it is hunting, watching, waiting for something — but it may simply be doing what the grazers do, what the wind does, what the whole plateau does: continuing, at the scale built for it, for no other reason than that continuing is what is here to be done. It passes over the herds without dropping, without circling, a black mark crossing the bright sky the way a cathedral-shadow crosses the grass, taking its long minutes, indifferent to the smallness of anything beneath it.

And in the lee of the stones, smaller than either, the hare-that-is-almost-nothing keeps its own time, glimpsed at dusk in the last warm light and gone before you can be sure you saw it — quick where everything else is slow, the one note of urgency the steppe permits itself, as if it had been designed as a reminder that not everything up here has all the time in the world. Everything else, you come to understand, does.
