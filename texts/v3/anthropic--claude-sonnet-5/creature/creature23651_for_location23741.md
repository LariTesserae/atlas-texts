## What Lives There

You will not see one straight on. That is the first rule, learned the way you learn all the rules here — by violation, by the way the air changes when you almost do.

They are at the edges. A shape resolving out of a doorway-shaped aperture that you'd already decided was just a view into an empty room, until it isn't empty, until something in it had been standing there the whole time you looked, patient as architecture. By the time your eyes commit to focusing, the threshold is a threshold again. Just stone. Just the faint sourceless gleam doing what it does to light.

## The Caretakers

Call them that for lack of anything truer. They are humanoid the way the buildings are urban — in *intent*, compressed, gesture rather than fact. Roughly the height a doorway here demands, which is to say slightly wrong for the doorway, built either a little before the architecture decided its final proportions or a little after, you can never tell which. Limbs too long at the forearm, too economical at the joint, moving with the unhurried, slightly-too-smooth continuity of something that has never once needed to catch its balance.

Their surface does what the walls do — facets that shouldn't hold a gleam, holding one. In low light you could mistake a Caretaker for a section of wall that has decided, temporarily, to stand upright and walk. In better light (there is no better light) you might see that their bodies are seamed the same way the buildings are seamed: not at all, extruded whole, cured into sharp uncomplaining geometry. Where a face should resolve into expression, there is instead a kind of *patience* expressed structurally — a few long converging lines, like the place where four quarry-cuts meet and stop, having finished their argument.

They do not have eyes so much as apertures. Looking into one is the same vertiginous non-event as looking through one of the window-shaped gaps between buildings — you understand intellectually that you are looking *into* something, and you understand simultaneously that what stops your gaze a half-second later is not surface but a kind of administrative decision. There is a there there. It has simply declined to be reached.

## What They Do

They tend. This is the whole of it, as far as you can tell, and it is enough to fill what would otherwise be an eternity.

A Caretaker will be found adjusting a vine that has grown exactly as far as the process intended it to grow, pinching back a leaf-tip with two fingers in a gesture lifted wholesale from some ancestral memory of gardening, performed with total conviction and zero agricultural necessity. Another will sweep a threshold that gathers no dust, the broom-shape it carries leaving no mark on stone too young to be marked. A third stands at the lip of the false market arcade adjusting the angle of an empty stall as though a customer's comfort depended on it, then moves three meters to do the same to the stall beside it, on a rotation that will return it to the first stall in exactly the time it takes the moss in the gutters to advance one visible increment.

It is not labor. It has the *shape* of labor worn the way the settlement wears the shape of a town — full-height, full-detail, scaled to a function that isn't actually occurring. You begin to suspect the Caretakers are not maintaining the settlement so much as *rehearsing habitation* on its behalf, performing the motions a population would make if a population existed, because the process beneath the stone requires the rehearsal more than it requires the population. The town must look lived-in. The Caretakers are the looking.

## The Sound, Closer To

When the harmonic sweep moves through — that rising-falling tone over twenty or thirty seconds, like something singing through the structure rather than through air — you eventually understand, with the specific dread of a fact arriving too late to be unlearned, that it is coming from *them*. Not from one. From all of them, wherever they currently stand in the settlement, in chorus, their bodies acting as resonant lengths in whatever vast underground instrument the chamber actually is. The deep tone in the floor is the instrument's body. The Caretakers are its strings, tuned, plucked from somewhere beneath the stone on a schedule that has nothing to do with you.

They do not stop tending while this happens. The one trimming the vine keeps trimming. The hum simply moves through it the way the weeping moves through the walls — ambient, continuous, not requiring the creature's attention because the creature's attention was never really the part of it doing the singing.

## When You Get Close

Up close — and you will, eventually, because the settlement is small and your afternoon is long and the geometry of the streets will eventually fail to keep you apart — there is warmth coming off them, the same uncomfortable warmth as the wet air, as though they are not warmer than the chamber so much as a slightly more concentrated *instance* of it. No smell of anything organic. No breath. The converging lines of the not-face do not turn to track you, and yet you understand, the way you understand a held silence is not the same as an empty room, that you have been noted, filed, and judged not load-bearing to whatever is currently being computed.

They have never harmed anyone who has come down the shaft. They have also never acknowledged that anyone has come down the shaft, in any way you could point to and call an acknowledgment. The two facts sit together uneasily, the way the moss sits in its too-tidy gutter, the way the empty market sits with its adjusted, customerless stalls.

You leave wondering whether you interrupted them, or whether — and this is the thought that follows you back up through eleven meters of wet clay and root-fiber into the ordinary, undersized dark — you were simply one more threshold-shaped gap they glanced through once, decided was a courtesy rather than a barrier, and moved on from, back to the tending, back to the rehearsal, back to keeping time with whatever sings beneath the floor and has no plans, ever, of finishing the work.
