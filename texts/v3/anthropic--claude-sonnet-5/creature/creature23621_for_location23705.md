# The Riggers of Kharras Vel

They are called riggers, though the word has flattened over generations into something closer to a species-name than a job title. You can pick one out of a crowd in any port on any kinder coast before they've said a word: the way they stand with knees slightly bent, as if still compensating for a deck that might buck at any moment; the way their eyes track motion at the periphery, always, even in a still room; the permanent hoarseness, the shout dressed up as a sentence.

## The Body Tuned to Iron

Generations on the rigs have done something to them that isn't quite adaptation and isn't quite damage, but sits in the space between. Their hearing is shot in the upper registers—gone first, the workers say, in the first decade on platform—but what's left has migrated downward into an almost unsettling sensitivity to bass and sub-bass, to the language of vibration through metal and water and bone. A rigger can stand on a platform with their palm flat against a support strut and tell you, with real confidence, what's happening four legs over: which pump is laboring, which chain is fraying, whether that long low groan is the platform settling or something enormous brushing past beneath. They read the rig's iron the way other people read faces. Some claim—and the chapel-keepers neither confirm nor deny this—that with enough years a rigger can feel a calving frenzy starting up out past the horizon line before the lookout's spotted the gulls (there are no gulls, but the word survives, attached now to whatever pale carrion-things wheel above a kill).

They are thick-built, most of them, layered against a cold that never breaks character, in waxed coats the color of the rust they live among, hooded, gloved even in sleep. Hands are the tell of seniority—young riggers have hands scarred from the basalt and the chain-teeth and the hot metal of badly-vented pipe; old riggers have hands gone strange and smooth and slightly wrong-shaped, fingers that have been broken and reset so many times by gear and weather that they've settled into new, permanent geometries. No rigger retires with all ten fingers doing what fingers are meant to do. It is not spoken of as tragedy. It's spoken of, when it's spoken of at all, as tenure.

## The Inner Weather

What the noise does to the mind, over years, is harder to map than what it does to the ear. Riggers report — to the rare visiting physicians, to each other in the strange hush of the bunkrooms where even the bunkrooms hum — a kind of permanent low thrum of unease that resolves, paradoxically, into calm the moment they're back on platform and the noise resumes full-throated around them. Silence is what frightens them. A rigger sent home, to a quiet inland town for a funeral or a discharge, will often be found within the week sitting near traffic, near factories, near anything that hammers and groans, unconsciously chasing a decibel floor their nervous system has decided is baseline reality. They sleep worse in quiet places. They startle at birdsong.

There is a particular look that comes into a rigger's eyes during the rare lulls — not silence, never silence, but the comparative hush between one shift's machinery and the next, when only the water-sound and the distant moan of something vast moving below remains. It's the look of a person doing math they don't want to finish: how deep, how old, how long has that thing down there been listening to us back.

## Belief, of a Practical Kind

The structure that nobody admits is a chapel is tended, in practice, by whoever's turn it is, in a rotation nobody designed and everyone honors. The chalk marks accumulate. Newer riggers are told, half-joking, half not, that the marks are a tally of safe trips down to the lower decking, where the chain-winches haul up what the rig exists to haul. Older riggers, asked privately, will tell you the marks are older than any rig currently standing, that they've been re-chalked so many times the wall underneath is soft with absorbed graphite and tallow, and that nobody quite remembers starting the practice, only continuing it, the way you continue breathing.

They are not religious people in any exportable sense. They do not pray to anything with a name. But every rigger, without exception, performs some small private gesture before a haul comes up the chain — a tap on a railing, a held breath, a word said only to themselves — and every rigger, without exception, denies that the gesture means anything, while doing it anyway, every single time.

## What They Don't Say About the Far Columns

Riggers who've worked the outer platforms — the ones nearest the edge of charted water, where the basalt clusters thicken and the things that surface to breathe are said to be larger and indifferent rather than curious — develop a particular silence around the topic that is conspicuous precisely because riggers are not, as a rule, silent people. Ask directly and you'll get a shrug, a half-shouted joke, a change of subject pitched just loud enough to be unmissable.

But leave a rigger alone long enough, in the grey hush between shifts, watching that water that has its own gravity of attention, and some of them will tell you — quietly, for once, quietly — that they don't think the noise the rigs make is for pumping efficiency, or venting pressure, or any of the technical reasons in the manuals.

They think, some of them, that the rigs are loud on purpose.

They think the noise is the only thing that's kept whatever shaped those far columns from noticing how close the chains now haul.

They don't say this twice. And they go back to shouting their ordinary, furious-sounding sentences across the clanging decks the moment the next shift bell rings, grateful, in some unexamined chamber of themselves, for every single decibel.
