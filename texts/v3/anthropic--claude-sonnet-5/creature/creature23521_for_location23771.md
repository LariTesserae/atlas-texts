# The Listening People of Vael Sorrow

You will not see them first. You will hear them not-being-there — the way a stretch of forest near a root-stake circle goes quiet when everywhere else roars, and you will assume coincidence, and you will be wrong only sometimes, which is worse than being wrong always.

They are called, in the few fragments of language anyone outside has managed to carry out of this place, something that translates badly as *those who keep the rooms quiet.* Call them the Hollow-folk, for lack of better.

## Body

They are built for the architecture, not the climate — which tells you something, because everything else here is built for the climate. Long-limbed, longer than seems efficient, with proportions that look wrong in any light bright enough to judge them by, which is one of the only mercies of this place: you will almost never judge them in good light. The limbs let them move through root-bridge and mid-canopy lattice the way the unseen climbers do, hand-over-hand, weight distributed, silent not through stealth but through sheer practiced economy — they have spent generations learning how a body moves through hollow rooms without making the rooms answer back.

Their skin holds no single color you could pin down; it seems to take a cast from whatever grey-blue light-memory is filtering through at the time, so that they read less as pigmented and more as *exposed* — like the underside of something that has never needed camouflage from sight, only from sound. Their eyes are large, pale, nearly without use in daylight and barely necessary in their own dark, vestigial in the way a sense becomes when a culture has fully committed to another one. What matters is the ears — set slightly forward, unusually mobile, rimmed with a fine fringe that you'd mistake for decoration until you watch it move independently, tracking, the way an animal's ear swivels to a sound the rest of its body hasn't reacted to yet.

They do not have much hair. What they have grows in patterns that look, at a glance, like the fissured bark of the lower scrub-tier — pale, striated, an unconscious or perhaps very conscious echo of the thing that shelters them.

## How they move through the world

Their lives are built around an economy of sound so total it amounts to a religion, though they would likely reject that word. Every action is weighed by its acoustic cost. They speak rarely, and when they do, in a register pitched to die quickly in the hollow air — clicks, low murmurs, a kind of compressed grammar that front-loads meaning into the first half-second of a sound before it can travel and betray them to whatever else is listening in the dark below the canopy-roof.

They do not sing. They do not call to each other across distance the way you might expect of people living in such vast, echoing rooms — that would be suicidal, here, where calls "arrive from impossible directions" and "double back on themselves" even when innocent. Instead they have developed a language of touch and proximity: hand against root-wall, a particular pattern of pressure that travels through the fused trunk-lattice itself, root-bridge as nervous system, message sent through wood instead of air. You could stand ten meters from two of them having an entire conversation and hear nothing at all.

## The stake circles

These are theirs. Not relics — not dead architecture left by a vanished culture, though the forest's age and the people's own quietness make every circle look like a ruin the moment you find it. They are maintained. The cord that looks gone to fiber is replaced, slowly, by hands that don't want to be seen replacing it. The diameter recurs and the stake-count recurs because both are exact, a discipline, possibly a measurement of something you don't have the framework to understand — acoustic geometry, maybe, a shape that does to ambient sound what the upper canopy does to wind: filters it, organizes it, makes a small bubble of hush in a world that has otherwise screamed continuously since before any of them were born.

They use these circles to do the one thing their economy of silence cannot otherwise permit: to gather. To be, briefly, in numbers, near each other, audible to each other at close range without fear, because the circle itself is doing the work of swallowing what they make. You would call it a town square if you could see what happened in it. You never quite can. They disperse before full dark, back into the hollow rooms, alone or in pairs, moving root-bridge to root-bridge like the long-limbed climbers they've spent generations learning to resemble.

## What they want, near as anyone can tell

Nothing from you. This is the hardest thing to accept about them — that their entire culture has organized itself around not being found, not making a sound that travels, not leaving more than they can help behind, and a stranger crashing loud and visible through their hollow rooms registers to them the way a falling branch registers to you: a hazard to note, route around, and forget. The notched ladder-trunks leading up toward the roaring tier, half-rotted now, were not abandoned because the people who made them died. They were abandoned because something up at that platform turned out to be listening back, and the lesson was taken, and the climbing stopped.

They have lived under this canopy roughly as long as the canopy has existed in its current form, which given the plateau's youth is not very long at all by the standards of old growth elsewhere — and yet they carry themselves like something ancient, unhurried, fully adapted, because adaptation here was never about time. It was about how completely you could learn to disappear into noise that never stops.

You will leave the canopy-steppe of Vael Sorrow having seen, at most, eyes at a distance, holding on you, considering, before they vanish without sound. This will not mean they were rare. It will mean you were, for exactly as long as they decided to let you be, the loudest thing in the room.
