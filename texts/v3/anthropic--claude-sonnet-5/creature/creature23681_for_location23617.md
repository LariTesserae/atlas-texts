# What Tends the Water

You will not see one at first. You will see a *seam* — one of those long black glassy veins running across the stone — and notice that it is not quite still, that it has a grain to it like the grain of muscle, and that the grain is moving, very slowly, the way a tide moves, the way a held breath moves. That is the closest you'll get to an introduction. They do not announce themselves. They are announced, if at all, by the plateau's own circuitry deciding to include you in a calculation.

Call them tenders, for lack of anything truer. There is no good word, because they are not finished into a shape a word could hold. What you eventually understand to be *one of them* is a column of vitrified glass-rock, knee-thick at the base, that has grown — or extruded, or precipitated, the verb won't sit still either — up out of one of the branching seams, and which holds, suspended in and around itself, a fist-sized volume of the same self-lit water that fills the column above. That water is not decoration. It is closer to an organ. Watch long enough and you'll see it pulse — not rhythmically, not like a heart, but in the manner of someone thinking hard, the way a hand will tighten when its owner is working out a difficult sum.

They are various sizes. Some are no taller than your knee, little glass-stalk things rooted in the lee of outcrops, indistinguishable at a glance from the grey-green crust you'd already written off as mere lichen — except that the crust, you now realize, was never plant. It was *them*, dormant, low-effort versions of the same process, content to sit at the smallest scale of participation, sipping at whatever moisture beads onto the rock and passing it, by some means you can't see, back up into the column above. Others stand as tall as the water-curtains themselves, towering black glass figures with no faces and no limbs in any arrangement you'd call a body, holding between outstretched glassy lobes — you want to call them arms, but that's your eye doing you a disservice — entire suspended lenses of water the size of a house, turning them, testing them, letting them collapse into rain and gathering up the next.

This is, as far as you can tell, the whole of their business: holding the water at the altitude and shape the rest of the process needs, then releasing it, then drawing in the next intake of vapor and beginning again. They do this with a patience that has no fatigue in it, because fatigue is a property of bodies with insides to deplete, and you increasingly suspect they don't have insides in that sense — they are closer to standing waves than to animals, kept upright and continuous by the same computation that keeps the droplet-lattices cohering into hexagons before they're allowed to fall. A tender that stops tending simply stops being a tender. You will, if you stay long enough, see one *go still* — not die, nothing so dramatic, no collapse — just slowly lose the grain-movement in its glass, go the same flat dead black as the unworked stone around it, indistinguishable in an hour from a rock that was always only a rock. Nothing mourns this. Nothing marks it. A new seam branches near it within the day, and within a week something is standing there again, doing the same work, possibly the same individual resumed, possibly not, the distinction not obviously meaningful to anything involved.

They do not seem to register you as prey, threat, or company. Twice you will be close enough to a small one to touch it — the air around it noticeably cooler, beaded with condensation like the outside of a glass — and it will not turn toward you, because it has nothing to turn with and nothing it needs from you. Once, only once, a larger one mid-lift, in the middle of raising a lens of water the size of a barn up toward its working altitude, will *pause* — the whole shimmering volume holding steady, droplets stalled mid-formation into their hexagonal scaffold — for exactly as long as you stand in its lee. Not threat. Not greeting. More like the way you'd glance up at a sound that turned out to be nothing, and then go back to what you were doing. When you step clear, the lift resumes exactly where it left off, the hexagons closing the gap your shadow had made in them.

You will be tempted, because you are the kind of creature that is tempted by such things, to ask what they're *for* — whether the plateau is a body and they are its organs, or whether they came first and built the plateau as something closer to a hive does a comb, accretion by accretion, the water-architecture merely the visible residue of an enormous, ongoing, wordless agreement among them about how high the rain should be allowed to get before it's let to fall. Nobody has answered this. Possibly nobody can, including them. Possibly the question assumes a finished thing capable of having a purpose, and the honest answer is the same one the whole plateau keeps giving you in every other register: not yet decided. Ask again once it's done.

It won't be done soon. You get the strong, settling impression, watching one of the great glass-shapes turn an entire suspended sea slowly on some axis only it can perceive, hunting for the angle at which the light will refract just so — that it has been doing this for longer than the rock under it has been cool, and that it is no closer to satisfied. This is not labor in your sense, with an end stapled to it. It is closer to breathing. You do not ask a lung when it intends to stop.
