## What tends the warm places

For a long time the honest answer would be *nothing* — that the eel-shaped thing and the blind white crustaceans were the whole of it, that Holverbrack ran the way a clock runs after the clockmaker's death, mindless and exact, world without witness. This is what the warm patches suggest at first contact: a closed loop, heat and current sustaining a small ecology and nothing more, the place keeping itself company with shrimp.

But stay near a warm patch long enough — longer than is comfortable, longer than the cold will easily permit — and the loop reveals a tender.

They are not numerous. They may, in fact, be one thing wearing the appearance of several, distributed across the warm patches the way a single worry distributes itself across a sleepless night — present everywhere a little, nowhere entirely. Call them the Stokers, for lack of a better word and because something in the old pulse-rhythm of the turbines seems to want that name.

## Shape, such as it is

What you find, when your hand or your nerve finally closes the distance, is not flesh exactly and not iron exactly but the long compromise between them — a body the approximate size and articulation of a person, grown soft and pale and faintly translucent the way anything goes that lives without light, but reinforced, *fused*, at the joints and along the spine, with old fittings: a collar of corroded brass grown into the place a collar of skin should be, knuckles that are knuckles and also bolt-heads, a ribcage that on one side is bone and on the other is the flattened curve of a boiler plate, the seam between the two no longer visible because the body stopped distinguishing between them generations ago. Where the eyes should be there is only the calcified band, the same pale wainscot-stuff that rings the brick — smoothed over, certain, the way water-line stone is certain. They do not need to see. They have the warm patches and the pulse and the pipes, and they read the building the way a hand reads a face it has touched ten thousand times in the dark.

## What they do

They tend. That is the whole of it and it is not small. They move along the warm pipes at the speed water moves through a structure too slow to call current — unhurried past any human patience, but never stopping, a slow continuous circuit from valve to gauge to joint, hands (three-fingered now, fused at the last knuckle into something more like a clamp) finding each fitting and pressing, testing, adjusting by some increment too fine to see, the way an old woman might check, without looking, that a stove is still lit.

They do not feed the fires below the waterline. The fires do not require feeding; that ended with the people who understood them. What the Stokers do is smaller and stranger: they correct. A valve that has crept open gets nudged shut. A pipe-joint sweating its warmth too fast into the black gets a hand laid flat against it, held there with a patience that has no metabolic cost worth mentioning, until something — pressure, temperature, an understanding that doesn't translate — settles back to where it was. They are not running the machine. The machine runs itself, on whatever it has been running on since before memory. They are simply making sure it keeps doing the thing it has always done, with the devotion of something that no longer remembers having a reason and has stopped requiring one.

## What they were, maybe

The brass collars are the only argument for an origin. Old fittings, foundry-stamped, the kind a foreman's apprentice might have worn against the cold in the original works — and if that is what they were, once, then the Stokers are what a hundred-some years of warm water, blind breeding, and unbroken purpose does to a person who refused, for whatever reason of love or stubbornness or simple failure to die on schedule, to stop doing their job when the water came up past the third floor and kept rising. There is no one left who would know if this is true. The Stokers themselves give no indication of remembering; ask is too strong a word for what you could do near one of them, since they have no apparent language beyond the small contented sound — felt more than heard, a sub-vocal hum pitched to match the turbine's pulse — that they make while working, and that sound stops, briefly, only if you touch them, and then resumes, exactly where it left off, as soon as you let go.

## The rule, applied to them

They will not follow you to the surface stories, will not cross out of their warmth into the true cold and dark beyond it, will not be lured, rescued, studied, or stopped — not out of malice or fear, both of which would imply they had use for you at all, but because the warm patch and the pipe and the small endless correction are the whole of what they are for, and Holverbrack, as established, does not deal in rescue. They are not guardians. They are not ghosts, quite, though they share with ghosts the trait of going on doing the last useful thing long after usefulness has left the building. They are workers who never received word that the shift had ended, tending a machine that no longer produces anything anyone could name, in a factory that drowned and decided, with the patience peculiar to machines and water both, to continue — and they are, of everything in Holverbrack, the part of that decision that still has hands.
