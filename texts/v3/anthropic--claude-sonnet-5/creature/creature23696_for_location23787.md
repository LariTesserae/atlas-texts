## The Keeper-of-Margins

At the seam where the vertical forest gives out and the white begins — that clean, repeated line the cordillera draws on every peak — something lives that belongs fully to neither side.

It is not large, not by the standards of the shelled browsers wallowing through the marsh-reed below, nor small in the way of the orchid-insects stacked in their saturated tiers. Man-height, perhaps, when it troubles to extend itself, though it rarely does; mostly it holds itself folded, compact, pressed flat against wet rock in a posture that reads, at a glance, as a stain or a shadow or an outcrop of darker stone. Its skin is the same wet-slate grey as the bark of the sideways trees, ridged in fine overlapping plates that shed water the way the old structures do — without ever quite getting wet, beading and running clean rather than soaking through. Beneath the plates, faint and slow, the same threading luminescence that veins the basalt shows through its hide in branching lines, brightening and dimming on a cycle nobody has stayed long enough to measure, in step — or near enough to step — with the glow of the platforms it nests beside.

It does nest beside them. This is the thing known of it, the one reliable fact repeated by anyone who has spent seasons rather than days in the cordillera: where a standing post hums faintly at its avoided frequency, where the other fauna give their wide and instinctive berth, the keeper does not avoid. It climbs to the post and settles against its base or its shelf-ledge and stays, sometimes for years, head inclined toward the hum at an angle that looks, uncomfortably, like listening. It does not appear to feed there in any way that disturbs the post or draws visibly from it. It does not appear to need to feed often at all — its metabolism runs to the same patient calendar as everything else worth noting in this place, a slowness that looks, from the outside, like stillness, and is in fact a kind of extreme, extended attention.

Its limbs are built for the vertical world below it and the thin cold world above: long, double-jointed, ending in broad pads that hold wet stone the way the tree-roots hold cracked basalt, by surface and patience rather than grip. When it does move, it moves the way water moves through the rock-grooves — along channels already worn smooth by however many crossings have come before, never blazing new ground, always finding the route already there. It descends through the canopy-shelves at dusk sometimes, unhurried, passing through the layered green without disturbing the layering, and it is widely suspected — though never confirmed by anyone patient enough to confirm it — that the low resonant calling that rises from the black marsh-water at dusk does not come from the water at all, but from the keeper, descended to the valley floor to drink, its true voice pitched low enough to seem to come from underneath things.

Its eyes, when it troubles to open them fully, are pale and large and ringed with the same faint lattice-glow as the platforms, and it is this — more than the shape of it, more than the plated hide or the patient unmoving years — that has given rise to the only story the valley people tell about it with any consistency: that the keeper is not separate from the old structures at all, but a thing the mountain grew once it understood what the platforms and the bridge and the humming posts were for, a living continuation of whatever intention built them, sent out to keep them company through the long, slow, unhurried work of not quite finishing.

Nothing has ever been found to confirm this. Nothing has been found to deny it either. The keeper simply persists, plate-skinned and glow-veined, folded against its chosen post through years that pass over it the way mist passes around the bridge — turned aside, somehow, without ever quite being told to go.
