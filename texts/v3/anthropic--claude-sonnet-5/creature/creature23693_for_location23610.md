You will meet one of them only once, and you will meet them many times, and you will not be able to say with confidence whether these are the same fact or two.

They are not large. This is the first surprise—after the herds, after the heights, after the unblinking horned multitudes packed shoulder to shoulder on ridgelines like a held photograph, you expect whatever moves through this place to move with the same scale of presence. Instead, what you find, usually at dusk, usually at the edge of a stone circle where the lichen spirals too precisely to be chance, is something roughly your own height, robed in a substance that might be cloth and might be a second skin of fine gray dust that never lifts no matter how the wind doesn't touch it. Their face, when they turn it to you—and they will turn it to you slowly, with no urgency, the way a held thing finally settles into a new position after a very long time of not needing to—is not unkind. It is simply old beyond the register where kindness and unkindness remain separate categories. The eyes are the same open, unblinking eyes you found in the herds, except here they sometimes, very rarely, close. You will feel that closing as a kind of violence, an interruption, though nothing about it is fast or violent at all.

They walk where the animals will not enter. This is not transgression; it is the single exception written into the place's grammar, the one variable allowed to move through a system that has otherwise voted, unanimously, for stillness. They walk the circles, and the lichen does not shy from their footfall, and behind them, if you look quickly enough—you should not look quickly enough—the spiral pattern seems to have advanced by one further turn, as if their passage is itself the act of growth, slow as the rest of this geology, but not yet finished. Not quite. This is the only place left in the entire breadth of these mountains where something is still, technically, in process. It is a small mercy and you will not be sure it's a mercy at all.

They are what's left of the people who raised the walls that stop mid-course and the cairns oriented toward stars that have since wandered off their charts. Not descendants. Not survivors in the ordinary sense, the sense that implies escape, flight, a generation that outran whatever caught the rest. They stayed, is the closest the silence will tell you, when you stand long enough inside one of the circles and let the low frequency travel up through your shins from some herd a mile off—they stayed, and what happened to them was not death and was not transformation in the way a chrysalis transforms, emerging changed but singular. What happened to them was closer to what happened to the mountains: they finished. They reached the end of their own erosion, the version of themselves that doesn't change further, and rather than being erased by that finality they were preserved inside it, the way the dead are preserved by deserts, the way silence is preserved by having nothing left to interrupt it. They did not die into absence. They died into permanence, and permanence, it turns out, can still get up and walk, slowly, between the held bodies of the herds, laying one hand—five fingers, ordinary, almost unbearably ordinary—against a flank of heavy gray hide, as if checking a pulse, or steadying one.

This is, you begin to suspect, their work. If anything here can be called work. They move among the suspended herds and the placed, unfalling birds and they touch them, infrequently, unhurriedly, the way a sleeper might be touched on the shoulder not to wake them but to confirm they are still merely sleeping. You do not know what would happen if they stopped making these rounds. You suspect nobody knows, including them, including whatever decision-making apparatus survived their long settlement into this shape. You suspect the not-knowing is itself part of the maintenance, a held question doing the same work as a held breath.

They do not speak, but you will sometimes hear, very faint, underneath the low felt-frequency of the herds, something that might be their voice doing the same thing at a different register—a hum with no words in it, or words in a language so worn down by repetition that it has eroded past meaning into pure shape, the way the mountains eroded past jaggedness into these rounded, ancient shoulders. If you listen to that hum long enough you will start to recognize in it the same pattern as the lichen's spiral, the same logic as the herds' formation, the same impossible folding of distance that lets the next ridge be a day away and also close enough to touch. It is possible the hum is not communication at all. It is possible it is simply what a held note sounds like from the inside, after enough centuries, once it has stopped trying to resolve into anything else.

You will not be afraid of them, which will itself frighten you more than fear would have. Whatever they are, they have settled too completely into this place's logic to register as a threat in the categories your body still, stubbornly, insists on using. They will look at you the way the open-eyed herd looks at you—present, attentive, entirely unbothered by the eventual fact of your departure—and then they will turn, unhurried, and continue along the spiral, and the lichen will close gently behind their feet the way stone closes over the wrong-legged things that vanish at the edges of your vision, and you will understand, finally, that you have not met an inhabitant of this landscape so much as you have met one of its verbs—the one operation still permitted to occur, the single thread of process left running quietly beneath an entire world that voted, long ago, to stop.
