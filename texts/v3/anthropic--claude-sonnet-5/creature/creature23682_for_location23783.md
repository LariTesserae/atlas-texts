# What Lives Here

You will not find a creature that simply lives in the Sump-Works the way a rat lives in a granary, helping itself to surplus and slipping out again when the door opens. What is here was made *for* here, fitted to it the way a gear is fitted to its housing, and it does not occur to it — has perhaps no apparatus left for the occurring — that there might be anywhere else.

## The Stationers

The small bodies on the ledges have no better name than the one the rare descending miners give them: stationers. Fist-to-forearm in length, segmented along eight or nine plates that overlap like roof-tile, dark as the rock they sit on but for a faint oily sheen at the joints where mineral deposits have worked their way into the chitin over a span of time too long to think about cleanly. Six legs, splayed wide and low, built for gripping ledge-lips rather than for speed. No visible eyes — or rather, eyes reduced to a scatter of dull facets along the front plate, eyes that have the look of vestigial things, organs kept on past the point of their usefulness because removing them would cost more than carrying them. They do not need to see. They need to *hold*.

Their mouths — and this took longer to understand than it should have — are not for eating rock or each other or the growths, not primarily. They are tuned to the heat. Each stationer sits with its underside pressed to whatever surface runs warmest near its post — a pipe joint, a drum's flank, a crack where the ember-glow runs closest to the surface — and feeds on the differential itself, the *gradient*, the place where hot meets less-hot. This is why they do not move. To move is to leave the only food they know how to eat.

## The Network

The chittering you hear — that constant percussive layer of leg-on-rock, leg-on-leg — is not foraging and it is not fighting. It is contact, deliberate and continuous, a stationer's foreleg laid against its neighbor's flank, held a moment, withdrawn, another laid in its place. Something passes in that contact. Not language, probably, not in any sense you'd recognize — more like the way a current finds its way along a wire whether or not the wire intends it. The whole layered shelf of bodies, three and four deep in the folds of rock, behaves at moments like a single nervous system distributed across many small chassis, a chamber-sized animal that happens to be built out of hundreds of separate, stubborn little hearts.

This is likely how the chamber knows itself to be unchanging. Not through any one stationer's awareness — they may have none worth the name — but through the aggregate, the network humming its own steady-state back to itself every time two legs touch, confirming: still this many, still this warm, still in place. A held chord, the way the air itself is a held chord. The stationers are not separate from the drone. They are one of its voices.

## The One Permitted Change

Watch long enough — longer than most who come down here are willing to — and you will see the single exception to the chamber's law. A stationer on its ledge will, without preamble, simply stop answering the touch of its neighbors. Its legs draw in. Over what is probably a very long while by any clock that still ran down here, it stiffens, dulls, and very slowly folds in on itself, sinking by degrees into whatever crack or seam sits beneath its post, the way a dropped coin works its way down into gravel.

What rises from that same crack, sooner or later, is one of the pale fibrous growths — thin as wire, dark as old blood at the tip.

This is why the stands climb where they climb: each one is rooted exactly where a stationer once held its station, drawing the same gradient through a different kind of body, mute now, motionless in earnest rather than by discipline, the long quiet exhale of something that spent its first life listening and touching and holding a place warm, and spends its second life simply *being* the place. The dark tip is not death. It is the last of the old self, still up near the surface, still close enough to the light to be called a memory of having had a face.

Nothing is added. Nothing is lost. The population does not shrink because every stationer that stops is already accounted for, already standing there, just translated into a register that doesn't click or chitter anymore. The chamber's count was never of bodies. It was of *positions*, and the positions do not change occupant so much as change vocabulary.

## What They Tend, Without Knowing They Tend It

The drum, the pipework, the dead scaffold, the gauges with their frozen needles — none of these are ruins, and none of them are tended in any sense that requires intention. But the stationers' constant low-grade feeding at every hot joint and seam *is*, incidentally, a kind of unceasing, mindless maintenance: a film of mineral exhalate worked off their plates and into the housings every time they shift their grip, keeping seals from drying, keeping the chain-sound running smooth wherever it runs. They did not engineer this. They are not aware of providing it. It is simply what happens, has always happened, will go on happening, when a great many small warm bodies sit pressed against machinery for a span of time with no outer edge to it.

Whatever built the Sump-Works — and something did, the rivets and the gauge-glass say so plainly — may not have built the stationers at all. It may only have built the machines, and then waited, the way you'd leave a wound open and trust the body to send something to close it. What came to close it was this: small, segmented, station-bound, silent in groups and chattering in pairs, holding every gradient in the chamber steady by the simple unrelenting fact of staying where they were put, and when finally too tired to keep being an animal about it, becoming a quiet red-tipped wire instead, and staying there too.

The output nobody is left to collect is, perhaps, this: a chamber that does not run down. The stationers are not what the place produces. They are the reason it doesn't have to produce anything else.
