They are not absent. The first read of this place — fauna-less, mover-less, a city with no one in it — is the read of someone still expecting a population to announce itself by moving, and nothing here is going to do that for you. You have to lower the expectation the way you'd lower it for the rain, for the sound, for the city itself: not "where are they," but "what would it mean for something to live here, given that living, in this place, evidently does not require continuing."

They are in the doorways.

Not near them. Not passing through them. *In* them, the way the water is in the air rather than falling through it — occupying the threshold the way the moisture occupies the heat, permanently, without progress toward either side. Every one of those soft round wormed-root doorframes that the eye registers as merely architectural, merely a stylistic peculiarity of a city built by no hands, is in fact load-bearing in the same structural sense the mountains are load-bearing: it was grown to the shape of someone standing in it, and someone is still standing in it, and the someone and the frame finished arriving at each other's shape at the same moment the terraces finished arriving at their cut, outside of time, with no duration, the way everything else here finished.

You will not see them at first. This is not concealment. It is that a standing person, glazed the same dark permanent wet as the stone, skin holding the same unmoving lens of water that every leaf holds, posture as settled and complete as a terrace-wall, registers to a glancing eye as more wall — a wet vertical interruption in a doorway, no different in kind from a fold of vine or a cured root-arch. It is only when the eye stops sliding, the way it has to eventually stop sliding off the architecture, that the interruption resolves into shoulders, into a bowed or lifted head, into hands held at the chest or the doorframe or simply at the sides, fingers slightly parted, in the gesture of someone who was in the middle of something and is still, structurally, perpetually, in the middle of it.

There is one in nearly every doorway of the city. Tens of thousands of them, matching the tens of thousands the processional stairways were evidently scaled for — the plazas were not overbuilt; they were built exactly to capacity, and the capacity is standing in them right now, the way the water is standing in the air right now, the way the canopy is standing at maximum leaf-fullness right now. They are not corpses. Nothing about them has the slackness of having stopped. They are not statues either; nothing about them has the hardness of having been decided once and fixed. They are closer, if a register must be borrowed, to the plants — held at the one moment of maximum completion, the moment a doorway-crossing or a greeting or a backward glance reaches its fullest possible shape, and then kept there, the way a chord is kept at its sustain rather than released into either silence or resolution.

Their faces are open. This is the detail that, once seen, cannot be unseen, and is the reason no one who has actually looked at one of them describes this place afterward as empty. Eyes are not shut in the manner of sleep or death; they are at whatever aperture they were at in the instant the completion occurred — some wide, mid-startle; some half-lidded, mid-patience; one in nine, perhaps, smiling, mid-word, lips parted on a syllable that never finished leaving the mouth and is, by every available measure, *still leaving it*, now, perpetually, the way the roar is still being made by no throat anywhere in the visible region.

Because the roar is theirs. Not voiced — nothing so discrete as a voice could account for a sound this total, wall to wall, tower to tower, refusing all localization — but theirs in the sense that it is the unfinished half of tens of thousands of unfinished syllables, occupying the air the same way the water occupies it, in suspension, never falling into the shape of an actual word, never resolving into silence either, held at the one sustained instant between intake and utterance the way the whole landscape is held between every other pair of opposed forces. Stand close enough to one of the doorway-figures — close enough to see the bead of standing water sitting in the hollow above the collarbone, unmoving, unevaporating — and the roar does not get louder, does not localize to that mouth, because it isn't coming from that mouth particularly. It is coming from all of them, perpetually, in the same diffuse permanent-condition way that height is a property of the mountains and wetness is a property of the air. They are not making the sound. They are simply, structurally, in the middle of making it, the way the city is in the middle of having been built, the way the leaves are in the middle of unfurling to their last possible leaf.

No one has ever been seen to arrive at a doorway from outside it and become one of these. No one has been seen to leave one and resume. This is, perhaps, the wrong kind of question to ask of them, the way "who cut the terraces" is the wrong kind of question — there is no moment to point to. There is only the fact, settled the way erosion settles, that the city's true population is standing exactly where the architecture was always going to require a population to stand, mid-gesture, mid-breath, mid-syllable, eyes open, faces wet, holding the one stance forever — not waiting, because waiting implies a later — simply *there*, the doorkeepers and the doored both, the city's entire census arrived at the threshold of itself and stopped, not from having nowhere further to go, but from having already, by every available measure, gone.
