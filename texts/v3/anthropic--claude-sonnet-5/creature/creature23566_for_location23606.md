## The Tenant

You will not see it. This needs to be said first, plainly, before anything else, because every other true thing about it depends on that absence. You will feel it — in the displacement of warm air against your face when you are sure nothing should be moving in that direction, in the way the standing water in a basin tilts and resettles for no reason you can locate, in the half-second the dripping seems to pause, as though something vast had just exhaled and the cave held its breath in sympathy. Then it will be gone, or perhaps it never moved at all, and you will not be able to say which.

It is old. Not old in the way the stone wants to be old — the stone is still arguing with itself about its own shape — but old relative to everything else that breathes here, old enough to remember the catwalk when its lacework of rust was still good iron, old enough to have been small once, drawn down through some long-collapsed throat as a larva no bigger than your hand, following warmth the way the roots once followed light and then gave it up. It found the pumps before they were ruins. It has outlasted whoever built them by a span of time it has no way of measuring and no apparent interest in measuring. Geology is its calendar.

Its body, such as anyone can describe it, is not built for shape so much as for spreading load. Soft, the way everything down here is soft from drowning slowly in its own moisture, but soft like wet leather rather than wet flesh — skin thickened and folded into shelves and ridges that match, almost exactly, the contours of the chamber walls it rests against, so that for long stretches it is simply more wall, more silt-crusted declivity, until the fungus colonizing its hide and the fungus colonizing the stone become a single indistinguishable bloom. It does not so much move through the throats between chambers as pour through them, redistributing itself, a great slow peristalsis of meat finding the path water already carved. Limbs, if you can call them that, exist mostly to anchor and to feel; sight was abandoned so long ago there's no vestige left to even call blind.

What it has instead, grown patient and enormous over whatever centuries it's had, is a hide tuned entire to vibration — every fold of skin a kind of drumhead, layered like the stone's own pale veins, each tuned to a slightly different register, so that it does not hear the gallery's noise so much as wear it. The arrhythmic drip. The skitter of carapace. The wet shifting of its own crowded tenants. And under all of it, the pistons — that steady mechanical pulse it has felt against its skin for longer than anything else here has been alive, a rhythm it has never needed to understand to depend on. Its own internal cycles — torpor and waking, the slow seasonal swell that precedes the laying — have long since entrained to that engine-thud, the way a body left long enough beside any heartbeat starts, helplessly, to share it. It does not know it tends the machinery. It has simply grown so used to lying against the warm housings, drawn by a heat it associates with safety, that its bulk presses certain valves home, completes certain circuits the original engineers wired for hands and feet, not flesh — and the system, fed this incidental maintenance for generations, has gone on running long after the people who needed it stopped coming back. Symbiosis by accident. Stewardship with no steward in it.

The small things — the blind crustacean swarms, the soft pale mats clustering on the ceiling, the eggs glistening in their warm clutches against the rock — are not its prey, mostly, though enough of them end up consumed that the distinction barely matters to anyone but them. They are closer to its garden, or its skin flora, or its children, depending on which clutch you're looking at; some of those egg-masses are its own, laid in the warmest crevices near the pump housings and left to develop on geological time, unhurried, indifferent to whether any particular clutch survives because there are always more clutches. The swarm lives on it, around it, sometimes — when it has gone fully still against a wall for a season — directly across its hide, mistaking the great slow rise and fall of its breathing for the rise and fall of water finding new channels.

It is not malevolent. It is not, particularly, anything you'd recognize as aware of you at all, except as one more displacement of warm air, one more set of vibrations moving through a medium already saturated with vibration, easily lost in the noise. It does not hunt so much as wait for the gallery to bring things to it, the way the gallery brings everything to everything eventually, given enough centuries and enough crowding.

If the gallery has a rule — *nothing finished, nothing empty* — this is its fullest expression: a creature that is wall and animal and unwitting machine-tender all at once, still growing, still spreading its slow bulk into whatever new cavity the patient water opens for it, indistinguishable from its own becoming. It will likely die the way the stone will eventually finish forming — which is to say, not within any span of time that means anything to you, and not in any way you will be present to notice.
