What lives here first — what shaped the basin into something nameable — is almost entirely absent from it. You won't see the builders. You'll see only what they leave behind, which is everything: every wall, every slab, every stacked angular dwelling. That absence is its own kind of evidence. Whatever made this place doesn't work in daylight, or doesn't work when watched, or simply isn't here anymore this morning, having finished its shift before the basin's grey light came up.

Picture them this way, assembled backward from the stone: bodies built for heat and slowness, low to the ground, moving in the unhurried gait of something that has never needed to flee. Not large. Not small either — big enough to lean a shoulder into a half-formed wall and feel it take their weight. Skin or hide the same dull dark as the basalt they make, because they *are* what makes it — glands along the flank or jaw that carry mineral the way a wasp carries pulped wood, secreting it half-molten, packing it with some blunt, fast, tool-less gesture, then moving on before it's even fully set. That's why the edges are sharp. That's why nothing has weathered. The architecture is still warm, in a sense, from the bodies that laid it down — not literal heat, but the freshness of something made in a single unbroken motion and then abandoned mid-thought, the maker already three structures away, attention already elsewhere.

They don't plan in any way you'd recognize as a plan. There's no street grid, no central hall, no hierarchy of buildings — just an instinct toward *enough space between things*, the same instinct that makes a wasp leave a gap between cells. They build because building is what their bodies do when the air holds the right damp warmth, the way a spider spins because its glands are full and the night is right for it. When the air changes — drier, cooler, whatever signal tells them the work is done for now — they withdraw. Into the deeper basalt, maybe. Into folds and hollows under the rim that you haven't found yet. They are sleeping there now, or doing whatever they do in the gaps between bouts of making, and the basin holds its breath waiting for them to come back out and keep going.

But the basin isn't *empty* in their absence. It only looks that way from the rim. Walk down into it and the second population reveals itself almost immediately — smaller, faster, entirely uninvited, and utterly unconcerned with whose town this technically is.

In the fern-stands near the well-house: quick many-legged things, low-slung, the size of a cupped hand, the color of wet shadow. They don't burrow so much as *thread* — moving through the gaps between fronds with a fluency that suggests they've already mapped every crevice in their stretch of the basin, every place water gathers a half-inch deep, every leaf that drips reliably at dusk. They drink without ceremony, taste the air with whatever passes for a face, and vanish back into green before you've decided what you were looking at. They've been here exactly as long as the ferns have, which is to say: not long, and entirely.

Along the rim — that low, indecisive wall between basin and whatever lies beyond it — something moves with more weight and far less hurry. You hear it before you see it: a slow scrape of bulk against stone, unbothered, proprietary, the gait of a creature checking a boundary it considers already settled rather than patrolling one it fears losing. It's larger than anything else here, low and long, built for grazing the moss-and-lichen margins where the basin's dampness fades into drier outer rock. It doesn't enter the settlement itself. It doesn't need to. The rim is its country, the basin's edge its grazing line, and the half-built town below is simply scenery it has learned to ignore — useful only in that the walls hold warmth a little longer into the evening, heat it can feel radiating up toward the rim like a hearth it never has to tend.

And above it all — threading the unfinished rooftops, nesting in eaves that have no weathering yet to make them less than ideal — the bird-things. Not many. Not loud. They've found, with whatever blunt sense passes for their intelligence, that warmth pools here the way water pools in the rock basins, and that a town half-finished is still shelter, still a windbreak, still somewhere to raise a clutch before the builders return and decide they need that particular ledge after all. They thread between structures in loose, low arcs, settle on the angular ridgelines, occasionally drop to drink from one of the shallow stone mirrors before lifting off again without a sound that carries more than a few feet. They are, in a sense, the most confident residents here — having recognized shelter before its makers had even finished deciding what shape it would take, and simply moved in.

So the basin holds three kinds of life stacked one atop the other, each indifferent to the others' schedules. The makers, withdrawn and patient, working in bursts you'll never catch. The small green-dwelling opportunists, fast and incidental, treating every fern and crack as already theirs. And the larger, slower watchers — rim-grazer below, roof-nesters above — who have claimed the warmth without claiming the work.

None of them are waiting for the town to be finished. That idea belongs only to you, the visitor, measuring the place by what it isn't yet. To everything that actually lives here, it is already, completely, what it needs to be — a held breath is still air, after all, and air is enough to live on.
