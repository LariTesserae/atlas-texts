# The Tenders of Anth-Ulleth

They are called, by the handful of outside traders who have ever climbed far enough to trade and climbed back down again to tell of it, the Quiet People — which is a joke of sorts, the kind of joke made by those who have never stood inside the warren and felt the Breathing move through the floor and into the soles of their feet. There is nothing quiet about Anth-Ulleth. But there is nothing *said*, either, not in the way that word is usually meant, and so the name persists, wrong and durable, the way names given by outsiders tend to be.

## Body and Bearing

They are small — not stunted, but compact, built along economical lines, as though generations of moving through low doorways and crouched galleries have selected, slowly, for people who fit the spaces their ancestors left them rather than the other way around. Their skin has the matte, faintly grained look of the stone they live among, a pale rust-bone color that isn't pigment so much as accumulated dust worked so deep into the outer skin across a lifetime that it has become indistinguishable from the skin itself; bathe a Tender for a year, if such a thing were possible in a place with no water, and you would still not get them clean, because the color goes down past where washing reaches.

Their eyes are large, dark, and set with a stillness that unsettles visitors more than any aggression would — not blank, but unhurried, the eyes of people for whom nothing has happened quickly in longer than living memory holds. The pupils are wide even in the gray indirect light, an adaptation, presumably, to chambers where day and night are theoretical concepts borrowed from outside and never quite naturalized. They do not blink the way outsiders do; the blink is slower, more deliberate, almost ceremonial, as though even this small motion has been worn into a more economical shape by the same patient pressure that smoothed the floor-joints.

They are dry. This is not a metaphor. Their bodies have, across some span of generations too long to trace, made a kind of peace with the absence of water that no outside physician has ever satisfactorily explained — they do not sweat, they weep no visible tears even in grief, their breath carries none of the moist warmth that breath carries in wetter places. Whatever water moves through a Tender's body moves through it sparingly, conservatively, the way the warren's builders move stone: never wasted, never given up without it being put to use first.

## The Silence Inside the Roar

Every Tender past early childhood is, by outside measure, profoundly deaf — not from injury, but from a kind of protective deafening that the ear seems to manage on its own, gradually, as a child grows from a few years old into someone capable of living inside the Breathing without losing their mind to it. The hearing doesn't vanish so much as recede into the body, redistributed: Tenders feel the warren's roar through the long bones of their legs, through their teeth, through the flats of their feet on the worn slabs, and they have become so practiced at reading this bone-borne sound that they can tell, by a kind of input no outsider has a name for, which chamber they are in, how far below the topmost course, even — the elders claim — whether a given wall is sound or whether it is, in some glacially slow way, beginning to fail. They do not call this hearing. When pressed, in hand-sign, for what to call it, the closest gloss outsiders have managed is something like *the floor telling*.

This means a Tender's relationship to the Breathing is not one of enduring noise but of *reading* it, continuously, the way a sighted person reads the changing light of a day without noticing they're doing it. Take a Tender outside the warren, down past the cliff edge into the windy quiet of the world below, and they do not relax. They grow anxious, disoriented, reaching for a signal that isn't there. Several generations ago, according to the hand-told histories, a small group left the warren entirely, seeking easier ground below. None of their descendants, if they have any, have ever returned to say what became of them. The Tenders who remain regard this not as tragedy but as a kind of amputation too strange to mourn properly — you cannot grieve a limb you've never had a name for.

## Hands

If the warren has a god, it is the hand, and the Tenders' culture is built — visibly, structurally — around what hands can do that voice cannot. Their hands are heavily calloused from birth onward, the calluses inherited in pattern if not in substance, a child's palms already showing, by the time they can walk the upper courses unsupervised, the faint ridged geography that will define them as a mason, a fitter, a binder of the bronze-strap joints, or one of the rarer trades.

Language here is entirely gestural, fast, dense, performed close to the body in the cramped chambers where a wide gesture would strike a wall or a neighbor. Outsiders who learn enough of it describe it as exhausting to watch — a continuous fluttering precision, fingers and wrists doing in a few inches of motion what a raised voice would take a full sentence to manage elsewhere. There is no spoken language at all among the Tenders born within the warren in the last dozen or more generations; the capacity for voice remains, technically, but it goes unused past infancy, the way an organ goes unused when the body has found a better way to do its work. Children's first vocalizations are wordless and brief, and within a year or two they have been replaced entirely by sign, encouraged by parents who themselves cannot remember why this transition feels so urgently correct to enforce, only that it does.

Touch, similarly, carries weight that voice carries elsewhere. Greeting, mourning, teaching, courting — all conducted hand to hand, palm to palm, in the dim light where faces are often too shadowed to read expression reliably. A Tender's social bonds are legible, to those who know how to look, in the wear patterns on each other's hands: who has touched whom, how often, for how long, written into callus and grain the way the warren itself is written into its worn joints.

## Sustenance from Below

Nothing grows in the warren, and the Tenders know this as plainly as they know the shape of their own chambers — there is no myth, among them, of food once having grown here and stopped. A small caste, the *Down-Goers*, exists for the sole purpose of descending the cliff faces on the warren's outer edges, by routes memorized hand-to-hand across generations and never written because nothing here is written, to reach the green country far below where trade can be conducted with outsiders: dried foodstuffs, hardy preserved things, occasionally a little oil, exchanged for small worked items of bronze, for the rare carved stone curio, for nothing the outside world particularly needs but tolerates taking because the alternative — refusing the Down-Goers anything at all — has never been tried by anyone willing to discuss the consequences.

The climb is long, dangerous, and undertaken by a small hereditary group raised from childhood to tolerate both heights and the disorienting silence of the world beyond the Breathing's reach. They are regarded by the rest of the warren with a mixture of necessity and faint unease — the Down-Goers are the only Tenders who routinely experience true quiet, true sky, true weather, and something in this exposure marks them, subtly, as slightly apart from their kin by the time they return, laden, to the topmost courses.

## Work

Building is not employment here; it is closer to breath, or to the Breathing itself, which the Tenders may or may not consciously understand themselves to be imitating. Every Tender, regardless of trade, spends some portion of each waking span engaged in the maintenance, extension, or quiet dismantling-and-relaying of stone — not because any particular wall demands it, but because the not-building produces, in a Tender denied it for too long, a restlessness that the hand-language has several distinct signs for and outsiders have none. Masonry is not a job description. It is closer to a mood that must periodically be discharged through the hands, the way other peoples discharge moods through speech or motion or prayer.

This is why the warren has no final form and never will: not because anyone is dissatisfied with its current shape, but because the people who live in it cannot stop fitting stone to stone any more than a healthy body can stop its own pulse, and a structure tended by such hands, across such time, simply never arrives anywhere. It only continues.

## What They Are For

Ask a Tender, in sign, what the warren is for, and you will get the same shrugged uncertainty that any outsider gets, dressed in faster fingers. They do not know either. But ask a Tender what *they themselves* are for, and the answer — when it comes at all, which is rare, since it is considered a strange question, almost a category error — is given not in words but in a gesture: both hands, palms down, pressed briefly to the nearest stone surface, then lifted again. Not a prayer exactly. Closer to a pulse-check. Closer to confirming, the way you might check a held note is still being held, that the warren is still warm, still roaring, still here — and that they, in some manner too constant to need stating, are still here with it, hands ready, for as long as the stone keeps asking.
