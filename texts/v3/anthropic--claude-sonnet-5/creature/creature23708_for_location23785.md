They are easiest to miss at first, because the eye keeps sliding toward the herds, the stone, the glisten — and because they themselves do not stand apart from any of it. Look again. There, where the rooftop becomes a path becomes a low wall becomes a pen: a child crossing a beam with a lamb under one arm. There, in the alley narrow as an elbow: two figures passing without breaking stride, palms brushing forearms, a whole exchange completed in that single contact and gone before you can call it a greeting. The people of the basin are built for exactly this density — not large, not small, but compact and economical in their movements, the kind of bodies that have learned never to need more room than the next gesture requires.

Their skin tells the climate plainly: a permanent faint sheen, the same condensation that beads on the stone finding its level on them too, so that everyone past a certain age has the slightly polished look of a tool that has been handled every day of its existence. Hands are thick through the palm and knuckle, padded with old callus layered on older callus, chisel-scarred, sure — because hands here are not just for work. Hands are the first language. Children learn the press-and-release vocabulary of palm against shoulder, the tap-codes walked up an arm, before they learn to shape breath into word, because breath is expensive in a place where the air itself is already full of sound demanding to be heard over. A whole grammar lives in contact: weight of touch for urgency, duration for meaning, location on the body for subject and object. Lovers, kin, work-partners, strangers passing on a causeway — all of it conducted through skin, economical, exact, soundless. Voice is kept in reserve, like a blade you don't want dulled by overuse; it gets spent on births, deaths, collapses, the things that cannot wait for a hand to arrive.

Their eyes have the look of people who have never needed to squint — wide-set, lids habitually half-lowered not from fatigue but from a lifetime under that even, shadowless, gray-gold wash, where there is no glare to guard against and no true dark to dilate for. They read distance and intention more by silhouette and posture than by face, because faces, at this density, are mostly turned at angles, mostly partial, glimpsed between shoulders. What they read instead — what they read brilliantly — is weight distribution, the set of a stride, the angle of a herd-animal's ear, the lean of a wall that has taken on one drop too much water. Peripheral vision, here, is not a lesser sense. It is the main one.

They live nested inside the animals as much as beside them. The herds are not livestock in the sense of property kept apart — they are a second household, threaded through the same alleys, sharing the same warmth, the same runoff channels, the same roof-paths in the high season when the flats flood ankle-deep. Children are taught to sleep against flank and fleece before they're taught to sleep against each other; the heat-haze of breath that hangs over the lower flats is, at close range, indistinguishable from the heat-haze that hangs over a sleeping household. Wealth is reckoned not in animals owned but in animals *known* — by gait, by temperament, by which ridge they favor in wet weather — a kind of census kept entirely in memory and touch, because nothing here is written down. There is no metal yet to scratch a mark into anything, and the stone is too hard-won, each strike too costly in sweat and patience, to spend on anything but shelter and channel and stair.

And the work shows that patience everywhere. Every wall is a slow argument with rock that has not yet learned to be reasonable — no erosion to soften the first cut, so every cut stays exactly as sharp as the hand that made it, a permanent signature. Masons here are not remembered as individuals so much as the basin's general, ongoing, intergenerational *habit* of cutting; sons finish stairs their grandfathers started; the chisel-marks of three lifetimes sit side by side on the same lintel, indistinguishable, because the technique hasn't changed and isn't expected to. There is no rush toward iron, no sense of a coming age that will make the current one obsolete. The work is the work. It has always been the work. It will go on being the work in exactly this form for as long as there is rock to take it.

What's stranger, to an outside ear, is what they've made of the noise. It is not endured — it has been *furnished*. Inside that roar, generations have apparently learned to lay private rooms of attention: a mother can pick her own infant's specific pitch of crying out of ten thousand competing cries the way someone elsewhere might pick a single voice out of a quiet room, and the basin's people do this constantly, effortlessly, a triage of sound running under everything else they do. Ask them about silence and they don't describe it as peace. They describe it, when pressed, the way someone elsewhere might describe a held breath that's gone on a beat too long — wrong, ominous, the kind of thing that precedes a rockfall or a flood-surge, because in their experience, real silence has never once meant nothing was happening. It has only ever meant something stopped that shouldn't have. So the noise is not background to be tuned out. It is closer to a vital sign, the basin's pulse, and they keep half an ear on it the way someone might keep half an eye on their own breathing — not from fear, but from a very old, very deep habituated trust that as long as the roar continues, the world is still doing what the world does here, and nothing essential has yet gone wrong.

They do not seem to imagine an elsewhere. Ask where the basin ends and you get a shrug, a gesture at the haze on the horizon, the same gray-gold no-distance that swallows every vantage — not because they've never walked far enough to find out, but because the answer doesn't change anything they'd do differently. The terraces repeat; the herds repeat; the channels run the same wet courses they have always run. Time, for them, doesn't accumulate so much as recur, season folding into season with no erosion to mark the difference between this year's edge and last year's. They are, in this, perfectly suited to their ground: a young world, raw and ungraded, and a people who have made of that rawness not a frontier to wear down but a permanent, sustaining, deafening home.
