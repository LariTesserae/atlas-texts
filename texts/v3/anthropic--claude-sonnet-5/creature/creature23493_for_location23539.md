**The Listening Herd**

The many-jointed ones are called, by no one, the *tally-walkers*, because that is what their movement suggests—a constant counting-off, leg after leg after leg in numbers that exceed any reasonable skeleton, each limb terminating not in a foot but in a flattened pad the gray-violet color of the rock itself, so that when they stand still, which is often, they disappear into the stone they're standing on and you only know they're there because the dripping stops sounding lonely.

They are the size of cattle, more or less, though "size" undersells them, because they don't have a fixed outline so much as a *suggestion* of one—the joints articulate in directions a skeleton shouldn't permit, folding the body smaller in moments of stillness and unfolding it taller when they gather at the water-seams, as if they are perpetually adjusting their volume to the available space, the way a held breath adjusts to the size of the lungs holding it. Their hide, if it is hide, is the same wet sheen as the basin floor, faintly granular, faintly warm to the touch despite the ambient cold, and along the ridge of each back runs a line of small apertures—not eyes, not gills, something else, something that opens and closes in a rhythm that has nothing to do with breathing and everything, it turns out, to do with the thunder.

This is the thing about the tally-walkers that no one who has stood in the basin for more than an hour can fail to notice and fail, also, to find unbearable: they *listen* to it. When the deep mechanical concussion rolls up through the rock, the apertures along their backs flare open in perfect synchrony, all across the herd, hundreds of them at once, drinking in the sound the way the rubbery colonies in the wet creases drink in whatever it is they drink. Between concussions the apertures stay shut, sealed, and the animals seem almost asleep on their feet, swaying very slightly, patient, waiting—not for food, not for danger, but for the next note.

They do not graze. They do not burrow. They do not mate in any way that has been observed, though calves appear among them with no visible gestation, simply present one twilight where they were absent the twilight before, already mid-stride, already counting. They gather at the seams where groundwater surfaces and they stand there for hours pressing their underbellies to the wet stone, and then they move on to another seam, and another, circulating through the basin in loose, leaderless processions that have the character less of migration than of *tending*—as though the herd is not seeking water so much as checking on it, the way you'd check on something fevered.

When the long pale swimmers surface near them in their braided schools, the tally-walkers pay no attention at all, even when a hunting shape passes within a body-length, even when the four-second frenzies erupt in the black water an arm's reach from where they stand drinking. They do not startle. They do not flee. It is as though the part of them that would recognize danger has been repurposed entirely for listening, and there was only room for one of those faculties, and listening won.

Twice an hour, when the thunder and the dripping lock into their single impossible chord, the tally-walkers are the ones you should watch, if you want to understand anything at all about this place. In that one frozen second, every aperture along every back stands open at once, fully open, wider than at any other time—and something passes through the herd that isn't visible and isn't audible but is somehow *felt*, a kind of collective exhale, as if the herd has just been told something, or fed something, or relieved of something—and then the chord breaks and the apertures snap shut and the animals resume their unhurried processions to the next seam, the next drink, the next long patient interval of waiting for the factory under the stone to speak to them again.

No one has ever found one dead. No one has ever found a tally-walker's tracks ending the way other tracks end here—abruptly, mid-stride, going nowhere. Whatever else in the basin is provisional, glitching, caught in some loop of suspended natural law, the herd is not. They are the one steady thing in a place built from inconstancy: the rock's own circulatory attendants, walking their unmapped rounds above whatever it is that hammers and pistons in the dark beneath them, keeping time with a heartbeat that isn't theirs, and has never once, in all the going-on of this place, needed to be.
