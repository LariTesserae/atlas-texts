The one who lives here is small for an adult, and stooped in a particular way — not from age but from a lifetime of moving through spaces built for a body slightly different from the body that's doing the moving, terrace-lips chiselled to the height the wall was when the work was started, the wall having since risen, or sunk, or simply changed its mind. The stoop has become permanent. It is not a deformity. It is a fit, worked out the way water works out a channel, by doing the same motion ten thousand times until the body and the space have agreed on each other.

The skin is the first thing you'd notice and the thing you'd have the hardest time describing afterward, because it keeps almost being one thing. In this light it reads as the ochre-red of the working stone; lower down, near the red glow, it takes on that same diffused suggestion-of-color, neither lit nor unlit. It is always slightly wet, not with sweat exactly — sweat implies exertion and this is closer to the bead-by-bead weeping of the walls themselves, constant, unhurried, sourceless. Where the skin is wet it shines; where it has been wet a long time, in the creases of knuckle and elbow and the backs of the knees, it has gone soft and pale, the same gone-soft pale as the bone awl left lying on a ledge three terraces up — and you understand, looking at that awl, that it was not abandoned. It was set down by hands that were, at that exact moment, going the same color. Tool and toolmaker keep the same hours with the damp.

The hands are the part that has finished becoming, or come closest to it. Everything else about this person seems provisional, mid-revision, but the hands are exact: thick through the palm, fingers blunt and short-nailed, the pads of them ridged and cross-hatched with old chisel-slips healed into permanent texture, so that the hand itself is a kind of terrace-map, a record of every wedge driven and every cord lashed. They move with the unconscious correctness of a tool that has been used so long it has stopped being separate from its work. You get the sense these hands could shape stone in the dark, by feel alone, and often do — the eyes here are not the primary instrument. The eyes are pale, slightly overlarge, accustomed to reading shapes out of steam-softened silhouette, and they have the unfocused patience of someone who has long since stopped expecting a single look to tell them anything true. Truth here arrives by tap, by drip, by the resistance of stone under a wedge — slow accretion, the same way the terraces themselves arrive.

The clothing, if it can be called that, is whatever the room provided and outgrew: cord-lashed leaf, layered like shingles, replaced rung by rung the way the ladder cords are replaced, so there is no single moment you could point to and say *this is when the garment changed* — only the certainty that it has, continuously, the way everything here changes continuously, becoming without arriving. It smells, faintly, of whatever the air smelled of an hour ago — mineral, or floral, or burnt sugar — because the leaf-cloth holds the steam the way everything here holds the steam, a little longer than it should.

They do not speak in any language you would recognize, because language here is mostly unnecessary — there is only the one room, the one ongoing task, and the few who share it have long since reduced communication to the register of the place itself: a tap that means *wait*, two taps that mean *hold this*, a particular rhythm of slapping a wet leaf aside that means *mind the drop*. When they do vocalize — and they do, sometimes, alone, working a ledge by the red glow — it comes out half-song, half-grunt, pitched to match the subsonic groan of the growing stone, as though trying to harmonize with the mountain rather than direct it. It isn't supplication. It isn't mastery either. It's closer to keeping time with something that doesn't know it's being kept time with.

What they want, if you could ask them and have it answered, is not for the terrace to stop. They gave that up — if they ever held it — longer ago than the oldest tool-mark you can find. What they want is simpler and stranger: to stay exactly one tap ahead of the rock's becoming, forever, so that nothing the mountain produces arrives unshaped, untended, unaccounted-for. Not to finish the terrace. To never let it get ahead of them. It is a kind of love that looks, from outside, exactly like labor, and from inside, you suspect, doesn't feel like either — just the single continuous gesture of a hand following wet stone wherever wet stone insists on going next, world without end, world still arriving, tap, tap, pause, tap.
