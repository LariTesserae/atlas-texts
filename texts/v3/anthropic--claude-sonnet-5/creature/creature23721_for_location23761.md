They are smaller than you'd expect, and softer-spoken than the noise around them should allow—a population that has learned to pitch its voice to cut through machine-chuff and wing-roar without shouting, a kind of clipped, economical speech that carries. Generations of heat have thinned them. You see it in the build: lean, long-limbed, skin permanently flushed at the cheeks and throat, the look of people who sweat in their sleep. They wear little—strips of oiled cloth, leather aprons for the forge-workers, nothing at all on the children who run the upper catwalks with a sure-footedness that makes your stomach drop to watch. What clothing exists is less about modesty than function: covering the joints where insects bite, covering the scalp where the guano-rain from the roosts finds you if you linger too long beneath the wrong hollow.

Everyone here has a relationship with the swarms that isn't quite fear and isn't quite husbandry. The old women who tend the smoke-smudges—banked, smoldering fires of damp fungus-wood kept burning at the chamber's social margins—are listened to with a particular respect, because they read the roosts the way someone above-ground might read weather. A shift in the undertone, a surge that comes a half-hour early, and they'll call it before the lanterns gutter, herding children indoors, weighting down the food-nets, while the rest of the town barely breaks rhythm. There's no separate caste for this, exactly, but there's a recognizable type: weathered, unbothered by things landing on them, hands quick and certain around netting and smoke-pots, the kind of person who's been bitten so many times the venom doesn't do much anymore.

The forge-and-pump people are a different sort—broader through the shoulder despite the general leanness, deafened young, missing fingers at a rate nobody remarks on. They work in relay shifts timed to the wheel's groan, and there is something almost liturgical in how they talk about the machinery, a vocabulary of coaxing and feeding and listening for trouble in a belt's whine, as though the pump-house were less built than grown, and needed tending the way the roosts needed smoke. The water-tower's riveted iron is patched in a dozen different hands' styles, a kind of accidental signature-wall: you can read three generations of repair in its seams if you know how to look, though nobody does, particularly. History here isn't kept. It's worn into the tools.

Children are everywhere and underfoot and oddly fearless, weaving between the fast pale things that course the pipe-runs as if they were just another moving feature of the architecture, swatting clouds of lantern-insects away from their faces with the same gesture they'd use to brush off rain. They're put to work early—running messages along the catwalks, minding the nets, learning which hollows are roost and which are merely dark—but there's play in it too, a kind of feral joy in being small and quick in a chamber built for things both larger and faster than the adults.

There is no clear leadership that announces itself, but there are elders—rock-elders, maybe, or just the ones who remember the gallery before this one, the chamber before this widening—who get consulted before a new cut is made, who walk the unfinished hollows at the chamber's edge with their hands trailing the raw stone like they're reading a pulse. Whether they believe the rock is alive in any literal sense, you can't quite tell. But they speak of it carefully, the way you'd speak of something still deciding what it wants to be.

What unites all of them—forge-workers, smoke-wardens, children, elders—is a particular, unspectacular endurance: a refusal to be surprised by water finding new paths, by swarms surging without warning, by stone settling unpredictably underfoot. They have built a town in a wound that is still closing, on the back of a darkness that outnumbers them a thousand to one, and they have done it not by conquering the place but by negotiating a long, damp, loud, ongoing truce with it—one renewed every day at the pace of the pump-wheel's turn, one nobody expects to end in their lifetime, and one nobody, watching them move through their work with that particular unhurried competence, seems especially troubled by.
