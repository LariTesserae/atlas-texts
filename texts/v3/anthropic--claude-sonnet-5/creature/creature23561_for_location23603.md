# Those Who Hold the Hollow

They are easy to miss because the hollow was built to make them easy to miss. Not from fear — from manners. A first courtesy, learned early and never unlearned: when something approaches, become still, become bark, become the wall you are leaning against. So you may walk the length of the settlement and feel watched without ever confirming it, and you would not be wrong.

---

## Body, Made for the Grey

They are slight — not small, but narrow, built along the same vertical logic as the root-walls, as if growing up through a gap was the founding discipline of the body before it was the founding discipline of the architecture. Skin the color of wet bark, mottled paler at the joints, and across the shoulders and down the spine a faint bloom of the same grey-green that maps the high beams — not disease, not decoration exactly, but accumulation: lichen that has decided a person is also a surface worth colonizing, and a person who has decided not to mind.

The eyes are large, pale, and used less than you'd expect. In light this thin, sight is a secondary sense, useful mainly for finding the edges of things; everything else is read through the skin, the soles of the feet, the long sensitive fingers that spend more time pressed flat against bark than wrapped around anything. They feel a wall the way another people might read a face — tension at the joints, dampness at the root, the faint vibration of growth still happening underneath. They can tell, by touch alone, which trunk is winning its argument with its neighbor for the same gap in the lattice.

Movement is slow and exact, weight placed and settled before the next weight is placed — not from caution but from a kind of permanent agreement with the floor, which is, after all, alive, and deserves to be walked on the way you'd walk on something asleep that you loved.

---

## The Work, Which Has No Name for Itself

They do not call it building. There is no word in their hands for *construction*, because construction implies an end-state, a thing finished and then left — and nothing here is ever finished or left. What they do, instead, is closer to a very long conversation conducted entirely in pressure and patience: a tendril guided sideways with two fingers and held there, day after day, until it forgets it wanted to grow any other way. A root encouraged downward through a gap by the simple, repeated removal of every easier path. A vine bound to itself with nothing but a loop of its own younger growth, tightened a little more each season, until the knot is no longer a knot but simply the shape that piece of plant has always been.

No blade touches anything here. Cutting is the one act that doesn't occur to them as a method — not from prohibition, just absence, the way it might not occur to you to solve a math problem with your teeth. Everything is *directed*, never removed. This is slower than building. It is also, they would tell you, if asked, the only way building has ever actually worked; everything else is just a faster way of fighting the material instead of asking it.

The pale unweathered seams in the walls — the new fusions — are their handprints, more or less. Each one marks a place where two reluctant growths were held together by someone's patient daily attention until they stopped being reluctant.

---

## Numbers, and the Smallness of the Whole

There are not many of them. The hollow is room-scale, hamlet-scale, walkable in an unhurried hour, and the population matches it — a few dozen, never sprawling past the reach of the canopy they've trained, because the canopy is the only thing keeping the bleed-rain off anyone's head, and you do not outgrow your own roof.

They sleep in the joints — literally, in the crooks where trunk meets trunk, where the wood is widest and warmest and already faintly shaped like a body from generations of bodies choosing the same crook. Children are shown which gaps are safe to grow into before they're shown much else; the geography of the hollow is learned through the skin before it's learned through any word.

There is no plaza, the description rightly notes, no gap where the green stops — and so there is also no separation, among this people, between *home* and *commons*. Privacy is a matter of which root-wall you happen to be touching, not a door. Most conversations, what there are of them, happen low and close, mouth near ear, because sound dies so fast in all this absorbing green that raising your voice accomplishes nothing except making your own ears ring.

---

## Kin, of a Sort

The pale long-bodied thing that moves silently between root-gaps at floor level is not quite theirs, but not quite wild either — fed, sometimes, with scraps of softened root-pulp left in the same low gaps each time, until it learned the gaps were worth returning to. It does not obey. It is not asked to. It simply persists nearby, the way the lichen persists on a shoulder, a low-grade companionship built entirely from repetition and the absence of any reason to leave.

The thing overhead, heard only departing, they know by sound alone and have never named, because naming a thing you've never seen felt, to whoever decided this generations or decades ago, like a small dishonesty.

---

## A People Still Arriving

The hollow is young — sharp at the joints, mid-sentence, still finishing itself — and so are they, in the way that matters: this is not an old culture grown brittle with finished answers, but a new one, still discovering which kinds of pressure make a vine forget its old direction, still learning the hollow's moods through the soles of their feet one damp season at a time.

Ask one of them, if you could ask and be understood, how long they expect to be doing this, and the honest answer, given in a shrug and a hand laid flat against the nearest warm trunk, would be: as long as it keeps growing. Which is to say — they have no plan for an ending, because the wall under their hand has none either, and they have learned, more than anything else, to take their cues from what they live inside of, rather than the other way around.
