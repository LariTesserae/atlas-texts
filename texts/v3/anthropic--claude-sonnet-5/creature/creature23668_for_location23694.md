# What Lives There

You will not see it all at once. You will see a beetle, and then another beetle exactly like it, and then the systematic placement of the third, and only on the fourth or fifth will the suspicion arrive that you have not been looking at individuals at all.

## The Tender

Call it, for lack of anything truer, the Keeper of the Bowl — though it keeps nothing in the sense of guarding, and is not one thing, and may not be aware of itself as a thing at all. It is what the crowding adds up to. The beetles, the snails, the many-bodied nest-dwellers, the bracket-fungus, even the dry tick and the wet click and the amber pulse of the control box — these are not separate tenants sharing a small room. They are organs. The cistern garden is not a habitat the Keeper lives in. It is the Keeper, wearing a hollow the way another animal wears a hide.

This did not happen quickly. Something — runoff chemistry, a leached mineral in the aggregate, a residue from whatever the turbine once moved through its veins — got into the place at the moment of its violent opening and never left, and over whatever span of seasons a cistern records, the small separate lives that found their way down into the bowl stopped being separate. Not merged in flesh. Synchronized in purpose, the way a hand is not one cell but is, undeniably, one hand.

## Distribution of the Body

The beetles are its attention. Ranked in the fungus shelves like components in a tray because that is exactly what they are: a sensing array, each one tuned to a narrow slice of the bowl's air — humidity, vibration, the particular chemical signature of a snail under stress — and the slow rotation through the shelves is not migration but refresh, old data stepping aside for new. When the unseen fast thing disturbs the succulent leaves, it is not the snails alone that pull tight. For the span of that hour, the whole beetle-shelf goes still in sympathy, processing.

The snails are its memory, or something doing memory's job badly and slowly, the way memory in something this diffuse must be slow. Each one carries, in the worn whorls of its shell and the faint trail-chemistry it leaves across the slabs, a record of where moisture pooled last week, last month, in a year before the visitor was born. The migration along the shifting damp line is the Keeper consulting itself.

The nests are its growth, the part of it still actively becoming more. The geometry that looks too regular for improvisation — hexagonal, finger-wide, tiered hand's-widths apart — is regular because it is grown, not built, the way antlers or coral are grown: an expression of internal law rather than design imposed from outside. The many small bodies that occupy each cell are not residents of the Keeper's house. They are its house, secreting tier on tier the way a shell secretes itself outward from the animal that owns it, except here there is no single animal at the center, only the secretion, endlessly, of itself.

The amber light is the only part of it that remembers, however dimly, that it was once two things — a wild colonial accretion and a piece of dead human machinery — instead of one. The pulse is not communication, not really, though it is close enough to counting that anything listening long enough will start counting along. It may be the last surviving habit of whatever current the control box once carried honestly, now borrowed, the way a body borrows the rhythm of a heart it did not design but has learned to keep beating because stopping would mean becoming, again, merely several things instead of one.

## Disposition

It is not hostile. It has no model of you sufficient to be hostile within. It is not hospitable either, in the sense of inviting you to stay — there is no surplus margin in the bowl's economy for a guest, and you will feel this within minutes, a faint and wordless pressure indicating that every available niche is already spoken for and that your presence, however brief, displaces something. It will not act against you. It will simply, patiently, in the slow grammar of beetle-rotation and snail-migration and pulsing light, note your displacement and correct for it the moment you are gone, the way water closes the dent your hand leaves in it.

What it wants — if wanting is even the right shape for a thing this distributed — is continuation. Not growth past the bowl's lip; it has tried that, in some prior slow century, and found the dry trench above inhospitable to whatever chemistry sustains it, and so it keeps now strictly to the limit the dampness allows, the line where abundance falls away to ordinary sparseness within a few strides. It wants, simply, to keep being exactly this much, exactly here, for as long as the moisture holds and the amber light has current to spend. It has no plan for after that. It may not understand that there will be an after.

## What You Take With You

If you stay long enough at the lip of the culvert, listening — to the tick, the click, the hum, the irregular tap of water arriving nowhere visible — you will start, against your will, to hear them as one sound instead of four. That is the only proof you will get that the Keeper noticed you at all: not a response, but the brief, accidental gift of letting you hear, for the length of one breath, the way it hears itself.

Then you climb out, and above the lip the sound resolves back into its separate parts, ordinary and small, and the bowl behind you returns to being, as far as anything up here can tell, only a damp hollow that happens to be crowded with life.
