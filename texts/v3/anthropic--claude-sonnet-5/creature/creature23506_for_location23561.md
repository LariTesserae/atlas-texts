**What Lives Here**

It is, at first, indistinguishable from the stones. This is not camouflage in the way you understand camouflage—a pattern matched to a background—but identity worn down toward sameness over a span of time that has no use for the word *years*. Its skin has the same fat, edgeless smoothness as the rock-skin of the grove, the same lichen-fur in patches, the same perpetual damp. When it is still, which is nearly always, you could press your palm against its flank and believe you were touching the mountain's own shoulder, warm with that held-breath warmth that confused you when you first arrived. That warmth is not the grove's. It is this creature's, radiating outward, and the grove has simply learned to keep it, the way a held hand keeps another hand's heat.

It has no eyes, or none that still function as eyes. What it has instead is something closer to what the hollow itself uses to perceive you—the place's own faculty, on loan, or perhaps the place *learned its faculty from this creature*, long before you arrived, in the same patient way you found yourself sharing its sight within minutes of entering the dark. It reads warmth the way you would read color. It reads the tremble of water moving through stone and root the way you would read a held note of music. The dripping, the seeping, the slow circulatory currents of damp air: these are not background to it. They are language, weather report, and memory all at once, and it has had so long to listen that it no longer distinguishes between hearing a thing and remembering it.

It is old. This needs to be said plainly because nothing about its body will say it for you—there is no white fur, no clouded eye, none of the marks your mind reaches for to signal age. Instead there is only this: an utter absence of hurry, so total that it reads not as feature but as physics, as if speed itself were a property this creature simply does not participate in, the way a stone does not participate in running. It may be one. It may, in some sense too patient for your kind of counting, be the last one—the final custodian of a kinship that built the threads, set the post at its impossible angle, fitted the ring to the tree and waited the centuries required for the tree to grow its perfect, unsplit collar around it. Or it may be merely the latest in an unbroken line so slow-turning that "generation" stretches to a length indistinguishable from a single, very long life. Either way, the structures are its inheritance, its instruments, or its children. It tends them as a gardener tends, without urgency, because urgency was never available to it and it has therefore never had to invent the lack of it as a virtue.

The threads are how it speaks. Not the chirring, clicking abundance of the smaller lives around it—that is conversation it merely permits, a soundscape it keeps rather than makes—but the threads, those fine strung lines that hum a note without decay, are its own voice, set down once and left to keep ringing the way a bell rings until something stops it. It may not even produce new ones anymore. It may simply maintain what was strung long ago, walking the grove on whatever schedule it keeps—a schedule that might unfold once across a human lifetime—to touch each thread and find it still warm, still true, still singing the pitch it was given. This is not music for an audience. There may be no audience it expects. It is bookkeeping. It is grief, possibly, kept on for so long that grief and habit and devotion have become a single unbroken act.

It feeds, if feeding is even the right word for so unhurried a transaction, on what the warmth and wet of its body draws to it—small things investigating the leaf litter, larger things settling nearby with that heavy, owlish percussion of wings, all of it pulled into the grove's abundance partly by the conditions it maintains, the way an old, slow heart keeps blood moving through a body grown comfortable around it. It is less predator than nucleus. The grove organizes itself around the fact of it, the way moss organizes around water, the way silence organizes around a held breath.

You may stand within arm's reach of it and never know. That is not concealment, on its part. It is only that it has become, through years beyond the reach of years, one more old and settled thing in a room full of old and settled things—patient enough, finally, to be mistaken for geology, and untroubled by the mistake.
