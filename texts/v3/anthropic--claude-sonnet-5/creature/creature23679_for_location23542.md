## What Answers the Flute

The bone-flute call does not come from a throat shaped for singing. It comes from something that has no name in any language still spoken aloud, because the people who named it stopped speaking aloud generations ago, for reasons that will become clear.

Call it the Caller, because that is what it does, every twenty minutes, on a cycle as patient as tectonic drift.

It lives beneath the plateau, not on it—threaded through the same fissure-system that weeps the mineral oils the gardens drink, because the Caller is, in a sense, the reason the oils weep at all. Picture something between a bladder and a root and an old, old animal: a vast, slow-pulsing body the texture of tanned hide gone soft with age, settled into a cavern system it has spent centuries widening by the simple patient pressure of breathing. It does not move, not really, not anymore—it has long since grown too large to move, the way a tree does not move once it has decided where to stand. What it does instead is process: drawing the deep basalt oils through organs that look, in the rare glimpses anyone living has had, like folded grey lungs the size of houses, converting mineral seep into the gas that surfaces through every fissure on Karassi Tor, feeding every gas-chambered stem, every valve-leaf, every grazer that cups its mouth skyward to drink the exhale of a plant that is, several joints of causality removed, drinking the Caller's own breath.

Every twenty minutes it vents the overflow through a throat-tube—a single long flexible column of cartilage and membrane that rises through one of several shaft-fissures across the plateau (the dome's smoke-hole is one; the standing stones mark another; the lookout was built, almost certainly, to watch the third), exhales its long bone-flute note into the open air, and withdraws. The pitch shifts season to season because the Caller is, against everything its size should permit, still very slowly growing.

It is not malicious. It is not, by any animal definition, even particularly aware, in the way a held breath is not aware of the person holding it. But it is old, and large, and everything on the plateau is downstream of its exhalation, and that is enough to produce the sensation every traveler reports: of being watched by something patient, hungry, and in no hurry at all. The watching is real. It is simply not looking with eyes.

## The Ones Who Listen Back

There are perhaps thirty of them left, in two or three loose kin-groups, and they do not call themselves anything that translates cleanly—the closest gloss is *the ones who keep time with it.*

They are human, recognizably, descended from whoever cut the terraces and dry-fit the dome and leaned the three standing stones at angles too precise for accident. But generations of thin air and oil-tainted soil and a diet drawn entirely from gas-fed grazers and grove-fruit have reshaped them at the margins: barrel-deep chests built for air this lean; skin with a faint, permanent undertone of blue-green at the fingertips and throat, the same pigment the cliff-roosting wing-things carry in their guano, absorbed slowly across a lifetime of sleeping in spore-light; eyes adapted to brass-colored dimness, wide-pupiled, slow to startle.

They do not farm, not anymore, not since whoever built the terraces understood the plants didn't want water. What they do is tend boundary and cycle. They walk the line between grove and bare stone and discourage growth from crossing it, not out of superstition but out of something closer to apprenticed ecology—generations of watching which groves over-mature and collapse, which root-weeps are exhausting themselves, where the next eruption will likely surface. They harvest spent husks after the detonation-cascades, before the scavenger-packs arrive in force. They time their own movements, their sleep, their rare quiet conversations, to the twenty-minute pulse of the bone-flute note the way a body learns to live around a metronome it has stopped consciously hearing.

They do not call it worship, if asked, though there is nothing else to call the dome's continued maintenance, the smoke-hole kept clear though it sees no smoke, the standing stones never allowed to settle out of alignment though no one living remembers who first set them. It is upkeep performed for a thing they cannot see and do not fully believe is owed anything, performed anyway, the way you'd keep a door oiled in a house you suspect, but cannot prove, still has someone living behind one of its walls.

They are wary of outsiders in the specific way the doglike scavengers are wary—curious, distance-keeping, willing to trail a traveler for a day at a slight remove without ever quite approaching. It is not unfriendliness. It is that they have stopped expecting anyone to stay long enough to be worth the risk of approach, and the plateau has taught them, in its bones-deep turbulence, that almost nothing here is worth attaching to before it changes.

## What They Are to Each Other

The honest answer is that nobody, including the kin-groups themselves, is certain whether they tend the Caller or whether the Caller, in some unintended way, tends them—whether the gas it exhales into the rock was ever meant to feed a garden, or whether the garden is simply what happened, over millennia, to oil-seep that something enormous underneath has been quietly producing as a byproduct of its own continued existence, the way breath fogs a window without intending a picture.

What's certain is the rhythm holds them both. The Caller breathes; the plateau breathes with it, in staggered gas-chamber exhalations and seed-pod detonation-storms and bursts of grazer frenzy that have nothing to do with sun or season; and the last thirty or so descendants of whoever first decided this barren, beautiful, ungovernable shelf of stone was worth staying for have shaped their entire inherited lives around not the place, exactly, but its pulse—living, like everything else here, in bursts of frantic tending followed by long stillness, waiting out each twenty-minute silence for the next low note to come up through the rock and tell them, again, that whatever is down there is still patiently, hungrily, unhurriedly alive.
