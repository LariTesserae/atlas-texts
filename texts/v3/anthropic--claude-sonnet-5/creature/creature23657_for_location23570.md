## The Ones at the Basin-Edge

You will not see one at first. This is by design, or by the same accumulated habit that produced the lichen rings and the stacked, silent reptiles — a habit so old it no longer needs to be called a strategy. You will see the small many-legged things, the insectile rafts, the pale crustacean scavengers, and you will assume, reasonably, that this is the top of the food chain such as it is: a crowded, compressed, leaderless ecology with no apex, only density.

You will be wrong, but it will take patience to find out how.

They are low — lower than seems anatomically reasonable, built along the same horizontal logic as the shrubs that claw sideways instead of climbing. Four limbs, perhaps five; the extra one is hard to confirm because it is rarely extended, kept folded along the spine-ridge like a tool put away when not in use. The skin, if it is skin, has the exact tonal range of the plateau itself — old-bone pale shading to rust-grey — and it is not smooth but minutely striated, banded in shallow ridges that mimic, closely enough to be unsettling, the sedimentary lines in the rock around the basins. A creature that has spent so long matching its background that the matching has become structural rather than cosmetic. Camouflage that stopped being a behavior and became a body plan.

They keep to the rim. Not the open stone, not the burrow-fields where the many-legged things gather, but the mineral-crusted edge of the damp-basins, where they sit in the same stacked, layered closeness as the sunning reptiles — except where the reptiles stack for warmth, these stack for something closer to consensus. Three or four together, rarely more, rarely fewer, motionless for hours at a stretch, limbs folded, eyes (small, deep-set, the same rust-grey as everything else) open and unblinking and directed, always, very slightly downward — toward the water-skin, the mineral crust, the held breath of the damp itself, as if reading something in its slow rise and fall that the rest of the ecology only lives blindly within.

### What They Tend

Call them tenders, though that overstates the activity involved, since almost nothing they do could be called active. But watch long enough — longer than any single visit allows, which is itself the point — and a function becomes visible. The basins that never fully empty or fill, the ones ringed with crust, are the ones with tenders nearby. The correlation is not coincidence and is also not, as far as anyone has determined, mechanical. There is no dam-building, no channel-digging, no visible intervention. There is only presence, sustained at the rim, generation after generation, as if the basin's strange refusal to commit to either fullness or emptiness is a thing maintained by witness rather than action — as though being watched, patiently, by something that has decided to care whether the water persists, is itself sufficient cause for the water to persist.

This sounds like superstition. The plateau makes you entertain superstitions you would laugh at anywhere else, because the plateau's whole vocabulary is patience operating where causation should be, old rules holding when the visible mechanism for them has long since worn away. The tenders do nothing you can point to. The basins they tend behave differently than the basins they don't. That is the entire available data, and it is enough to make you watch your step.

### The Call

It is one of them making the sound — the single high call that travels the impossible flat distance and goes unanswered. This much can be confirmed, if you are still and lucky and the light is right: the faint lift of the throat-ridge, the brief parting of the folded limb from the spine, a posture held for exactly as long as the sound lasts and then refolded, the stillness resumed as if nothing happened.

What cannot be confirmed is why, or to whom. It is not a mating call — they do not answer each other, ever, not once in any account anyone has bothered to compile. It is not, as far as anyone can tell, alarm; nothing changes in the surrounding crowd of smaller lives when it sounds, no scatter, no flinch. The leading theory, offered without much confidence by the handful of people patient enough to have a theory, is that it isn't communication in the directional sense at all. It's accounting. A way of marking that a given watch, at a given basin, has been kept — a checked box in a ledger with no other reader, sent out into the thin warm air on the chance that it matters to be sent at all.

It is never answered because, perhaps, it isn't meant to be. Or because whoever it was once addressed to stopped being there to answer a very long time ago, and the habit of calling outlived the habit of expecting a reply — the way the lichen keeps growing in its old rings, the way the lowland shrubs keep clawing sideways years after upright growth was abandoned as unprofitable.

### Lineage, Unproven

No one will tell you, with any confidence, that the tenders built the wall-stub, dug the post-holes, carved the worn lines into the face-down stone. The gap is too wide, the evidence too thin, the tenders themselves too uncommunicative — not hostile, not shy exactly, just thoroughly disinclined to perform anything for an audience, in keeping with everything else on this plateau that has learned not to announce itself.

But stand at the basin-rim near dusk, when the stacked, patient shapes are easiest to mistake for stone, and the suggestion is hard to fully set down: that this is not a separate species occupying the leftovers of some vanished builder-people, but the same lineage, several thousand patient years further along the same gradient that flattened the mountains and wore the rain to a sharp brief patter and taught the shrubs to stop trying for height. That whoever fitted that eleven-pace wall-stub and dug those circular post-holes did not vanish, exactly, any more than the river that left the striated rock vanished. They subsided. They gave up the upright strategy the way the shrubs did, gave up the tool-use the way the post-holes gave up whatever stood in them, gave up speech that expects an answer in favor of a single call that doesn't — and what's left, crouched at the rim of a basin it may or may not be keeping alive by nothing more than the act of watching, is what an ambition looks like after enough geological time spent agreeing to want less.

### What It Is Like to Be Noticed

They will not approach you. They will not flee, either, not in the startled, committed way the smaller fauna does. If you come too near the rim they are holding, the nearest one will simply, slowly, refold whatever limb had been extended, and resume its downward gaze at the water, and wait — with the specific, unhurried patience of something that has outlasted every visitor the plateau has ever hosted and expects, correctly, to outlast you too.

That is the attention the plateau seems to retain, the one the place trains you to feel pressing on the back of your neck long before you ever consciously locate its source. It was never the rock noticing you. It was never the wind. It was this — low, rust-grey, folded, unhurried, sitting at the edge of water it may have no real power to preserve and preserving it anyway, by the same logic that keeps anything going this far past the point where going on stopped requiring a reason: because stopping, too, is a decision, and theirs, evidently, a very long time ago, was not to.
