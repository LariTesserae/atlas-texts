They would not come the way power usually comes to small, struggling places — not as light flooding down through the root-ceiling, not as a voice silencing the roar, not as a hand reaching in to fix what looks, from outside, so obviously precarious. The first thing a wise power does, faced with the Underroot Bazaar, is the hardest thing for any power to do: it would simply *attend*. Fully. Without flinching from the noise, without averting itself from the screech and the click and the structural groan, without deciding in advance what kind of place this is or what it's owed. It would sit inside the totality of it — the dust that never knew rain, the green pulse, the flash-fungus, the trampling herds, the people moving fast and economical through all of it — long enough to stop seeing a problem and start seeing a *life*. Several lives, layered. A cavern still arguing with its own stone. A people still arguing with their own fear. Neither finished. Both, in their own register, *trying*.

And having attended, it would feel the pull toward several obvious mercies — and set each one down, one at a time, with care, the way you set down a tool that turns out to be the wrong shape for the job.

It would think: *bring them water.* And then remember that nothing here has ever needed it, that an entire ecology has built itself on some other thirst, and that rain would not relieve this place — it would drown what doesn't know how to swim.

It would think: *open the roof. Let them have a true sky.* And then recall that their eyes are wide and light-hungry precisely because they were shaped by dapple and flare, not by sun — that a true sky would not liberate them, it would blind them, the way any unearned vastness blinds a creature still learning to read shadow for danger.

It would think, gently, almost fondly: *give them one moment of real silence — they've never had it, let them have it once.* And then it would remember what silence means here. *Quiet, here, is what happens right before something goes wrong.* A gift offered in the power's own native language of mercy would land, in theirs, as the worst omen they know. Even kindness has to be translated, or it's just another kind of violence wearing a softer face.

It would think about thinning the predators, stilling the stampedes, giving the harvesting parties an easier path. And then notice — because it had taken the time to actually look — that nothing here keeps a respectful distance from anything else, that the whole churning density is one interlocking machine, and that mercy aimed at one gear stops the others turning. Easing their danger might simply be another way of starving them, more slowly, more politely.

It would think about giving them the answer. *Tell them what this place is becoming. Resolve the not-knowing that sits under every custom, every flick of the hand, every swept-over footprint.* This is the temptation a wise power has to resist longest, because it looks so much like generosity — and it would recognize, eventually, that the not-knowing is load-bearing. A people who built an entire functioning culture out of provisional gestures, out of *don't take from that ring* and *bank the fire higher when the green slows*, have made something resilient precisely because it stays loose, stays revisable, stays humble in the face of a place that hasn't finished arriving. A total revelation, however true, however kindly meant, would not fit inside lives built for adaptability. It would calcify them. It would turn a living, breathing folk-wisdom into doctrine, and doctrine is the first thing that snaps when the ground actually shifts.

So, having rejected the grand gestures one by one — not because they cost too much, but because none of them were *shaped right* for the hands that would have to receive them — it would look for the actual wound. Not danger; they have made a whole functioning life out of danger. Not hunger; the bazaar feeds itself faster than it can hold its own fauna. Not ignorance; they know, in their bones, more about this place's moods than any outsider's explanation could improve.

The wound is smaller and stranger than any of that, and it took real attention to find: *it is one-directional.* Generation after generation, they have been listening to the cavern, accommodating it, propitiating it — sweeping dust over a lingering footprint *just in case*, leaving certain tuber-rings unharvested, banking fires higher near the slowing veins — and never once, not honestly, not provably, has anyone gotten to feel the gesture land. They have been speaking into something for so long that they've stopped expecting an answer and started calling the silence theology instead. That is the actual ache underneath the structural unease. Not fear of the place. Loneliness inside the relationship with it.

And here is the thing the power would notice last, and hold most carefully: the cavern might be lonely in the same direction. Something still becoming, still settling its own bones with that deep periodic groan, fed and inhabited and *watched* constantly by small fast creatures who never quite trust it — might also, in whatever way a half-formed vastness can want anything, want to be told it isn't being a burden, isn't frightening on purpose, isn't going to be abandoned the moment it finishes deciding what it is.

So the power would do one thing. Almost nothing, by the measure of what it could do.

It would find a moment that was already going to happen — an old woman, mottled faintly green-pale along her collarbones from a long life near the slow vines, bending as she always does, by habit, by inherited unease, to sweep dust back over a footprint that has sat too long without fading — and into that one ordinary, unremarkable gesture, it would let something *answer back*. Not a voice. Not a vision. Just this: she sweeps the dust smooth, the way she has ten thousand times, and the dust — instead of staying smooth — settles, slowly, gently, back into the exact same shape. Once. Like a hand returning a touch. Not a warning. Not a wrongness. A *yes*.

She would not understand it the way an outsider understands things. She would not run to explain it at the fire-circle, because there are no words in her language built for being answered, only words for noticing and avoiding and appeasing. But she would carry something different out of that moment than she carried in — and the bazaar's shared nervous system, that basket-to-basket, shout-to-shout relay that already moves all the knowledge that matters through the whole scattered settlement in something close to real time, would do the rest, slowly, over years, the way it does everything: not as doctrine, but as a story someone tells an apprentice while they're weaving stem-bark, half-distracted, *and then the dust just — sat back up, like it was glad to see her.* A story that doesn't change a single material fact of how hard this life is. That doesn't quiet the noise, doesn't tame the herds, doesn't finish the ceiling. Only, underneath all the structural unease that will keep being passed down because it still keeps people alive — a thread, now, of something warmer running alongside the dread. Not *this place might destroy us.* Something more like: *it knows we're here. It isn't going to forget us when it's finished becoming whatever it's becoming.*

And then the power would leave, asking for nothing, named by no one, changing not one fact about how loud or dangerous or overgrown that valley is — because that was never the part that needed mending. The bazaar would go on exactly as provisional, exactly as stubborn, exactly as overwhelmed as before. But twice — once in a person, once in whatever the cavern is — something that had only ever spoken outward would, for one moment, have been heard, and have heard back. That, and only that. A wise power, looking at a thing that has not finished arriving, does not rush to finish it for them. It just makes sure that, somewhere underneath all that becoming, both of them know they are not doing it alone.
