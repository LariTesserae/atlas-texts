It would come down slowly, the way everything here is required to move — not because it must obey the bowl's laws but because anything moving quickly through that thick warm air would announce itself as a kind of violence, a blade through water, and it has no wish to be a blade. It would learn, before it did anything else, to hold its voice the way they hold theirs: close to the body, flattened, costed out before spending. It would spend a long time — long by its own reckoning, which is not the Underglade's, though down here it would let the difference go slack — simply listening to the held breath of the engines, the arrhythmic drip finding its basins, the click and swallow of footsteps on wet metal, before allowing itself to think the word *help*, because it would already suspect, correctly, that the word is dangerous here.

The temptation, when it came, would come in the shape of obvious mercies. Open the throat of the sinkhole wider and let real daylight reach the floor. Thicken the water until rationing became a memory rather than a discipline. Reach into the unlit tier and silence whatever moves there, so the held boundary could finally be let down. Each of these would occur to it, and each it would set aside — not because it lacked the power, but because it would recognize, watching a crew rethread a belt-drive in the morning and train a vine along the same housing in the afternoon, calling both acts by the same unremarkable name, that it was looking at something already whole. A people who have made of scarcity not a wound but a grammar — who gesture less so they might live closer together, who speak low not from fear but from a kind of acoustic courtesy, who log their water-debts the way other towns log marriages and deaths — are not waiting to be rescued. They are in the middle of a long, exact, mostly successful work of living, and ease poured into the middle of that work would not be a gift. It would be a flood. It would loosen the very muscle — the economy, the close attention, the slow soft strength built only by sustained heat — that holds the place together. To save them from their conditions would be to unmake who the conditions had made them into, and the power, being wise as well as benevolent, would understand that these are not always the same mercy.

So it would not open the sky. It would not silence the deep tier. It would not hand them abundance.

What it would do is smaller than all of that, and would take the Underglade's own logic as instruction rather than obstacle. The bowl already lived with a few mysteries folded into its rhythm — the condensation that drips on a schedule no one quite explains, the way newcomers stop hearing the engines after a season, as if the place itself were doing them a kindness too embedded in the architecture to be noticed as one. The power would simply add to that thin existing seam of unexplained grace, rather than cutting a new one. It would find — in the deep wet logic of root and pipe and stone, where geology and machine had already blurred past telling apart — one basin, one family's water-catch that ran chronically slow, and it would lean on it, gently, permanently, the way a hand might lean on a scale without ever being seen to touch it. Not abundance. Just enough. A margin against the dry spell that isn't dry, just drier, made very slightly wider than chance alone would have made it — wide enough that no one in the generation now living, or the one after, would lose a child to a season of bad luck and worse rationing. Discovered, if at all, only as the kind of thing an old woman might mention to a grandchild: *that catch has always run a little better than it should. No one knows why. Mountain's own kindness, maybe.* And left there, uninvestigated, folded into the bowl's own respected category of things known and not explained.

It would do one more thing, smaller still. In the unlit tier, where the watchfulness runs downward instead of up, it would not remove what is there — only hold it, the way a hand might hold a door that wants, on its own, to drift shut. Whatever moves in that dark would go on moving, would go on being heard and not quite seen, would go on costing the Undergladers their low respectful fear, because that fear is load-bearing too; a boundary kept is a kind of muscle, and a people with nothing left to hold the edge of their world against grow strange in ways scarcity never made them. But it would make certain — quietly, permanently, in the same unannounced register as the water — that whatever lived down there would never grow hungrier, or bolder, or larger than the watching could comfortably bear. The held breath would stay held. The boundary would stay a boundary, never tightening into crisis, never slackening into forgetting.

And then, perhaps, one mercy more, aimed not at the bowl's machinery but at its memory: among the children who absorb the bowl's gravity by their tenth year and stop, mostly, wanting to leave, it would touch nothing in their wanting. It would not push any one of them up the rope-lift toward a surface that owes them nothing. But in the handful who never stop wanting it, who carry the ambivalence all the way into adulthood without it softening into settledness, it would make sure the climb, when finally attempted, did not kill them on a slick stair in poor light — a missed handhold turned firm, a moment of vertigo passed through rather than fallen through. Whether they stayed above or, as most do, found the air too thin and came back down changed and a little ashamed and were folded back into the basin-courtesies without much comment, would be left entirely to them. The power's part would end at the threshold. It is not in the business of deciding where anyone belongs.

And then it would go quiet, the way the engines are quiet, the way the place itself has taught everything that lives in it to be quiet — not absent, but folded into the held breath, indistinguishable, if anyone ever thought to look, from luck, from geology, from the simple fact that some sinkholes are kinder than others for reasons no one living can name. It would leave no mark anyone could point to. It would ask for no gratitude, expect no shrine, want no credit, because credit is its own kind of loudness, and this is a place that taught it, more than it taught the place anything, the value of speaking low.

The Undergladers would go on as before — tending engine and vine in the same unhurried motion, logging their water-debts, ducking the scaffold with the stooped economy of people who know exactly how close the ceiling is, their children dreaming upward and choosing, mostly, to stay. Nothing about their lives would announce itself as touched. That, the power would understand, was the only form the help could take and still be help, in a place that had already, on its own, worked out how to live so carefully, so well, in the warm thick middle distance between a sky it can't quite reach and a dark it has chosen not to disturb.
