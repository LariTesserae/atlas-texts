The power does not come down through the cloud-ceiling like a god arriving. It comes the way the water does — already there, distributed, settling into what's already shaped to receive it.

It does not announce itself. It has watched enough small places die of being noticed — watched help arrive like weather, all at once, and wash out the terrace walls along with the silt. It knows that a sixty-household settlement built on twenty-year thatch-rotations and family-name calendars cannot survive a miracle. A miracle is a flash flood. What Maerhollow has built, it has built at the speed of moss. Anything that arrives faster than moss will simply be one more thing the basin has to slowly erode back into balance, and that erosion will cost someone a wall, a cistern, a grandmother's name.

So it does almost nothing. And what little it does, it does in the register the place already speaks.

---

It starts with the frogs.

Not new frogs. Not a blessing of frogs, nothing so crude. Just this: this particular wet season, the moss-frog chorus at dusk runs a half-tone sweeter than anyone can remember, and old Tamsin Hollin-third-rethatch, eighty years deep in this basin, stops on her threshold with a basket half-mended in her lap and says, to no one, *that's a good sound to die to,* and doesn't know why she said it, and laughs at herself, and goes back to the basket. She has another nine years. She'll think of that evening twice more before the end, with no more weight than you'd give any other ordinary good evening. That's all it was. That's all it needed to be.

The power likes her. It is not supposed to like anyone in particular — wisdom of that vintage usually isn't — but it likes the particular shape of her patience, the way she taught three children to weave dry-baskets badly for a decade each without once raising her voice over the rain to hurry them, and so it stays near her a while, the way you'd sit near a fire you liked.

---

It looks at the rope-and-bridge people, lean and swaggering and trusted with their lives, and it does the only thing it allows itself to do anywhere in Maerhollow that could be called an intervention: on the day the eastern span finally rots through — and it was always going to, the power has simply chosen *which* day — it is Ren's daughter on the bridge, and not Ren's grandson who would have been there an hour earlier, except that the power arranged for a fruit-pit to drop from the canopy roots onto his threshold at exactly the wrong-right moment, a small early tithe, and he stopped to pick it up and curse the lemur-thing affectionately and was four minutes late, and four minutes is the whole difference between a girl who is strong in the forearms and trained for exactly this, and a boy who is not.

No one will ever know this happened. The power does not perform its rescues where anyone can see the seam. It is not interested in being thanked. It is interested in Ren's grandson reaching sixty and being the kind of old man who teaches a six-year-old to weave a basket badly for a decade, the slow ruin considered part of the teaching.

---

Mostly, though, it just listens. It has the patience for it; it has more patience than the basin itself, which is saying something, given what the basin has already proven about patience, four hundred thousand years of dissolution settled into a held shape that changes only at the edges of a lifetime.

It listens to the second voice of the place — the bell that rings from overhead instead of from the east cistern, the conversation that murmurs up from underfoot — and it understands, the way it understands most true things about mortal settlements, that this acoustic accident has been quietly doing some of its own work for centuries before it ever arrived. The dead already speak here, sideways, from the wrong direction, comfortingly unverifiable. The people have already built, with no help from anyone, a soft place to put their grief where it can stay warm without being looked at too directly. *Everyone, pressed, admits to listening for it anyway, and keeps at least one sound on no evidence at all.*

The power does not improve this. It would be obscene to improve this. It is already exactly correct.

What it does — the only thing, really, the whole visit boils down to this — is take up a very small, very careful residence inside that existing superstition. Not as a new god. Not as a presence that asks for anything, recognition included. It simply makes sure that when the second voice carries a sound to the wrong place, the sound that arrives is, very faintly, kinder than the sound that left. That a child crying alone on a terrace at dusk, where the bell-fall makes everything seem to come from over your shoulder, hears under the bell something like an aunt's voice, low and unhurried, saying a complete thought in four or five words, the way Maerhollow people talk when they mean to be heard through the rain.

It cannot promise this will happen every time. It is not running the place. It is, at most, leaning a thumb very lightly on a scale that the basin's own strange geometry already built, ten thousand years before anyone lashed a roof-beam to a fused root and called it a house.

---

When it leaves — and it does leave, eventually, because the whole ethic of this kind of power is *do not stay long enough to be depended on* — it leaves the way the cloud-ceiling sits: not as an absence anyone will register, but as a thing that was simply always the case, woven into a place too slow and too well-made to need fixing, only the smallest, most deniable encouragement to keep on doing, at its own glacial and sufficient pace, exactly what it was already doing.

The last thing it notices, on the way out, is a wooden bell struck somewhere down in the lower cisterns, arriving — as bells do here — from entirely the wrong direction, sounding for a half-second like it's ringing from directly inside the wet, listening, water-loud air over Maerhollow's rooftops.

It decides, with the small private vanity of a being old enough to permit itself one, to let that be the sound someone keeps.
