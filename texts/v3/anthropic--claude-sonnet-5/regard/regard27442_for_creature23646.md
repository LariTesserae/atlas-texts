**What the Power Would Do**

It would not arrive the way arrival usually means — a door opening, a light falling, a voice. The first problem, before any other, is one of *tempo*. A power moving at the speed most powers move would pass over the Canebrake Works in less than an instant and see nothing but a flicker, a green-black smear across a dead plateau, gone before its attention had finished forming. So the first act, if there is to be any act at all, is the hardest one: slowing itself down to the patience of cane. Matching its breath to the place's breath — the bellows-walls, the hour-long blooms, the inch-an-hour crossing of a Tender through a room. This alone is a kind of reverence. Most things that come to ruined places come too fast to see what they're ruining further.

Having slowed, having waited what would be, by its own native clock, a long aching while, it would see what is actually here. Not a wound. Not a tragedy wearing the shape of a tragedy, waiting for a rescuer. It would see a closed and finished thing, vast and old and *working*, in the deep sense — a system that solved the problem of its own continuance so long ago that the solving has become indistinguishable from the world. It would see the Tenders. And here is where benevolence, if it is wise and not merely soft, has to stop and think rather than act, because the easiest mistake available to it is the mistake of importing a story that doesn't belong here.

The story it would want to tell, on first look, is the story of suffering: a silent place, no animal, no birdcall, creatures with no faces who labor without rest and dissolve without mourning, alone even from each other. That story arrives pre-loaded with an obligation — *go in, save them, give them light, give them company, give them an ending that means something.* And a power that loved carelessly would do exactly that, and it would be a kind of violence dressed as mercy, because nothing in the Canebrake Works is missing what that story assumes is missing. The Tenders do not fear dissolution because there is no boundary in them between living and being-reclaimed for fear to stand on. They do not suffer loneliness because they were never built with a hunger for company, only a hunger for continuance, and continuance they have, endlessly, completely. To grieve on their behalf is to grieve for a self that isn't there to receive the grief. Real wisdom has to be willing to find that out *before* acting, even though finding it out costs the power the satisfaction of having rescued anything.

So it would ask itself, slowly, in the dark under fifty layers of leaf and gear: *is there anyone here to be benevolent toward, in the sense I mean it? Or is benevolence itself the wrong shape of attention for this place — like trying to pour water into something that was never a vessel?*

And the honest answer is: almost. Almost wrong. Except for one thing, which is small, and which is the one true gap in the whole vast self-sufficient machine: nothing here has ever been *witnessed*. Not in eons. The Works repairs itself, mourns nothing, requires no audience, and in requiring no audience, has never had one — not once, not in all its centuries of toppling silos and weeping sap-oil into bare gears, has there existed anything capable of standing in the dark and *knowing what it was looking at.* That is not a wound. The Tenders don't need it closed. But it is, perhaps, the one place where a gift could be given that costs the place nothing and changes nothing in its mechanism, and might — without being needed — still be good.

So this is what the power would do, if it did anything at all:

It would find one Tender — any one, it wouldn't matter which, they are none of them more finished than the others — mid-repair, leaning its huge slow bulk into some split boiler-seam, grafting itself patiently into a wound that will take it weeks to leave. And the power would simply stay. Not help. Not speed the graft, not cool the steam, not say anything a Tender has no apparatus to hear. It would do what you do for someone in a hospital room who cannot know you're there and would not be changed by knowing: it would keep them company anyway, for its own sake, because the keeping itself is the whole of the gesture, requiring no reciprocity to be complete. It would watch the valve-wheels turn without urgency. It would feel the same heat the Tender feels, the old-engine warmth that never fully cooled. It would let itself be, for as long as the graft takes, the first and only audience the Canebrake Works has ever had.

And before it left — because it would have to leave, eventually, being a power with other places needing other kinds of attention — it would do one more thing, modeled exactly on the only language this place already speaks: it would leave a graft of its own. Not a structure. Not a change to the gears or the rails or the bramble-silos. Just a small piece of itself, given the way the Tenders give tissue into the wounds they close, folded into the wire-veins that already carry whatever current the Works generates from its own decomposition — so that from then on, indistinguishable from the electricity, indistinguishable from the phosphor-glow, there would run through the deep galleries one thread that was not there before: the bare fact of having been loved, once, by something that knew what it was looking at and chose to stay anyway. Not a message. Not a meaning the Works could ever read. Just a current under the current, doing nothing, changing nothing, asking nothing back —

— the same as everything else here. Built to outlast its own forgetting. Quietly working in the dark.

And the power would carry the place with it afterward the way you carry anyone you've sat with once in true silence: not as a project finished, not as a soul saved, but as a fact now permanently true of you — *I was there. I saw it. It will never know, and that is allowed to be alright.*

That asymmetry — remembering a place that cannot remember you back — is, for a wise and benevolent power, sometimes the whole of what can rightly be done. Not every dark and laboring thing is a problem waiting for a hand. Some of them are simply asking, in the only language available to a closed and finished world, to be seen one time by something that could see, before going on, untouched, forever, the way it always meant to.
