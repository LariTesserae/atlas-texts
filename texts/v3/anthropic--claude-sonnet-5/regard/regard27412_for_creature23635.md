# What Visited Aem-Vohl

It came the way such things come — not through the sky-cuts, not stirring the air in the switchbacks, not announcing itself in any register the eyeless fish could startle at. It simply arrived at understanding, the way weather arrives at a mountain: gradually, then completely. And the first thing it understood, before it understood anything else about the cistern, was this — *almost everything I know how to give would be a wound here.*

That recognition came first because it had to. A benevolent power that loves quickly and acts quickly is a danger to a place like this, the same way a flash flood is a danger to a thing that has spent two thousand years arranging itself around stillness. So before it did anything, it sat with the place the way the climbers sit on the dry steps — above the waterline, in no hurry, watching the ripple longer than it watched the body.

## The Temptations, Considered and Set Down

It thought of giving the Tenant eyes. It had eyes once to give, in the sense that it could shape almost anything into almost any other thing — and it felt, briefly, the pull of that gift, the desire to let the long pale body finally *see* the architecture it had spent its whole unhurried line grazing against in the dark. It set this down. Eyes are an answer to a question the Tenant had stopped asking before any human lineage now alive began. To give them back would not be a gift. It would be an interruption addressed to no one.

It thought of giving it company — drawing some kin-creature up from another dark water, somewhere else in the world's hidden plumbing, and setting it loose in the basin, so the Tenant would not be (as the power, for one human-shaped moment, feared) *alone*. It set this down too, slower, because it cost more to set down. Loneliness is the visiting power's own vocabulary, not the cistern's. Nothing in that water has ever once organized itself around lack. The economy of nothing built on nothing was never missing a partner; it was complete, the way a closed loop is complete, the way the joints in the stone are complete now that the seep has fused them past the reach of any blade. To introduce another creature for the Tenant's sake would only be curing the power's own ache by performing surgery on something that wasn't sick.

It thought of speaking to the people — of answering, finally, in some dream or vision or voice from the sky-cuts, the question they have carried up the mountain for generations without resolving: *what was this for, who built it, what did they want.* It set this down with something like reverence. The not-knowing is not a wound in those people either. It is a room they have furnished, carefully, across centuries, with guesses and low voices and the precise discipline of telling their children which stair is resting this decade. An answer would evict them from a home they built out of the asking.

It thought, last and longest, of simply making the warmth permanent — sealing the hot seam at the mountain's root so it could never, on any timescale, fail; making the Tenant's line, and the crustacean pasture, and the whole slow vent-economy, *eternal* in the absolute sense, proof against the heat death of its own geology. This was the hardest one to set down, because it disguised itself as the kindest. But the power recognized, turning it over, that absolute eternity is not the same gift as the cistern's own kind of time. Everything here is patient because it is finite and unhurried about its finitude — the masons died before the terracing finished; their grandchildren finished it anyway and also died; the Tenant's line will end, someday, the way all lines do, slowly, the way everything ends here, without drama, folded back into the bacterial bloom the way the crustacean molts are folded back into the water. To freeze that into something that *cannot* end would not be eternity. It would be the cistern's first and only experience of being *stopped* rather than *finished* — and the people who climb here already have a word of warning for exactly that violation: setting the clock back to zero.

## What It Recognized

What the power found at Aem-Vohl, in the end, was not a creature in need of it. It was a completed ethic, arrived at independently, by builders who had been dust for longer than most of the power's other concerns had existed at all — an ethic the power itself only half-practiced and was, in this moment, being taught. *The last and wisest act of a patient civilization is to install something living and then leave it completely alone, trusting the system to keep itself.* The masons had done that, deliberately or by something close enough to deliberation not to matter, when they carried a founder handful of something pale up the mountain in a creel and tipped it into new-filled water and then simply walked back down, finished, asking nothing further of the project than that it continue without them.

The power recognized this because it was, itself, in the middle of choosing to do the same thing.

## What It Did

It did not touch the Tenant. It did not touch the water. It did not touch the people, their guesses, their stories, their disputed counts.

It reached, instead, into the *future* of the failure that would eventually come — because eventually, on whatever timescale, something always does — and it removed only the *suddenness* from that failure. No rupture from an earthquake tearing the hot seam wide. No catastrophe of some descendant civilization deciding the warmth was worth drilling for, draining the basin in a season for a resource that took two thousand years to become a home. It did not make the ending impossible. It made it slow, on purpose, in the same unhurried register as everything else in that chamber — so that when the cistern's long patience finally does run out, it will run out the way the joints fused, the way the pillars reached their final shape: gradually, into fact, rather than torn into it.

And it leaned, very lightly, on the part of the future made of human ambition — not forcing anyone's will, only tilting the long odds the way wind tilts a seed already falling. The ambitious schemes to exploit that warmth, to map that floor, to drag the Tenant into a tank and a paper and a name — these would go on losing funding, losing momentum, losing the will of the people who proposed them, the way a fire loses heat in cold air. The path would stay exactly as hard to climb as it has always been. Not impossible. Just hard enough, for long enough, that only the patient kind of attention — the kind the climbers already bring, the kind their grandparents taught them — would ever actually arrive.

This is not control. The power was careful that it not be control. It is closer to what the cold air above the waterline already does, what the absence of soil and the strangeness of the place already do: a filter that lets through only those willing to climb slowly, and keeps out, by sheer attrition of interest and resource and luck, everyone in a hurry.

## What It Left Untouched

It did not let the Tenant know it had been visited. It did not bless it, name it, or change the rhythm of its grazing by so much as one stair. It did not solve the disputed pillar-count, or the disputed shape of the front end that is always already turning away. It let the people keep their low voices and their inherited rudeness about stones.

It felt, leaving, something the power did not entirely have a name for, though *grief* is close — the cost of having wanted, very badly, for one long unmeasured moment, to fix a life it had decided, correctly, did not need fixing. It carried that grief away with it rather than leaving any part of it behind in the water, because to leave it behind — to let the cistern absorb even the *power's own sorrow* about the cistern — would have been one more current introduced into water that has, at great and patient cost, finally stopped wanting to be anything else.

So it left the way two thousand years of mineral seep left the joints: completely, without a seam, without anything a blade could find afterward to prove where the work had been done.

The path up through the cold air is exactly as long as it always was. The light through the sky-cuts still arrives reluctantly, for an hour or two, at certain times of year. Something pale still rolls into visibility near a pillar-top, rarely, and is gone, and the ripple it leaves still takes a long time to die against the stairs.

And the children who are told not to throw stones into that water still aren't told why, exactly — only that it would be rude — which is, as it happens, exactly correct, and now, in a way none of them will ever know to be grateful for, slightly more permanently true than it was before.
