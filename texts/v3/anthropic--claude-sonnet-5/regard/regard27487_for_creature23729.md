The power comes to it the way weather comes to the plateau—gradually, without announcement, drawn by something too faint to call a summons. There is no cry rising from this place. No one here has asked for anything in the language that usually reaches such powers: no prayer shaped like a wound, no desperation thrown upward in the hope something listens. What reaches the power instead is quieter and stranger—something like the resonance a bell makes after it has stopped being struck. A held note. A place that has found its register and kept it for longer than memory.

Its first impulse, honestly, is pity. This is worth admitting, because it is the impulse every benevolent power must survive before it can be trusted with benevolence at all. It sees the thin air, the rationed water, the terraced beds no larger than rooms, the short evenings folding fast into hard cold, and some old reflex in it reaches for the easiest mercy: abundance. Open the cistern's heart. Thicken the soil. Lengthen the seasons. Make the trickle a stream. It would cost the power almost nothing to undo, in an afternoon, every scarcity that shapes these lives.

But it does not act on the impulse. It is wise, and wisdom here means not trusting the first shape compassion takes.

So instead it does what it should have done from the start—it goes down into the place rather than over it. It takes the long ascent the way any stranger would, feeling the air thin with every switchback, arriving winded at the plateau's edge the way the hamlet's rare visitors always do, so that whatever judgment it eventually makes will be made from inside the difficulty and not above it.

It watches a child learn to carry water without spilling, small hands tense with concentration, an old woman nearby not offering help, only watching, letting the lesson cost what it costs. It watches the unspoken rotation at the troughs—no argument, no negotiation, just bodies arriving in an order so old it has become a kind of grace. It watches a man repair three feet of wall his great-grandfather first set, matching the old stone's lean with a craftsman's unhurried care, as though continuing a sentence someone began speaking generations ago and has not yet finished. It watches someone leave a small sprig at the corner of a wall and walk on without ceremony, without explanation, the way you'd nod to a neighbor.

And slowly the power revises its first reading. It begins to see what is easy to miss from very far above: that hardship and suffering are not the same thing, and that this place, despite everything that is hard about it, is not suffering. There is no cruelty here, no despair worn smooth into resignation. There is, instead, an unforced gentleness—the specific gentleness of creatures who have made an old, working agreement with scarcity and have not been broken by it, but shaped by it, the way wind shapes stone without destroying it. The economy of their speech is not coldness. The smallness of their lives is not poverty. Lineage matters more than name because they have built meaning out of continuance itself—each act of carrying water, each repaired wall, each unspoken rotation at the trough a small renewal of a vow no one remembers swearing.

The power understands, then, what abundance would actually do here. It would not lift a burden. It would dissolve a structure. The rotations would have no reason to exist. The terraces, no longer rationed, would lose the quiet discipline that makes tending them a form of devotion rather than chore. The cistern, no longer precious, would stop being half-shrine. Generations of inherited care—care that exists only because care was once necessary—would have nothing left to be care about. The power realizes it has been on the verge of mistaking a flourishing equilibrium for a stalled one, mistaking the absence of expansion for the absence of life. This place is not failing to grow. It has simply finished arriving, and has spent its long life since then doing the harder, quieter work of staying.

So the power does not flood the cistern. It does not warm the winters. It does not make the soil generous or the air thick. It leaves the shape of the hardship exactly as it has always been, because the hardship is not separate from what it loves about this place—it is the loom the place is woven on.

But it does not leave with nothing done, either, because benevolence that changes nothing at all is sometimes only cowardice wearing wisdom's clothes. What it gives instead is small, and aimed not at comfort but at continuance.

It does not enlarge the spring. It only guards it—quietly, invisibly—against the kind of single catastrophe that could break a lineage that has otherwise weathered everything else: the freak years of total drought, the rockfall that could choke the channel for good, the sickness that could take a whole generation's children in one bad season. These the power turns aside, not as miracles anyone will notice, but as a long string of near-disasters that simply, inexplicably, do not quite happen. The hamlet will go on attributing its survival to its own discipline and to the plateau's old permission, and in a sense, this will not even be wrong, since the power's hand is folded inside the same permission they have always sensed and never explained.

It takes up, too, a quieter task that suits something outside of time better than it would suit anything inside it: it remembers what the place itself cannot. The cairns near the plateau's edge weather smooth eventually, the flat marker-stones losing their names to lichen and wind faster than any family can re-cut them. So the power becomes, without ever saying so, the keeper of what erodes—holding every name the stone eventually forgets, every life the rotations and rows and repaired walls eventually fold into the general, nameless continuance of the place. Nothing is lost twice. The stone may forget; the power doesn't.

And once—only once, and not as proof of anything, not as a gift that announces itself as a gift—it answers an offering. Some small sprig left at the corner of some particular wall, on some particular evening, is met not by an absence but by the faintest, deniable sense of having been received: a warmth that isn't from the failing sun, a moment when the wind seems, for just a breath, to hold rather than push. The person who leaves it will say nothing, because there is nothing sayable. They will only walk back down the lane a little less burdened than before, and if anyone notices a change in them at all, they will call it age, or grief settling, or simply a good night's rest. This is exactly as it should be. The power has not come to be believed in. It has only come to be, once, quietly true to the shape of a question this place has been asking for longer than anyone living can recall: does the plateau permit us to stay. The answer, given without ceremony, is yes. It always was.

Then the power withdraws, the way weather withdraws—not absent, only no longer announcing itself. It does not return often. It does not need to. It watches, across the long unhurried turning of generations, the same patient cycle continue: fog pooling and burning off in the lower lane, hands learning not to spill, walls outliving the men who set them, the trickle keeping its slow accustomed pulse. Nothing here is becoming anything else, and the power has decided, in the end, that this is not a problem to be solved but a rare, hard-won kind of completeness to be protected—and so it protects it, the way the dry-stacked walls protect what little soil there is: not by changing what's inside, but by holding, very quietly, against everything that might otherwise wear it away too soon.
