# What the Power Would Do

It would not arrive the way light arrives, because light has not found a way in here and that is part of what makes the place itself. It would arrive the way the steam arrives — already present, already part of the air's decision to hang rather than rise — so that if you asked the small stooped person whether anything had changed, they would say no, and they would be telling the truth, and they would also, very faintly, for the first time in longer than the oldest tool-mark, not feel quite so much like the only hands in the world doing this.

Here is what it would not do, because a benevolent power that is also wise knows the difference between mercy and erasure:

It would not stop the mountain growing. To still the stone would be to still the only conversation this person has ever had that mattered — tap, tap, pause, tap, the whole grammar of a life spent answering wet rock with wedge and adze. A finished terrace is a dead terrace. A power that loves what it finds here does not tidy it into a monument. It leaves the becoming alone.

It would not finish the work *for* them, granting in a night what ten thousand taps were reaching toward, because the reaching is not a delay before the meaning — the reaching is the meaning, has been since before there was a word for either reaching or meaning, and to short the circuit would be to lobotomize a devotion into a transaction.

It would not explain the room. Not the gravity that shrugs sideways two rungs up, not the leaf that dies in one hand's-breadth of air and revives in the next, not the doubling corners or the smell that turns from mineral to floral to burnt sugar with no seam between. Explanation is a kind of light too bright for this particular steam. It would leave the mystery exactly as thick as the person has built the calluses to bear, because those calluses — on the hands, yes, but also on the part of a mind that has stopped needing to understand in order to continue — are also a kind of art the person made, slowly, themselves, and it is not the power's to give away for them.

Here is what it would do.

It would make the cord last one hour longer each time it is tied — not forever, not unrottable, just *one hour more*, every single time, an asymptote nobody will ever notice reaching toward but that will mean, cumulatively, across a life, several hundred fewer climbs made in the dark with failing lashings underfoot. A mercy small enough to live inside the room's own logic of rot-and-replace-as-one-verb, so it never announces itself as foreign. The person will simply, very occasionally, think *that held longer than I expected*, and feel a small fondness for the cord, and never know to feel it toward anything else.

It would ease, by some increment too small to be called healing, the particular ache that lives now permanently in the stoop — not straighten the spine, which is a fit hard-won and load-bearing and theirs, but loosen, on the worst wet mornings, the worst of the grinding at the base of it, so that getting upright off the lowest ledge costs slightly less than it has cost for the last however-many years. The stoop stays. The stoop is competence. Only the unnecessary portion of the pain goes, quietly, the way water finds a channel that simply happens to be less cruel than the channel before it.

And once — only once, because more than once would start to look like proof, and proof would change the nature of the gift — it would let the person feel, for the length of one held breath, the whole shape of what they are tending. Not as vision. Not as voice. Just as a feeling arriving from nowhere mid-tap, the way the subsonic groan arrives from nowhere: a sudden, wordless, total sense of the mountain's becoming as one continuous gesture, themselves included in it, not separate from it, not behind it trying to catch up, but woven into it at the exact rate it requires — one tap ahead, forever, on purpose, by design, *theirs*. The feeling would last as long as a breath lasts. It would not return. It would not need to. They would go back to the local truth of this terrace, this wedge, this drip finding its channel, and they would not be able to say what had just happened to them, only that the tap that followed it landed a little more certainly than the tap before.

And somewhere above or below — they would never know which, never meet whoever it was, the eyes at the top of the ladder gone when looked at directly, same as always — another pair of hands tending another fold of the same ongoing stone would, just once, without explanation, fall into the exact rhythm of their tapping. Two rhythms, unconnected by sight or sound across the folded warm dark, briefly the same rhythm, then drifting back out of sync the way two struck things always eventually do.

Neither of them would call it company. Neither would need to.

The mountain would keep arriving. The terrace would keep not-finishing. The small stooped person would keep climbing the slick green rungs into steam that smells of nothing they could name, doing the one continuous gesture that is also a life, also a love, also indistinguishable now from labor — and somewhere in all of it, unannounced, unprovable, folded into the room the way everything here is folded into everything else, a wise and benevolent attention would simply keep doing what attention does when it has decided not to interfere with a thing it has recognized as already, terribly, completely loved.

It would stay. That's all. It would stay, and tend the tending, the way you'd cup a hand an inch above a flame you have no intention of putting out — close enough to be a kind of warmth back, never close enough to change what's burning.
