The power comes to the rim the way anything must come to the rim — slowly, because the elevation demands it, because the thinness of the air up there seems designed to make arrival a kind of penance, a thing earned rather than granted. It stands where you stood. It looks down into the bowl of green-dark noise and feels, for a while, nothing at all except the size of what it is looking at — which is to say, it feels the wrongness of its own first instinct, which is to measure.

Because the first instinct of any power looking down into a small, loud, struggling place is to ask what is missing. More light. More water. Less death. Less effort. A door, where before there was only the long patient work of cutting paths through vine-growth with stone. And the power turns this instinct over for a long time, the way the basin's people turn over a difficult question — not rushing to answer it, letting it sit until its shape becomes clear by itself.

What it eventually understands, watching long enough to stop being a stranger to the rhythm, is that everything it might be tempted to give would arrive as a kind of theft.

If it widened the basin, let in more light, thinned the press of green — the people would lose the gap-shaped lives they've spent generations building themselves into. Their posture, their listening, their whole inherited grammar of ducking and bowing and reading the wrongness in a tremble of leaves: useless, then. A people who fit themselves brilliantly to a hard place, suddenly living in an easy one that no longer asks anything of the bodies they were given.

If it silenced the noise — stilled the insects, thinned the birds, gave them quiet — it would be taking away the medium they think in, the acoustic intimacy that taught them to lean their heads together and speak mouth-to-ear, that made closeness an architecture instead of a choice. A quiet basin would not be a kindness to them. It would be a kind of solitude they never asked for, dressed up as mercy.

If it gave them ease — lifted the labor of re-lashing beams each rain-season, of training vines that would otherwise close every path, of holding the old negotiated truce with the green by continuous, unglamorous effort — it would dissolve the very thing the elders sing about. Their pride is not in having subdued the basin. It's in still being in relationship with it, generation after generation, hands still needed, the relay still running. Take away the need for the next pair of hands, and you've quietly ended the song mid-verse, replaced meaning with mere survival.

So the power does not widen, does not quiet, does not ease. It sits a long while with the discomfort of having so much capability and so little that it can rightly use. This, it understands, is most of what wisdom is: the holding of power very still, like a hand cupped near a flame, near enough to warm, far enough not to scorch.

But — and here is the "if anything" — it does not leave with empty hands either, because benevolence that only refrains is not yet love, only caution wearing love's clothes. So it looks for the narrowest possible door. Not a door into ease. A door into continuity.

It finds, three generations back and three generations forward, the threads that almost broke and never quite did — a fever season that nearly took every child born that year, a storm that nearly opened the canopy wide enough to let the basin's whole fragile dimness collapse into something the green couldn't recover from, a single elder, decades hence, who in the story's other branching dies before passing on the smoke-language to anyone, so that a thread of meaning the town didn't know it needed simply stops, mid-sentence, and is never picked back up. These are not hardships. Hardship is the basin's native weather, and the power leaves hardship alone, the way you'd leave a forest's understory alone, knowing the understory needs its losses to stay what it is. These are endings. Different in kind. A hardship can be sung about afterward. An ending leaves no one to sing.

So: the fever breaks one night, for no reason the healer can name, in a child who was already being readied for mourning. The storm, at its worst hour, slides four degrees off the line it was tracking, for no reason the wind can be asked to explain, and opens a different, less vital seam of canopy instead. The elder, on the night he meant to die quietly in his sleep with the smoke-language only half-taught to his apprentice, wakes instead, inexplicably, and lives eleven more months — long enough.

No one in the basin ever learns that anything was touched. There is no vision, no voice from the rim, no sign left to be puzzled over by the elders' fire. The power does not want to be found, because being found would turn into a story, and the story would turn into expectation, and expectation is its own kind of theft — the theft of the basin's hard-earned, unsentimental relationship with chance.

What it leaves instead — the only thing it allows itself to leave — is a single, unattributed line that begins turning up, after that season, in the work-songs sung while beams are re-lashed and roof-thatch replaced. No one remembers composing it. It doesn't scan quite like the older verses, doesn't carry a name the way the old call-and-response lines do, crediting some long-dead grandmother with the wit to have first sung them. It simply enters the rotation, the way a new bird sometimes enters a chorus and is accepted without comment because the chorus has room for one more voice.

The line says, more or less, in the basin's particular cadence: *the bowl is old, and so are we, and something old is glad of it.*

They sing it without asking what it means. It fits the rhythm of the lashing-work, the in-and-out pull of fiber over old growth-ringed beam, and that's reason enough to keep it. It costs them nothing to sing. It changes nothing about the rain-seasons, the insect-bands, the long unglamorous negotiation with the green that will need doing again next year and the year after that, exactly as before.

But sometimes, an old man pauses mid-knot, head bowed under an archway cleared head-high before he was born, and feels — without knowing why, without wanting an explanation, the way you feel a held breath in a room before realizing no one has spoken — held.

That is all the power does. It decides that is enough.
