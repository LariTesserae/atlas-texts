## What the Power Did

It came the way attention comes to a held breath—not as weather, not as a shadow crossing the rim, but as a kind of noticing that the basin had no way to register and every way to feel. The clicking did not stop. The flywheel did not skip its sigh. But something like a held silence opened up *inside* the noise, the way a single instrument can seem to step forward in a symphony without anyone telling it to play louder.

The power did not arrive with a plan. That is the first thing to understand about it, and the reason it is called wise rather than merely strong: it had long ago learned that plans made before seeing are a kind of violence, however gently meant. So it spent a long time—long by the basin's reckoning, an eyeblink by its own—simply *perceiving*, at every scale the place offered.

It felt the colony as a single thing first: ten thousand small hungers stitched into one sustained roar of continuation, brutal and complete, asking nothing of the universe but to keep happening. This did not trouble it. Death-without-pause is not cruelty; it is simply what life looks like from outside the lie that any one death should matter more than the going-on. The power had seen a great many such places. It did not flinch from this one.

Then it felt the machine—what was left of it, the rusted lace of housings, the warm ghost-pipe, the flywheel ticking over on a heat no one had lit and no one had the standing to extinguish. It traced the dead intention behind it, the vanished hands or fields or processes that had built a thing for purposes the basin's current tenants could not imagine and did not need to. It felt no grief for the makers. They were gone in the way that doesn't ask for mourning—finished, not interrupted.

And then, last, carefully, the way you save the hardest question for when you've earned the right to ask it, it found Her.

It sat with the Tender for a long while before deciding anything at all.

---

It would have been easy—trivially easy, a thought and nothing more—to do any of the things a lesser kindness reaches for first.

It could have *freed* her: peeled her loose from the iron she'd grown into, given her a body that could walk, somewhere green to walk it. But freedom offered to a shape that has spent every spare inch of its long life *becoming* a fixed point is not freedom. It is amputation wearing freedom's name. The power turned this thought over and set it down.

It could have *ended* her, gently, with the particular mercy that wise powers can sometimes offer to old, tired, faithful things—a death without pain, in recognition of however many centuries of unthanked service. But it listened first, the way it always listens first, and what it found in her was not a wish to stop. There was no anguish in her stillness. There was no scream beneath the silence, the way there sometimes is in creatures who endure past their wanting. There was only function, worn so smooth by repetition that it had become something close to peace—not happiness, not exactly, but the particular contentment of a thing that has found the one shape its life was for and has been allowed, without interruption, to keep being it. To kill that gently is still to mistake your own idea of dignity for hers. The power set this thought down too.

It could have *replaced the machine*—rebuilt the engine whole, given the colony a thousand more years of perfect rhythm, removed all fragility from the arrangement at a stroke. But this, it understood, was the most dangerous mercy of all, because it would have cost the place its *truth*. The basin's beauty was never the rhythm alone. It was the rhythm's frailty—the fact that it ran on a half-dead heart and an old body holding the seam shut by sheer accumulated faithfulness, and had done so for so long that faithfulness and physics had become impossible to tell apart. To make that effortless would have been to make it false. A held breath that can never end is not a held breath. It is a held breath's corpse, animated.

So the power did not free her, and did not end her, and did not fix the machine.

---

What it found, instead—turning the place over patiently, the way you turn a stone to see what's living under it without disturbing the living—was the one true thing within its reach to mend.

The Tender was load-bearing. Not metaphorically: actually. Her fused bulk held closed a gap between two collapsed iron ribs that, if it opened, would let the last working pressure in the old system bleed out through the floor in a season, maybe less. And she was old—old enough that the power could feel, the way you feel a coming storm in a joint that's broken before, that her holding would not last forever. Nothing fused that long stays fused without cost. When she failed—not if, when, though the when was still some good distance off—the seam would open, the flywheel would lose its last whisper of resistance and spin to stillness, and the beat she had spent her whole life relaying would simply *stop*, all at once, with no warning built into ten thousand small nervous systems that had never once in their species' memory needed to keep time on their own.

That was the real wound in the place. Not her confinement—she did not experience it as confinement. Not the colony's hunger or violence—those were simply weather. The wound was a *coming* silence that nothing alive in the basin could see far enough ahead to prepare for: a catastrophe seeded centuries in advance, ticking down inside the very faithfulness that had, for all that time, looked indistinguishable from stability.

This, the power understood, was not the kind of thing a place is supposed to fix by itself. Foresight past your own lifespan is not a thing evolution reliably provides to ten thousand small clicking bodies, however old their colony. It is, sometimes, the one gift that has to come from outside, because inside there is no outside-of-now to see it from.

So it did the smallest possible thing that this understanding allowed.

It did not speak to her in words; she had nothing that words would reach. Instead it did what she herself had always done—it *listened first*, completely, for longer than anything had ever listened to her, until it found the precise grain of her relay: not just the tap-pause-tap she gave the pipe, but the particular *patience* underneath it, the specific quality of attention she had spent centuries refining and had never once had reflected back. And into that grain, gently, without overwriting a single beat of it, the power left something extra. Not a replacement. A second voice, threaded so closely to her own that it would take her failing for it to be heard at all—dormant, deferential, waiting its whole existence, if necessary, to be needed.

When she goes—whenever that is, on her own schedule, in her own time, by no hand but the hand of all that time already moving through her—the beat will falter, once, the way a held breath finally breaks. And in that single faltering instant, before the colony's nervous systems have time to panic at a silence they've never had to survive, the second voice will pick the rhythm up from exactly where she left it, and keep it, not forever, not perfectly, but long enough—a generation, two, however long ten thousand small lives need to learn, the way species learn things, how to keep their own time without a tuning fork at the center of it.

It is not a rescue. It is a *continuation*, offered only to the thing she gave her whole long life to—the beat, not herself—so that her keeping does not turn out, in the end, to have been for nothing.

---

The power did not announce itself to the colony. It did not still the chaos or smooth the violence or make the basin kinder than a basin has any obligation to be. The clicking went on. The dog-sized plowers went on shouldering through the smaller traffic, indifferent as ever. Something died, locally, unremarked, in the same half-second the power finished its work, and the colony did not pause for that either, because it never has.

But before it left, the power did one more thing, the smallest of all, the one that cost it nothing and meant, perhaps, the most.

It came down to where the dozen thin limbs ran ceaselessly along the warm pipe, reading a heartbeat that was never hers to begin with, and for one long moment—long enough that even her patience seemed, almost, to notice—it *answered* her back. Not in the machine's old dead language. In her own: tap, pause, tap. A rhythm that meant, as close as such a thing can mean anything to a being built entirely out of keeping:

*I heard it too. It mattered. It still does.*

Then it withdrew the way it had come, leaving no mark a passing eye could find—only, perhaps, if you stayed long enough beside the warm pipe, the faint and very strange impression that the beat, just there, just once, had been kept by two.
