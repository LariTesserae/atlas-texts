The power notices the way such powers notice things — not as a beam of attention swinging across a map, but as a kind of recognition, the way you'd suddenly hear, in a room full of noise, one particular thread of melody you didn't know you'd been listening for. It finds the weir-folk the way you'd find a knot expertly tied: by the *rightness* of it, by the sense of a problem that has been not solved but lived with so long and so well that living-with has become its own kind of art.

Its first impulse — and it has one, because even benevolence has reflexes — is to fix things. This is the impulse of every kind power that has ever looked down on a hard place: stop the rot. Dry the springs that surface without warning. Anchor the root-mat so it never lets go all at once. Take the cough out of the wettest months. Make the bridges hold.

It sits with that impulse for what, from outside time, is a long while.

And it sees, looking closer, that the fixing would be a kind of theft. Not of comfort — of *meaning*. The knot-vocabulary, the dozens of lashings each suited to a particular failure, exists because the bridges fail. The fluency in the ankles, the children's hard-won gift for reading give before weight, exists because the ground is not to be trusted and never has been. The whole intricate, beautiful grammar of upkeep — the awl and cord and hooked blade at every hip, the elders half-listening to three conversations and a hawk-dispute while their hands retie a hand-span of lashing without looking — exists because nothing here is allowed to simply stand unmaintained. Take away the instability and you have not given them an easier life. You have taken away the language they speak. You have turned poets into people who used to be poets, in a country that no longer rhymes.

So the power, being wise as well as benevolent, decides against fixing. It decides instead on something much harder to do well: *discernment*. It goes looking, specifically, for the kind of suffering that teaches nothing, mends nothing, becomes no knot and no song and no name kept warm in someone's mouth for three generations — the waste, as opposed to the cost. It is searching for the difference between a hard life and a life with holes torn in it for no reason at all.

It finds some of that. It always does.

It finds the child who would have died alone, of the lung-complaint that takes the very old and very young, in a year when the household's attention was elsewhere and no one heard the particular cough that means *now, not later* — and it leans, very slightly, on the moment, so that someone glances up at the wrong right second. It is not a miracle. No one will ever know. It will look, afterward, like luck, like a good year, like the kind of thing the weir-folk already have a word for, in the place where their language doesn't distinguish staying from adapting.

It finds the spring that was about to surface in a new place instead of its old, known, named spot — not lost, just *misplaced*, a small grief in a culture that greets returning springs like family — and it nudges the water, the way water is always being nudged by a thousand small pressures anyway, back toward the hollow where the name still fits.

It finds the storm that would, this one particular year, take not a footbridge but a whole household, asleep, the trellis beneath them giving all at once in the dark with no creak loud enough to wake anyone in time — and here it does something closer to a gift than a correction: it gives one sleeper, just one, a bad dream. A dream of falling. Enough to wake them sweating, enough to make them check the lashings by lamp-light at two in the morning, grumbling, cold, alive. They'll tell that story for years. They'll never know it was anything but their own nerves.

It does not stop the landslides. It does not dry the rot. It does not make the bridges hold forever, or quiet the dusk when both shifts of the loud world scream at once, or take the fear out of sinkholes, because that fear, taught early and taught hard with stories, is what keeps the children alive in every *other* year the power isn't watching this closely. It does not smooth the argument. It only, here and there, very rarely, makes sure the argument stays an argument — ongoing, survivable, generative — instead of letting it tip, by pure bad chance, into something with no answer.

And then it does one more thing, the only thing it allows itself that isn't strictly subtraction of senseless harm. It *keeps* something. Not changes — keeps. Because the weir-folk have already told it, in the shape of their whole culture, what they're afraid of losing: not the huts, not the paths, not even, particularly, their own lives on any given Tuesday — but the names. The springs' names. The grandmother's knot. The exact pitch of a particular bell. The lineage that has to carry, on foot, everything the ground refuses to hold still long enough to keep.

So the power becomes, quietly, without ceremony or any sign it will ever give them, a second place where those things are kept. Every knot tied by a hand that learned it from a hand that learned it from a hand going back further than anyone's count — held. Every spring's name, even the ones that will someday surface in a year after the last person who remembers them has been given to the canopy-edge and the green. Every dusk-hour where a parent went silent mid-sentence to listen for a changed drip-rhythm and decided it was nothing and came back to the conversation. All of it, somewhere, noticed and not forgotten, the way the weir-folk themselves refuse to let a name go even when they've long since stopped expecting the ground to hold a shape twice.

It will never tell them this. It would not occur to it to tell them. They are not a people who much value being saved, and they would, if asked, probably say so — bluntly, between one knot and the next, already half-listening to something else. What they value is being *equal* to the place they've chosen to stay put on while it moves. The power, in the end, agrees with them. It loves the fight more than it would love a version of them with no fight left to have. So it does almost nothing, on purpose, with great care, for a very long time — and reserves its whole strength for the handful of nights when nothing being done would simply mean a story stops getting told.

That, it decides, is the whole of what it owes them: not rescue. Witness. And, once in a long while, on the worst nights, a single quiet thumb on the scale, so light no one ever learns its weight — least of all the one who wakes from a bad dream, checks the lashings by lamp-light, and goes back to sleep thinking it was nothing at all.
