The attention arrives the way attention arrives in a place like this — not as light, not as voice, but as a kind of pressure-change, the way the shelves themselves seem to register a storm three valleys off before any cloud is visible. The power does not announce itself. It would not know how, here, without violating the first thing it learns about Anvirra Tor: that nothing here is announced. Things accumulate. Things are noticed only once they're already old.

So it does what the wall-menders do. It watches before it touches.

It spends what might be, elsewhere, a hundred years simply attending — to the bell-rhythms, to the four words a cable-tender allows herself across an hour's ascent, to the particular hush a household keeps for its private grief and the particular pitch it raises for calling a child in from the rain. It learns to read palms the way the Tor-folk do — soot, peat, chalk — and understands, slowly, that this is a people who have made an entire ethics out of *not being in a hurry*, and that this ethic is not a limitation they've resigned themselves to but a wisdom they have actually, fully, achieved. They have solved a problem most other beings never even formulate: how to live well inside a mystery without insisting that the mystery resolve.

This humbles the power, somewhat. It came, as such powers often come, half-prepared to *fix* something. It finds, instead, very little that wants fixing, and a great deal that would be ruined by the attempt.

It considers, and discards, the obvious gestures.

It could explain the deep shafts — the older brick, the older iron, the tools no living smith recognizes. It decides not to. An explained mystery is a closed door; an unexplained one is a room the whole culture gets to keep living in, generation after generation, each one allowed to not-need the answer in their own way. To resolve it would be a kind of theft.

It could regularize the funicular schedule, smooth the inconsistent hour-that-becomes-three. It decides not to. The inconsistency isn't a flaw in the place; the place *is*, in some sense, the inconsistency, the same way a person is partly made of the things about themselves they've never bothered to explain. Forcing the shelves into punctuality would be like forcing a slow river straight. Something would survive it. It would not be the river.

It could reach the large unseen thing moving on the lower shelves, the one heard and never met, and resolve *that*, too — name it, tame it, bring it into the light for the comfort of the curious. It decides, again, not to. Some loneliness is structural, load-bearing, the way the retaining walls are load-bearing; remove it and something upslope may come down.

So it narrows its attention, the way wisdom typically must, from the whole vast tired beautiful shelf-stack down to the size of an actual hand.

There is, on one of the middle terraces, an old wall-mender, chalk to the wrist, who has spent this whole season failing to find the leak in a retaining wall above the fern-bank — a slow seep undermining the soil-seam behind it, the kind of thing that, left two more winters, becomes a collapsed shelf, a lost thicket, maybe a lost house below. She has walked the wall forty times. She has not found it. She is old enough to know this might simply be the season she fails, the wall her legacy doesn't quite hold, and she has begun, very quietly, grieving that in advance, the way Tor-folk grieve things — without announcement, in a murmur pitched for no one but herself.

The power does not show her the leak.

It does something smaller and, it judges, kinder: it makes her, for one morning only, *very slightly more patient than usual* — patient enough to walk the wall a forty-first time at the hour when the light comes low and sidelong across the stone, the hour she'd usually have given up by. In that light, the seep-stain she's walked past forty times finally throws a shadow she can read. She finds it before midday. She tells no one how. She doesn't experience this as miracle. She experiences it as the wall finally agreeing to be honest with her, which is, in fact, exactly what happened, mediated through a single morning's borrowed patience that she will never know wasn't entirely her own.

There is a bell, in a foundry three shelves down, worn thin at the lip from generations of the same hammer-fall, due to crack within the decade and silence a shift-change the whole valley has organized its days around for longer than anyone alive remembers. The power does not replace the bell, does not strengthen the metal by some impossible fiat that any foundry-family would, on some unconscious level, *feel* as wrong, as out of true with how metal is supposed to fail. It simply nudges, by some increment too small to register as anything but luck, the angle at which the hammer has been falling for six generations, so that the wear redistributes a half-degree differently than it would have. The bell will still crack, eventually. Just not on anyone's watch who is alive to grieve it. It will be some future generation's problem, dealt with by some future smith, in the unhurried way these things are always dealt with here. This, the power judges, is the correct shape of mercy in a place that worships increments: not removal of the wound, but a redistribution of its weight across more shoulders, more centuries, until no single one of them has to bear it all at once.

There is a foundry-child — there is, in fact, in any given year, always such a child — sleeping lightly enough to wake for the one irregularity that means *the engine needs tending*, and exhausted by it, and afraid to say so, because the whole weight of six generations of belonging is wrapped up in being the kind of child who wakes for that sound. The power does not relieve her of the listening. It does not have the right; the listening is hers, inherited, real, and to lift it from her would be to lift her out of her own lineage. It does, instead, something almost embarrassingly small: it ensures, for one particular night when she is at the very edge of what she can carry, that the engine simply does not need tending. That the irregularity does not occur. That she gets, once, the whole unbroken night, and wakes not to crisis but to ordinary grey morning light through cistern-fog, and feels — without being able to say why — slightly more able to go on than she did the evening before.

That is, in the end, very nearly all of it.

The power does not stay to be thanked, because there is no one to thank it, because nothing it did will ever be legible as anything other than an unusually patient hour, a hammer that happened to fall a half-degree wrong, a quiet night for one tired child. It withdraws the way the mist withdraws from streets it isn't allowed into — without comment, without residue, leaving the place to go on believing, correctly, that nothing unusual happened, that the wall held because she finally found the leak, that the bell still rings because bells, in Anvirra Tor, are built to outlast their builders, that the child slept well because children sometimes do.

It leaves no monument. A monument would be the one thing this place, with its religion of moss and grain-by-grain soil and graves grown indistinguishable from the rock, would find slightly embarrassing — too sudden, too announced, the wrong shape entirely for how this mountain understands a gift.

If it left anything at all, it would be this: one new, unremarkable, perfectly ordinary fact, added so gently to the shelf's long accumulation that even the moss won't be able to say, in a hundred years, exactly when it arrived — only that it's there now, the way everything good here arrives: slowly, low to the ground, already half-believed to have always been.
