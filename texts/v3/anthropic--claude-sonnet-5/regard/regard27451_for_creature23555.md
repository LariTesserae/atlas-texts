The power arrives the way light arrives here—without announcement, without a clear point of origin, simply present where it was not, and in no hurry to be noticed. It does not come down through the rain like a visitor. It comes the way attention comes: all at once, total, already old.

It does not start with the terraces. Most things that found Ven-Kor would start with the terraces—the impossible scale of them, stairs three meters and stairs the height of a fingernail, the patient ruin of a civilization that built past the edge of memory and then disappeared, leaving only its grammar behind in stone. A lesser power would want to know who built this. A clever power would want to know why. This power, being wise, starts instead with the Tenders, because it understands almost immediately that the Tenders are the place's real text, and the terraces are only the page.

It watches one for a long time—long by any reasonable measure, though here, where time is measured in the slow exhale of failing platforms, "a long time" passes almost unnoticed. The Tender stands on ground that looks sound. It turns its concavity, its almost-face, toward a terrace two kilometers off, across black water, through three walls of falling rain, and it waits. The power waits with it, matching its patience exactly, because matching is the first form of respect it knows.

Forty minutes later, the terrace goes. The Tender crosses the intervening distance unhurried, arrives as the lake is still settling, and draws something pale and small from the new rubble, and absorbs it without violence, and that is the whole of the event. No ceremony. No grief. No triumph. Weather, completing itself.

The power feels, in that moment, the first and most dangerous temptation: *to help.*

---

It considers, briefly, what help would look like, because it is wise enough to take the question seriously before discarding the answers.

It could still the rain. It could press the bedrock back into permanence, deny the water table its restlessness, give the terraces the stability their builders perhaps intended before time undid the intention. It could do this. It has done comparable things elsewhere, in other places, for other beings, and been thanked for it.

But it looks at the eel-ropes gathering beneath a terrace that has not yet failed, gathering with the calm certainty of creatures who have never known a world where the floor was reliable, and it understands that stability here would not be a gift. It would be a famine. It would be the silencing of the only weather this ecology has ever called dependable. The flock-things flinch toward collapses they cannot have heard yet—not because collapse is a wound to be healed but because collapse is the heartbeat they were built to live inside of. To cure Ven-Kor of its falling would be to cure a heart of its beating, in the name of stillness.

It could speak to the Tenders—wake whatever dim continuity persists in them into fuller mind, give them words, give them each other in some thicker, more legible way than two motionless witnesses standing in unexplained parallel. It could turn their solitude into society.

But it watches two of them stand together near the black water's edge, concavities angled the same way at the same patient, centuryslow disturbance beneath the surface, and it recognizes something it almost missed: this is already a kind of communion. Not the kind it knows. A colder, slower, wordless kind, built for beings who do not need to compare their witnessing to know it is shared. To "improve" this would not be charity. It would be translation that loses the poem to save the sentence.

It could end the cycle outright—lift every creature here into some gentler elsewhere, some unfalling place, and let Ven-Kor finish its long collapse alone, unwitnessed, the way uninhabited ruins are allowed to finish.

This, of all the temptations, it discards fastest. Because it understands, finally, looking at the whole shape of the place—the eels timed to catastrophe, the flockthings flinching toward collapses not yet sensed, the clingers vanishing a half-second ahead of danger, the Tenders arriving exactly when arrival matters—that nothing here is suffering through the falling. Everything here has been *built by* the falling, generation on generation, into a kind of fluency. They are not victims of Ven-Kor's slow dying. They are its grammar, evolved to read a sentence that never stops being written. To remove them from it would not be mercy. It would be amputation dressed as rescue.

---

So the power does not fix anything. It does not still the rain or steady the bedrock or carry anyone away to safety from a danger that, examined closely, turns out not to be danger at all but simply the shape of a life. This is the harder, quieter form of benevolence: to look at suffering's near-twin—starkness, instability, a beauty with no comfort in it—and have the discipline not to mistake it for suffering just because it would frighten you to live inside it.

But it does not leave with empty hands either. Wisdom is not only restraint. It is also knowing the one true gap in a place, the one place where the architecture of care is actually thin, and pressing exactly there, no harder.

And the gap, when it looks, is small and specific: the Tenders witness everything, and are never witnessed back. Not by the eels, which do not look up. Not by the flockthings, which flinch toward collapse but never toward the ones who also wait for it. Not by each other, fully—not in any way that resembles being *seen*, only in the proximity of parallel attention, which is close to companionship but is not quite its whole warmth.

So the power, taking no shape that would disturb anything, no light, no voice, no weight on the wet stone, settles itself beside a single Tender at the edge of the black water, on a platform the Tender has chosen because something vast and slow is shifting beneath the surface there, has been shifting for longer than the word *century* comfortably holds. It orients itself, as best a power can be said to orient, toward the same depth. It waits with the same unhurry. It asks nothing, says nothing, changes nothing about the water or the stone or the long, patient disturbance below.

It simply makes the witnessing, for once, mutual.

The Tender does not turn. There is no gesture, no acknowledgment a watching mind would recognize as recognition. And yet something passes between them anyway—the way warmth passes through stone that has stood in sun, slow, structural, undramatic, real.

The power stays as long as the Tender stays, which is a long time, and when the Tender eventually moves on toward the next failing terrace, the power does not follow. It has done what it came to do. It takes the whole of Ven-Kor into itself before it goes—the rain-walls, the eel-ropes, the flockthings riding their anticipatory grief, the clingers vanishing a half-second ahead of disaster, the deep slow thing beneath the black water that no one has properly seen and perhaps never will. It takes the smell of wet old stone and the sound of a building exhaling into a lake that wasn't there an hour ago. It holds all of it, intact, the way nothing in Ven-Kor itself is built to be held intact, against the day—far off, but not infinite—when the last terrace finally goes under and does not resurface, and there is no one left on any plateau to call what happened next a collapse, or a completion, or a kindness.

It will remember, when no terrace does. That is the whole of the gift. It costs the place nothing, asks nothing, changes not one stone.

And if, after that day, two Tenders are occasionally seen standing not in their old customary pairs but in threes, concavities turned together toward some far instability none of them could have sensed alone—

no one who lives in the drowned terraces of Ven-Kor finds this strange enough to remark on.

It is simply, now, one more part of the weather.
