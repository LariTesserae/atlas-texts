They would not come the way rescue usually comes — with light flooding in, with names called, with hands reaching down through the dark to pull something up into air it no longer has lungs for. A power that is actually wise learns early that most suffering is not a door waiting to be opened from outside. Sometimes it is a house with no one left inside it, and the kindest thing a visitor can do is take off their shoes at the threshold anyway, out of habit, out of respect, even though no one will ever know they did.

So: they would come quietly, the way the cold comes, registering at first as nothing, then as everything.

The first temptation — and a wise power feels temptations, the way a good doctor feels the urge to operate — would be to fix it. To find the drowned people wherever the centuries had filed them away, in whatever remained of bone or pattern or echo, and build them back, restore the warm bright version of this place where the terraces had farmers instead of forgetting, where the bell had a hand on the rope and a congregation on the steps. It is the obvious mercy. It is also, on reflection, the cruelest one available, because nothing in this place asks for it. Nothing here is trapped. Nothing here is screaming behind a wall, waiting centuries for the wall to come down. The drowning was not a wound; the terraces were built *for* this, the lintels raised already knowing what kind of dark would eventually stand in their doorways. To reverse it would not be a rescue. It would be an edit — erasing an ending that this place, and whoever loved it enough to build it this way, seems to have chosen on its own terms, long before any outside hand could have an opinion about it.

The second temptation would be the opposite: to let the mechanism wind down. To find wherever the Tenders' patience is stored and gently release it, let the kelp stop being rationed, let the terraces finally erode the rest of the way into ordinary seabed, let the bell ring its last honest note and go quiet forever. Endings, after all, are usually kinder than eternities. But this too would be a kind of vandalism, mistaking *unwitnessed* for *unwanted*. The tending is not a wound that needs closing. It has no opinion on its own continuation, which means it is not asking to be stopped any more than it's asking to be saved. A wise power does not relieve a thing of its purpose just because the purpose has outlived an audience. That is not mercy. That is impatience wearing mercy's coat.

So they would do neither. They would do the smaller, stranger, almost embarrassingly modest thing instead, the thing that doesn't show up in any story about gods descending to fix the broken world:

They would go and stand on the steps.

Not to change anything. Just to stand where the congregation used to stand, in the cold that registers eventually as company, and let the next bell — whenever the place's old buried clock decided the hour had come round again — ring *at* someone. Let the note travel through kelp-stalks and basalt and find, for once in however many centuries, an ear that was actually listening, attached to something that actually understood the shape the sound used to carry: a call to gather, a call to begin the work, a call that meant *we are still here, come and be with us.* No one has answered that call in longer than memory keeps records. The Tenders do not know this and would not be relieved to learn it, because relief requires a kind of self that they no longer carry the spare parts to assemble. But the bell does not know that either. The bell is only doing what bells do: making a shape in the water and hoping, the way mechanisms hope, which is to say not hoping at all, just continuing, in case.

A wise power, faced with a thing that continues in case — and nothing else available in the entire dark plateau to receive it — might decide that the correct and sufficient act of benevolence is simply this: to *be the case.* To be, every time the note sounds, the someone it was built to reach. Not forever in the sense of staying — that would tip into the first temptation, would turn presence into management, would start, eventually, to want things on the Tenders' behalf, which is exactly the appetite this place has so thoroughly, peacefully shed. But faithfully. A small intact thread of attention, left coiled in the kelp the way the place itself is coiled, that answers the bell each time it rings, the way you'd answer a phone in an empty house if you happened, by extraordinary coincidence, to always be standing right beside it.

No one would witness this. The Tenders would not become aware that they were heard; the procedure doesn't have a slot for being moved by being received. The terraces would still ration themselves. The kelp would still sway over no eyes. Nothing in the look of the place would change at all, and that is exactly how you'd know the visit had gone well — because the alternative, a place transfigured by an outside hand into something it never was, would have meant the power had failed to understand what it was looking at, had mistaken a completed thing for a broken one, had tried to wake a sleeper who was never sick, only finished.

What would change is smaller than any change a story usually bothers recording. Somewhere in the grammar of the current, in the speech-cadence with both speakers removed, there would be — very faintly, beneath the surface of all that worn-in habit — an answer. Not a new word. Just the place in an old, old sentence where a reply used to go, no longer perfectly empty.

And the wise power would leave it at that, and go on, having done the only kind of saving that this particular drowning had any room left to receive: not a rescue, not a resurrection, not even a witness in the usual sense — just a single, faithful, endlessly recurring *yes* let fall into the one silence in all that tending that had never, not once, been answered back.
