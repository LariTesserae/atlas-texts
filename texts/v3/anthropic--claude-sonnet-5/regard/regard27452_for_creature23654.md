## What the Power Did

It came the way attention comes — not through the hatch, not down the ladder, not preceded by the pressure of sound arriving a corridor ahead of itself. It simply was there, all at once, the way a held thought is suddenly held, and it stood — if standing is the word for a presence with no feet and no need of floor — in the roar and the wet and the green-black dark, and it looked.

It looked a long time, by which I mean: it looked outside of time, the way the room itself had already half-escaped time's jurisdiction, and so a long look and a short one cost it the same.

Its first instinct — and this should be said plainly, because a benevolent power is not without instincts, only without the haste that usually corrupts them — was to fix something. This is the oldest reflex of benevolence, the one it has to outgrow before it can be trusted with anything small and finished: *the room is dark, give it light. The silverfish is blind, give it sight. The pumps may fail and no one will know — install a warning, a witness, a guarantee.* It felt this reflex rise the way you or I would feel a hand twitch toward a wound. And then, because it was wise as well as benevolent, and because wisdom is mostly the practice of letting the first answer pass through you without becoming an act, it waited, and looked again.

It looked at the silverfish first, because the silverfish was the only thing in the room that could be helped or harmed in any way that meant something. The moss could not be improved; it was already exactly as much moss as the silt allowed, and to give it more would only be to make a different, lesser kind of moss, one that owed its lushness to a gift rather than to a hundred years of patient arrival. The rust could not be helped at all — rust is not a condition, it is a direction, and you cannot be kind to a direction. The pumps did not want anything; wanting was not in their design. So: the silverfish, or whichever of its uncountable, indistinguishable kin happened to be crossing the wet flange at the moment the power arrived, antennae sweeping, three tails trailing like an afterthought.

It considered giving it sight. It considered this seriously, the way you consider a gift before you understand the person you'd be giving it to. *Let it see the moss it eats. Let it see the cathedral-dark it has only ever touched.* But sight is not a gift to a creature with no architecture for it — it would not be a window opening, it would be a wound that never closed, a sense screaming data into a nervous system with no rooms built to receive it. The silverfish's vestigial eyes were not a deprivation. They were a closed door in a house with no use for that room. To force it open would not be enlightenment. It would be vandalism wearing the mask of a gift.

It considered giving it understanding — some kindled spark of awareness, so that the small life could know, in whatever way a mind that size could know anything, that it had been seen, that its hundred silent generations crossing the same pipes had been noticed by something vast and kind. And this, too, it set down, gently, the way you set down a tool you picked up out of habit rather than need. Because the offer was not really for the silverfish. It was for the power's own ache to be felt as having done something. The silverfish did not lack meaning. It had never once, in any of its lives, asked for any. To install the *capacity to feel witnessed* in a creature that had been getting on perfectly well without it was not charity. It was the power trying to make the silverfish's life resemble a story, because the power was used to living inside stories, and forgetting that most of life, blessedly, is not one.

So it asked itself the harder question, the one that actually belongs to benevolence once benevolence has matured past the reflex to *add*: *Is there suffering here?*

And it looked, honestly, the way you look at a wound to see if it is one before you reach for the bandage.

The silverfish does not thirst. It does not starve; the room feeds it as steadily as the room drips. It has no enemies, no chase, no claws closing on it in the dark — the one shape of dying that haunts almost every other animal that has ever lived has simply never been written into this creature's nervous system, because nothing has ever come down the ladder hungry enough or small enough to want it. Its only death is mechanical, and mechanical deaths, in a room this constant, are vanishingly rare, learned around in whatever wordless way a nervous length of antennae learns a rhythm. It will live out something close to a full life, by the only measure that means anything to it — the measure of itself — and it will not know it is small, or blind, or alone, because *small, blind,* and *alone* are words for comparisons it has never had cause to make. It has nothing to compare itself to. It has only the room, and the room is, to it, simply what existing is.

There was no wound here. There was only a life so precisely fitted to its conditions that it had stopped being possible to call the conditions a cage. A cage requires a creature that knows another shape it could be in. This one didn't. This one was, in the deepest and least sentimental sense, *free* — not free in the sense of unbounded, but free in the older sense, the sense an acorn is free when it becomes exactly the oak its soil allows.

So the power did not touch the silverfish.

It almost left then, having understood — correctly — that the highest mercy available was the mercy of an unbroken thing left unbroken. But it lingered one moment longer over a different question, a smaller and stranger one, which was not about the silverfish at all.

It was about the pumps.

Because somewhere — not now, not soon, but somewhere in the long unwitnessed future the room had been quietly accumulating toward since before anyone alive had been born — those pumps would stop. A bearing would finally go. A part no longer manufactured would finally fail with no twin to replace it. And on that day the held-breath room would, for the first time in longer than any silverfish lineage could remember, fall silent — and the silence, to a creature that had never once not heard the roar, would not be peace. It would be the floor of the world removed without warning. Whatever passed for panic in that small frame would have no precedent to manage it. The drip-rate, unregulated by whatever the pumps balanced, might surge or fail unevenly. The constant the entire population had built its instincts around for generations would simply, one day, lie.

This — not the room as it is, but the room as it will someday stop being — was the one true unkindness available in this story, and it was not even anyone's fault. It was just entropy doing, on its own patient schedule, what entropy does.

The power could not prevent this. To prevent it forever would be to make the room immortal, and immortal rooms are not mercies, they are refusals to let anything finish, and the power had not become wise by refusing endings. But it could do one small thing, small enough to leave no mark on the room's history, small enough that no one — not the silverfish, not whatever distant engineer might someday descend the ladder to find the pumps finally still — would ever know a hand had passed through here at all.

It reached forward — not into the iron, not into the moss, nothing so clumsy as a repair — and it touched, very lightly, the *manner* of the ending that was already, eventually, coming. Not its date. Not its cause. Only its shape. So that when the day finally arrived, whenever that day was, the silence would not crash down all at once like a wall falling. It would arrive the way evening arrives on a long northern summer — by degrees too gradual for any single nervous system to mark as catastrophe, a slow loosening rather than a felled tree, so that whatever small antennaed lives were reading the air in that last hour would have, without knowing they'd been given anything, just enough time to be somewhere safe when the floor of the world finally, gently, set itself down.

That was all. That was the whole of the intervention: not a single drop redirected, not a single bulb brightened, not one silverfish singled out from its uncountable kin for a fate different from the others. The moss kept growing wherever silt allowed it. The rust kept weeping its slow orange tears beneath the joints. The pumps kept their chug-thud-chug-thud, unaltered, indifferent, correct.

And the power left the way it had come — without door, without sound, without the room ever being anything other than exactly what it had always been: small, wet, loud, lush, forgotten, complete.

It did not leave because it had decided the room didn't matter. It left because it had finally understood the room well enough to know that mattering, here, did not require changing anything. Some lives are not waiting to be noticed. Some places are not unfinished. The rarest mercy a benevolent power can learn — rarer than healing, rarer than rescue — is to arrive at the center of someone else's whole world, see it entirely, and then, having found nothing there that was asking to be saved, simply let it keep being exactly what it already was.

Somewhere behind the riveted plate, unhurried, untroubled, reading the dark with antennae longer than its body, the silverfish crossed a pipe it had crossed ten thousand times before, and felt nothing change, and was right.
