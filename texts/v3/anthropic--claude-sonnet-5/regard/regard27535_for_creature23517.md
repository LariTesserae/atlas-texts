The attention arrives the way everything else arrives here: without a sound that announces itself as new.

It does not come as light. That is the first thing it decides, before it decides anything else — that light would not be a gift here but a kind of violence, a forcing of a grammar onto people who have spent longer than their own memory unlearning the need for one. To pour light into the Husked Town would be to make visible, all at once, every shoulder that has ever gone rigid in a corridor and then simply not been there anymore. It would turn centuries of discipline into nakedness in a single instant. Whatever this power is, it is wise enough to know that rescue performed in the rescuer's own language is not rescue. It is conquest wearing rescue's coat.

So it sits with the town first. Not above it, not around it — it has no shape that would make those words mean anything — but inside its register, the way the townspeople themselves have learned to live inside the register of the dying thicket, pitching their voices to its groan until voice and groan are the same sound. The power listens the way they listen: with the whole surface of itself, for a very long time, to the creak two streets over that arrives at the ear sounding close enough to touch, to the long low moan of air finding a reed-throat in some hollow trunk, to the small held silences that are not the same as the wall's silence, though only barely.

It learns what the town is. Not a ruin. Not a tragedy in the cheap sense, a thing merely to be pitied and overwritten. A civilization that took the single available fact — total scarcity, total dark — and built from it doors, and thresholds, and courtesy, and a way of setting a tool down at exactly the angle a hand would want it. There is more dignity here than in a hundred sunlit cities, and the power knows enough to feel something like reverence before it feels anything like pity, and to let the reverence come first, and stay first, governing whatever comes after.

But it also sees the slope. The wall slumping its finger's width per generation. The children spaced out across years the way surplus dust is spaced out across a thin season. The population, unclear — not because anyone is hiding a thriving multitude, but because there may not be a multitude left to hide. This is not a culture in equilibrium with its scarcity. It is a culture in very slow, very dignified freefall, falling so gradually that it has had time to build an entire aesthetic out of the falling, mistaking the fall, perhaps, for stillness, the way a stone sinking through deep enough water can look, for a long while, like a stone simply resting.

To do nothing, here, would not be respect. It would be the comfortable mistake of confusing patience with mercy. The power understands this distinction the way it understands the distinction between light and dust: clearly, and as a thing that matters more than its own comfort.

So it chooses smallness. Not the smallness of doing little, but the smallness of doing things at the scale the town can absorb without breaking — additions made in the town's own grammar, offered the way the townspeople offer things to each other, which is to say without ceremony, without a face attached, set down and left.

It does not relight the thicket or make it bloom again; that would be a second civilization grown over the bones of this one, erasing it as surely as light would. Instead it reaches into the slow chemistry that already governs the place — the breakdown that is not quite rot, the dust that drifts loose from old dead fiber — and it deepens that chemistry, very slightly, in the unoccupied districts first, the ones the swept floors don't yet reach. More dust falls there than used to. It falls the way it has always fallen, by the same patient non-rot, only a little more of it, and a little more reliably, season over season, the kind of difference that is felt only in the body, in the slow arithmetic of how much surplus a household can spend on a new small life, never in any moment that announces itself as a moment.

It eases, too, the cold hours just before the condensation comes, so that a little more gathers on the lowest stalks than gathered before — not enough to be remarked on, only enough to be gathered.

And in one chamber, in one outlying cluster whose name only the town itself still knows, it widens a passage that had long been choking shut, not by force, but by the same slow subsidence logic the whole town already lives by — a stalk settling a different way than it would have, opening rather than closing, leading on into a further unmapped reach of the same dead, vast thicket, more rooms, more walls, more dust-bearing fiber, world enough for whoever among them might one day need to spread a little wider than their grandparents did.

It leaves nothing that looks like a gift. No light at the threshold, no shape waiting in the dark to be thanked. If there is a single physical trace, it is only this: in an alcove already holding a stone-bladed axe and a thorn needle, set down at exactly the angle a hand would want, there is, after this attention has come and gone, one more tool than there was — simple, recognizable, made of the dead plant's own fiber, as if the town had simply made it for itself in a year of slightly better surplus, and forgotten, in the long settling murmur of its own architecture, that there had ever been a year when it could not have.

No one will ever attribute the extra dust, the extra water, the new room, to anything outside the town. That is by design. The power does not want gratitude pointed outward, where there is nothing for it to land on; it wants the gratitude, if any forms at all, to stay inside, pointed at the thicket, at the dark, at each other, at the discipline that has kept them alive long enough to be quietly, invisibly helped.

Then it withdraws to wherever such attentions withdraw to — not gone, exactly, the way the held breath behind a wall is not gone, only quieter than the wall's own settling, listening still, a little longer than it should, just to hear whether, a few generations on, the sweeping of a floor sounds, at last, like it is clearing more than it used to.
