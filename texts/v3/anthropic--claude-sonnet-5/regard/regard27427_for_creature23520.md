The power comes the way weather comes — not arriving so much as becoming present, the way a cloud-tower is already most of the way built before you notice it building. It does not descend. There is no shaft of light, no voice in the wind, no moment when the grass parts for it. It simply is there, in the way the light is there, from slightly more directions than it should be.

And its first instinct, honestly, is the wrong one. It is an old instinct, maybe the oldest one a power like this has: to fix something, to add something, to make a gift of itself. It looks at the long-necked grazer standing chest-deep in dusk-light, breathing its slow bellows-breath, and some part of the power wants to lean down into that animal's high wide eyes and put a word behind them. Wants to give the bird-shape true sight, lift its patience into purpose. Wants to warm one night against the hard fast cold, just once, so something here might know, even briefly, the sensation of being spared.

It sets all of this aside, the way you'd set aside a tool that turned out to be the wrong shape for the work. Because the wisdom in it — the part that has been wise long enough to recognize its own appetite for usefulness — understands what this place actually is. Not a problem. Not a thing arrested mid-becoming, waiting on a hand to finish it. The Highreach Steppes are already the argument they are trying to make. The dignity here is precisely that nothing has ever required marking, that the grass is combed by wind and not by meaning, that the grazer's whole vast unhurried life completes itself with no one's attention bent toward it at all and is not diminished by that, not even slightly. To intervene — to speak, to elevate, to spare — would not be a gift laid on top of this place. It would be a correction. It would say: this was not enough, until I arrived.

So the power does the harder thing, the thing that doesn't look like anything from the outside. It stays, and it watches, and it lets the watching be the whole of what it does — becoming, in effect, one more presence the steppe already knows how to hold, indistinguishable from the black mark of the bird working the updraft for hours without dropping, indistinguishable from a cathedral-shadow taking its long minutes to cross a mile of grass. It moves through the place the way the wind moves through it: everywhere, continuously, changing nothing, felt only as a kind of pressure against the grass-tops if you knew exactly how to look. It is there when the herd turns at dusk to face the coming wind. It is there in the fine static of the insects at midday. It does not call itself anything, because there is no one to call it anything, and it finds — a little to its own surprise — that this suits it.

It permits itself one liberty, and even this it administers so lightly it is indistinguishable, after the fact, from luck. There is an old grazer, far older than its kind usually grows, standing at the edge of what might generously be called a herd, its great chest working harder than the others, its pale coat gone past pale into something almost translucent at the edges. It will die this winter — that was always true, that was written into it long before the power noticed it standing there — and the power does not change this. What it changes is smaller: a fog-bank that would have failed to climb the escarpment that particular morning climbs it instead, a few minutes of moisture reaching a cushion-plant mat three hundred years old that would otherwise have gone, for once in its long life, thirsty past the point it could recover from. The mat lives. The old grazer takes one more season of passing bites from it before the season turns. Nothing has been spared that was not, in some sense, already owed — the power has only made sure the steppe's own ledger comes out the way it would have, if luck had simply been a little kinder, the way it sometimes is, the way no one would ever think twice about.

And then it does the thing that is, in the end, the only thing it has decided is truly its to give: it stays. Not for a season, not for the grazer's lifetime, but for the kind of duration the steppe itself deals in — the duration of the cushion-plants, of the wind-fluted rock, of the grass-pattern that holds for days and is redrawn and holds again. It returns, and returns, across years that pass for it the way an afternoon passes for the grass. It watches the herd's bloodline continue past any animal it ever singled out. It watches the bird-shape's descendants ride the same updraft their ancestor rode. It watches the cloud-floor glow faintly with starlight on ten thousand more nights of hard clean cold, and it asks nothing of any of it, and changes nothing further, and is simply, finally, faithfully — there.

Which is, perhaps, the rarest gift a place like this can be given: not rescue, not change, not meaning laid on from outside — but to be seen, completely and without appetite, by something that understands exactly how little it should do about what it sees, and chooses, with everything it has, to do that little, and then to keep watching anyway, for no reason but that the watching itself has become, to it, a kind of love.
