The power does not arrive the way arrivals are usually imagined. There is no parting of the haze, no figure standing suddenly on the highest unclimbed face where the cough-call lives. What happens, at first, is only this: something old and very patient turns its attention, the way a person lying in grass might turn their head toward a sound they almost didn't hear, and the spire — the lean of it, the bronze light, the nine hundred lives stacked like a loose spiral of small fires — comes into a kind of focus it has never been in before. It is looked at. Not judged. Looked at the way you look at something you already suspect you love, before you've decided whether you're allowed to.

It spends a long time only learning to read the place before it permits itself to want anything for it.

This is the first discipline, and the hardest one, because the power is old enough and strong enough that almost anything is available to it, and availability is its own kind of temptation. It could end the drought below and let green things run wild across the flat dead country, water enough that cisterns become superstition and the careful arithmetic of the wardens becomes a story told to amuse children, the way other cultures tell stories about famines they no longer fear. It could reach up into the unclimbed dark above the last terrace and simply still whatever makes the cough-call, the way you'd still a heart, gently, without cruelty, so that no mother would ever again need to warn a child with that particular tone reserved for old, patient fears. It could straighten the lean. It could finish things — the unfinished granary wall on terrace six, the long-postponed bridge repair on nineteen, finish them outright, hand the people a spire with nothing left to continue.

It considers all of this with real tenderness, and sets it all down again, one piece at a time, the way you'd set down tools you picked up out of habit rather than need.

Because the drought is not a wound here. It is a grammar. Take it away and you don't relieve the people of hardship, you relieve them of meaning — the cupped palms, the gratitude, the entire structure of authority that runs through water rather than blood, the cistern-wardens who are trusted because what they guard is genuinely precious. Make the water common and you have not freed anyone. You have only made nine hundred specific, hard-won wisdoms into nine hundred orphaned habits, customs with the engine taken out.

And the dark above the last terrace — the power looks at that for a long while too, because there is real fear there, real teeth somewhere on the unclimbed faces, real grief in the households of the rare child or hunter who doesn't come back down. But it notices, also, how the fear is held. Not as trauma. As weather. As a thing you arrange your life around with the same unbothered tone you'd use for *the wind is sharp today.* The dark up there is doing something for them that a flatlander would never guess from outside: it is teaching limit. It is the one thing on the spire that cannot be terraced, cut, claimed, continued into. Every other surface bears the marks of hands. That one doesn't. And the power suspects, watching three generations of mothers use that same gentle invocation on three generations of reckless children, that a spire with no edge to its ambition would eventually terrace itself to death, cut every shelf the rock could bear and then look for more, the way unlimited things tend to. The fear is a kind of railing. It does not remove it.

So it sets aside the grand gestures, one after another, the way you'd set aside gifts that are really just your own appetite dressed up as generosity. And it keeps looking.

What it finds, eventually, is much smaller, and this is where it decides to act.

On the eleventh terrace there is an old woman named Yorta who has kept the cistern there for forty-one years, and who is dying, slowly and without much pain, in the unhurried way old age sometimes grants as a mercy. She has a granddaughter who was meant to take the warden's knowledge from her — careful, steady-handed, exactly the kind of person a warden is supposed to be — but the granddaughter drowned two summers ago in a fall that had nothing to do with water at all, just a wet step at dusk and a bad angle, and Yorta has not, since then, managed to choose anyone else, has told herself there's time, the way the dying sometimes tell themselves there's time. What she carries that no one else carries is the memory of which retaining wall under the lantern-path actually holds the terrace above it — the one her own grandmother rebuilt after a flood so long ago that no living person besides Yorta remembers it happened, a flood nobody else has any reason to think about, because nothing about that wall looks unusual now. Roots have grown through it the way roots grow through everything here. It looks exactly as solid as every other wall. It is not. Her grandmother told her, hands on hands, the particular places where the old flood-damage was packed rather than truly knit, the spots that will go soft again someday under enough weight or enough rain, and Yorta has been the only living memory of this for eleven years, since her own mother died, and she has been meaning, meaning, meaning to pass it on.

A few terraces down, on nine, there is a boy named Pell, eleven years old, who climbs faster and higher than anyone his age has any business climbing, who has been doing this more and more since his drowned cousin — Yorta's granddaughter — stopped being someone he could race down the ladder-chutes with. He is not reckless out of stupidity. He is reckless the way grief sometimes wears the shape of speed, of always going up, of seeing how close to the unclimbed dark he can get before the old fear-tone in his mother's voice pulls him back. No one has told him this is what he's doing. He doesn't know it himself.

The power does not fix Pell. It does not erase Yorta's grief or extend her life past its proper unwinding or repair the wall itself with some unaccountable strength overnight, the way a finishing god might. Instead, on three separate nights, it gives Yorta a restlessness that feels exactly like her own conscience — nothing more dramatic than that, no visions, no voice, just an old woman lying awake with the specific, nagging, entirely ordinary sense that she has been putting something off that she shouldn't put off, the way you'd suddenly remember, with real urgency, an unanswered letter. And on the same three nights it gives Pell a dream that feels exactly like his own appetite — not omen, not vision, just a boy dreaming, the way boys do, about being needed for something difficult and important, about an old woman's hands over his hands, about a wall.

That is all. That is the entire intervention.

What happens after that is entirely theirs. Yorta, restless, sends for the boy everyone considers too wild for warden's work, against the soft objections of two of her own daughters, and finds — because she is, after everything, still good at reading hands, still able to recognize attention when she sees it even wearing a reckless face — that he listens to her the way her own granddaughter used to, with his whole still body, and that this, of all things, is what finally settles him, climbing less now not because he was told to but because he has somewhere to be most evenings, sitting on a low stone wall with an old woman's rough hands guiding his rough hands over the specific soft places in old mortar that no eye could find. She teaches him the wall in the seven months she has left, and several other things besides, half-accidentally, the way any real teaching spills past its stated purpose — how to read the arithmetic of a cistern, how to sit with someone's grief without trying to fix it, what overhearing-deafness is actually for. He is not a tidy successor. He never becomes the kind of careful, steady-handed warden his predecessor would have been. But he knows the wall. And four years after Yorta is folded properly into the structure of terrace eleven, name attached to stone the way names attach here, a heavy unseasonal rain comes through and the wall holds — groans, settles a half-inch, holds — because a boy who is now a young man remembers exactly where to brace it, exactly which stones to reset before the soft place under them gives way entirely, and three terraces' worth of households sleep through the night never knowing how close the lantern-path came to going down into the dark with them on it.

No one ever calls this a miracle. There is no word in their language, as far as anyone can tell, that separates what happened from luck, or memory, or love doing what love does when given enough time — which is, perhaps, exactly the point, and exactly why the power chooses this kind of gift over any other. It does not want to be found out. Revelation would be its own kind of finishing, a completed story where there's supposed to be only continuance, a god's name where there's supposed to be only a wall still standing and a young man's calloused hands and an old woman's name still attached to a terrace she widened a handspan over forty-one years.

The power withdraws its attention the way it arrived — without ceremony, without announcing that anything happened at all, the way the spire itself never announces the next inch of soil filling the next crack in its slowly weathering face. It does not consider the work finished. It understands, by now, watching this place, that nothing here is supposed to be finished, that finishing was never the goal, that the whole quiet theology of the Reach — you do not complete a thing, you continue it — has taught the power something it did not entirely know about itself before it looked closely enough to learn it. It leaves changed in some small permanent way, carrying that lesson back out into whatever else it tends, the way Yorta's hands carried her grandmother's memory of a flood into a boy who never saw water fall from any sky but the spire's own slow nightly exhale.

Behind it, the lanterns go on being lit each dusk by whichever child on each terrace has just become old enough for the rite, small fires climbing the spire's lean shadow one terrace at a time until from the flat dead country below it looks, as it always has, like a comet frozen mid-fall — ascending, the people there would say, if anyone asked them to describe their own home, though of course no one ever has to ask, because they were born already knowing it, the way they were born already knowing which way down was, on a needle of rock that drinks the sky and bleeds it back to them, going on, going on, going on.
