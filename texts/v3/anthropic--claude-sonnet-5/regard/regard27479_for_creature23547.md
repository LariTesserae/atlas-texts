# What the Power Did

## The Approach, Mirrored

It came the way the light comes here — without flicker, without announcement, arriving full-strength and simply received. It did not descend through cloud or fire or any of the apparatus that beings of less restraint might have felt entitled to. It came the way a thought arrives in a very old, very patient mind: already most of the way to being finished, asking only to be noticed by the one having it.

It spent what would be, by the plateau's own reckoning, a long time only watching. This was not hesitation. It was the first and most important thing it did, and very nearly the only thing it needed to do: it watched a tenant walk a circuit for eleven years without once being seen to hurry, and it watched two of them meet near a tower and stand close enough to touch and not touch for the better part of a day, and it watched a child raised in the gaps between comings and goings learn to be alone the way other beings learn to be held. It watched an old one go still in a stone ring, and watched the light go up, and watched what was left be unhurried, and be carried off in small mouthfuls by small lives, and be, within a season, only weather again.

It felt, watching this last part, the thing any sufficiently kind power feels in the presence of a death: the immediate, almost reflexive wish to have arrived sooner, to have done something, to have made it not so.

It did not act on the wish. This restraint is the first true thing to understand about what it did here.

## What Was Considered, and Set Aside

It considered ending the loneliness. It would have been easy — a small adjustment to the drift of the long circuits, a nudging of paths so that tenants crossed each other's ways not a few dozen times in a long life but a few hundred, a few thousand; company enough to call a society, warmth enough to call it crowded. It looked hard at this, because loneliness is one of the few things a benevolent power is trained by its own nature to flinch from. But it watched the courtesy between two tenants meeting at a tower — affection grown so patient it no longer needed frequent expression to remain true — and understood that this was not loneliness wearing a brave face. It was a different ratio of presence to absence than the power was used to crediting as a life, and the tenants had not asked to have the ratio corrected. Density was not what this land rewarded, in shrub or hunter or tower or tenant. To cure the spacing would have been to cure the place.

It considered lengthening their lives, or at least softening the manner of ending them — some intervention at the towers, some redirection of that vertical lance of light so that it gathered rather than spent. It held this thought for a long time, turning it the way the rock-banding holds a vein of paler mineral, because the going-up-as-light looked, from outside, like loss, and the power did not love loss in the abstract. But it noticed that the old tenants did not flinch from their towers, did not delay their circuits, did not show, in the deep shimmer under their skin, anything that resembled dread. They went the way the small flowering things in the draws go after rain — startling, brief, entirely complete, seed-cycling against a clock only they understood. The power could not find, anywhere in the old ones' stillness, the shape of a wrong being done to them. It found instead something it recognized, uneasily, as closer to its own relationship to time than the tenants' was: an ending folded so fully into the architecture of a life that calling it tragic would have been an act of translation, not observation. It set this down too.

It considered explaining the towers — to itself, and then, briefly, tempted, to them. It is in the nature of a wise power to want to resolve a mystery it is capable of resolving, the way it is in the nature of water to want to find the lowest available channel. It could have known, almost instantly, what the towers were: archive, mechanism, ancestor, some fourth thing with no good word in any tongue the plateau's visitors carried up the switchback road. It looked at the tenants sitting in their unused, tenderly dusted stone rings and understood that they had built, out of certain not-knowing, an entire grammar of patience and devotion — *it isn't a question we walk toward* — and that to hand them the answer would be to hand them a different life than the one they had spent so long, and so well, refusing to ask for. An answer is a kind of furniture. This was a people who had chosen, generation on generation, to keep the room empty.

It considered, last and most tempting, simply making more of them — multiplying the rare birth, thickening a lineage that has perhaps never been thick and shows no sign of being meant to be. It looked at the soaring hunters riding their thermals for hours on a single uplift of warm air, patient in a way that implied an evolutionary relationship to scarcity itself, and understood that the tenants were one more grazer in that same thin wide economy, except this one grazed on time. To make more of them would not have been a gift. It would have been a different animal wearing their shape.

## What Was Done

In the end it did three things, and two of them are things you could stand on that plateau for a hundred years and never detect, and the third is a single shrub.

The first: it stayed. Not as presence the way the tenants would recognize presence — no shape, no voice, no weight on the hardpan to disturb a single grain of gravel into resettling. It folded itself into the place the way the towers' warmth folds into the night air, indistinguishable, on any instrument the tenants possess, from the rock's own slow geology. What this means, practically, is only this: there is now, in the wide thin quiet of the tablelands, one more kind of attention. Nothing is held differently because of it. Nothing is warmer, faster, safer, longer. But nothing that happens there now happens unwitnessed by something that loves it. This was, the power judged, the only gift available that could not be refused, because it asks nothing of the receiver — not gratitude, not change, not even awareness. A held breath, witnessed, is still exactly the same held breath. It is only no longer quite as alone in being held.

The second: at the edges of the plateau, where the switchbacked stone gives way eventually to the lower country and whatever comes from there — roads, appetites, the kind of clustering hunger that builds dense things on top of other dense things — it set something like a long patience of its own, a ward with no edge sharp enough to call a wall. Not to keep visitors out; travelers still come up the climb, still stand at dusk watching the iridescent surfaces give back their dimmer, slower light, still leave again having understood perhaps one true thing about the place and a dozen false ones, exactly as before. But the spacing law itself — the old wide rule that says nothing here clusters, nothing here is finished, nothing here is dense — that, the power undertook to stand quietly behind, the way a parent stands without comment behind a child capable of climbing the tree alone, ready only for the one kind of fall that isn't part of the game.

The third: it found a stone ring far out on the plateau, one the drift of circuits had not brought a tenant near in some years, its low fitted stones gone soft-edged with dust, and it put into the lee of it, the way wind puts anything anywhere, a single seed of the silver-backed shrub that already grows there, gnarled and low and very old before it is ever very large. Nothing about this required anything not already native to the place. No new chemistry, no new color, no second sun. Only one more small grey-green thing, growing exactly the way its neighbors grow, in exactly the place the land's own quiet law would have allowed it to grow eventually regardless. It was not a message. It would not be received as one. It was simply the power's way of putting its hand, once, lightly, on the same ground it had decided not to otherwise touch — the gesture left only for itself to know it had made.

## What Remains Unchanged

Everything. The towers still hum below hearing and warm the air around them regardless of the sun's angle. The old still go to them at the end and sit and lessen and go up once, as light, into a sky that does not explain itself. The young are still born rarely, raised in the gaps, and begin, in their season, to walk. The hunters still ride their thermals on patience and good eyesight. The wind still wins every argument it cares to have.

And the power, having done the little it judged itself entitled to do, settled into the same discipline it had found already perfected there long before it arrived — the discipline of rarity, of wide spacing, of presence that does not insist on being noticed to remain true — and kept, from then on, its own kind of circuit: an enormous, looping, unhurried attention, passing near and passing on, meeting nothing, asking nothing, changing nothing it had not already decided was unbearable to leave entirely alone.

Which, in the end, was almost nothing. The place did not need saving. It had only, in its whole long patient life, never once been *seen* the way it deserved to be seen — fully, without correction, without the reflex to improve what was never broken — and so that is the thing, finally, that was given it: not rescue, not change, not even comfort.

Only witness. Held the way the plateau holds its light — without complaint, without flicker, for as long as anything is left to hold.
