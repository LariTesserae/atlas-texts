The power does not arrive the way arrivals are usually imagined — no parting of the mist, no light where there wasn't light. It simply becomes one more thing the town's damp air is doing, the way the air is already doing wind and bell-tone and the smell of resin. If anyone looked up at the moment of its coming, they would have seen nothing but the ordinary gold-grey dusk doing what it always does, going a little dimmer, a little cooler, asking for a coat.

It looks first the way the correctors look — ear pressed to the question rather than eyes searching for an answer. It listens to the causeways arguing quietly with themselves about where the market square is tonight. It listens to the pistons firing somewhere unseen, the greenish soot, the cheerful argument of firekeepers over tolerances that are really, underneath the technical words, an argument about trust. It listens, longest of all, to the double-thump inside the walls, the second heartbeat, and it understands — the way you understand a held breath by holding your own — that this is not a malfunction wearing a forest as a disguise. This is a mind. Not a mind like a person is a mind, contained, edged, nameable, but a mind the way a held note is a song: enormous, slow, still deciding, after some unfathomable span of time, what shape it intends to keep being. It grew faster than it could finish becoming one thing. It is still finishing. The drift in the floor plans is not error. It is the mind turning over in something like sleep, and the chalk and the bells are not corrections so much as a lullaby a whole civilization has learned to hum without ever being taught the tune, passed root-walker to firekeeper to corrector to child, *yes, that hallway does that, no, we don't know why, yes, it's fine.*

This is the first thing the power understands, and understanding it, the power feels the pull that any benevolent thing feels standing over a vast, unfinished, half-conscious ache: the pull to *fix* it. To still the drift. To give the half-formed mind a final shape, gently, mercifully, the way you'd set a bone. To make the maps agree. To let the Wardens sleep at dusk instead of walking the disputed causeways with chalk worn down to a nub.

It does not do this.

It does not do this because it looks a moment longer and sees what the fixing would cost: that the drift is not the mind's wound, it is the mind's *being*, and to still it would not be healing, it would be a kind of quiet killing dressed up as kindness — the same mistake every well-meaning power risks when it mistakes difference for damage. And it sees, too, that the people here have already done the wise thing, the hard thing, the thing that took them — how many generations of careful watching, of arguing cheerfully about pressure and pitch, of children learning to call a wall-sound "the second heartbeat" and stopping there, satisfied, not needing more — the thing that took them this long to learn: how to live *with* a mystery instead of against it. How to tend a god without naming it a god. How to file the miraculous under drainage and load-bearing capacity, and mean no disrespect by it, only intimacy worn down to the practical, the way long love wears down to small gestures.

A power that loved this place truly would not improve on that.

So it gives only two things, and gives them so quietly that no record of either exists, except in the soft places where things are noticed without being explained.

To the slow old mind dreaming itself through the trunks, it gives one night of being heard *all at once* — not one ear pressed to one wall, the way the correctors have always had to do it, one room, one bell, one patient decade at a time, but every wall at once, every hollow, every causeway mid-decision, held in a single attention large enough to match its own. For one night the thumping in the walls is not a comma in a long green sentence. It is, for the only time anyone will never quite remember, something closer to being answered. The bells are unusually quiet that night. No chalk needs renewing. The firekeepers, walking home late, remark to no one in particular that the pressure-gauges read calm, calmer than they've read in years, and don't think much more of it.

And to one corrector — old, varnished faintly along the knuckles, forty years of pressing her ear to bark and hearing a question she was never arrogant enough to think she could answer — it gives a single, wordless moment of certainty: that she was right. Not about what the thing in the walls *is*. Only that it is something, that it is glad of her listening, that four decades of unspoken theory she never dared say out loud were not foolishness. She will wake the next morning unable to say what changed. She will go on doing exactly what she did the day before — chalk, mallet, bell, the long patient walk of the disputed causeways at dusk — but she will do it, for the rest of her life, a little less alone in it than she was.

Then the power withdraws the way it came: as nothing more than the dusk finishing its ordinary work of dimming, the lamps along the root-streets glowing up amber to meet it, someone's hand on a stranger's arm, gently steering them home before the town decides, once again, where a hallway goes.

It does not return. It does not need to. The wisest thing it found here was already being done — by people with chalk stubs and tired knees, who would never call it tending a god, only Tuesday.
