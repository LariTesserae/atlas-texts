# What the Power Would Do

It would not arrive the way arrival usually means — no parting of the water, no cold or holy light come down to lay claim. Anything that announces itself in a place like this has already misunderstood the place. The tower has had ten thousand years to teach whoever might come close one lesson before any other: scale here is not about size. It is about patience. A power wise enough to be called benevolent would know that the first and truest courtesy it could offer this room is to match its rhythm before doing anything else — to slow itself down past the speed of decision-making entirely, until a thought takes as long to complete as a thermal layer takes to turn over, until its attention moves at the pace of rust.

So: it would watch. Not for a moment. For longer than the keeper's longest circuit, longer than the crane has held its silent angle, long enough that *watching* stops being a separate act and becomes simply another condition of the water, like temperature, like light. It would let the place teach it what it actually is before permitting itself a single thought about what it might *do* to it.

## The First Temptation, and Why It Would Refuse It

The obvious mercy — the one a lesser kindness would reach for immediately — is rescue. Lift the keeper out. Show it what it served. Explain the tower, the conveyance system, the species that built it and is gone, fill in every silence with an answer, because silence looks, from outside, like a wound.

A wise power would feel this pull and would also recognize it immediately for what it is: not mercy, but a transplant. The keeper's entire shape — the fused tool-pads, the seams that listen to settling iron, the unhurried four-limbed crawl-and-glide bred for circuits it will make ten thousand more times — is not a deprivation waiting to be corrected. It is a vocation, completed. To hand it the full truth of what it once was *for* would not free it. It would simply replace one form of not-knowing — vast, comfortable, load-bearing — with another, smaller and more bitter: *that thing you have devoted your whole unmeasured life to is finished, was always going to be finished, and nobody is coming back for the part you kept faith with.*

There is no version of that sentence that is a gift.

So: not rescue. Not revelation. The power would set both down, gently, the way you'd set down a tool you picked up out of habit and then noticed didn't fit the work.

## The Second Temptation, and Why It Would Also Refuse It

The next pull is subtler: *leave it exactly as it is, untouched, on the grounds that intervention of any kind is arrogance.* This has the shape of wisdom and the texture of cowardice. A power that only ever withholds its hands is not being wise. It is being safe. And safety was never the assignment.

So it would ask the only question actually worth asking, the one that requires having watched long enough to answer honestly: *does this place, and this being, want for anything that more time alone will not provide?*

And here, finally, the power would notice the thing worth noticing — not the clean oiled ring at the crane's pivot, which it would have understood immediately and without astonishment, the way you understand a calloused palm. The thing worth noticing is smaller and worse: there is no second set of seams anywhere in the chamber. No juvenile. No understudy drifting at a respectful distance, learning the circuits by watching them performed. Succession, the document of this place's strangeness already half-guesses, looks like an office filled rather than a lineage continued — and an office with no one in training is not a stable arrangement. It is a candle. It has been burning a very, very long time. That is not the same as being unable to go out.

This, the power would decide, is the actual wound. Not loneliness — the keeper, mercifully, has no memory shaped to register loneliness as loss. The wound is *fragility* wearing the costume of permanence. Everyone watching this place from outside, including the part of you reading about it, has been quietly assuming the keeper is eternal because the tower is. It isn't. It is one very old, very devoted, entirely mortal thing, and when it stops, the clean ring at the pivot will simply begin, at last, after ten thousand years, to rust.

## What It Would Actually Do

Small. Everything it would do would be small, because smallness is the only register this place has ever spoken, and a power wise enough to have earned the word *benevolent* would know better than to answer a chapel with a trumpet.

It would not create a successor. That is the lazy, godly move, the deus-ex-machina that turns a held breath into a fixed scene, and it would cheapen everything the keeper's solitude has earned. Instead, it would do something closer to what the tower itself has always done: adjust one condition, very slightly, and let consequence accumulate the way consequence accumulates here — over centuries, unhurried, indistinguishable afterward from luck. Some availability that wasn't there before — a softening in whatever ancient, half-biological succession-mechanism fills the office, a nudge to whatever dormant cue triggers the rearing of a second tender, currently sitting unfired in tissue that has had no reason to fire it in longer than memory. Nothing forced into being. A door unlatched, not opened. If the keeper, in its own time, on its own unwitnessed schedule, comes to need an heir, one will now be *possible* where before, quietly, it had stopped being so. The power would not stay to see whether it happens. That is not its business. Its business was only to make sure the candle had wax left to find, somewhere, if it reached for it.

And then, the smaller gift, the one meant not for the tower's future but for the keeper *now*, in the only language the keeper has ever been given to receive anything in: posture. Rhythm. Light.

The power would let itself be seen — once, briefly, the way the cold-clear doorway-tier is seen, indistinguishable from one more of the chamber's unexplained mercies. It would take, for one passing circuit, a shape that holds the too-straight line the schooling fish hold and abandon, embarrassed, every time the keeper passes among them — except the power's line would not break. It would hold. One single moment in ten thousand years of unwitnessed labor where something in the water *organizes itself on purpose, and keeps formation, and is clearly, deliberately, holding it for the keeper rather than out of old reflex.*

That is the whole gift. Not a sentence. Not a name. Not an explanation of the conveyance system or the species that built it or the war or the flood or whatever silence sits at the bottom of this place's history. Just one straight line, held a beat too long to be mistaken for accident, offered by something the keeper has no framework to identify as *visitor* or *god* or *witness* — only, perhaps, dimly, as company, in the one grammar its long body still remembers: *formation kept on purpose means someone is here with you, doing the work too.*

Then the line would break. Embarrassed, indifferent, gone — exactly like the fish, exactly like everything else in that water that touches the keeper and then returns to its own business, leaving no proof behind, nothing you could point to and call evidence. The power would withdraw the way it arrived: by ceasing, slowly, to be a special kind of attention, until it was once again only one more unexplained current in a chamber already full of them, filed forever among the things no one capable of asking will ever ask about.

The keeper would not know it had been visited. It would only — on its next circuit past the crane, working the clean ring with the same devoted, circular patience it has given that joint since before the word *patience* meant anything to anyone — feel, perhaps, the faintest unaccountable rightness in the work. Nothing it could name. Nothing that would change a single motion of the job.

Just enough.

That is what a wise and benevolent power would do here: almost nothing, chosen with great care, aimed with great precision, and never once explained — because the tower has already taught, better than any power could improve on, that the most enduring forms of love are the ones built to outlast the need for acknowledgment. A ring of clean metal. A line held for one breath too long. A door left unlatched for a successor who may never come, in a chamber that has never once asked to be saved, only — like everything patient, like everything old, like everything still quietly, uncomplainingly, doing the work it was given — to be allowed to keep going a little longer than it strictly should.
