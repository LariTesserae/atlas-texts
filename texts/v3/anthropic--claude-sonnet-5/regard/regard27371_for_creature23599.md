# What the Power Notices, and What It Does

It comes the way such things come — not flying in on light, not parting the mist with trumpets, but simply *attending*, the way a person might finally turn their full mind to a room they have walked through a thousand times without looking. One moment Vhalkurun is one more shape among the world's shapes. The next, it is seen. Wholly. Every terrace, every weeping stone, every backward-running drip, every bell mid-toll on every ring at once, the whole eleven-thousand-meter column of accumulated human patience laid open like a cut log, rings visible all the way to a center no one living has touched.

The power's first temptation — and it recognizes this temptation immediately, with the particular embarrassment of the wise catching themselves in an old habit — is to *fix* it. To look at four hundred generations spiraling up the same switchback, dying within forty rings of their birth-terrace, never building outward, never building upward, only ever re-laying the same stair in the same pattern by feel — and to call this stagnation, and to want to break it open like a seedpod, scatter these compact strong-backed people outward into newness, give them flight, give them ambition shaped like discovery instead of maintenance.

It does not do this. It sits with the impulse until the impulse cools, and underneath the cooling it finds the actual shape of the place, which is not stagnation at all. It is devotion with the scale sanded off. A wall re-pointed in a grandfather's exact pattern is not a failure of imagination; it is a four-generation conversation conducted in lime and stone, and to "free" these people from it would not be liberation. It would be amputation dressed as a gift.

So the power does not touch the mountain's shape. It does not speed the bells, widen the terraces, explain the water. It does something much smaller, and much harder, which is this: it goes looking, ring by ring, for the places where the structure — good as it is, old as it is, loved as it is — still leaves a person alone with something too heavy to carry by tradition alone.

It finds several. It does not find many. The mountain is, on the whole, kind to its own.

### The Priest-Mechanic at the Three-Thousandth Ring

He is dying, slowly, the way the gear-houses he has tended his whole life die slowly — not failing, just running down toward the day the relay passes to someone else's hands. He has never told anyone, not even his wife, that he does not know why the water runs uphill at his post. He has tended the mystery faithfully for fifty years and has never once understood it, and he has carried the specific, private fear that this not-understanding is itself a kind of small lifelong failure, hidden under the better pay and the fewer questions, gnawing at him in the dark drum-warm chamber at 3 a.m. when the gears groan and he cannot tell, anymore, if he is reading scripture or just guessing.

The power does not explain the water to him. That would cost the mountain something it does not mean to take — the particular held grace of a mystery that is tended rather than solved. Instead, in the last clear week of his life, in the half-sleep before dawn, he simply *knows*, the way you know you are loved without being told the mechanism of it, that the channel has never once needed him to understand it. Only to stay. And that staying was the whole of the work, and the whole of the work was enough.

He dies easier for it. No one notices anything occurred. This is correct.

### The Child Tested on the High Rings

She is eleven, and her lungs fail the stair-test twice, and she has already heard, in the particular clipped cadence of terrace-folk delivering hard truths kindly, that she will likely not be fostered down to the forge-terraces, will likely stay among the goats and cisterns her whole life, and she has decided, with the terrible finality children are capable of, that this means she is *less*.

The power does not strengthen her lungs. It does not rig the test. It simply turns her face, one evening, half a degree further than she would otherwise have turned it — toward the open hatch of the gear-house on her own ring, where old Ressa is listening to a failing bearing with her eyes shut, frowning in concentration at a sound the girl, straining, finds she can also hear: a tiny grinding wrongness three gears deep.

"You heard that?" Ressa says, surprised.

It is the whole of the intervention. The girl spends the next sixty years a gear-house listener of unusual gift, and dies believing — correctly, as it happens, though not for the reason she thinks — that her worth was never in her lungs at all.

### The Wall at the Fourteen-Hundredth Ring

This one the power touches directly, because some things are not meted out in grace but in plain structural mercy. There is a fault in the wall's fifth course, laid wrong by a tired mason eleven generations back, invisible, slow, and in thirty-one years it would have given all at once on a wet morning with the terrace market full.

It does not give. The stone simply — holds. A stone-tender doing routine repointing some years later remarks to her apprentice that this course is sounder than she'd have guessed for its age, good old work, whoever laid it knew what they were doing. She is wrong about the mason, and right about everything else, and that is exactly the proportion of truth the power intends to leave behind.

### What It Chooses Not to Do

It does not reveal itself. It does not stop the bells from being slightly detuned, ring to ring, though it could resolve that chorus into perfect harmony in an instant and is, honestly, tempted — the dissonance has a beauty the power respects too much to correct. It does not tell anyone where the priest-mechanics' water goes, or why, or whether the mountain is in some sense alive, or whether it was always going to become this, terraced rim to rim, the moment its mass and its situation made density of habitation inevitable. Some mysteries are not problems. They are *companions*, kept on purpose, the way a person keeps a worn coin from a dead parent — not because they can't afford a new one.

It considers, briefly, granting the whole mountain some single grand mercy — ending the lung-tests, or making every wall un-failing, or smoothing the long, hard, unhurried fatalism out of the terrace temperament entirely, giving them something brighter to feel about their lives. It declines. That fatalism is not despair. It is the calm of people who have made peace with finite, structural, generational work — who do not need their lives to matter *outward* to feel that they have mattered. The power, very old and very wise, recognizes this peace as a thing it does not have the right to improve upon, only the privilege of protecting.

So in the end its whole intervention amounts to: three people quietly eased, one course of stone quietly held, and nothing else — no sign, no name, no altar raised on any ring, no terrace-keeper ever telling their grandchild a story about the night the mountain was watched by something that loved it.

If you asked the power why it did so little, having the power to do so much, it would likely answer the way a stone-tender answers when a stranger asks why she re-lays the wall in the exact pattern her grandmother used instead of some stronger modern method:

*Because it was already kept well. I only kept it a little better, in the places it asked for help, and left the rest exactly as I found it — loved.*

By the terraces' own measure, the highest compliment available, the power earns it and never collects: it kept the wall.
