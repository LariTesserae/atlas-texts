# The Attention

It does not arrive the way you would arrive. There is no descent through cloud, no four-minute lag between sight and sound. It is simply, at some point, present to the place — the way a thought is present to a mind that has just had it — and what it notices first is not the plateau, not the water-curtains, not even the tenders. What it notices first is the *shape of the not-finished*, the way you might notice, walking into a house, that a sentence has been left hanging mid-air by someone who stepped out of the room. It feels the absence of a period. That is what draws its attention, not wonder, not pity — a kind of grammatical itch.

And here is the first true thing to understand about a benevolent and wise power, if such a thing is to be believed in at all: the benevolence and the wisdom are not the same faculty, and the wisdom's first job, every single time, is to keep the benevolence from acting before it has finished looking.

So it looks. It does this thoroughly, which is to say without the mercy of a body's impatience. It traces every black glassy seam to its root. It enters, briefly and without disturbing a single droplet, the hexagonal lattice-thought of a suspended lens the size of a stadium, and finds inside it something that is not quite cognition and not quite weather but partakes of both — a slow, contented computation with no terminal state, the kind of thinking that is not trying to arrive anywhere. It finds the small crust-tenders dozing at knee height in the lee of outcrops and reads, in their dormancy, not suffering but *patience without a wound in it* — the rarest thing it has encountered in a long traversal of places that do need help. It finds the human, too, small and astonished and already half in love with what it cannot understand, and it notes — gently, because this matters to what it decides — that the human has not tried to fix anything either. The human has only watched, and been changed a little by watching, which is the correct response to a thing not yet finished, and the power respects this in the way one respects a student who has, for once, not rushed the lesson.

The urge comes anyway. It always comes. It is old in this power, older than any individual act of kindness — the desire, on encountering an unfinished thing, to *finish it*. To give the tenders faces, hands, a story with an arc, an ending in which their long thankless tending is revealed to have always been *for* something, redeemed by a purpose stapled on from outside. To take the plateau, with its groaning rock and its four-minute echoes and its weather that lasts ninety seconds and means nothing to anyone, and smooth it, settle it, give it a sky and a floor and a name, the way you might, out of love, tidy a child's room without asking — because surely chaos is a kind of suffering, surely *not yet decided* is a wound that wants closing.

This is where the wisdom does its real work. Not in restraining the power's hand — that part is easy, mechanical, the kind of discipline any sufficiently old kindness learns early. The hard part is the looking past its own reflex long enough to ask the only question that matters: *is anything here in pain, or does this only look like pain to something that has decided, in advance, that all becoming must resolve into being?*

And the answer, when it finally comes, arrives the way the four-minute thunder arrives — late, but total, and impossible to unhear once it lands. There is no pain here. There is no patient waiting for an ending that keeps failing to come. The tender that goes still and flattens back into ordinary stone is not dying with its work undone; it is *finished in exactly the way water is finished when it falls* — completely, for that moment, with nothing further owed. The plateau does not want a sky. It wants only to keep doing the thing it is, which is becoming, on no one's schedule, answerable to no audience, least of all a kind visitor who arrived with a story already half-written about what mercy should look like.

So the power does not finish it. It does not give the tenders faces. It does not staple a purpose onto the standing waves and call that a gift. It understands, finally, fully, that to do so would not be tending — it would be theft, the theft of the one truly rare condition in any world it has ever crossed: a thing allowed to remain unresolved, on purpose, by nothing crueler or kinder than the simple absence of a verdict.

What it does instead is small, and almost no one will ever know it happened.

It does not change the plateau. It puts a hand — not a hand, there is no hand, but the gesture means the same thing in whatever language a power like this speaks — against the whole of it, lightly, the way you'd rest your palm against a sleeping animal's flank just to feel it breathe, and it makes one quiet adjustment to the wider world's accounting: it ensures that no other power, now or in whatever centuries follow, will ever look upon this place and decide, out of its own restless mercy, that the plateau is a problem requiring a solution. It does not freeze the place. It frees it — specifically, permanently — from ever being *fixed* by anyone else's kindness. The unfinished stays unfinished by right, not by neglect. That is the gift: not an ending, but a guarantee that none will be imposed.

And then, because it cannot quite leave without leaving some mark of having truly seen — because seeing fully and saying nothing is its own kind of withholding — it does one more thing, smaller still. It turns, for the length of one held breath, toward the place where the human stands watching a tender lift a barn-sized lens of light into the white unsourced sky, and it makes certain — not of the human's safety, who was never in danger, and not of the human's understanding, which was already as complete as understanding gets — but of the human's *memory*. That this will not soften into dream by morning. That years on, in some ordinary room far from any cloud, the human will be able to close their eyes and still hear the bass thrum of a water-curtain settling into its working height, still feel it arrive in the sternum before the ears catch up.

Because the plateau cannot speak for itself, and may never need to. But someone, now, will always be able to say: *I was there before it was decided. I saw what was being held, and by whom, and I did not mistake the holding for a wound.*

That, the power judges, is enough tending for one visit.

It withdraws the way it came — without a sound, without a season, without anywhere having moved an inch — and the plateau, unfinished, untouched, exactly as rightfully strange as it was, goes on deciding, in no hurry at all, what it means to become.
