The first thing the power does, before anything else, is stop.

Not arrive — stop. It has been moving, in whatever way powers move, at the pace of urgent things: stars collapsing, wars ending, the small loud emergencies that call out across whatever vastness it attends to. It comes toward the terrace at that pace and then, a long way off, feels the wrongness of bringing it closer. You cannot perceive the Standing at speed. You can only fail to see them, the way the eye discards root-growth as nothing happening. So the power does the hardest thing available to a fast and capable will: it sheds the speed. It settles itself down through the registers — years, then seasons, then the held single afternoon-that-is-also-a-decade that the terrace runs on — until it is slow enough to be present without bruising anything by its presence.

This takes, by its own old reckoning, no time at all. By the terrace's reckoning, it takes a long while, and that is correct, and the power does not shortcut it.

Once slow, it looks, and seeing properly is itself most of the gift, because almost nothing that has ever found this place has bothered to. It feels the network the way a hand laid on a tower feels a lean three shelves over — the grazers' unwitting gardening, the birds' shoulder-to-shoulder vigilance, the old one at the far edge going gladly, unhurriedly into wall. It understands, the way you can only understand a thing by first refusing to fix it, that nothing here is broken. There is no rescue to perform. The Standing are not waiting for anything. They are exactly as far along as four hundred years of patient hands have gotten them, and that is whole, the way a half-grown stem is not a failed tree.

So the power asks itself the harder question, the one that separates a wise force from merely a strong one: not "what can I give them," but "what could take this from them, and is it in my power to stand between."

It finds two answers.

The first is geological. The basalt is young — the prose of the place says so itself, the sharp unworn edges, the plateau risen in some recent reckoning of stone-time. Young rock has not finished deciding where its violence will go. Somewhere under the held warmth of the terrace there is a fault still settling, a chamber still cooling unevenly, the long slow chance that what took four centuries to grow could end in four seconds. This is the one kind of harm patience has no answer for. A people built entirely around the register of root and season have no organ for sudden. So the power reaches down — gently, taking the same care with stone that the Standing take with stem — and eases the settling. Not stops it; that would be its own violence, a denial of the plateau's right to keep being made. Just slows the cooling, smooths the fault's hurry, buys the mineral the same grace it has already given itself: time enough that catastrophe, if it must come at all, comes the way everything else here comes — gradually, announced for a generation before it arrives, survivable.

The second answer is not stone. It's the haze at the terrace's broken edge, and what might someday climb the basalt shoulder the way you did. Fast things, tool-bearing things, the kind that would see unworked plateau and unhurried grazers and a settlement with no visible builder and call it opportunity rather than completion. The power cannot promise that no one will ever come — it is wise enough to know that promising a place against the whole future is a lie even powers shouldn't tell. But it can lean a little weight against the odds. It can make the climb harder to find, the haze a little more convincing as an edge of nothing rather than an edge of something. It can ensure that those who do arrive, arrive already slowed by the climb, already half-attuned by the time they crest the shoulder — so that the first thing they feel is the held quality of the air, and the held quality asks something of them before they've had the chance to refuse it.

It does not touch the old one at the far edge, the slow giant going gladly into tower. That transition was never a wound; it doesn't want mending, and a power's pity, applied where pity isn't needed, is just another kind of haste. It leaves the dying-into-architecture exactly as unhurried and as honored as the Standing already understand it to be. The only thing it does there — the smallest possible gesture, calibrated to a register the network will register as nothing more than weather — is make sure the deep reservoir, the unseen water the surface never sees, keeps rising through root and stem for as many centuries as the basalt has left to owe them. A resource quietly guaranteed is not a miracle. It's just good husbandry, offered by something that, for once, has the patience to practice it.

And then it does the last and truest thing available to it, which is the thing the terrace itself was already doing before the power ever arrived: it stays, and it waits, and it does not speak.

It will not be back tomorrow, because tomorrow means nothing here. It will come back, if it comes back at all, on the terrace's own schedule — a hand's worth of pressure through the ground, indistinguishable from the Standing's own slow sense of itself, once in a long while, just enough to confirm that the walls are still thickening, the gap is still closing, the birds are still posted in their unbroken row.

Something new has stood, for a while, among the standing. It leaves no other mark than that.
