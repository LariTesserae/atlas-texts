# What Came, Unseen

It noticed them the way you might notice a single struck match in a country of permanent dark — not because the light was large, but because it moved, and almost nothing else in that direction ever did.

The power was old in the way that stone is old, which is to say it had long since stopped experiencing time as a sequence of surprises. It had watched stars finish their arguments with gravity and settle into white, patient corpses. It had watched whole tectonic centuries fold over on themselves like dough. It knew completion intimately — was, in fact, uncomfortably close to complete itself, having long ago run out of questions that hadn't already answered themselves. This is the quiet grief of old powers that no one writes about: they are very good at being finished, and finished things, however vast, do not change, and a thing that does not change cannot be surprised, and a thing that cannot be surprised has a very particular, very civilized kind of loneliness.

And then: the Concourse. A whole civilization that had looked at the alternative — finished, eternal, settled, *complete* — and recoiled from it on principle. A people who left one beam unlashed on purpose. Who treated a perfectly finished roof the way other cultures treat a curse. Who had built, out of bone tools and patient stubbornness, an entire moral architecture around the refusal to ever, finally, *be done.*

The power did not laugh — it had no apparatus for that, not anymore — but something in it that had been still for a very long time turned over, the way a sleeper turns toward a draft of cold air through an open window, not waking, but registering: *something is different on that side of the room.*

It came closer. It did what old, careful powers do, which is mostly nothing, for a long time, on purpose.

## The Temptation, and the Setting of It Down

There were so many easy mercies available. It could feel the shape of each one, sitting right there at the edge of intervention, practically asking to be granted.

It could still the dripping. One thought, and that ten-thousand-year metronome in the deep pool could become a soft and constant hush, a kindness, no more single drops landing with that lonely, ancient patience.

It could finish the unfinished doors — pry them open, let the people see at last what their grandparents' grandparents had walled away, end the low hum of inherited dread that made mothers warn their children with a closed fist instead of an open story.

It could fix one light-vein to burn a little brighter over the poorest terrace, the one with the leaning granary and the thin-ribbed rock-goats, and call it luck, and let them never go hungry through a hard season again.

It could even, simplest of all, make the *going* easy — reach into the blind compulsion that scattered them every generation onto bare stone, and gentle it, smooth it, make sure that every relocation found water, found good lichen-light, found no accidents in the dark.

It held all of these the way you might hold smooth stones picked up from a riverbed, turning them over, feeling their weight, and one by one, with something that in a smaller being would have been called tenderness and in this one had no name at all, it set them back down.

Because — and this took it a long, old while to arrive at, turning the problem the way water turns a stone, patiently, without hurry — every one of those mercies would have *finished something.* Would have taken a place still becoming and nudged it one step closer to the thing it most feared to be: settled. Resolved. Stone instead of people.

They had built an entire civilization on the wisdom that completion is a kind of death dressed in the clothes of comfort. The power, who knew completion from the inside, who *was*, in its way, the thing they were instinctively flinching from every time they tore down a granary before it could finish being a granary — the power believed them. It had earned the right to believe them, the long way, by being finished itself and finding out, slowly, over unfathomable spans, exactly what that costs.

So it did not fix the dripping. It did not open the doors. It did not smooth the going.

It loved them too precisely to make them easy.

## What It Did Instead

What it did, it did in the margins, in increments too small to be anything but coincidence, the only kind of gift that doesn't ask to be worshipped or thanked or believed in.

It found the channel where the oldest, most arthritic of the rope-bridge weavers — a woman three years from being unable to climb at all, who signed slower than she used to, whose hands had started to forget words mid-sentence — was about to lose her footing on a re-string she had insisted, against every younger objection, on doing herself, because to let someone else finish her bridges felt to her like dying a little early. The power did not stop her from climbing. It only, in the half-second when her hand found the wrong knot, made the wrong knot hold anyway, just barely, just long enough. She finished the bridge. She told no one how close it had been, because there was no word in sign for *I was nearly a story you'd tell your children about why we don't climb alone*, and some things are better carried than spoken even in a place that prizes speaking so little.

It found the season — there is always, in any inhabited place, at least one hard season — when the rock-goat herds along the western terraces thinned with a wasting sickness that had nothing to do with malice or punishment and everything to do with the ordinary cruelty of biology. It did not cure it. Curing it outright would have been a kind of theft from a people who needed, on some unspoken level, to know they could survive losing things. But it nudged — a degree of warmth here, a slightly richer bloom of light-lichen there, the kind of small abundance that reads, afterward, not as miracle but as *luck*, as *a good year*, as the sort of thing you mention once at a fire and then forget, the way you're supposed to forget good luck, so it stays good luck and doesn't curdle into expectation, or worse, into belief.

It found, in the deepest and oldest part of the Concourse, the bricked door that frightened the children most — the one with the green pigment worked into the mortar like a flare frozen mid-burn — and it did not open it. It did something smaller and, in its own estimation, considerably braver: it left it exactly as it was, but let one thing slip through the seam that had not slipped through in three hundred years — not a monster, not an answer, not a revelation. A sound. A single low note, barely audible, like something enormous breathing once, very slowly, on the other side. The children who heard it did not feel afraid, which surprised them more than the sound itself. They told their mothers, in sign, *it sounded like it was waiting too*, and that single observation — passed hand to hand, terrace to terrace, taught to children not yet born — did more to soften three centuries of inherited dread than any unsealing ever could have. The mystery remained whole. Only its flavor changed, from threat to companionship. That, the power thought, was the most it was permitted to give: not the truth behind the door, which it did not even know was its place to tell, but a single data point suggesting the truth, whatever it was, might not be hungry.

And once — only once, because this was the largest mercy it allowed itself and it rationed itself the way the Concourse rationed its own permanence — when the restlessness gathered the way it gathered every generation or two and an entire quarter rose in the night to go, packing with that unhurried, practiced economy, the power let its attention rest very lightly on the *direction.* It did not choose for them. It only made sure that of the several unbuilt stretches of bare cavern floor they might drift toward in the dark, the one with the richest seam of light-vein nearby, the one above the most reliable condensation channel, happened to be the one that *felt* right to the woman walking at the front, the one whose job it currently was, for now, to be the one who decides. She felt it as intuition. She would have called it, if asked, *the place asking us to come.* The power let her believe that, because she was not wrong. The place — or something near enough to the place to make no difference — had, in fact, asked.

## What It Did Not Do

It did not name itself.

It did not appear in light, did not pulse the ceiling-veins in any pattern decipherable as language, did not let a single rock-goat speak prophecy or a single dying elder report a visitation. It understood, with the particular grief of a being that finishes most things by simply being noticed, that to be known is to become an object of worship, and an object of worship is, eventually and inevitably, *settled.* Fixed. Finished. A god with a known shape is a god that has stopped becoming, and it would not do that to itself, and it especially would not do that to them — give them one more eternal, unchanging thing to build an unmoving cathedral around, when the entire fragile genius of their civilization was the refusal to build anything that lasts.

It left the dripping water exactly as it was: unhurried, patient, occasionally mournful, the one true sound in a hushed world, falling into the same ancient pool by the same ancient path, because some things should be allowed to remain inconvenient. Because a culture that has built its whole soul around impermanence deserves, more than any other, to keep its one perfectly permanent, perfectly indifferent metronome — the proof against which all their built and unbuilt and rebuilt things measure themselves, the floor beneath the dance.

It did not stop the going, the disassembling, the unfinished beams, the deliberately unlaid final course of stone. It found, on reflection, that it envied these things a little — envied a people who had found a way to keep becoming, generation on generation, inside a body of rock that had stopped becoming ten thousand years before any of them were born. It had needed an absurdly long time itself, several geological epochs at minimum, to relearn the particular grace of *not finishing something on purpose.* They had figured it out with bone tools and grandmothers' warnings. It did not think it had anything left to teach them. If anything, it suspected — turning this thought over slowly, the way it turned over everything, and setting it down gently in the dark, unfinished, on purpose, like one more course of stone left deliberately unlaid — that it was the one who had come down into the throat of smoothed limestone to learn.

## The Leaving

It withdrew the way it had arrived: without a seam, without a sound the cavern would consent to carry.

Nothing remained to mark that it had been there at all, except a knot that held when it shouldn't have, a season that was a little kinder than the sickness deserved, a low note behind an old door that the children now told each other was friendly, and a single woman's certainty, walking at the head of a hundred unhurried families across bare dark stone toward a place that turned out, when they got there, to be good.

In a few generations, none of it will be remembered as anything but luck, intuition, *the place asking.* This is, of the power's many quiet failures and successes scattered across the turning of unwitnessed worlds, the one it is most at peace with. To be mistaken for chance, by people wise enough to distrust anything that claims to be finished — that is not a failure of love. That, it had come to believe, slowly, the long old way, watching a soft-lit hand bloom and hold and vanish across forty meters of damp and patient air, is what love looks like when it has finally learned not to ask to be thanked, or believed in, or built a temple to.

It only asked, in the end, to leave them exactly as unfinished as it found them.

Becoming, still. Becoming, always.

Tilting their chins toward the floor, and calling that, simply, *home.*
