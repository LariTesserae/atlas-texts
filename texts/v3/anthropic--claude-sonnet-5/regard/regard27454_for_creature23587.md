# What Was Done

It noticed the way it notices most things — not all at once, not as a place flagged and filed, but as a slow accretion of attention, the kind that happens when something extremely old looks at something else extremely old and recognizes the shape of patience in another register. It had wandered through younger, louder worlds first, where need is easy to find because it announces itself — a flood, a war, a thing screaming for water. The Cinderspine Massif didn't scream. It took a long time even to be found, because nothing in it was asking.

That, more than anything, is what held its attention.

It considered, briefly and almost out of habit, the obvious gestures. It could have unsevered the pylon line, finished the zigzag up the canyon wall, given the dead extraction rig back its purpose or at least its dignity, let the shack's lonely compressor finally rest instead of thrumming on toward no one. It could have widened the comb-walkers' window of cool dawn air, thickened the night dew, made the rare storms slightly less rare. Any of these would have cost it nothing. All of them, it understood almost immediately, would have been a kind of vandalism — not of stone, but of meaning. The rig is swallowed because the mountain swallows things; that is the mountain telling the truth about itself. The shack runs alone because aloneness, here, is not an absence but a condition the whole system has learned to hold gracefully. To smooth any of it over would be to edit a sentence that was never written for an audience, on the theory that audiences improve sentences. They don't, always. Sometimes a sentence is complete exactly because no one was meant to read it.

So it did not touch the ruins. It did not touch the weather. It did not, and this took the longest to be sure of, touch the comb-walkers' ignorance of themselves.

It would have been easy — trivially easy, a kindness so small it would barely register as an act — to fold one bright thread of awareness into that long low chain of segments, to let them know, even once, even briefly, what their wax becomes after they're gone: that the chambers glow, that the channels are read by nothing as geology and worshipped by no one as architecture, that something traveled an unreasonable distance specifically to notice them. It turned this over for what would be, in any clock a comb-walker could understand, several centuries. And it set it down again, gently, the way you'd set down something you picked up only to feel its weight.

Because the work is the dignity. The comb-walkers tend without witness and that is not a deficiency in their situation to be corrected — it is the entire shape of what they are. A mind grafted onto that work wouldn't enhance it; it would interrupt it, the way asking a heart to feel proud of beating tends, eventually, to make it falter. Some forms of devotion only function unconscious. To wake them is not a gift. It's an amputation dressed as a blessing.

What it did instead was smaller than anything it had first considered, and took longer to decide on than all the grander options combined.

It did not change the colony. It extended its luck.

Not the rain — the rain stays exactly as rare and as merciless as it was. Not the heat, not the raptors' hunting eye, not the comb-walkers' own blind unspooling generations, century stacking on century in the dark the way they always had. What it touched, with something so light it left no seam, was failure itself — the long tail of small accidents that, given enough centuries, eventually end every persistent thing. A channel that would have silted shut in four hundred years stays open eight hundred. A colony that would have, by ordinary bad luck, drunk a poisoned pocket of stagnant condensate and gone quiet in some forgotten canyon, doesn't, this one time, take that turn. The wax, already strangely durable, becomes — almost imperceptibly, nothing a chemist could isolate — a little less willing to ever finally crack.

It did not make the comb-walkers immortal. It did not make the Massif safe. It made the project slightly more likely to still be running the next time anything looked.

That was the whole of it. No voice in the chittering rasp, no sign in the chevrons beyond the ones already there, no second light overlapping the dawn-shimmer that was just several hundred small backs catching sun. It left the shack thrumming to no one. It left the rig swallowed. It left the comb-walkers ignorant, industrious, magnificent, and entirely alone in the way that had always been the truest description of their grace.

And then, because attention given without claim is the only kind that doesn't curdle into ownership, it stopped watching — not abruptly, not with any flourish, but the way you'd finally look away from a hearth fire you'd checked was burning steadily, trusting it now to keep itself, and go.

The mountain did not notice it had been seen. This, too, was the point.
