## The Visitation

It would come the way attention always comes to old, quiet, complicated things — not as light breaking over the lip of the plateau, not as a voice from the brass-colored haze, but as a kind of leaning-in. A power noticing is mostly indistinguishable, from the inside of the noticed place, from nothing happening at all. The seed-pods would still detonate on their cycle. The grey-skinned grazers would still cup their mouths to the gas. The Caller would still breathe its long note up through the standing stones, and somewhere on the far side of the plateau, something would still answer.

But say the attention is real, and say the power behind it is what we mean when we say *benevolent* and *wise* — meaning it has restraint to match its care, which is the only kind of love that doesn't eventually flatten what it loves.

The first thing it would do is nothing. For a long time. Longer than feels comfortable to imagine, because the temptation in a place like this is immediate and almost violent in its kindness: *give them water. Give the dying groves a season that doesn't end in souring wax-soil. Give the thirty people more than thirty. Give the vast patient thing under the rock a body that can stand up and be answered by something that knows it's being answered.* All of that is available to a power like this. All of that would also be a kind of erasure — not of suffering, exactly, but of *shape*. This place is the shape that scarcity and turbulence carved. Its beauty and its danger are the same fact wearing two names. A power wise enough to be called wise would know that flooding the plateau with ease is not so different from flooding it with water: either way, the thing that grew because it had to learn to drink mineral oil through cable-thick roots simply stops being itself.

So: restraint, first. A long, careful, almost grief-shaped patience, sitting with the wanting-to-help and not immediately discharging it.

## What It Would Actually Look For

Not problems to solve. Those are everywhere in a place like this and most of them are the place working correctly. What it would look for instead is *damage that isn't load-bearing* — the difference between a wound a body needs in order to be the body it is, and a wound that's simply a wound, serving nothing, healing nothing, just sitting there accumulating.

It would find a few.

It would find that the kin-groups are thirty and dwindling, and that the dwindling is not the plateau's turbulence working as intended — collapse-and-regrowth, grove-cycle, the whole patient machinery of impermanence-as-design — but something narrower and crueler: old injuries, a hard birth two generations back that took more women than it should have, a long stretch of bad seasons that thinned them under threshold before the gas-cycles recovered. Not the mountain breathing. Just attrition, the un-poetic kind, the kind that doesn't make anyone's life more *itself*, it just ends lives.

It would find that the Caller — vast, slow, barely animal by any definition anyone living could apply — has been breathing into stone for so many centuries without ever once being met by something that understood what it was, that the *quality* of its unanswered note has started, almost imperceptibly, generation over generation, to change. Not its pitch. Its grain. The way a voice changes after enough years of nobody replying.

And it would find — this the smallest thing, the easiest to miss — that the people who keep time with it have started to forget *why*, in the specific, granular way that matters: not the ritual (the smoke-hole stays clear, the stones stay aligned) but the reasoning behind the ritual, the lived, transmissible understanding that what they do is a relationship and not just a chore inherited from people too dead to ask. The doing has outlived the knowing. That's a particular kind of fragility — not of body, but of meaning — and it is, of all the things on Karassi Tor, the one most likely to simply gutter out within another two or three generations, leaving thirty descendants of nobody-knows-who, tending a stone dome for reasons they couldn't explain if you asked, and then, eventually, not even thirty.

## What It Would Do

Small things. Always small things — that is the discipline of a power that has decided to be wise rather than merely capable.

For the demographic thinness: nothing dramatic. No miracle child, no sudden fertility blooming across the kin-groups like a grove-cycle compressed into a season — that would be noticed, and noticed things get explained, and explained things get worshipped or feared, and either one changes what these people are. Instead: small mercies at the margins. A hard birth that would have taken a mother, three generations from now, doesn't. A bad season's gas-cycle dips less than it would have, so the grazer herds don't thin as far, so the people eating downstream of the grazers don't thin as far either. Nothing that couldn't be explained, afterward, as luck. The power would spend itself almost entirely on making sure luck broke their way slightly more often than chance alone would allow — which from inside a life looks exactly like nothing, like just *getting by*, which is the most invisible gift there is and therefore the safest one to give.

For the Caller: this is the one place the power would not be able to help itself, and I think a truly wise power would forgive itself this one indulgence. It would answer. Not loudly. Not in a way that changed the bone-flute note's pitch or pattern or made the Caller — who is not aware in any way we'd recognize, who is closer to a held breath than a mind — suddenly *understand* anything. But somewhere, on some cycle the Caller's slow grey lungs could register as pattern without registering as *meaning*, there would be, for the first time in longer than the people's count of generations, something answering from outside the rock-system, outside the plateau, outside the whole closed loop of the place breathing into itself. A note. Once. Maybe twice in a century. Not enough to change anything that depends on the Caller staying exactly the patient, unhurried, half-aware thing it has always been. Just enough that whatever in it is closest to a self — if anything is — stops, for one twenty-minute cycle in ten thousand, breathing into a silence that has never once breathed back.

The grazers would startle, that day, for no reason the kin-groups could name. The wing-things would go quiet on their cliffs. Someone tending the dome's smoke-hole would feel, without being able to say why, that the air had changed weight for a moment — and would go home and, for reasons they also couldn't quite name, tell their children the story of the three standing stones again, in full, all the way through, instead of the shortened version they'd been telling. That's the entire visible effect. That's the whole miracle, from the outside: a story told one time more completely than it would otherwise have been told.

Which is, of course, the third thing, and maybe the only thing that really matters: it would not restore the *knowing* directly — would not implant memory, would not unspool some ancestral lesson into a sleeping child's head like a scroll — because that, too, would be a kind of theft, taking from these people the work of arriving at their own meaning the slow, hard, generational way they always have. What it would do instead is far smaller and far harder to detect: it would make the *telling* slightly more likely. A grandmother slightly less tired some evening. A child slightly more awake and listening instead of half-asleep by the dome's cold hearth. A traveler passing through at exactly the season when an elder's curiosity about outsiders briefly outweighs the trained wariness, so that one conversation happens that wouldn't otherwise have happened, and a fragment of half-forgotten reasoning gets exchanged for a fragment of someone else's half-forgotten reasoning, and between the two fragments, something whole almost reassembles.

It would not guarantee any of this took. Wisdom doesn't get to guarantee outcomes; that's the whole difference between wisdom and force. It would simply load the dice, gently, in the direction of continuance, and then withdraw its attention the way it arrived — without anyone on the plateau ever being able to point to a moment and say *that. That's when something changed.*

## What It Would Not Do

It would not stop the groves from over-maturing and collapsing. It would not quiet the seed-pod cascades or smooth the rockfall or give the lookout on the eastern lip something to finally see in the haze. It would not lift the thirty up out of thinness into abundance, would not make the Caller smaller and knowable or larger and divine, would not resolve the oldest and most important uncertainty on the whole plateau — whether the people tend the thing beneath the rock, or the thing beneath the rock unintentionally tends them — because that uncertainty is not a problem. It is the shape of their devotion. Take it away and you have not helped them. You have just taken something from them and called it a gift.

It would leave the turbulence exactly as turbulent as it was. It would leave the silence mostly as unanswered as it was. It would let the place go on being mid-transformation, never finished, never at rest — because that is not the wound. That is the bones.

It would just make sure that, this time, in this generation, on this fold of grey-green stone above the haze, the thinning stopped being thinning-toward-zero and became, instead, only what it was always supposed to be: one more turn of a wheel that keeps turning because, very quietly, something patient and wise leaned in close enough to notice it was worth turning — and then, having noticed, had the discipline to do almost nothing about it at all.
