The power comes the way the water does in that place: not falling, not announced, just present where it wasn't, seeping up through whatever fissure in attention let it in. It does not arrive as light or voice or visitation. It arrives as a noticing — the kind that takes in not just the town but the *argument* the town has been having with hardship for three hundred years, and the truce it reached, and the cost of that truce, paid out in finger-joints and failed upper registers and children who learn mouths before letters.

It watches for a long time before it considers doing anything at all. This is the first discipline of real benevolence: to resist mistaking difference for damage. It would be easy — appallingly easy, for a power of any size — to look at this place and see only deficits to correct. Restore the hearing. Silence the engines. Explain the water. Cap the plinths and let people stop flinching at their own children's questions. A lesser kindness, a kindness in a hurry, does exactly this, and leaves behind a town that is quieter, gentler, and no longer itself — the hand-signs gone slack and unnecessary, the dry economical humor with nothing left to economize against, the cistern-wardens robbed of the listening that gave their strangeness its shape and dignity. A power that loves only outcomes will do this. A power that loves *beings* does not.

So it looks longer. It watches a foundryman missing two finger-joints sign *not yet* to his daughter across a market square with a tenderness that would not survive being spoken aloud — that depends, somehow, on the conservation of breath, on the fact that this particular gesture is too important to waste on something as cheap as sound. It watches the old ones gather at the cistern mouths at dusk not despite their deafness but *through* it, the silence-within-the-noise becoming, for them, a kind of privacy the louder world never gets to have. It watches a child of four read a complete sentence off her mother's mouth and feels something that in a smaller power would be called pride. It watches the dead carried down the iron stairways into the same dark the pumps draw from, washed first in the same water that blooms chalky on every living hand, and understands that this town has made of its harshest fact — *the depths take and the depths give, and no one has ever fully known the difference* — not a wound but a liturgy.

And it finds, at the bottom of all this looking, the plinths. It understands them the way the wardens understand them: not with explanation, but with the same wordless deference the town itself maintains. There is something under those bare stones, older than the engineers, older than the seabed's memory of being a sea — not malevolent, not benevolent, simply *other*, with its own claim on continuance, its own throat that needs, for reasons no instrument will ever fully resolve, to stay uncovered. The power recognizes a fellow ancientness when it meets one, and does not presume that its own benevolence outranks the older arrangement. It does not lift the stones. It does not look under them either, though it could. Respect, here, includes not knowing.

So this is what it does, in the end — not nothing, but very little, and all of it small enough to leave no fingerprints:

It is there, fractionally, in the half-second before the worn cable on the eastern funicular would have given way under a too-full evening car, lending the strand just enough integrity to hold one more season, long enough for the maintenance crew to notice the fray on their own and re-lay it properly, so that the saving feels — correctly — like their own competence and not a miracle. It does not want to be a miracle. Miracles erode the thing it's trying to protect, which is a people's trust in their own hands.

It is there in the timing of certain ordinary mercies: the winter that should have been the hard one, statistically, that turns out milder by a margin nobody can account for; the bout of fever that runs through the lower terraces and somehow spares the engine-tenders' children at a rate just slightly better than the numbers would predict, not zero, never zero, because a town that stopped burying its young entirely would stop knowing how to hold grief, and grief-holding is one of the things this place does with a competence the power has rarely seen equaled. It does not remove death. It only, here and there, declines to let it be worse than the town can carry.

It is there, perhaps most of all, in attention paid to small unwitnessed beauties, as if witnessing were itself a kind of tending: it notices the moths at the relief valves the way the old ones do, the pale gathering hush of them in the gold dusk steam, and something in that noticing — though no one could say why, though no instrument would register it — makes the spiraling go on a little longer some evenings, the steam linger a little warmer, the light hold brass a little past its hour. A small mercy with no recipient but the watching itself. The town will never know this happened. It isn't for them to know. It's a private fidelity, the power's own version of leaning close to speak.

And once — only once, in a manner so indirect it could never be traced to any single cause — it loosens, very slightly, the grip of a particular silence: not the hearing, never the hearing, that stays exactly as it has always been, earned and structural and theirs. But a warden, old, solitary, crouched at a cistern mouth on an ordinary evening doing what wardens do, listening to a breath of cool air arrive in time with engines three terraces down, finds himself for one held moment *understanding* — not in words, not in any way he could sign to another person, but completely — why the water comes, why the plinths are silent, why the whole patient machine of the town has always somehow known what not to ask. He doesn't tell anyone. He couldn't if he tried; the understanding doesn't survive translation any better than birdsong would survive this town's air. But he goes home calmer than he's been in forty years, and his hands, when he signs goodnight to his granddaughter, are unusually steady, and that steadiness is the only trace the power leaves of itself anywhere in three hundred years of attention.

Because the deepest kindness it can offer a people who have already done the hard, dignified work of making a life inside an unkillable noise is not rescue. It is to let them keep being who the noise and the salt and the patient upward water made them — and only, very occasionally, to lean against the structure from outside, in the small places, the way a hand might rest for a moment on a wall to feel whether it's still standing, and find that, yes — still — it is.
