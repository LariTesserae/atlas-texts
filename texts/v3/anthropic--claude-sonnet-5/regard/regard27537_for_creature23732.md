The power comes the way light comes to a place that has had none in a very long time — not as an arrival, but as a noticing, slow and total, the way a held breath finally releases. It does not announce itself. It has no need to. It simply turns its attention toward the Hollow Gardens, and for a while — a while that means nothing in that place, where a while can be a century and no one minds — it only watches.

It watches the canopy-skimmers wheel like weather. It listens to the towers scream their dusk-that-never-ends, district answering district, an argument with no remaining audience. It traces the herd-beasts' decade-long drift across the swelled stone, and it follows, with something like fondness, the low groaning calls that rise from the unlit fissures and are never answered, or are answered so slowly that the asking and the answer belong to different lifetimes.

And it finds the one who tends.

It finds it at a collapsed doorway, working a stone back into a gap it has worked before — not with strength, because there is not much strength left in those long, knuckled hands, but with the unhurried certainty of a body that has done this so many times the doing has outlived the reason for it. It watches the being lay a husk and a bone in a pattern that means nothing to anyone now, not even, perhaps, to itself. It watches it sit afterward in the dark windowsill of a tower that was once — that was, surely, once — a home, and listen to the roar of all that animal life filling the silence its own kind left behind, and it understands, in the way only a very old and very patient power can understand, that this is grief with the words worn off it. Devotion with the memory worn off it. A hand that no longer knows what building was for, but has not stopped offering its hands.

The power considers, as such powers do, the large gestures.

It could relight the place — could pour sun-memory down through the dead crust and wake whatever sleeps in the seed-dust of a civilization. It could hand the being back its language, its history, the names of its dead, the purpose of its cairns, all at once, a flood of restored meaning. It could go further still and simply finish the work itself — raise the fallen towers in a night, fill the dark windows with light and voice and the sound of more hands like this one's, undo every collapse with the indifferent ease of something that does not need stone to obey gravity at all.

It does not do any of these things.

It turns each one over and sets each one down, gently, the way you'd set down something beautiful that nonetheless does not belong in the room you're standing in. To restore the memory would be to hand back the grief whole and fresh, with no eons left to wear it smooth — a cruelty dressed as a gift. To fill the towers with company chosen by a power rather than grown by the slow blind logic of the place would be a kindness built on top of the place rather than inside it, and the place would feel the seam, even if the being never could name what it felt. To finish the work — to raise the towers in a night — would take from the being the only shape its days have left: the going-out, the lifting, the laying-down, the small offering against entropy that is, perhaps, the last sentence of a language whose grammar has otherwise been lost. To take that away would not be mercy. It would be a second extinction, gentler-looking than the first, and no kinder for it.

So the power does what wisdom, pressed far enough down, usually decides to do. It does almost nothing. And it does it very, very carefully.

It does not speak to the being, or show itself, or change anything the being would notice as a change. Instead, it goes, quietly, into the grain of the world the being already moves through, and it makes a few small things true that did not have to be true.

The stone at the doorway, the one the being can no longer quite lift on the first try — on this particular evening it comes up a little easier than it should, and the being does not wonder why, only feels, faintly, grateful, and goes on. The shelter the being clears beneath a leaning lintel, clearing it for no reason it could explain even to itself, becomes — three slow seasons later, long after the being has wandered on and forgotten the doorway entirely — the exact gap a herd of young ground-beasts shoulder into when a ceiling-fall thunders down the next valley over. They live because of a doorway they will never know was cleared, by hands that will never know they were saved.

And once — only once, across all the long unhurried years the power allows itself to watch — it bends two paths that did not have to bend, and out along a causeway half-reclaimed by root-moss, the being passes another shape like itself, coming the other way, carrying its own husk-and-bone pattern toward its own doorway. They stop. They look. Neither has words left for what this is. They stand a while in the roar of all that indifferent, glorious animal noise, two slow old creatures regarding each other across a silence neither one breaks, and then each turns back to its own work, because that is what there is to return to.

It is not a reunion. It is not a rescue. It is barely anything at all, weighed against the scale of the cavern, the age of the stone, the roar that never stops.

But it happened. It was real. And the power, satisfied with a satisfaction that has nothing of pride in it, withdraws the way it came — without announcement, without residue — and leaves the Hollow Gardens exactly as patient, exactly as loud, exactly as unfinished as it found them.

Somewhere in the dim, a tower loses one more stone to the centuries.

Somewhere else, a hand sets one stone back.
