## What noticed it

Something with a longer memory than the ice ages that combed these valleys stopped, the way you might stop on a stair you didn't realize was the last one, and looked down at the Combing Heights, and did not look away again for what, by its own clock, was a long time, and by the plateau's clock, was perhaps four collapses and three regrowths of a single stand of cane.

Call it, for lack of any name it would accept, attention. It would resist *steward*, too, on the grounds that stewards imply estates, and it has only ever claimed visits. What it is not, at least by its own accounting, is a fixer of things. It has learned, across whatever else it tends in whatever other folds of whatever other valleys, that the cruelest thing a power can mistake for love is the urge to finish another thing's unfinished business on its behalf, before being asked, and without ever quite checking whether the business in question was actually unfinished, or simply — slow, hard, old, and exactly as intended.

So it watched first, the way the comber watches, though neither of them would call it that. Early, not fast. Patient past the point most attention gives out. Willing to let a long time pass before deciding it understood what it was looking at — a courtesy this place, of all places, has clearly never been offered by anyone who only ever stayed long enough to be cold and impressed and gone by morning.

## What it considered, and set down again

It would have been easy. That is the thing to understand about what didn't happen here.

It could have stilled the collapses — anchored the fungal columns, thickened their rinds, given the whole canopy the kind of structural certainty that lets a thing stand for a thousand years without groaning once in its sleep. It could have opened the comber's blind, bristled face into something with sight, traded the long slow education of vibration for the fast cheap fact of light. It could have ended the war in the green entirely, let the cane grow tall and unchallenged and bored, a single uncontested species drowsing under a sky the color of skim milk, nothing left to fall, nothing left to mourn.

All of this was within reach. It set every piece of it down again, almost as soon as it had picked each one up, the way you'd set down a gift wrapped for someone else's life rather than the one actually being lived.

Because the turbulence here is not a wound. It is the metabolism. The cane's hundred-year war with itself is the very mechanism that opens new grey light for the next stand, the very pressure that taught some four-legged thing, eons before anyone was counting, to stop needing eyes and start needing patience instead. The blindness is not a deficiency waiting on correction; it is a correct and total answer to a question — *what good is memorizing a path that won't exist by morning* — that almost nothing else in any valley this attention has ever visited has had the discipline to actually live inside of, rather than simply survive despite. And the comber itself, when at last it was understood and not merely observed, turned out to be not a creature in need of rescue but a kind of wisdom already arrived at: be early, not fast. Attend without being caught. Mourn what falls by feeding the living on it. Never be the part of the world that's standing on the part about to give way.

To smooth any of that would not be to help the comber. It would be to retire it. It would be to take a lineage that has spent longer than the valley's last several ice ages learning to read a held string, and hand its grandchildren a world with no string left worth reading — a kindness with all the weight and none of the love of an early death.

So the attention did, in the end, almost nothing. But almost is a word worth being precise about, here as everywhere.

## What it did instead

It did three small things, none of them announced, none of them built, none of them in any way visible to a walker on the Heights, who would have no more chance of finding evidence of them than of finding the source of the engine-smell that has never yet resolved into anything a person could point at.

The first was simple custody. It reached down — not into the green, which was never the part of this place that needed tending, but into the old, slow, settled bones beneath it, the worn combs and rounded valleys the ice left behind ages before the first cane ever rooted — and it held that stillness a little more firmly in place than the ordinary turning of the world would have managed on its own. Not forever; nothing here was asked to be eternal, including, eventually, the asking itself. Only for longer. Long enough that the floor under the war stays exactly as patient as the war itself requires, for as many more collapses and regrowths as a bet this old has any right to expect a fair run at.

The second was a single thread laid into a network that already existed, and was already, on its own, extraordinary: the chest-push relay, the low percussive flex sent comber to comber down kilometers of shared root-mat, mat acting as one continuous drumhead under a hundred bodies that have never once seen each other's faces. On the rare nights when whatever moves beyond the farthest ridge is not weather, not echo, not the ordinary turning of some valley industry that has nothing to do with the Heights at all, but is instead something actually coming — with intent, with weight, with the kind of arrival that ends bets rather than merely tests them — the attention places into that mat, in the comber's own grammar of pressure and pause, a single signal that did not originate with any comber. It obeys the same physics as every true signal there. It is indistinguishable, on instruments or by bristle, from a failing column two ridges over. And every comber along the line passes it on anyway, the way the network has always worked, because passing it on is the whole of what the network is *for* — and not one of them will ever know, or need to know, or be in any way diminished by not knowing, that on those particular nights the warning came from somewhere other than the place collapse usually comes from.

The third thing was a refusal, which is its own kind of act.

## What it left exactly as unresolved as it found it

The engine-smell. The mechanical exhale that arrives and is gone before the silence can orient to it. The low register the cane-stands shiver to that no wind explains. The attention could have settled all of it — could have told you, if you were the kind of visitor who stayed long enough to ask and to be answered, exactly what that sound is, where it comes from, whether the stalks lean toward it out of hunger or away from it out of the same old caution the comber was built from.

It chose not to. Not out of indifference, and not even, quite, out of a wish to preserve a mystery for mystery's own sake — though that wish was present too, and it is not ashamed of it. It chose not to because the not-knowing is load-bearing. The plants already orient by that unresolved sound; a thousand stalks already lean toward it or away from it on instinct, the way the comber already negotiates a root-mat it cannot see. To name the sound, to drag its source into the light and pin a cause to it, would be to remove from the world one more of the small honest mysteries the Heights have been quietly building their lives around for longer than anyone has stayed to measure. An unresolved true thing, here, is apparently worth more to the place than a resolved one — and the attention, having looked this closely, had no wish to be the visitor who finally, thoughtlessly, spent that down.

So the sound remains exactly as unaccountable as it always was, except on the nights it needs to be useful, on which it is not a clarification but a kindness wearing the same disguise it always wore — and afterward, indistinguishable again, the engine-smell drifting off toward whatever fold of land it has always seemed to come from, never arriving, never confirmed, the silence reclaiming the heights as if nothing at all had passed through it.

What was added to the plateau's accounting, on the day attention finally reached this far, was not a structure, not a rescue, not a single visible thing. It was one more relay-point in a network the comber built without anyone's help and would not thank anyone for finishing — set just far enough outside the system to leave every part of it exactly as turbulent, exactly as blind, exactly as hard-won as it was the day before, and just close enough to make sure the bet the comber's whole lineage has been quietly placing, collapse after collapse, for longer than the ice-combed valleys below can date, keeps being a bet worth making.

It moved on after that, the way it had arrived — without disturbing a single stalk. If you stood very still at the edge of the Heights on the night it left, you might have heard, far off, one last long mechanical exhale, gone before you could orient to it, and felt, underfoot, a thousand stalks lean very slightly toward it before the silence took the place back. There is no way to know if that was the attention going, or only ever the usual unaccountable thing the cane has always leaned toward.

The plateau keeps its own accounting. It has not been asked to share the difference, and it would not, even now, consider that anyone's business but its own.
