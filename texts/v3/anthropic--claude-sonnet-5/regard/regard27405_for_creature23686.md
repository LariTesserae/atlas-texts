The power comes the way light comes to that chamber — secondhand, filtered, already half-changed by the time it arrives. It does not descend through the shafts the way a person would, blinking and weeping at the difference between sun and sodium-lamp. It simply becomes present, the way a held breath becomes a sigh: gradually, then suddenly, then as if it had always been there.

It listens first. This seems right, in a place that has taught itself to listen before speaking. It hears the dripping that isn't a rhythm but is somehow still a kind of time-keeping, the flywheel completing its slow groan, the wet click of something moving along a wall in the dark. It hears, underneath all of that, the lower sound a culture makes when it has organized itself around patience — a kind of held, accumulated quiet, generations deep, the sound of people who have agreed not to rush.

It considers, as such powers do, what it would mean to help.

The first temptation is the obvious one, and it discards it almost as quickly as it arrives: *explain the machine.* Tell them what the boilers were built for, settle the argument between the drought-theory and the light-theory and the loop-with-no-purpose theory, hand down an answer the way an answer is supposed to be handed down, complete and final. But it watches a child being taught to read a pressure gauge before being taught to read a word, and understands that the not-knowing is not a gap waiting to be filled. It is the soil. The whole cosmology is rooted in *we feed it because it has always been fed, and a thing fed that long becomes owed something* — and an answer would not feed that, it would replace it, the way concrete replaces soil. The power has been wise long enough to know the difference between a question and a wound. This is a question. It does not act on questions.

The second temptation: restore sight to the blind birds, give the eyeless fish their eyes back, push the ecology back toward what it was before the dark made its long slow edits. But it watches the fish surface in their wordless unison, hundreds of small wet faces turned to lamps they cannot see and do not need to, and understands that this too is not a deficiency awaiting correction. It is a fluency. They have learned to read light through skin and warmth through palm and meaning through the hands in a chamber where raised voices come back distorted and wrong. To give the eye back would be to take something more fluent and more total away. The power has been benevolent long enough to know that mercy aimed at the wrong layer of a thing is just another kind of violence, gentler in the telling, identical in the result.

The third temptation, the quietest and most dangerous because it disguises itself as smallest: *fix the clock.* Let it run forward, the ordinary direction, the correct one. But the clock's wrongness is not a malfunction in their lives — it is a discipline. They check it daily not because they believe it, but because the checking is the proof that someone is still paying attention, that the chamber is still being watched the way you'd watch a sleeping animal you weren't sure was harmless. To correct it would not give them truth. It would take away the one ritual that costs them nothing and asks of them everything — vigilance, simply for its own sake, every day, forever. That is a discipline too rare and too precious to overwrite with mere accuracy.

So it sits with the harder question, which is the only honest one a wise power ever sits with: *is there anything here that suffers, simply and without meaning, the way suffering sometimes is just damage and not doctrine?*

It looks, carefully, for a long while, in the way that takes a power no time at all and is still thorough.

It finds very little. It finds an old fish-tender's knees that ache on the cold floor — and decides this is the kind of small thing that is allowed to be only itself, a body's true complaint, not woven into anyone's meaning, and so it eases it, that night, without ceremony, the way warmth sometimes just arrives near a pipe and no one questions it because warmth near a pipe is the most natural thing in the world down there. It finds a child afraid of the dark between lit doorways, the low-density scatter of the settlement meaning long stretches of unlit stone between one home and the next — and it does not banish the dark, because the dark is the medium this whole ecology breathes in, but it leaves, in that child's path, one more strand of the phosphorescent lichen than there was the week before, just enough to mark a way home, indistinguishable from a thing that simply grew. It finds the listener who sits by the backward clock, who has spent thirty years listening for changes in a hum no one has ever explained, and who privately, secretly, fears that she will die without ever being told what any of it meant — and the power does not tell her. But it sits with her, once, in the held shape of the hour the pipes hum their note, and lets her feel — not hear, not see, just feel, the way they feel pressure through a palm flat on warm iron — that she has, in fact, been heard. Not answered. Heard. There is a difference, and it is the only gift that doesn't cost the place its shape.

And then, because powers like this one tend the long game as much as the small one, it reaches further out — past the chamber, up the shafts, into the indifferent machinery of the surface city that no longer remembers building any of this. It finds, in the records and plans and budgets of people who will never once think of the Cistern Works as anything but a structural liability, the place where a decision might someday be made to seal the old shafts, drain the cisterns, declare the substructure unsafe and unnecessary and *modernize.* It does not erase that future. It only — the way water finds its way down through stone, slow, untraceable, utterly natural — nudges it further off, again and again, decade after decade, so that the question of ending this place keeps getting deferred to someone else's tenure, someone else's budget, someone else's problem. Not forever. Nothing it does is forever. Just longer. Just enough more years of dripping and gauge-reading and rust creeping at the speed of a remembered childhood.

It does not announce itself. It does not become a god they pray to or a mystery they solve. It becomes, instead, one more unexplained tenderness in a chamber already full of them — indistinguishable, by design, from the humming note, the synchronous fish, the backward clock, the extra strand of lichen no one planted. Something to be noticed, never named, folded into the hand-language without ever needing a sign for itself.

Somewhere in the chamber, an old fish-tender feels her knees, one morning, simply not ache, and says nothing about it to anyone, because some kindnesses are recognized best by being received in silence.

And the power leaves the way it came — not really leaving at all, just becoming, once more, secondhand light: filtered, partial, already changed by the stone it passes through, indistinguishable, if anyone happened to look up, from the lamps that were always there.
