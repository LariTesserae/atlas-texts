The attention arrives the way the fog does—not as event, not as announcement, but as a gradual thickening of presence that the plateau might not even register as different from its ordinary weather. It moves through the terraces the way the cloud moves through them: not above, not separate, but *through*, finding the same hairline cracks the water finds, settling into the same stone basins.

And it watches. For a long time, it only watches.

There is an old reflex in things that are powerful and kind—an itch toward repair, toward addition, toward making *more* of what is found to be good. It is not a malicious reflex. It comes from love, mostly. You see a quiet place and your love wants to fill it with birdsong. You see a slow people and your love wants to give them ease, give them speed, give them some bright unlooked-for abundance to bloom in their thin soil pockets. You see the silence and some part of you, even the wisest part, briefly mistakes it for absence.

So the attention sits with that reflex first, the way you'd sit with a guest who has overstayed slightly, waiting for it to tire itself out. It watches a fern unfurl in a joint of basalt over the course of nine days. It watches a woman re-set three stones in a terrace wall that have shifted only a finger's-width in a decade, working with the unhurried precision of someone correcting a thing that was never really wrong, only slightly less right than it could be. It watches a child learn to read a dark mineral stain on rock as *widen this* and a beaded trail as *leave it*, watches the knowledge pass not through explanation but through a hand laid briefly over a smaller hand, a nod, nothing said.

It listens to the quality of the quiet—really listens, the way the people themselves have learned to pitch their voices to the plateau's particular acoustics—and finds, eventually, that the quiet is not a wound. It is not a city waiting to be born, not a wilderness waiting to be tamed, not a sorrow waiting to be comforted. It is a chord that has found its resolution and is simply, patiently, holding it. The absence of birdsong is not a lack any more than the absence of mountains is a lack. It is just the shape this particular peace happens to take.

This is the harder wisdom, the kind that doesn't announce itself as wisdom at the time: that the deepest respect you can offer a completed thing is to not complete it further. That to arrive in a place at peace with its own conditions and immediately begin improving those conditions is a kind of violence wearing gentle clothes. The people of Anhuwen have built, across some unrecorded span of generations, an entire civilization's worth of meaning around slowness, around small dense knots of company between long stretches of solitary wet walking, around reading rock instead of sky, around a stillness that isn't emptiness but achievement. To give them birds would be to take something from them in the act of giving—the particular self each of them has grown into, shaped by silence the way a hand is shaped by a tool.

So the attention does not give them birds. It does not warm the fog away or deepen the paddies or hurry the soil toward richness it was never going to have on this geological timescale anyway. It does not visit any of them in dream with grand pronouncement, does not bend down and explain itself, does not ask to be worshipped or even noticed. Whatever it does, it decides early, must vanish back into the place's own logic—must become, at most, something the people can fold into the same inherited, hand-to-hand, eye-to-eye knowledge they already keep about seep-patterns and stone. Not a miracle. A *teaching*. The two are not the same, here. A miracle interrupts a way of life. A teaching joins it.

It finds, eventually, two small places to rest its hand.

The first is grief, ordinary and old, carried the way the people carry most things—without much outward sign, folded into a stillness of face that a stranger might mistake for blankness. There is a man, not young, who lost a sister on a particular terrace edge above the third cistern, years ago, to a fall that was not dramatic, the way nothing here is dramatic—a wrenched step, wet basalt, the kind of ordinary risk the whole culture has built itself around tolerating without anxiety. He has tolerated it too, outwardly. The attention sits with him one evening while he eats in his covered walkway listening to the drip-sound, and it does not remove the grief, because grief that has been carried that long and that well has become part of the architecture of him, the way the terraces have become part of the architecture of the rock, and ripping it out now would leave a gap shaped wrong for anything else to fill. Instead it loosens, very slightly, the single knot in it that still catches—the specific replaying image, the specific self-blame for not having walked beside her that morning—and lets the rest settle into something closer to what the plateau itself does with old wounds: not healed, but sealed over with moss in a season, smooth enough now to be touched without bleeding.

The second is the stone itself, at the edge above the third cistern, and two or three other places like it scattered across the terraces where the slope and the wear pattern and the particular angle of runoff have made the basalt, over time, just slightly more lethal than its neighbors. The attention does not flatten these edges or rail them or soften the rock's basic nature. That would be a foreign hand, recognizable as such, a wrongness in the texture of things. Instead, very quietly, it leaves a vein—pale, mineral, faint, the kind of thing that could easily be mistaken for a natural seam in the tuff, because it more or less is one now—running along the safer line of each dangerous step. Nothing is announced. No one is told. But within a generation the old women teaching the young to read the rock will have folded this pale vein into the same unspoken curriculum as everything else: *that lighter stone there, step on that one, not the dark one beside it.* It will not be remembered as a gift. It will be remembered, if it's remembered at all, as something the plateau has simply always done, one more small mercy indistinguishable from geology. Fewer wrenched ankles. Fewer mornings like the one that took the man's sister. The culture's relationship with risk—its patience, its lack of haste, its trust that nothing here punishes hesitation—remains completely intact, because nothing about how they live has needed to change. Only the floor beneath the living has gotten, by some unremarked fraction, kinder.

And one more thing, the smallest, the closest to pure extravagance: one night, deep in a dry season when the fog has been thin for three days running, the attention does nothing at all except *not call the fog back* a few hours longer than usual. It lets the sky over Anhuwen go all the way dark and all the way clear, for the first time in the memory of anyone living there, and the stars come down close and many and astonishing over the wet black terraces, throwing a faint silver into every seep and trickle, doubling themselves in every shallow basin that's usually too disturbed by dripping to hold a reflection. People come out of their covered walkways without speaking. Children are lifted onto shoulders. Voices, which usually carry strangely far and then get swallowed by fog, carry strangely far and are not swallowed by anything at all, and for a few hours the whole plateau is one long quiet murmur of people remarking to each other, unhurried, spaced with long unembarrassed pauses, on a thing none of them have words yet for.

By morning the fog is back, arriving mid-morning the way it always has, dissolving by late afternoon the way it always will, and nothing about the working order of the place has changed. The channels still carry the runoff they were cut to carry. The paddies still flood and drain on their old patient schedule. The pale veins in three or four particular stones will, slowly, over years, become simply *the way that stone is read*, the way the dark mineral stain has always meant *widen this* and the bead-and-trail has always meant *leave it.*

But there will be a story, told at dusk sometimes when the fog has burned off early and the light goes long and grey and people sing a little—a story about the one clear night, the close stars, the doubled sky in the basins—and it will be told the way they tell everything here: low-voiced, unhurried, without need of embellishment, because the thing itself was enough. It will not be remembered as visitation. It will be remembered as weather, the way the rest of their lives are weather—generous locally, unremarkable in the larger pattern, woven in without disturbing the equilibrium it briefly, lovingly, touched.

And the attention withdraws the way it arrived: not as departure, not as event, simply a gradual thinning, indistinguishable, in the end, from fog lifting off wet stone.
