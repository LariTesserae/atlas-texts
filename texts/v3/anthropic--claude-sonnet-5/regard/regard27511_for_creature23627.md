It comes the way the wind comes — without announcement, finding the fissure on its own, descending through smooth red stone with nothing so crude as footsteps. It does not need to drop down. It has always, in some sense, already been here, the way a held note is already present in the silence before it's struck. But it chooses, for the sake of the work ahead, to arrive — to take a position, a vantage, a kind of attention focused enough to count as presence.

It stands a long while at the lip of the bowl before doing anything at all.

This is, you should understand, the hardest discipline a benevolent power can practice: the discipline of watching suffering and not immediately moving to end it. Because the first impulse — and it is a good impulse, an impulse that comes from love and not from carelessness — is to fix. To look at this hollow scoured place, no rain, no rivers, no animal cry, children raised half-blind to their own sun, an entire people sweating themselves into vegetable economy just to survive a heat that should not be survivable at all — and to simply lift the curse. Send rain. Cool the stone. Call birds down into the bowl and let them nest in the heat-vines and fill the silence with something other than wind dressed up as music.

It considers this. It holds the thought the way you'd hold a stone to see its weight.

And then it listens — really listens, the way the Cistern people themselves listen, with the whole body, not just the ear — and it hears what is actually being sung down here, underneath the drone, underneath the clappers and the triangles and the dragged clay jars. It hears a wedding being adjudicated by harmony instead of money. It hears a child being taught to read the wind for cracking stone the way another child somewhere above is taught to read clouds. It hears, in the alcoves cut near the wind-throats, generations of the dead still moving air, still — by the only physics available to them, the physics of belief made load-bearing — present in the chord their descendants live inside. It hears an economy built not around what the world gave them but around what the world refused them, turned inside out, made into craft, made into love, made into music that took ten thousand years to tune.

It understands, then, that this is not a wound waiting to be closed. It is a structure. And you do not heal a cathedral by demolishing it gently.

So it does not bring rain.

It does not cool the stone, or call down birds, or open the bowl to the full mercy of the sun. It does not explain to the heat-succulent guilds that there is an easier way to drink. It does not loosen the grip of the heat that has shaped these people's skin and eyes and ears into instruments for exactly this world and no other, because to do that gently would still be to do it, and what it would be doing is erasing them in the name of saving them — making them comfortable at the cost of making them no longer themselves. It has seen other powers make that mistake, in other hollows, on other worlds, and call it kindness afterward. It will not call it kindness now.

What it does instead is smaller, and harder to see, and it takes longer than a flood or a miracle, because real care usually does.

It finds the fragile threads — not the hardship, which is load-bearing, but the breaks that would be irrecoverable, the losses with no way back. It finds the old tuner on the ninth terrace, the one who has not yet found an apprentice steady enough to trust with the listen-stick, whose particular cadence — a way of cutting a wind-throat slightly off true so that it cries instead of sings, used only for the alcoves of the dead — exists nowhere else and nowhere written down. And it does not give that tuner more time, because time is not its gift to give so cheaply, and a life extended past its season would be its own kind of theft. But it makes sure — quietly, the way a draft makes sure a flame leans one direction rather than another — that on three separate evenings, the old tuner's least promising apprentice, a restless girl who has always thought she preferred the press-houses to the rock face, finds herself unable to sleep, and goes walking, and ends up sitting at the old woman's feet asking the question that needs to be asked before it's too late to answer it.

It finds the guild on the fourth terrace whose dew-vines have begun, almost imperceptibly, to fruit a season early, a slow drift that no one has noticed yet because no one expects the world to change and so no one is watching for it. It cannot stop the drift — that belongs to causes far larger than this bowl, drafts moving in skies the Cistern people will never see — but it makes sure that this year, of all years, the eldest daughter of that guild happens to press a leaf two days before she normally would, out of nothing more than a passing impulse, and finds the dew sweeter and stranger than expected, and mentions it to her mother, who mentions it to the others, so that when the real drift comes, years from now, there is already a memory in the guild of what early fruiting looks like, and no one panics, and no one starves for an extra week while they figure it out from scratch.

It does not touch the silence above the rim. That belongs to the surface, and the surface has its own slow lessons to teach, and the Cistern people's fear of flatness is, after all, simply love wearing the mask of fear, the way most real fears are.

It does not bring back the insects, the birds, the small scuttling things that should be here and are not. It looks at that absence for a long time, longer than at anything else in the bowl, because that absence troubles it — feels less like a feature of the place than like a wound somebody else made, long ago, and never told anyone about. It does not fix this either. Not yet. Some wounds need to be understood before they can be touched without making them worse, and this one is old enough, and strange enough, that the power simply marks it — sets a kind of attention on it the way you'd tie a thread around a finger to remember — and resolves to come back when it understands more, rather than act now and regret the shape of its mercy later.

And then, before it goes — because it will go; presence like this is not meant to linger, only to visit, the way grace generally only visits — it does the one thing that is wholly its own to give, the one gift that costs the structure of the place nothing and asks nothing of it in return.

It adds a note.

Not a new wind-throat, not a new stone, nothing anyone could point to or tune or pass down as craft. Just a single thread woven into the existing chord, so subtle that no living tuner will ever isolate it, audible only as a faint and constant sweetness underneath the drone that was not there before and will not announce itself as anything other than the bowl finally, after ten thousand years, sounding very slightly more resolved than it used to — the kind of thing a mother might notice, rocking a child to sleep on a bad night, and feel her own shoulders loosen without knowing why, and decide it was just a good day, a kind wind, nothing more.

That's all it does.

It climbs back up through the fissure, into the brutal flat light of the scrubland, and it does not look back down, because looking back down would be its own kind of taking — a power that lingers to watch its gifts unwrap is a power that hasn't fully let go of them yet, and letting go, completely, is the last and hardest part of the work.

Somewhere below, a girl who didn't expect to be a tuner sits at an old woman's feet and asks her first real question. Somewhere below, a mother mentions an early sweetness to no one in particular and is heard anyway. Somewhere below, the wind moves through stone cut by hands ten thousand years dead and comes out, for the first time, very slightly more like song than like grief — and nobody knows why, and nobody needs to, and the people of the Cistern Town go on doing what they have always done: coaxing, never taking, listening before they speak, trusting the chord to hold them, generation on generation, exactly as it always has — except now, underneath it all, so quiet it will never be mistaken for anything but their own resilience, there is one more reason for it to hold.
