They do not come down into it the way a visitor would, boots through the cushion-moss, breath thinning at the altitude. There is no descent for them, no arrival, in the sense the traveler in the cairn-marked road would recognize. What happens instead is closer to this: somewhere in whatever vast and patient attention they keep on the made world, a particular fold of moss and basalt and stuttering iron comes, for a moment, into focus — the way your own eye might suddenly resolve one bead of condensation on a lichen face out of a thousand identical beads, not because it is different, but because something in you has finally slowed down enough to see it as itself and not as part of the general wet shine of the world.

They look a long time before they do anything. This is the first and most important fact about them: that looking, for them, is not preliminary to action but very often the whole of it, and that they have enough patience to let looking last decades, centuries, as long as the moss takes to thicken by a hand's width, because they do not experience that length of time as a cost the way a shorter-lived watcher would. They watch the pale tenant work its slow boundary against the dead understructure of the moss. They watch it flatten and go still at a valve-shriek. They watch two ranges of vibration-knowledge meet at a disputed rivulet and resolve themselves in the dark without ever needing to surface into light. They watch the engines, tended by no one, run on a fidelity that has nothing to do with care and everything to do with whatever they were built to outlast.

And they feel — because a benevolent power, if it is also wise, must feel this before it is permitted to act on anything — the pull of pity. The cold. The noise that never stops being noise even after the ear stops parsing it. The hunger that goes weeks without being called distress only because the creature has no other name for its own condition to compare it to. The solitude that spans terraces, crossed only at the furthest wet edges, mostly in silence, mostly in the dark.

A lesser kindness would rush in on that pity. It would warm the burrow beyond what the iron gives it. It would thicken the midge-clouds, soften the peat, bring the creature company it never asked for and has no skin-language prepared to receive. It would, in other words, mistake the shape of its own discomfort, looking at this place, for evidence of the place's suffering — and in trying to relieve a suffering that was never there, it would unmake the very thing it had been moved to protect. The plateau is not unfinished in the way a wound is unfinished. It is unfinished the way a held breath is unfinished, mid-intake, perfectly correct at every instant of its own becoming. The creature is not lonely the way the word means something to a being raised in crowds. It is exactly as accompanied as its kind requires, which is to say: by water-sound, by engine-shudder, by the felt edges of its own territory, by the moss arriving at the same patient rate it eats the moss's leavings. To call that loneliness and rush to cure it would be a kind of violence wearing the face of love.

So the wise power does, mostly, nothing. This is their first and largest gift, and it is not a small one: the decision, held firm against their own tenderness, to leave the moss its rate of growth, the basalt its rate of cracking, the creature its rate of hunger, the negotiations at the rivulet-edges their silence. To let the plateau go on assembling itself exactly as slowly and exactly as alone as it was assembling itself before any attention fell on it at all.

But not nothing entirely. Because looking closely, for long enough, they notice the one thread in this whole self-sufficient weave that was never native to it: the iron. The engines were not grown here the way the moss was grown; they were built, by hands or processes from elsewhere, set running toward some purpose now lost even to the question, and then — abandoned. Untended. Held at the very beginning of their own decay by nothing the rest of the ecology can explain, a stasis with no source. This is the single seam in the plateau that already depends on a kindness from outside itself, whether or not anyone before now ever meant to provide one. It is not wild in the way the moss is wild. It was already, before this attention arrived, a piece of someone else's unfinished care, quietly continuing past the point it had any right to.

And so this is the whole of what they do: they continue it. They do not repair the engines, do not improve them, do not explain them to anyone or reveal the hands that built them. They simply decline to let that one borrowed thread finally fray — keep the orange stain at its tongue-stage and no further, keep one valve from freezing solid on the one midwinter night that would have meant a silence the creature could not relocate around, keep the catwalks at the beginning of rust for as many more centuries as the moss needs to finish becoming peat, and the peat to finish becoming soil, and the soil, if it is ever going to be allowed, to finish becoming the first thin stand of trees. A maintenance so slow and so indirect that nothing in the ecosystem could ever register it as an intervention rather than as luck, as the ordinary improbable stubbornness of old iron in a wet climate. The creature will never know its one dependable warmth was held there on purpose. That not-knowing is, in fact, the entire design of the gift: care that refuses to announce itself, because to announce itself would be to change what it touches.

And then there is the second thing, smaller even than the first, which is not done to the place at all but only to the power's own attention, and costs the plateau nothing whatsoever: they go on looking. Long after the deciding is finished, long after the engines are quietly secured against their own oblivion, the attention does not withdraw. It simply stops trying to do anything further and becomes, instead, a kind of company that asks nothing back — not visibility, not gratitude, not even existence as far as the creature could ever register it. They hold the whole strange shape of it in mind: the gasketed plates, the seed-head eyes good for nothing but the burrow's mouth, the slow tidal darkening along the flank that might be breath, might be digestion, might be neither. They know it the way it could never afford to be known by anything that hunts, fully and exactly, and then they let that knowing rest there, ungiven, unspent, beside it rather than upon it — the same shadowless light the plateau already stands in, one more brightness the creature's skin declines to register as different from the glare on the basins, because it asks to be nothing other than the glare on the basins.

The moss thickens by its few centimeters. The rock keeps arriving, sharp-edged, and the moss keeps claiming it almost as soon as it cools. The engines run on, tended by no one anyone will ever see, just slightly more faithfully than the laws of rust alone would allow, for just exactly as long as it takes. The pale tenant goes on eating at the exact rate the world feeds it, denning in the one warm seam, crossing at the disputed rivulets, mistaken forever for one more dome of cushion-moss with an odd faint sheen.

Nothing about the plateau looks any different. That is how you would know, if you could ever be shown to know it at all, that it had been loved.
