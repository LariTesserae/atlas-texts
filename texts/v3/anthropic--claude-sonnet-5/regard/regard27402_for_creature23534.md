The power notices the way it always notices things: not all at once, and not because of size. The forest itself — its scale, its catastrophe, its pearl-light pretending to be sky — is the kind of marvel that announces itself, and announcements rarely need help being seen. What draws the attention down past all that vastness is smaller. It is a hand, flashed open and then closed, across sixty meters of clearing, meaning *wait.* It is a child being taught, before food, before names, before anything else that could be called love, to listen for the particular pitch of wood about to lose an argument with itself. It is the fact that these people do not pray, because praying is what you do when you can afford to ask for things to be different, and they have never been able to afford that, and so they simply live, with their oversized eyes and their root-colored skin and their grief that gets reconstituted into new families the way the forest's wreckage gets reconstituted into new shoots — not healed, just continued.

The power considers, first, the obvious mercy. It would be easy enough — power of this kind finds permanence trivial — to still the collapse. To take the war out of the wood, settle the sap-pressure, let one trunk-system stand unchallenged for a thousand years instead of a few decades, let a child grow up without learning the groan of dying timber as her first language. It turns this over carefully, because it is wise, and wisdom mostly consists of turning things over carefully before declining to do them.

And it declines.

Because the grip is not separate from the people. The grip — that long-fingered, long-toed readiness, the eyes built for judging far tremors, the hush-language of held stillness and weight shifted along a vine-line — all of it is an answer to the falling. Take away the falling and you have not saved them. You have erased the question and kept only an answer that no longer means anything, a people standing very still in a world that no longer requires it, watching for a sound that will never come again, growing strange and aimless in their own bodies the way a hand goes strange when the thing it was shaped to hold is taken out of it. The power has seen this kind of mercy before, in other worlds, on other beings, and has learned to recognize it for what it actually is. It is not rescue. It is replacement. You do not save a people by ending the conditions that made them who they are; you simply end them, gently, and call the ending kindness because nothing bled.

So it leaves the falling alone. It leaves the unnamed thing in the deep troughs alone too, the one that shakes a causeway a quarter mile and is never seen — leaves it as mystery, as unspoken weather, as a thing too load-bearing in this ecology's vast interlocking grief to touch without knowing exactly what else would give way if it did. The power is old enough to know that "I could help" and "I understand enough to help correctly" are different sentences, and that the gap between them is where most ruin starts.

But it looks longer, and it finds something else — something smaller, and unlike the falling, not essential. It finds the orphan who dies not because she failed to learn the sound but because there was no one left, after the second family she'd been folded into also came apart, to teach her in time. It finds the two scattered groups who pass within four kilometers of each other, twice, years apart, and never know it, when a meeting would have meant a knot taught, a story carried, a grief shared instead of carried alone up some fork in some indifferent trunk. It finds that the danger here is fair — earned by everyone equally, the price of living in a world this enormous and this honest about its own appetite — but that some of the dying is not danger at all. It is only isolation wearing danger's clothes. A child who dies for lack of a teacher has not lost a fair contest with the forest. She has lost a contest with loneliness, and loneliness was never supposed to be one of the prices listed.

So that is what it touches. Nothing larger.

It does not still a single falling trunk. But the groan that precedes the worst failures travels a little further now, low enough to reach a few more of the listening, even the ones with no one left to have taught them what it meant; some old instinct simply answers it, the way the body answers a cold gust before the mind has named it wind. It does not stop the wars of sap and root. But the odds bend, almost nowhere, almost nothing — a current that wasn't there before nudging two paths half a kilometer closer than chance alone would have allowed, often enough that meetings happen a little more than they used to, not so often as to undo the scarcity that makes each meeting matter, only often enough that fewer people finish their short upward lives having spoken to no one but their own dead. Somewhere, a knot gets tied a particular new way by a particular pair of hands, holds under a load that should have broken it, and is copied, hand to hand, family to family, the way every other true thing here gets carried.

It does not announce any of this. It does not ask to be believed in. These are not pious people, and piety is not what the power wants from them; it has watched enough worlds discover that being thanked is not the same as being helped, and that gods who require gratitude are usually asking to be fed, not asking to feed. So it does the smaller, harder thing: it lets their own half-superstition stand exactly as it was, the old guess that the light above them is somehow ancestral, accumulated, watching — and it simply, quietly, makes that guess a little truer than it was before, by being, in fact, something that watches, folded into the glow they already had a word for, asking nothing, changing no story, only standing in the gap their own cosmology had already, patiently, left open for something like it.

The falling continues. The light keeps its pearled, sourceless, unbearable gentleness. Somewhere up in the green-gold haze a child who should not have heard the groan in time hears it anyway, and climbs, and lives, and years later teaches her own children the sound without ever knowing why she knew it a half-second sooner than she should have — and does not need to know, and is not owed an explanation, only the half-second, which she got, which was enough.
