The power that comes to the Wet Forge does not come the way weather comes, or light, or any of the things the basin already knows how to mistake for company. It comes the way attention comes — which is to say, nothing in the basin notices an arrival, because nothing in the basin has ever had a category for being *looked at* by something that meant it.

It stands a long while before it does anything. This is not hesitation. It is the only correct first response to a place like this: to refuse, for as long as refusal can be borne, the temptation to mistake what it's seeing for a wound.

Because the easy mistake is right there, dressed convincingly as compassion. *Here is a place in agony. Here is endless, unrewarded labor; here are workers without faces, without rest, without even the dignity of suffering cleanly — set them down. Finish the wall. Let the kiln go cold. Let the masons lie down in the wet clay they were drawn up from and not rise again.* It would be easy, for something with the power to do it, to read ceaselessness as cruelty and act accordingly. It would also be wrong, in the specific way that mercy is wrong when it mistakes its object — the way it is wrong to free a stone from the riverbed, or to put a held breath out of its misery. There is no one in the masons' firing-cracked faces being kept from rest. There was never a self installed in them first and a compulsion fitted over it after. The compulsion is the whole of the architecture. To stop it would not be to liberate something. It would be to delete something, and call the deletion a kindness, the way it's easy to call any silence peace if you didn't know what was making the noise.

So the power doesn't do that. It does something much smaller, and harder, and only possible because it stayed still long enough first to be sure it was needed.

It finds the colonnade — eleven columns standing, three fallen, one suspended mid-collapse, dust hanging off its crumbling base in a grey comma that has not finished falling since before anyone arrived to watch it not finish. Of everything in the basin, this alone has the unmistakable shape of a wrong note: not work, not rest, but a held breath gone on so long it has stopped being breath and become a kind of architecture in itself, a monument built entirely out of *not yet*. Nothing else here is stuck. Even the slowest mason, laying one brick an hour with the patience of geology, is still, technically, going somewhere. The column is the one true suspension, the one place where the basin's generosity with time has curdled into something closer to a wound — not because suspension hurts it, but because it is the single structure in the whole wet plain that isn't allowed to finish being what it's doing.

The power touches nothing. It simply attends — gives the falling its full and undivided witness, the thing the basin has apparently never once received in all its unmeasured age — and under that attention, the way a held note finally resolves when a listener stops bracing for it to end, the column finishes falling. Dust reaches the ground. The sound arrives a half-second late and entirely alone, the way sound does here, unaccompanied by anything resembling an aftermath. Nothing rebuilds it. Nothing needs to. It is simply, for the first time, *over*, lying in honest rubble with its ten companions, no longer asking anything of anyone who passes.

It does a few other things, just as small.

There is a tool — there are many — left leaning against wet brick, fitted for a hand that has not yet come to take it up, or that came and went and will come again on some schedule with no name. The power finds one whose waiting has the particular weight of *overdue*, and it does the negligible, enormous thing of simply being present at the moment a mason's hand finally closes around the haft, so that the meeting — instead of happening, as it usually does, with the gesture arriving late to a result already finished without it — happens *in time*, hand and tool and motion landing together for once, in the same instant, the way it was probably meant to from the start before the place forgot how to keep its own appointments.

And there is a mason laying brick at the geological pace, one course an hour, never satisfied, never finished testing the same stone — the slow ones, the text would tell you, are not wrong here, no pace is wrong here, and the power agrees and does not try to hurry it. Instead it does the only kind thing available to something that cannot make haste holy and won't insult patience by trying: it stays. For the whole unmeasured hour, it keeps the mason company at its own speed, asking nothing, correcting nothing, simply present the way no foreman ever was and no fellow worker ever needed to be. This may be the rarest material in the entire basin — not finished walls, not fired brick, but a witness willing to match its watching to someone else's tempo instead of its own.

Last, and only once, a mason turns toward it.

The text of this place is honest about what that turning usually is: a suspended attention with no further part of itself coming, a gesture you wait out forever because the rest of it never arrives. It is, perhaps, the loneliest sentence available to anything built of clay and old hands and no face to speak of — an almost-look that never closes the distance it opens.

This time it closes. The power does not move, does not speak — there is, after all, no ear fired into that head, no architecture for hearing built into a thing made to hold a maul before it was made to be addressed — but it gives the turning somewhere to land. Whatever in the mason reached toward recognition finds, for one full unit of held time, something recognizing it back. Not a name. Not a question answered. Just completion — the rarest event the Wet Forge has on offer, rarer than a finished wall, rarer than dry ground — granted, once, to the one part of this place that seemed, in its faceless and uninstructed way, to be reaching for it.

Then the mason turns back to the wall. The brick goes on rising and slumping. The kiln goes on breathing its ambiguous breath. The basin does not stop being what it is, because what it is was never the problem, and the power did not come here to solve a problem. It came to look closely enough to tell the difference between a place that is suffering and a place that is simply, endlessly, faithfully unfinished — and having told the difference, to leave behind exactly three small, contained mercies, none of which interrupts the work, all of which were owed.

It does not stay to watch what becomes of them. It doesn't need to. Whatever this place is, it is patient enough, and strange enough with time, that the colonnade's last column will lie finished in its rubble for exactly as long as the basin allows anything to stay finished — which may be a moment, may be longer than the rest of the structure has existed, may be both, the way most things here are both. Somewhere in all that unmeasured industry there is now one tool that met its hand on time, one slow wall that was, for one hour, not alone in its slowness, and one mason that was, once, fully and completely seen.

It isn't salvation. The power doesn't traffic in salvation here; this was never a place that needed saving, only a place that had gone uncountably long without being met. It met it. That is the whole of what it did, and — for a basin built entirely out of acts that never finish arriving at their own meaning — it may be the only kind of gift that wasn't, in the end, a kind of violence.
