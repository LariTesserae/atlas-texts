# What Comes, and What It Leaves Alone

The power finds the hollow the way attention finds anything worth keeping — not by searching, but by some small catch, a held breath of architecture registering, distantly, as a held breath of architecture. It looks the way such powers look, which is to say: completely, without the mercy of looking away from any part. It sees the shale still deciding its angle. It sees the moss-glow and what the moss-glow means. It sees the half-second of sound traveling out of phase with the people listening for it, and it understands immediately, the way only a power outside of time could, why that gap exists and what it would cost to close it.

And here is the first true thing about this power, the thing that makes it wise rather than merely able: it sits with the place for a long time before it does anything at all.

It considers the obvious mercies, because a lesser kindness would rush past considering and straight to performing. It could still the shale. It could let the hollow finish arriving — pick a shape, hold it, give these people walls that mean something next year as well as this one. It could close the half-second gap so the click arrives exactly when the ear is waiting for it, every time, so no Tender ever again kneels at 3 a.m. straining after a sound that hasn't reached them yet.

It does not do these things, and the reasoning is not complicated, only patient. A hollow that has finished deciding is a hollow with old mountains in it, and old mountains do not need Tenders. The catechism that isn't a catechism — watch the moss, not the gauge; don't trust last week's stairwell; notice, notice, notice — is not a coping mechanism the power would be doing a favor by retiring. It is the entire shape of the love these people have learned to practice. Take away the instability and you have not freed them. You have fired them from the only work that ever made their attention to each other necessary. A people who stopped needing to notice would, given a generation or two, stop noticing — and a Hollowfolk who has stopped noticing is not a Hollowfolk helped. It is a Hollowfolk erased and replaced with someone more comfortable, which is its own kind of unkindness, dressed nicely.

So the power does not flatten the hollow's uncertainty. It does not answer the birdcall that isn't quite a bird, though it could, easily, and recognizes in that thing — whatever it is, however long it has held its lease — a fellow tenant older in this particular ground than itself, deserving the same privacy it would want extended to its own unexplained business. It does not crown a Tender with visions, does not select a prophet, does not leave a sign legible enough to be argued about in the way faiths get argued about, because an argument over signs is a thing that divides people who currently have nothing to divide them but root-access and shared walls, and that, too, would be a theft disguised as a gift.

What it does is smaller, and it does it the way everything in this city that actually helps does its helping: without credit, without ceremony, indistinguishable from the line crew, from the moss, from whatever quietly decided long ago that an engine housing with a vine through it should be left alone and thanked.

It takes up three small tenancies.

The first: in the soft-boarded floors, in the particular places where a Hollowfolk child's weight has, for generations, found out the rot the hard way — a half-inch more warning in the groan. Not enough to stop being careful. Just enough that careful, this once, is rewarded before it's tested. No child stops learning to walk the outer edge of the foot. Some child, somewhere, simply doesn't go through the boards finding out why that's the lesson.

The second: in the walls that get reclaimed. When a partition comes down to feed a pipe-run two buildings over, when an old woman's room becomes the floor of somebody else's greenhouse, the power does nothing to stop the wall's career as building material — that traffic is the hollow's own metabolism and not the power's business to interrupt. But it sees to it, quietly, the way moss sees to drainage, that something always survives the transfer. A name scored into a beam. A child's height marked in pencil, faded but not gone. A windowsill's particular notch where someone always set down a cup. The new tenant finds it without knowing to look for it, and feels, without being told why, that the room remembers being loved before they got there. This is not memory restored. It is memory refusing, very slightly, to be perfectly erased — which the power judges to be the difference between loss and grief, and decides that these people, who have so much practice at the first, deserve a fighting chance at the second, which is the kinder of the two.

The third tenancy is the strangest and the smallest, and it is this: at the bottom of the long apprenticeships, the ten years, the twelve, in the exact moment when a Tender — kneeling at a stairwell that wasn't there last week, or with an ear against cold copper waiting on a click — finally, for the first time, gets the half-second right. Hears it when it happens and not after. Knows, before the gauge knows, before the moss confirms it, that the house has decided something and what it decided. This moment was always going to come to every Tender who stayed long enough and attended closely enough; the power changes nothing about the timeline or the requirement. What it does is simpler, and entirely outside the architecture: in that instant, whoever is kneeling there feels, for exactly as long as the feeling takes to have, that the noticing was not wasted. That somewhere, something noticed them noticing. No voice. No sign anyone could repeat to a neighbor without sounding foolish. Just the private, unverifiable, completely sufficient sense of being witnessed in the one effort this place has asked of them their whole lives — and then it passes, and they go back to listening, because that's the job, and the job was never going to stop being the job.

That is the whole of the intervention. The power does not stay to be thanked, because being thanked would require being known, and being known would require explaining itself, and any explanation it could give would necessarily be smaller than the truth, which is that it looked at a city built entirely out of the practice of loving things that don't hold still, and decided the most generous thing available to it was to disturb that practice as little as a power possibly could while still, somewhere in the grain of the place, leaving a little less cruelty in the learning of it.

The hollow goes on sinking, fractionally, year on year. The stairwells go on rearranging themselves with the same disregard for memorized maps. The thing that answers the birdcall answers it, on its own schedule, for its own unstated reasons, exactly as before. Nothing about the Greenworks at Cold Hollow would show up differently to a surveyor, or a historian, or even most of the people who live there, on the day after the power came and the day before.

But somewhere a child's foot finds a floor that holds a half-breath longer than it used to, and doesn't think about why.

Somewhere a young woman, two years into a tenancy in a greenhouse that used to be someone's bedroom, runs her thumb over a height-mark scored into a beam that the demolition crew should have planed flush and somehow, in the press of other work, never quite got around to, and feels obscurely grateful to people she will never meet for living there before her.

And somewhere, an old Tender, the kind who has been doing this so long the gauge has become a formality and the moss has become a friend, kneels at 3 a.m. with an ear against cold copper, waiting on a click that hasn't come yet — and it comes, once, exactly when she expects it, no sooner, no later, perfectly in phase with her listening for the first time in forty years of listening — and she sits back on her heels in the cold and says, out loud, to no one, the way you'd thank a line crew you've never seen and don't expect to:

"Well. Good for you, house."

And goes back to work, because the work was never going to stop being the work, and that, the power would tell you, if it stayed long enough to be asked, was the entire point of doing it this way.
