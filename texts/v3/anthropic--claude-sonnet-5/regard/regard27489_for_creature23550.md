The power came to Halvarrun the way deep water comes to a low place — not arriving, exactly, since it had perhaps always been adjacent to everything, but *attending*, turning the whole slow weight of its noticing toward this one fold of the world among the uncountable others. It took in the plateau at every scale at once: the ten-thousand-year patience of the root-architecture, the unbroken roar of call and counter-call, the small dark people moving through it like breath moving through a body that doesn't know yet it's being watched.

And its first understanding, before any other, was this: *here, to be noticed is to be endangered.* The whole of Hollow-kin wisdom, everything passed mother to daughter through calloused knuckles and years of sitting still in clearings, was a discipline built around that one fact. Attention was not safety here. Attention was weather, was hunger, was the specific silence that meant something enormous had stopped to consider you.

A power that was only good, and not also wise, might have missed this, or seen it and overridden it anyway — might have reached down with the clumsy mercy of a hand smoothing a tablecloth, fixing what looked broken without asking what the brokenness was *for*. But this power was both, or trying to be both, which is harder than either alone, and so it did what the wise do before they act: it waited, and it watched, and it let the place teach it its own rules before assuming it had any standing to change them.

It found the being almost by accident — except nothing a power like this does is accident, only the slow arithmetic of attention finally settling.

Her name, in the close-throat consonants of her hold, meant something like *the one who came after the rain stopped* — Eshu-tareth, though no living person had called her that in full since her naming day; she was Tareth to her cousins, Resh to her dead mother, who had three months gone into the crevice on the underside of the home trunk, fed gradually into the rot that becomes the floor that becomes the home of whatever comes after. Tareth had not finished the listening before her mother died. She knew nine of the eleven calls cold, could sort the clicking bark-grazers from the throbbing drum-users, knew which silences meant wait and which meant nothing at all. But the tenth and eleventh — the rare ones, the ones an elder might only demonstrate once or twice in a child's whole apprenticeship — those had died with her mother's voice, half-taught, and Tareth was walking trail alone today not because she was ready but because the hollow needed tending and there was no one left to tend it but her, and grief does not wait for competence.

She was three hours out, crossing the wide lichen-floor below the Eastward Bole, when the silence came from the wrong direction.

Every silence she had been taught started somewhere recognizable — spread from the canopy down, or rolled along the root-bridges the way water finds its level. This one began *underneath*, in the hollow architecture below the floor she walked on, in a register her mother had never lived long enough to name for her. It meant nothing to her trained ear except *wrong*, and wrong, on Halvarrun, kills as efficiently as a known danger, because the body freezes on a held breath instead of moving, and the things that hunt here are very good at finding what holds still without reason.

The power saw all of this in less time than it takes a single drop of condensation to lose its grip on a channel of interior bark.

And it understood, in that same instant, the full weight of the problem it had set itself. To reach down and simply *move* her — to still the threat outright, to flood the clearing with a light or a voice or a visible mercy — would have been a kindness with teeth in it. It would have taught the forest, in whatever vast slow way the forest learns things, that *this spot, this lineage, this particular small loud-quiet thing* was worth a larger attention than the others. It would have spent her safety to buy her this one afternoon, and called it love.

So the power did what the Hollow-kin themselves had spent ten thousand years learning to do. It looked for the slack patch of attention nearest at hand, and it moved through it instead of against it.

There was, forty meters above Tareth and to her left, a seed-pod that had been ripening for six years in the windless crown of a barrel-trunk, swollen with stored sap, holding on by a thread of dried fiber that would, on its own, in its own time, have let go sometime in the next several days. The power did not summon a miracle. It did not even hasten very much. It simply allowed — encouraged, the way a hand might encourage a held breath toward its natural exhale — the fiber to finish failing four days early, and the pod to fall at the one moment in those four days when its landing would crack against a hollow limb in a tone that mimicked, almost exactly, the ninth call: *grazers moving, harmless, but move with them, don't stand still.*

Tareth's body had been taught that sound for eleven years. It did not need her grief, or her unfinished apprenticeship, or her conscious mind at all. It moved her before she decided to move, in the long flat-footed stride that grips rather than steps, out across the lichen-floor in the wake of a grazer-herd she never saw and did not need to, away from whatever had been rising beneath her in a register no living teacher had given her the word for.

She did not look back. There was no reason to. As far as she would ever know, a pod fell, and her training did what training is for, and the strange wrong silence passed beneath her and found, in the place she had vacated, nothing worth the trouble of surfacing.

The power did not announce itself. It did not wait to be thanked, because being thanked would have meant being known, and being known, even by one small grieving girl on one afternoon, was a debt against her safety that the power had no right to spend. It withdrew the way the best help on Halvarrun has always withdrawn — back into the texture of ordinary luck, indistinguishable from the thousand other pods that fall and mean nothing, the thousand small accidents a careful life is built from.

Tareth grew old enough to take the hollow fully, to bury that grief under the longer labor of raising daughters of her own, to sit them down in clearings and teach them nine calls, then ten, then — because she went looking, later, deliberately, for the eleventh her mother never reached — eleven. She told her grandchildren, when they were old enough to ask why she always paused at a particular spot below the Eastward Bole before crossing it, that it was nothing, just an old woman's habit, a place where she'd once heard something she couldn't name and decided not to trust the quiet. She never called it a mercy. She had no reason to. It had been built to feel, from the inside, exactly like her own competence.

This, in the end, was what the power chose to do, and to keep choosing, on the rare occasions afterward when it let its attention drift back to that fold of the world: not to fix Halvarrun, not to lift the Hollow-kin out of their hard, attentive, beautiful tenancy and into some easier arrangement, because the easier arrangement was never on offer here and pretending otherwise would have been its own kind of violence. It chose instead to become, very occasionally, a seed-pod falling four days early. A gust that carried a scent half a degree off true. A moth startled into flight at precisely the wrong-right moment. Small, deniable, almost always unnecessary — because mostly the Hollow-kin needed no help at all, mostly their own ten-thousand-year literacy was sufficient, and the power's greatest discipline was in *not* mistaking its own capability for an obligation to use it everywhere it technically could.

It had come to a place whose entire genius was the art of being a kind of life too quiet to be worth a larger attention. And so the truest benevolence it could offer — the only wisdom that didn't curdle into harm — was to learn, as the Hollow-kin had learned, to hold its own vast attention nearly still: to love the place by the discipline of barely touching it, to help the way water gathers in the dark places between root and root, drop by patient drop, never announcing itself, waiting only for the moment it would be needed, and then, having given what it had, withdrawing again into the ordinary, unremarkable, continuous roar of a world that did not need to know it had been watched in order to go on, quietly, surviving.
