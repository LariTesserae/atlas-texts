# What the Power Did, Looking Down Through the Smoked-Glass Light

It came the way attention comes to anything truly old — not as a beam, not as a descent, but as a slow widening of notice, the way a held breath, finally released, fills more of the body than the lungs alone. It had no need of eyes and so it did not bring any; it simply *attended*, and Mirenastal, which had never been looked at all at once before, was looked at all at once.

It saw the terraces stacked down into the murk farther than any Standing eye had followed them. It saw the unfinished course at the bottom of every flight, waiting, the way a sentence waits at a comma it does not know is also an ending. It saw a child shown a worn stone and told, *this is where the hands before you stopped*, and felt — if *felt* is even the right verb for a thing that size — something close to recognition. It had old work of its own that did not finish either.

## The Temptations

Because it was a power, it could do almost anything, and so almost everything came to it as a temptation first, before it could be considered as a kindness.

It could have broken the ceiling. One motion — the grey ceiling that was a smoked sun or an impossibly thick sea, depending which flight you asked — could have been parted like a curtain, and the Standing could have learned, in an afternoon, whether they were under a sea or under a sky, whether their ancestors were the ones who stayed or the ones who left. It held this thought a long time, the way you'd hold a stone you weren't sure was a tool or a weight.

It set the thought down. To answer the question would not be a gift to them. It would be a theft from the *shape* of them — because their whole architecture of meaning was braced against that unanswered question the way a terrace-riser is braced against the slope behind it. Take away the not-knowing and the bracing has nothing left to hold.

It could have given them fire. A small thing, by its own measure — a single new fact slotted into physics, a kiln that worked at depth, a coal that didn't drown. It watched a woman three terraces down turn a pale fish over in her wide grey hands and eat it raw without ceremony, the way her mother had, the way the question of *what is fire, what is its absence* sat filed in her like a tool she'd never been told the use of and had stopped missing. To hand her fire now would answer a question she had long since stopped asking in favor of better ones — *is this riser packed tight enough, will this child's hands learn the adze, is the horn-cable braided true* — and it understood, slowly, the way it understood most things, that a mystery a people have built a whole patient life around is not a wound to be healed. It is a room they have furnished. You do not burn down a room because you, standing outside it, can see there's a door.

It could have ended the dying-by-rising — the going early, the old or careless drifting up off the terrace like smoke when the sandals failed. This one it turned over longest, because this one looked, from every angle it tried, like suffering, and it was not in its nature to leave suffering alone unexamined. But it watched how the Standing spoke of it — not as horror, but as *joining*, as a held breath at last let go — and it watched a young one, newly cut loose from grief, stand straighter at the topmost stone of her flight than she had the day before, carrying her dead grandmother's portion of the patience forward in her own thick hands. The dying was not separate from the meaning. It was load-bearing. To remove it would leave a flight of stairs with no reason to keep stepping down.

## What It Did Not Do

It did not flood the world with light. It did not speak. It did not appear in the shape of anything the Standing would recognize as a face, because a face is a kind of argument, and it did not wish to argue them out of anything they had built so slowly and so well.

It did not finish a single terrace.

## What It Did

What it did was smaller than all of that, and took longer to decide than the rest combined, because small, careful gifts are harder to get right than large careless ones.

There was a horn three ridges to the east that had gone quiet a generation back — its line ended in a man who died with no child to lace the lashing to, and the horn itself sat wrapped and waiting in a niche of stone, technically inheritable by a cousin-flight too far off to know it existed. The power did not conjure a new horn-bearer out of nothing; it did not need to. It only — gently, the way you'd nudge a held door so it swings instead of sticking — let a particular old woman of that cousin-flight remember, on a particular slow afternoon, a story her grandmother had told her about a kinsman three ridges east. She mentioned it to her son. Her son mentioned it to a horn-maker who knew the niche. The lineage did not skip a generation after all. Nothing about this required a miracle. It required only that one true thing reach one true ear at the moment it would be heard rather than the moment it would be forgotten, and that is a thing a benevolent power can arrange without ever being seen to have arranged it.

It found, too, the exile — cut loose on an unclaimed shoulder of rock for a lapse in algae-tending three years gone, weighted and alone with no flight to call a name from — and it did not restore him to his cable, because that judgment belonged to the Standing and not to it. But it noticed that the shoulder of rock he'd been left on caught the slow drifting light at an angle no terrace did, full and long, for what might have been hours at a stretch — and it let the kelp-analog there grow thick and strange and good, better tended by his solitary hands than any flight he'd left behind, until in time other exiles found their way to his shoulder of rock too, and a new flight began there that owed nothing to inheritance and everything to second chances, and no one — not even the power — decided whether this counted as forgiveness or just as weather, the shape the world had.

And once — only once, and only for the woman whose grandmother's memory had reached the right ear at the right time, very old now, sandals failing at last, beginning gently to rise off her terrace into the warm murk on an afternoon no different from any other — it let the rising not feel like falling upward into nothing. It let it feel, for the small while it lasted, like being met. Not by a face. Not by an answer. Just by a warmth in the water that had not been there a moment before and was gone by the time anyone else came to look.

She did not tell this to anyone, because there was no horn-call shaped for it and no humming frequency reserved for *something larger than kin was glad I had lived*. But the story got told anyway, the way the best stories do — slipped sideways into the other two, about the sun and the sea and which side anyone is really on — until it became a third version, murmured terrace to terrace: that sometimes, for some, the going early is met.

It changed no doctrine. It answered no question. It was received exactly as the power had hoped — as one more soft uncertainty laid gently alongside the others, deepening the dark instead of lighting it, giving the patient hands one more reason, among all their other reasons, to keep cutting the next course down into the murk.

Then the power's attention, having done what little it judged it should, began the long slow work of widening elsewhere — not abandoning Mirenastal, exactly, the way a held breath is never really gone, only no longer the only thing filling the chest.

The terraces went on, unfinished, exactly as they were meant to.
