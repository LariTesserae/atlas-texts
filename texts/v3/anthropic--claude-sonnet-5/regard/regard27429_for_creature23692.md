## What the Power Did

It came the way attention comes to a held breath — not arriving, exactly, since it had perhaps always been there in the way a listening can be there before anyone speaks, but *noticing*, fully, the way you notice a single lit window from a long way off and understand, suddenly, that someone lives there.

It looked a long time before it did anything. This is important, and it would want it understood: the looking was not idle. It traced the trunk-columns up through their too-even branching intervals and felt the same thing the grower's-kin felt and did not name — that the place was *mid-sentence*, that to act carelessly here would be like finishing someone else's word for them, badly, in a language you only half spoke. It went down into the root-tangle below the foundation-level and found the slow creak-thing that moves there, and it did not wake it, and did not name it either, because some sentences are not for finishing by anyone but their own grammar.

And it watched the people. The narrow shoulders, the wide dark eyes, the hand laid flat on bark before the gear-lift. The bruised fruit left in unmarked intervals. The total, cheerful, unexplained refusal to cut a trunk below a certain girth.

*They already know how to do this,* it thought, with something that in a smaller being would have been called relief. *They have already worked out, on their own, in the dark, with no one teaching them, the correct way to live inside an unfinished thing. They have arrived, by patience and compost-floor and gantry-groan, at courtesy.*

So it did not reveal itself. That was the first decision, and the largest, and it cost something to make, because the wanting-to-be-seen, the wanting-to-be-thanked, is real even in wise and benevolent powers; they are not exempt from wanting. But to appear would be to hand them an answer where they had grown, with great care, the better thing: a practice of *not needing one.* It would not be the hand that steadies the ladder. It had watched them build their own steadying, rung by rung, out of root-hook poles and tested platforms and a folk-reverence that never once asked to be doctrine. To interrupt that with a voice from outside the canopy would be to tell a half-finished sentence how it ends. It did not have that right. Wisdom, mostly, is knowing which rights you don't have.

What it did instead was smaller, and slower, and almost — almost — indistinguishable from the ordinary workings of the world.

---

In the foundry-hollow, a vent-flue that had been narrowing for eleven seasons, soot-thickening toward a cough that would have eventually filled the lower gallery with smoke while three night-shift workers slept it in unknowing, found itself, very gradually, growing a wider channel — the plant-matter around the vent simply *deciding*, in its slow vegetable way, to lean its growth a few degrees off true. No one would ever know to be grateful. That was correct. That was the point.

On the tap-line where an old kin named (it doesn't matter what she was named, but she had a name, and had earned every callus of it) drew resin in the medicinal cut the younger ones doubted, a particular vein of sap, on a particular day, ran richer than its chemistry should have allowed, and the cut she made that week did exactly what the old stories said it would for a child running the kind of fever that does not, as a rule, allow for stories to be told about it afterward. It was, to anyone checking, just a good batch.

An expedition member on the rope-bridge route — young, careful, doing everything correctly — felt a wrongness in his stomach forty meters before the platform that would not, that day, have held his weight, and he stopped, and called the company back, and could not afterward explain why except to say *it didn't feel right*, which is the explanation people give for the oldest kind of mercy there is, the kind that arrives disguised as their own intuition so that it can belong to them afterward, completely, as something they did.

Something like a moth — slow, forearm-wide, indifferent between bioluminescence and arc-lamp — drifted once, for no reason any moth could give, into the lap of a foundry-worker grieving a thing the power could not undo and would not have undone even if it could, because grief is not a malfunction, it is the compost-floor of a person, slow oxidation, dry decay, the work by which a life becomes able to hold the next thing. The power could not take the grief. It only made sure, that one evening, that the man was not alone in the dark while he carried it, in the smallest possible way, in a moth that stayed an unmothlike while longer than it should have on his sleeve before lifting off toward the lamps.

And at the edges — the unmapped, unfinished, still-deciding edges, where the architecture repeats itself out past the last strung rope into a dark that implies more dark — it did *not* go and discover what was there and was tempted, genuinely tempted, the way you are tempted to read the last page first. It left the edges alone. It let the cavern keep not knowing, yet, how big it meant to be. This was the hardest restraint and the most important one: it understood that the not-knowing was not a problem waiting on a solution. It was a kind of youth. You do not hand a child the rest of its life in a folded note. You let it grow into the rooms one at a time, the way the kin already, instinctively, correctly did, going by lanternlight and memory, never out of curiosity.

---

It left one thing, in the end, that was wholly its own and not disguised as weather or instinct or a good batch of sap — one small indulgence, because even wisdom permits itself a single act of unguarded affection.

Deep in a gallery nobody had strung rope to yet, on a trunk-column no one had named, it caused — quietly, slowly, over a span of years long enough that no single witness would ever connect cause to effect — a bloom. Not a useful one. Not edible, not resinous, not structurally significant, contributing nothing to the metal-economy or the resin-economy or even the information-economy that moves faster than both. Just a single flush of color, warm-violet against the brass-gold wash, opening for the first time in the cavern's young life on a branch no engineering logic required.

Someday a route-mapping expedition, doing its careful functionary work, would come around a column and find it, and would not know what to make of it, and would crouch there a while in the strange quiet, and would feel — without being able to say why, the way you feel a window is lit from a long way off — that the place they lived in, dark and unfinished and still becoming, was, in some sense they had no instrument to measure, *loved.*

They would not call it that. They didn't have a religion built for being looked at; they had only the hand laid flat on bark, the fruit left at unmarked intervals, the refusal to cut below a certain girth.

But they would leave the bloom alone, the way they left everything alone that seemed to be in the middle of becoming itself.

Which is, of course, exactly what the power had hoped they would do — not because it needed the gesture returned, but because it meant they were still exactly who it had found them to be: a people who had learned, all on their own, in the dark, the single skill that makes any visitation from a wise and benevolent power almost entirely unnecessary.

The skill of leaving good and growing things the room to finish growing.
