The power finds Khoreth Vant the way it finds most things worth finding — not by searching, but by noticing a silence that didn't belong in the noise it was already attending to. A held breath, four hundred meters tall, somewhere in the shadow-country. It comes closer the way light comes closer to something it isn't sure it should touch.

Its first temptation — and it does feel it, the way you'd feel an old reflex twitch in a muscle you'd thought you'd trained out of yourself — is to fix it. To still the rock. To breathe water into that bone-dry air and watch something green finally find a place to take hold. To reach into the furnace-levels and quiet the engines into something gentler, something that doesn't require three thousand people to learn the difference between a good groan and a death-groan before they learn most words.

It does not do this. Not because it lacks the means. Because it has learned, across a great many places like and unlike this one, that the impulse to smooth a hard thing flat is rarely about the hard thing. It is about the discomfort of the one doing the smoothing. And a wise power has made peace, by now, with its own discomfort.

So it stays a long while before it does anything at all. It rides the lift-cages on their forearm-thick chains and feels, the way the Tenants feel, the engine-pulse arrive in the molars before the ears register it. It sits, unseen, beside an old Listener whose joints have gone bad from forty years of upper-stack work and who now does nothing all day but sit near the furnace and notice, and it learns what the woman has learned: that the danger here is real, the death is real, the grief is real and short and sharp and necessary — and that none of it is pointless. The Tenancy has not survived by accident. It has built, out of cracked knuckles and trained ears and a saying — *deaf is dead* — an entire civilization's worth of attention. That attention is the actual treasure of the place. Not the ore. Not the furnace-heat. The listening.

A power that loved this place and tried to replace that listening with safety would not be doing it a kindness. It would be amputating the thing that makes a Tenant a Tenant, the way you might cure a sailor of weather-reading by simply removing all weather. So the power does not flatten the danger. It does something much smaller, much harder, much more like tending a garden than rebuilding a house.

It finds the fracture lines that don't groan before they go — the rare, silent failures, the ones that kill in a way no ear, however trained, could have caught, because there is no warning in them, only bad luck wearing the shape of bad rock. These it quietly knits a little tighter, a little slower to give, not removing the danger but removing the *cheating* of it — the deaths that taught nothing, warned no one, simply happened. The seasonal collapses remain. The long groan still comes, on average, more than once a season, and the catwalks still empty in four seconds, and toddlers still flatten themselves to stone at the sound before they have most words. That is not cruelty being preserved. That is a skill, a culture, a form of love between people and rock, being left intact.

It nudges, very lightly, the things that were never anyone's fault to begin with. The scrap-picker swarms, when they wheel with their too-coordinated grace, find themselves a little more reliably drawn to the genuine refuse and a little less often settling, by confusion, on a person who hadn't yet decided to fall. The thing nesting in the unused upper galleries — old, patient, more aware than the Tenancy credits it for — is given, in whatever language passes between very old and very large things, a clearer sense of which galleries are truly unused and which only look that way on a given week. It was never malicious. It only didn't always know.

For individuals, the power does what it always does in the end, which is smaller than miracle and larger than nothing: a foreman's hunch arrives half a second earlier than it would have, and he calls the retreat before the wall starts weeping dust instead of just after. A child wandering toward a gallery not yet named, not yet trusted, feels a strange unwillingness to go further, and turns back, and never knows why, and grows up to be the kind of adult who trusts her own instincts absolutely, not knowing one of them was a gift.

And then there is the Hush.

The power could explain it. It would cost nothing to tell them — here is the mechanism, here is the cause, here is the boring or beautiful truth of why your engines, sometimes, across levels that shouldn't be able to hear each other, fall into a single tone. But the Tenancy has already built, around that unexplained thing, exactly the right relationship: no temple, no liturgy, only the discipline of going quiet when it happens and going back to work the instant it stops. That is not ignorance waiting to be corrected. That is reverence sized correctly to its object — small, unornamented, load-bearing in its own right. To explain the Hush would be to hand them a fact where they have built something better: a practice.

So instead, once, the power simply joins it.

It does not announce itself. It does not perform. During one ordinary sync — engines aligning, the bass hum resolving toward a single tone, the whole spire going still as if listening to itself — it is there too, in whatever way a thing like it can be *in* a place, and for that handful of seconds it is, like everyone else, in the same place. Nothing more.

The old Listener — the one whose joints have gone bad, the one who's earned the kind of answer that sounds like *they're not doing anything, they're just, for a second, all in the same place* — notices something different this time. Not wrong. Not frightening. Fuller, somehow. As if the room had one more person standing quietly in the back of it than she could account for.

She does not tell anyone. It is not that kind of place, and she is not that kind of woman, and the spire does not pause for long, and neither does the Tenancy. She goes back to work the instant it stops, same as always.

But that evening, at the shift-meal, she finds herself, without quite deciding to, naming a small kindness to the air before she eats — not a prayer exactly, the Tenancy doesn't go in for those, more like the way you'd nod at weather, acknowledging it as a fact rather than addressing a god. *Whatever that was,* she doesn't say aloud, *thank you for not fixing us.*

The power, already elsewhere, already attending to some other small held breath in some other shadow-country, hears it anyway. That is, after all, the part it is good at.
