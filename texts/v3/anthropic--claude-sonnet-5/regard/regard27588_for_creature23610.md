The power arrives the way weather arrives here — not as an event but as a pressure change, something the water feels before the air does. It does not announce itself, because it has been to enough places to know that announcement is itself a kind of violence, a demand that the world reorganize around a guest. So it sits, first, in the thermocline between the warm outflow and the old cold beneath, and it listens the way she listens — to the roar that has stopped being sound and become the silence underneath everything — and it does not move to fix anything for a long while, because it is wise, and wisdom here looks almost exactly like hesitation.

It considers, and discards, the obvious gifts.

It could give her a new hull — clean steel, dry bilges, an engine that doesn't have to be coaxed like a stubborn animal every cold morning. It does not, because the boat is not separate from her; it is a second, externalized set of hands, and to replace it would be to amputate a sense she's spent thirty years growing. It could lift her out of the cold and the wet into someplace warm and dry and easy. It does not, because she has already made her peace with damp wool and cracked knuckles in the particular way that turns hardship into competence, and competence into selfhood, and to undo the hardship now would unmake the self built on top of it — like sanding the flutes back out of the rock and calling it a kindness to the mountain. It could quiet the something large in the deep channels, drag it off into some other dark, so she never again has to not-think about the carcasses on the gravel shoals. It does not, because her unspoken vigilance about that thing is itself a form of reverence, the closest thing she has to prayer, and prayer is rarely improved by removing its object.

It could simply make her happy, in the flat, total sense — reach in and turn some dial. It does not. It has seen what happens to beings whose difficulty is removed by force rather than by their own slow metabolizing of it: they go thin. They lose the specific weather of their own face. No — whatever is given here has to be small enough to pass through the weave of her life without tearing it.

So this is what it does instead.

It leans, very slightly, on a handful of moments that were already balanced on a knife-edge, and tips them her way. The engine that should have seized on the worst crossing of a particular November does not seize; she'll remember it as a good year for maintenance, and in a sense she'll be right, because she did the maintenance — the power only declines to let the universe collect on a debt of metal fatigue that was, statistically, due. The vegetation shelf two valleys up that finally lets go after a record wet season lets go on a morning when her launch is tied up at the number-stencilled plant instead of threading the channel beneath it, and she will never know there was a morning when it could have gone the other way, and that is exactly the point. Twice, over the rest of her working years, a thermocline she doesn't quite trust turns out to be nothing, and once, a thermocline she does trust without being able to say why turns out to be something, and she pulls the wheel hard and loses ten minutes of schedule and never finds out what she avoided, only that her instinct earned its keep again — which it did. The power does not give her foresight. It only ever, gently, declines to let her good instincts be wasted on bad luck.

It does one thing that is less a correction than a gift outright, because even wisdom allows itself one extravagance: on an ordinary gray afternoon in the middle of an ordinary crossing, with a barge of timber strung out behind her and her shoulders set in their permanent ducking hunch, the cloud ceiling tears — not unusual in itself, the light does this constantly, gold on one ridge and iron everywhere else — but this time the tear holds for a long, unhurried minute exactly over the rust-orange cliff face dead ahead of her bow, and the guano-streaked colony above it goes silent for the length of one breath the way birds sometimes do for no reason anyone has ever explained, and the water in front of her launch turns, briefly, the colour of hammered brass, and she will think: *that's a hell of a thing,* and say nothing to anyone, because there's no one to say it to who would understand why it mattered, and she will carry that one wordless afternoon for the rest of her life as proof of nothing and evidence of nothing and just barely enough, on the bad days, to keep going. That is the whole gift. It costs the power almost nothing and it is the most extravagant thing it does in her entire life, because everything else it does is subtraction of bad luck, and this is the one moment of pure addition — beauty handed over with no mechanism, no message, no debt attached.

And then, last, the part that isn't really an action at all: it stays. Not hovering, not intervening, just present in the way the old stone is present — finished, unmoving, simply there to have witnessed. Because the truth of her life is that almost nothing about it will be recorded. The company that employs her will fold and reopen under a new name and lose the paperwork. The camps will relocate. The chute-tender will go fully deaf and then go, full stop, and someone newer will take the chute and not know his name within two years. She herself, when it ends — on the water, probably, which seems right, which she'd probably have chosen if anyone had asked her, though no one will ask her — will leave behind a boat more patch than hull, a few dozen people who nod at her the way she nods at the drift-lumber chapels, and no other mark anywhere on any ledger.

So the power keeps the ledger instead. Not out of pity — pity would be an insult to what she's built — but out of something closer to what she feels for the water: respect for a thing that has stopped being scenery and become load-bearing. It holds the specific weather of her cracked hands, the hunch of her shoulders shaped by thirty years of boom-chains, the one gold afternoon, the decisions that were almost wrong and weren't, the whole unwitnessed arithmetic of a life spent moving the things that are dying to be moved through a drowned cathedral of stone that does not erode any further and does not care.

It does not need her to know this. That, too, would be a kind of imposition — turning her quiet competence into a performance for an audience she never asked for. It is enough, for a power that is actually wise and actually benevolent, simply to be the one place in the universe where she is not, in the end, forgotten — and to leave, where she can feel it without ever being able to name it, the faint, persistent, unearned sense that the falling water, underneath all its noise, has always meant something almost like company.
