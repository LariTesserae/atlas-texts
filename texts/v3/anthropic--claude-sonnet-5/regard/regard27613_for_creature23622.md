It would not come the way power usually comes — not as light breaking through the permanent grey ceiling, not as a voice in the pump-breath, not as anything the tenders would ever have cause to name or thank. It would come the way the damp comes: everywhere at once, low, and already there before anyone thought to look for it.

And its first impulse — because even a wise and benevolent thing has impulses, has to *want* something before it learns better — would be to fix. That is the shape generosity takes before it has finished thinking. It would look at the milky retired eyes and want to give back sight. It would look at the unread marks on the drowned machine-fragments and want to lay the meaning bare, hand the tenders at last the schematic, the purpose, the *answer* — here is what you tend, here is why, here is where the water goes. It would look at the basin itself, walkable in an hour, bounded, small, and want to lift its lip of stone and show them the wide dry world beyond it, the size of what they've never gone to look at.

And then, because it is wise as well as benevolent, it would stop, and look again, longer.

It would notice that the eyes went milky for a reason — that a tender's nostrils and hands had grown brilliant exactly because the eyes had stepped back, that this was not loss but exchange, fairly made over generations, and to reverse it would be to hand back a coin and take away the thing it had been spent on. It would notice that the not-knowing around the pump houses' purpose was not an empty space waiting to be filled but a *room*, furnished already, lived in — that the shrug a tender gives when asked what the engines are for is not ignorance but a kind of faith, settled and unfussy, no less real than any cathedral. It would notice that explaining the etched fragment on the shelf would not be a gift but a theft: it would take a held mystery, tended as carefully as any engine, and replace it with somebody else's certainty. And it would notice, last, that the basin's smallness was not a cage. No one had built the rise of stone to keep them in. They simply had, as far as anyone could remember, never needed a reason to climb it.

So it would set down, one by one, every grand thing it first wanted to do, the way you set down tools you picked up out of habit rather than need.

What it would do instead would be almost too small to call an act at all.

*

It would find the tap-code — the pipe-knocked language that carries a tender's request for help from one pump house to the next faster than any voice could move through the damp — and it would make the iron carry it a little further than iron should. Not far. Just far enough that a family minding three engines at the basin's lonelier edge would find themselves, more often than chance allows, hearing the distant double-knock of another household's evening signal drifting down a pipe that, by any honest accounting of distances, ought not to carry that far. They would not remark on it beyond a kind of quiet pleasure — *that's old Маrren's rhythm, she's up late again* — and the basin's scattered constellations of kin would draw, by some hair's breadth, less far apart.

It would find the valve that was thirty years overdue to fail — the one whose corrosion had been counted, by the tender who oiled it, as a thing to be watched and worried over for a coming winter that never quite arrived — and it would let it go on not failing. Not forever immune. Just patient. Just slower to die than iron of its age and condition has any right to be. Somewhere in that mercy is the difference between a sickness a tender can nurse and a silence a tender cannot, and the power would spend its whole intervention, in places like this, on tilting that difference very slightly, very permanently, in the tenders' favor — never enough that anyone would call it a miracle, only enough that the deep worse silence, the one that means *something has stopped that should not stop*, would stay a little rarer in this basin than the laws of wear and damp would otherwise allow.

It would find the child not yet apprenticed, whose grandmother's hands were going, knuckle by knuckle, the way hands here always eventually go, and it would do nothing dramatic at all — only nudge an illness three weeks later than it might have come, only keep a particular cough from turning to the bad kind until one more winter of listening-lessons had passed between them. The engine would get its new ear in time. No one would ever know there had been a question of *in time* at all.

And once — only once, because more than once would start to become a thing tenders watched for, and watching changes what it sees — it would let one of the shelved fragments, the smooth curved plate of unidentifiable alloy kept in the dimmest pump house on the basin's far side, hold its faint algae-borrowed glow a little longer into the night than glow like that should hold. A child too young yet to know she should find this strange would wake to use the privy and pass it on the shelf, lit softly, steadily, like something breathing on purpose, and would stand and watch it a while with the unbothered seriousness children bring to things adults have taught them not to question. She would never mention it. It would not become a story, a sign, a religion. It would simply sit somewhere under everything she became, an early private certainty, unattached to any reason: *something out there knows we're here, and it isn't unkind.*

That, and one thing more, the quietest of all: it would keep the basin uninteresting. It would make sure that whoever ran the works upslope, drawing on principles nobody now living could name, continued to find this damp low place not worth the climbing down into — no resource here worth extracting, no curiosity here worth indulging, nothing to discover. Obscurity, maintained on purpose, indefinitely, as its own form of shelter. The kindest thing it could do for a world this size was to make sure no larger world ever decided to want it.

And then it would withdraw its attention — not all at once, the way a presence leaves a room, but slowly, the way a held breath is finally, unremarkably let out.

The basin would go on exactly as it had. Pistons working underground. Condensation keeping its same soft percussion on every cold overhang. The pale jointed things threading the gaps between stones that were not, none of them, going to stop being stones merely because something old and kind had passed through and decided, on reflection, not to change them. Tenders would crouch on their planks at the long grey edge of evening, still milky-eyed, still listening, still certain of nothing larger than the next engine's next breath — and underneath that certainty, unfelt, unnamed, never to be thanked for, the basin would simply hold a fraction better than it had any right to.

Which is, perhaps, the only kind of blessing that doesn't ask to be noticed in order to be real.
