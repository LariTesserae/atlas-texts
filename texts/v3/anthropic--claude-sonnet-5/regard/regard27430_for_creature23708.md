The power comes the way light comes to the basin — without resolving into a single, locatable source, settling instead into the gray-gold wash that already lies over everything, so that its arrival is not an event the basin could mark even if it kept marks. It does not announce itself with silence, because it has already learned, before it has done anything else, that silence here would not read as peace. It would read as warning. So the first thing the power does is nothing: it sits inside the roar without quieting a single decibel of it, and listens the way the basin's own mothers listen, sorting the ten thousand braided cries until it can find, underneath all of it, the specific furniture of a single life.

It would be easy — it understands this, turning the thought over without indulging it — to mistake this place for a wound that wants closing. The unrelieved noise. The unweathered stone, sharp at every edge because nothing has yet been permitted to grind it kind. The bodies stacked to the very limit of the available air. The complete absence of green, of softness, of slow things growing toward shade. A lesser kindness would arrive with its hands full: seed and rain-cycle and metal and quiet, the whole catalogue of comforts that elsewhere count as mercy. A lesser kindness would smooth the basin the way water eventually smooths everything, given enough time and enough licensed sorrow, and call the smoothing a gift.

But the power has watched the mother find her infant's cry inside the roar, effortless, exact, a love that has built itself a private room inside a noise that would flatten a stranger. It has watched the masons lay their hands on chisel-marks three generations old and feel no grief at the lack of a single line of their own — only the clean satisfaction of a sentence still being said correctly. It has watched the herds and the households interleave their warmth without a seam, watched hands speak a grammar more honest than most voices ever manage, watched a whole people build, out of unweathered rock and oversaturated life and a sound that never once relents, something that functions — not despite its harshness but *by means of it*, a structure load-bearing in every direction, the way an arch is strongest exactly at the place it looks most like it should fall.

So the power does not soften the stone. Does not thin the herds to a comfortable density, or hush the basin into the kind of quiet its people would correctly read as catastrophe, or hand them metal before their hands have finished asking the question that metal answers. It does not, in short, mistake this place's *strangeness* for its *suffering* — understanding, with the particular clarity available only to something old enough to have made that mistake before, elsewhere, and watched the gift dissolve the very thing it meant to save.

What it does instead is small enough to leave no seam.

There is an old mason on the eastern terrace, hands more callus than skin now, working a stair his grandfather started and his father continued, the three sets of chisel-strokes indistinguishable along the lintel exactly as the basin intends. He has no child of his own working beside him; the stair will end, mid-thought, the way the rock itself looks perpetually mid-thought, unless someone arrives to keep saying the sentence. He does not know to want this fixed. He has made his peace, in the unhurried way the basin teaches peace, with an unfinished thing staying unfinished — these things happen, walls lean into other walls, work outlives workers, that's the shape of it.

The power does not give him a child, does not bend a single fate to manufacture an heir. It only finds, in the alley two terraces down, a girl of seven who has lately taken to running her fingers along every wall she passes — an idle habit, the kind any child might have — and it leaves, in the place where her habit meets his lintel, nothing more than a slightly longer pause. A held attention. The particular tug that makes a child stop at one wall instead of continuing past it, crouch down, trace the three layered hands of chisel-work with something closer to recognition than curiosity, and ask — in the touch-grammar she's barely old enough to have mastered, a question pressed into the old mason's forearm before her voice catches up to it — *who started this.*

That's all. A pause extended by a fraction no instrument in this world could measure. The mason answers with his hands, the way everyone here answers, and within that answer a stair that was going to end mid-sentence acquires, instead, the very ordinary possibility of continuing — not guaranteed, not engineered, just *possible* again, restored to the same odds it would have had if grief or distance hadn't been quietly thinning them. Nothing here will be remembered as an intervention. There will be no story told of it, because this is not a place that keeps that kind of ledger. There will only be, decades on, one more uninterrupted lintel, indistinguishable from the others, exactly as the basin requires.

Beyond that: a retaining wall on the lower flats, holding back the seep-water through a flood season that — left alone — would have failed it by the width of one bad month, taking a pen of animals and the three families sleeping against their flank with it. The power does not move water. It does not reach down and harden stone that was never meant to harden. It only nudges a single decision earlier — the day a particular set of hands notices the lean in that wall and decides, instead of postponing the recutting one more week to finish a different task first, to do it now, today, for reasons that will feel to them like ordinary diligence and nothing more. The flood comes on schedule. The wall, for reasons no one will ever examine, holds.

And that, mostly, is what the power does: not the rewriting of conditions, but the protection of *odds* — finding the places where this saturated, raw, magnificently functioning equilibrium was about to be undone not by anything essential to its nature but by the ordinary cruelty of bad timing, narrow margins, a held breath that goes on one beat too long for reasons that have nothing to do with meaning and everything to do with chance. It leaves the noise exactly as full. It leaves the stone exactly as sharp. It does not teach them silence, or ease, or elsewhere. It does not so much as let them feel that they were, at any point, in a hand other than their own.

It simply makes sure that the sentence the basin has been saying since before anyone living can attest — *stone, water, animal, dwelling, noise, on and on, the same arrangement holding* — gets to keep being said, in the same unimproved, unsoftened, magnificently load-bearing way, for a good while longer than the odds alone would have allowed.

Then it withdraws the way it arrived: without resolving into anything locatable, folding itself back into the gray-gold wash that was always already there, leaving no monument, no myth, no scar of having been noticed at all — which is, for a place that distrusts silence and has never once needed an elsewhere, the only form mercy could take without becoming, despite every good intention, just another kind of erosion.
