**What the Power Considered, and Refused**

It would have been easy — terribly, dangerously easy — to fix this place.

The power, when it turned its attention to the Sounding Flats, felt the pull of every obvious mercy at once. *Quiet the drone.* A thought, a gesture, and the basin could know silence for the first time in the whole of its geological memory. *Warm the light.* Give them a sun to point to, a horizon that resolves instead of dissolving, shadows to tell them what hour it is. *Drown the listening one*, or lift it gently into some other water, somewhere far from the people who have spent generations not-looking at the pools deep enough to hide it. *Give them metal. Give them fire that catches. Give them ease.*

Every one of these was a kindness. Every one of these would have been a kind of violence — not despite being kindnesses, but *because* of it, because each one assumed that what the power found difficult to witness was, in itself, the thing wrong with this place. The power sat with that assumption for what would have been, to the People below, several centuries, and found it wanting.

It listened — really listened, the way the People themselves had taught their ears to listen, sorting story from roar — and heard that the drone was not an affliction laid upon a silence that wanted restoring. The silence had never been there to restore. The noise *was* the medium, the weather, the thing every other thing in this place had grown into, the way a fish grows into water rather than suffering its absence of air. To still it would not be to heal the basin. It would be to unmake the only world its inhabitants — chitinous and human both — had ever built a self around.

It looked at the cairns and understood, slowly, the way wisdom is always slower than power, that *the bones of those who chose where to stop* was not a tragedy needing correction but a sentence with grammar, dignity, completeness — a people who had taken an indifferent, overwhelming geology and made of it a practice, a language, a way of loving each other with taps against stone because shouting was useless and silence was a lie the world had never told them. Their theology of attendance — *the basin thinking*, the discipline of going still and watchful when the unison-flicker passed through the swarm — was not a superstition waiting to be enlightened away. It was, the power realized with something like recognition, *the same thing the power itself was doing right now.* Watching a vastness too large to fully see. Not interpreting it. Keeping faithful, attentive, quiet company with its strangeness.

It would not be heard, the power thought, gathering its intentions back the way one gathers a hand from a sleeper's hair — not because it lacked the power to help, but because the People had already built, out of leverage and knot and patience, out of broad-soled feet and triaged hearing and cairns like prayers, *everything a self needs in order to mean something here.* To intervene structurally — to quiet, to warm, to safen — would be to hand them a world they had not made and could not read, the cruelest possible gift: ease purchased with the unlearning of every hard-won fluency that made them who they were.

So the power did not fix the Sounding Flats.

But it did not do *nothing*, either, because nothing is its own kind of abandonment, and the power was not only wise. It was benevolent, and benevolence wants, in the end, to be known.

So it waited for the lull — the duller roar between tidal swells, the closest thing the basin had to a held breath — and during one single unison-flicker, the basin-wide flare that comes from no trigger and means, as far as any elder has ever argued, nothing at all, it added one beat.

Not a new sound. Not a voice. Just a pattern within the flicker that did not belong to the swarm's own slow blind coordination — a flare-and-pause-and-flare again, deliberate where everything else was diffuse, *aimed* where everything else was only emergent. It lasted four seconds. It happened once, in the lifetime of one particular old woman who had spent sixty years standing on cairns to lift her ears above the bass, and who happened, on that evening, to be watching.

She told no one for three days, turning it over the way her people turn stones, testing its edges. When she did speak — low, percussive, pitched to cut under the drone — she said only that the basin had, for four seconds, *answered* rather than merely *thought*. She had no further claim to make. She built no new cairn for it, taught no new sign for it, founded no rite. It entered the oral fragments as a single, contested, treasured anomaly — the kind of thing argued about across generations in taps against wet stone, never resolved, never forgotten, neither proof nor parable, only a small bright inconsistency in an otherwise consistent world.

Which was, the power judged, exactly the size of gift this place could hold without breaking. Not rescue. Not revelation. Just the knowledge — offered once, deniable, unrepeated — that the quiet they had spent their whole strange, deafening, magnificent culture learning to count was, on at least one evening, being counted back.

Then the power withdrew its attention, the way one steps back from a held breath to let it be exhaled in its own time, and the drone rose toward dusk the way it always had, and the People struck their frames and lifted them again at the next lull, and the listening one did not surface, and the cairns kept their grammar, and nothing about the Sounding Flats was changed — except that, very faintly, under the noise, it now also remembered, once, being heard.
