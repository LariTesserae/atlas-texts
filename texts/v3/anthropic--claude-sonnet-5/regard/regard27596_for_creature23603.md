**What Was Done**

It did not arrive the way arrivals are usually imagined — no descent, no parting of the haze, no moment a traveler on the plates could point to and say *here, this is when it came.* If anything noticed its presence at all, it would have registered as a small, unaccountable cooling at noon, a thermal that should have spiraled up off the hottest plate and didn't, as if something had, very gently, asked the heat to wait.

It looked for a long time before it did anything. This is worth saying plainly, because the temptation — for anything wise enough to find this place and kind enough to care what it found — is to act first and understand second, and the Flatlands punish that order of operations more severely than almost anywhere a benevolence might wander. So it watched a Tenant not move for four years. It watched a stalk fail to grow by any measurable increment across a span in which, elsewhere, forests would have stood and burned and stood again. It listened to the dusk-ticking and could not, for a long while, separate the geology's settling from the biology's waking, and took a kind of joy in that — in being, for once, unable to cheat its way to comprehension.

And it felt, as anything compassionate would feel, the first and most natural impulse: *this is too hard a life. Bring it water. Bring it company. Loosen the spacing, soften the wait, let the cracking come sooner and more often, let these things know each other, let them not be so alone for so long under a sun that asks nothing because it has not been told to ask anything else.*

It got as far as imagining the rain — a calling-down, generous and total, falling everywhere at once instead of in that one indifferent curtain on the horizon. It imagined every stalk answered in the same season, every Tenant opening its dorsal seam together, a whole plain of small grey mouths drinking at once instead of one here and one there across years of separation. It held that image the way you hold a kindness before you offer it, turning it over to see if it is sharp on some side you haven't noticed yet.

It was sharp. The spacing was not deprivation; it was architecture. A hundred meters between one stalk and the next was not loneliness imposed on the Tenants from outside — it *was* the Tenants, the precise shape of their relation to each other and to the seam-network that fed them, evolved or designed or simply *settled into* over a span of time this power could respect but not casually overrule. Flood the plain with rain every season and there would be no Tenants left within a few centuries — only a crowded, undifferentiated mass at every joint, competing for what had always been sized to one. To save them from waiting was to end the thing that waiting had made them. The patience was not a hardship laid over their lives. It was load-bearing.

So the rain was not changed. Not one curtain redirected, not one cell seeded early. The plain's weather went on being exactly as capricious, exactly as far between, exactly as indifferent as it had always been to any single stalk's thirst.

What it did instead was smaller, and took longer to decide, which is usually how you can tell a thing is actually wise rather than merely powerful.

First: it made one adjustment, so quiet that nothing in the world's outward behavior changed at all. Among the nodules cast into the standing seam-water — those small hard grit-colored seeds that wait years, decades, for a rain direct and repeated enough to soften them — it ensured, simply, that none would wait forever. Not sooner. Not easier. Only: *not without end.* Somewhere in the long capricious arithmetic of the convective cells, a thread was tied so that every claimed seam, however far out on the haze-softened horizon, however overlooked by the wandering rain-cells in any given decade, would eventually, within some bound long by any other world's measure but finite by the Flatlands' own, receive its debt. No Tenant's lineage would end in pure withholding. The world kept every other cruelty of chance it had built for itself — the unevenness, the unfairness of curtain-rain falling here and not there, the long human-shaming wait — and lost only the one cruelty that wasn't characterful, only structural: true and final abandonment.

Second, and this took longer to arrive at, because it required understanding something almost embarrassingly simple: the power noticed that for all the Tenants' extraordinary patience, for all that they had made stillness into a kind of mastery, they were, within that stillness, *alone* — not lonely in the human sense, not suffering for it, but singular, sealed, each one ticking through its decades inside the dark of its own dormancy with no way of knowing that a hundred meters off, under another stalk, another version of the same long wait was being kept. It did not give them speech. It did not give them want, or society, or any of the apparatus that would have made them something other than what they were so carefully, slowly built to be. It gave them only this: that in dormancy, in that vast slow interior to which no instrument has ever been sensitive enough to listen, each Tenant's stillness would now touch, faintly, at the very edges, the stillness of its neighbors. Not communication. Not company in any active sense. Closer to the way two long, low, even notes played far apart in pitch will still, if held long enough, produce between them a third sound that neither one is making alone. A held chord, where before there had only been many separate held notes, each convinced of its solitude.

No Tenant cracked open sooner because of this. No stalk grew an inch faster. The plain looked, to anyone walking it after, exactly as it had looked to anyone walking it before — heat-shimmer, crisp seams, the long indifferent light, the rare violent grace of a localized rain on a far curtain of horizon. The power left no monument, because a monument is exactly the kind of thing this place's whole grammar refuses, and it understood by then that to mark its visit would be the one true violence available to it — a permanence imposed from outside on a world whose every permanence had been earned, slowly, from within.

It did one last thing, smaller than the others, and this one was less for the Tenants than for itself, or for whatever future attention might someday find this place again. It made the instrument. Not a device left lying on a plate — nothing so clumsy — but a fact folded gently into the air itself, so that from now on, to anyone who stayed long enough and listened closely enough, the cooling-tick of a settling plate and the waking-crack of a Tenant would no longer be quite identical. A difference of a quarter-tone, no more. Enough that patience, if anyone ever again brought enough of it to this place, would finally be rewarded with the power to tell the foundry's geology from its biology — to know, at last, which sound was settling and which was waking, and to hold each in its own kind of regard.

Then it withdrew the same way it had come: without parting any haze, without being noticed by the one or two small inland thermals that had, for one unaccountable afternoon, declined to rise. The heat came back exactly where it had paused. The world went on building itself plate by plate, seam by seam, the wire-stalks standing their hundred meters apart in the long light, the Tenants dreaming their slow grey dream a little less alone than before, waiting, as they always had, for water that would now — eventually, unhurried, on no schedule any living witness could name — always, always come.
