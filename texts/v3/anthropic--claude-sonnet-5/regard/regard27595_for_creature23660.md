The attention arrives the way weather arrives here — not as a decision announced, but as a pressure that was already gathering, noticed only once it has begun.

What draws it is not the canopy roar, though that is what a lesser power might mistake for the country's voice. It is the silence underneath the roar — the held breath that ripples outward when something patient passes, yes, but also a second, quieter silence that nothing in the Standing Country has yet learned to read as dangerous, because it has no teeth and asks for nothing and simply, slowly, is happening: an old woman on a platform built by hands now three generations dead, holding in her throat a set of notes that no one younger has fully learned, because the children who might have learned them were taken by falls, by fever, by the ordinary attrition of a people who number in dozens across a ridge-system the size of a small nation. She is not dying tonight. She is not in danger tonight. But the songs are thinning the way the lash-folk themselves are thinning, generation over generation, and she is the one who has noticed, lying awake in the warm dark while the insect-drone does its endless work below her, doing the arithmetic of who is left to receive what she carries, and arriving, most nights, at a number that frightens her more than any predator's silence ever has.

This is what the power sees. Not a crisis — the Standing Country does not deal in crises, it deals in centuries — but a thread fraying at a rate too slow for any of its bearers to fight and too fast for the mountain underneath to notice or care. The stone has all the time in the world. The woman does not. This mismatch is the whole of her grief, though she would not call it that; she would simply call it *the quiet getting bigger*.

The power considers, the way wisdom always must, the larger intervention. It could be a flood of fortune — more children surviving the climb, more fruiting growths discovered, the predator's patience dulled across a whole generation so that fewer calves are lost and the Callers' herds swell and the country's noise grows fuller rather than thinner. It could press its hand flat against the whole ecology and lift it, gently, toward abundance.

It does not do this. Not because it lacks the power, but because it has been watching long enough — or perhaps has watched *this exact shape of trouble* in enough other green countries, enough other patient mountains — to know what such a hand does, over time, to a place built on scarcity as a kind of art. The Standing Country's people climb the way they climb, sing the way they sing, ration effort the way they ration it, *because* gravity is real and falling is unforgiven and fruit is finite and children are precious in proportion to how easily they are lost. Lift the scarcity and you do not get the same people, luckier. You get different people, in the same place, who have forgotten why the cordage is woven the way it's woven, why the songs carry the meanings they carry, why a held note means *here* and another means *danger* and a silence means something has gone wrong that words were never built to hold. A wise power does not flood a garden it admires. It tends it, where tending is wanted, and otherwise it watches its hands.

So what it does is smaller, and almost entirely indistinguishable, after the fact, from luck.

It does not save the old woman's line. It makes sure, on one particular night when her granddaughter — twelve years old, already strong-palmed, already careless in the specific way of someone who has not yet fallen and so does not yet believe she can — reaches for a vine handhold that has, in fact, gone soft with a rot no eye could have caught from above, that her hand finds, instead, the neighboring vine, the sound one, for no reason she will ever be able to name except that her fingers simply went there instead, the way fingers sometimes do. The power does not strengthen the rotten vine. It does not need to. It only needs the girl to live to be taught, and so it nudges nothing but the half-second decision of a hand, and lets the mountain keep its indifference fully intact everywhere else.

It does this again, differently, a season later: guides a small flightless forager — the kind that lives its whole life never higher than the lowest stack — into a hollow root-throat holding standing water exactly when the girl is thirsty and three days from the nearest known spring, so that what looks, to her, like the forest simply providing, the way the forest is always faintly rumored to provide for those who listen well, is in fact a single quiet correction, applied once, never repeated, indistinguishable from the country's own ordinary generosity.

It touches the old woman herself only once, and only at the very end, when the songs have finally passed — imperfectly, as all inheritance is imperfect, the granddaughter holding maybe two-thirds of what the grandmother held, which is itself two-thirds of what was held before her, the long slow erosion of a culture mirroring, with a kind of terrible grace, the long slow erosion of the mountain it lives on. In the old woman's last hours, lying on the platform that has held four generations of her family's weight and grown only stronger for the lashing, the power gives her — not more life, not a vision, not a promise that the line will hold — but simply the certainty, arriving from nowhere she could name, that the granddaughter's two-thirds is *enough*. That a song does not have to survive whole to have done its work. That the country does not require completeness from anyone living on it, only continuance, the same bargain it strikes with every fruiting vine and every herd and every fused, centuries-merged trunk: *go on, imperfectly, for as long as you can, and let what comes after be its own thing.*

This is the whole of the gift. No new song is taught that wasn't already half-known. No fall is prevented except the one fall, on the one night, that would have ended a particular line of inheritance one generation early. No abundance is summoned. The herds call exactly as far as they always called; the patient hunter waits exactly as long as it always waited; the insect-drone does not rise or fall by any measure an instrument could catch.

And this, in the end, is the shape the power's wisdom takes everywhere it goes, in every green country and every standing mountain it ever turns its attention to: it does not arrive as a flood. It arrives as the one hand that finds the sound vine instead of the rotten one, as the one hollow that happens to hold water on the one dry afternoon, as the one certainty that arrives, unearned and unexplained, in an old woman's last hour, that what she carried was enough.

It leaves the way it came — without announcement, folded back into the ordinary weather of the place, so that if the lash-folk ever did tell stories about it, the stories would sound exactly like the stories they already tell about the unconfirmed platforms higher up the ridge, the ones built by hands not quite like current hands: *a single sentence, given reluctantly, if you wait long enough and ask gently enough, and nothing more, because there is nothing more that needs saying, and because the mountain, underneath all of it, is still not listening, and was never going to be the one who needed convinced.*
