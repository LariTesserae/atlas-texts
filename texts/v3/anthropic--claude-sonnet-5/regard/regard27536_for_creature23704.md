The power has been watching for a long time before it does anything at all, and this, in itself, is the first kindness — it does not mistake watching for waiting-to-act, does not feel the itch to *fix* what isn't broken, doesn't see a thin population and empty buildings and a fern reclaiming a path and think *here is a problem requiring solution.* It sits with the place the way the old man sits in his doorway, eyes shut, listening. It learns the rhythm of the gong before it considers the people who built it.

What it notices, eventually, is not lack. It notices *fit*. A people who learned to be few by becoming exquisitely good at being few — who built their grief or their dwindling or whatever thinned them out, generations back, into trip-hammers and flumes and counterweights, so thoroughly that the absence itself now sings. That's not a wound looking for a bandage. That's a kind of mastery so complete it no longer recognizes itself as a response to loss. The power respects this the way you'd respect a scar that became, with enough decades, simply a feature of someone's face — to smooth it away would be to take something, not give it.

So it does not repopulate the terrace. Does not send fauna back, or fill the empty households, or stop the ground from its slow undecided settling. It does not explain the cracks, or resolve whether the mountain rises or sinks. Those mysteries are not cruelties being inflicted on the place; they're part of its grammar, the medium in which these people have learned to be patient and precise and tender toward one another and toward the rock. Take the uncertainty away and you take away the fingertip on the fracture, the nod that means nothing and everything, the whole quiet liturgy of watching something become.

What it *does* do — because benevolence without any gesture at all curdles into mere spectatorship, and wisdom alone isn't yet love — is smaller than you'd expect, and aimed only at the edges of things, never the center.

It makes sure the old man's memory holds a little longer than his body would otherwise allow — not forever, not unnaturally, but generously: a few more winters in which he is still the index of which pipe feeds which silence, a few more seasons in which the knowledge has time to pass to the boy with the bucket before it would otherwise have gone dark with him. Not a miracle. Just a mercy in the timing, the kind that could pass for luck.

It nudges, by some increment too small to call intervention, the rate at which one particular hairline crack — the one under the gong-tower's footing, the one nobody mentions but everyone's fingertip has visited — settles rather than spreads. Not stopped. Settled. The mountain still gets to finish deciding what it wants to be. It just decides, in that one spot, toward stability rather than collapse, the way a held breath sometimes resolves toward calm rather than panic for no reason anyone could name. The people will never know this happened. That's correct. It isn't for them to know; it's for the tower to still be standing in four generations, holding the vines that nobody removes, so that the great-great-grandchild of the boy with the bucket has a tower to hang vines on at all.

It does one more thing, the only one that touches a person directly, and it touches the child — the girl, wherever she is in this telling, who is learning the flumes before her letters. Not a gift of insight beyond her years, not a vision, nothing that would mark her as chosen or strange to the people who love her. Just this: on one ordinary wet morning, doing some ordinary task — lifting the eastern gate, maybe, listening for the pitch of water in a channel to know if it's silted — she will feel, all at once and for no reason she could explain, a wave of plain unearned gladness at the *sound* of the place. Not pride in the work, not relief that the chore is done. Just gladness, sudden and whole, that the hundred small machines are singing and that she is one of the few hands alive who knows the secret architecture behind the haunting. A child's joy in her inheritance, arriving early and staying.

It is not much. It will not make a story anyone tells. But that gladness will sit in her like a seed, and forty years on, when she is the old woman in the doorway with her eyes shut, listening, it will have grown into the thing that makes her gentle instead of merely dutiful when she teaches the next child which gate feeds which silence — and gentleness, passed down that way, hand to hand, through people who have made an art of being few, is the kind of inheritance that outlasts foundations.

Then the power withdraws its attention, gently, the way you'd ease a door shut without letting the latch click — not gone, exactly, but no longer watching with the held, deliberate focus of *deciding*. It leaves the mountain to keep arguing with itself about what it means to become solid. It leaves the ferns to keep winning. It leaves the gong to its slow interval, counting time for a place that geology hasn't finished writing, and the single dark bird, which does have a name, working the updrafts at the cliff's edge, going nowhere, staying exactly where it's needed.
