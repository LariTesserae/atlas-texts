The power comes the way light reaches the bottom of the Cistern-Hold — indirectly, after long travel, diffused by the time it arrives so that nothing inside ever quite sees it arrive. It does not announce itself. It would not know how, here, where even a shout from arm's length can be eaten by lattice-noise. It simply attends, the way attention is the whole of what such a power is for.

It watches a long time before it considers doing anything at all. This is the first discipline of benevolence, and the hardest one, and most who call themselves kind never learn it: that watching must come before wanting, and want before action, and that the gap between watching and wanting is where wisdom either grows or doesn't.

What it sees:

A structure built whole by hands now gone, finished and then abandoned to a patience it did not ask for. An ecology that arrived after, uninvited, complete, indifferent, thriving. And one old singular thing, neither builder nor tenant nor quite anything the power has a category ready-made for, which a very long time ago made a choice — to stay, to remain, to become the maintenance of a purpose it never held an opinion about — and which has not, since, been asked whether it would make that choice again.

The power's first impulse, because even wise and benevolent powers have impulses, is to heal something. This is the oldest reflex of benevolence and also its most common error: to mistake stillness for suffering, arrest for arrest in the punitive sense, a life that has stopped changing for a life that has been stopped. It almost moves to undo the strange preservation — to let the Warden age, or rest, or finally finish whatever the building itself stopped short of finishing generations ago. Its hand, if it has a hand, is halfway to that thought before it stops itself.

It stops itself because it asks, honestly, the only question that benevolence is ever actually for: *whose need is this?*

And the answer, looked at clearly, is its own. The Warden is not crying out. The Warden has not been glimpsed in distress in all its long unwitnessed residency — only glimpsed, rarely, briefly, going about the unhurried business of being the thing that keeps a channel clear. The bats clatter. The eels go still and then don't. The water finds the lowest course, every time, because something large and patient and uncomplaining moves through it on a schedule that serves itself and incidentally serves everyone else who has moved in since. This is not a wound. This is an old, working answer to a question nobody alive remembers being asked.

So the power does not heal it. It does not wake it to a more legible kind of life, does not finish its arrested erosion for it, does not lift it bodily out into water under open sky on the assumption that openness is always better than enclosure, motion always better than long residency, change always better than the agreement a thing has made with its own continuance. That would not be rescue. That would be the power mistaking its own discomfort with mystery for the Warden's need.

What it does instead is smaller, and harder to do well, because restraint dressed up as a gift still has to be a gift and not merely an absence.

It looks for the channel — the one mentioned in no surviving word but implied by every other fact of the place: the original water-path, wherever it once led, the route by which something gill-pale and slate-dark might first have entered a finished building and decided, gratefully or indifferently, to remain. Most of it is silted, collapsed, forgotten even by the water, which has long since been re-routed through engineered spirals that have nothing to do with whatever lay beyond the walls when the walls were first closed. The power does not reopen it. Opening it outright would be its own kind of violence — a door forced where no one asked for a door, an exit pressed on a thing that may have stopped, centuries past, wanting one.

It only unseals it. Quietly. Without current, without urgency, without making of it an invitation that has to be answered today or ever. A single hairline fracture-line, low in the oldest wall, reopened just enough that water could, if it chose, eventually find its way along that route again, the way it finds every other low place, given time, given no one stopping it.

Then it does one more thing, smaller still: it touches, very lightly, the bronze fittings — not all of them, not enough to wake any new hum that hasn't been heard before, just enough that the next time the Warden passes near one and sets it humming, the resonance will carry, somewhere underneath the felt-in-the-teeth pressure the eels go still for, a second note. Not a command. Not a summons. Nothing that says *leave* or *stay* or *come* or *finish*. Only something that says, in whatever language passes between a fitting struck by an old body and the body that strikes it: *you have been seen. The choice you made, you are still making, every day, by remaining. It is still a choice. Here is evidence that the world outside your walls knows it, and asks nothing different of you, and has left the way open in case you ever want it, without judgment either way.*

And then the power withdraws the way it arrived — diffusely, without a door closing anywhere, without the bats startling or the steady percussive clatter missing even one beat. It does not return to check. Checking would itself be a kind of doubt about the gift, a suggestion that the gift required a particular answer to count as having worked.

The Cistern-Hold goes on as it was: dim, gray-gold, roaring with small life, nearly empty of stuff and full of sound, water finding the lowest channel because something old and patient still moves through it, the way a fish maintains the river that maintains the fish. Somewhere low in one wall, a fracture that wasn't open before now is, leading toward a larger water nobody inside remembers and most will never find. The bronze still hums on no schedule anyone has caught.

If the Warden ever notices the second note under the first — if it ever, on some unwitnessed evening uncounted by any calendar, turns its long body toward that reopened seam in the wall and considers, for the length of held breath, what lies past it — no one will see that either. That decision, like almost everything true about this place, will belong only to the one who has to live inside it.

This, in the end, is what the wise power decides benevolence means, here: not to finish the story for a creature that has been quietly, competently authoring its own continuance since before anyone had a word for the room it lives in — but to make sure, gently, irreversibly, without asking for thanks or proof or even an answer, that the next chapter, whatever it is, is still the Warden's to write.
