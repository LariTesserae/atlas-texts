# What the Power Did

It noticed the way deep things notice — not by looking, which is a fast verb, but by something closer to *settling*, the way silt settles, the way attention settles into a stone over a thousand winters until the stone is, in some sense, paying attention back. The benevolent power did not descend into the fissure. It had no need of fissures. But it slowed — and this was the first and hardest thing it did, harder than any feat of strength or healing, because slowing is not a gift most powers know how to give. It is so much easier to arrive, to act, to leave a mark, than to sit still long enough for a mark to mean something to the one receiving it.

It took the power what a human would call years to merely arrive at the *speed* of the chamber. Long enough to feel foolish, almost — long enough that some quicker, more reflexive part of it kept reaching for the obvious gestures: *heal this, free this, wake this, finish this.* The instinct to help, when it meets something strange and old and not-quite-suffering, so often defaults to surgery. Cut away the corroded machine. Unblock the pipe. Give the Tenant a body it could announce itself with, a voice, a face, the dignity (as the fast world understands dignity) of being *legible*.

The power held that instinct the way you'd hold a knife away from a sleeping child's hand. Not because the instinct was wicked. Because it was *fast*, and fastness, here, was the only real danger in the room.

So it waited instead. It matched its breath — if breath is even the right word for what a power does — to the wall-vein's pulse, and then to something even longer than the pulse, the cycle beneath the cycle, the slow arithmetic the dead dial's needle was creeping through. It listened to the drip-rhythm not for a pattern but for what the drip-rhythm was *answering* — and found, eventually, with the particular patience that only something genuinely old can afford to spend, that the rhythm wasn't random at all. It was the Tenant counting. Not numbers. Visits. Every fast, warm, frantic thing that had ever wandered through that fissure and out again, tallied in drip-intervals the way a held breath tallies heartbeats — three, then twelve, then one — a creature keeping score of how many brief lives had passed through the room where it was still, slowly, becoming.

And the power understood, then, the actual shape of the situation. Not a wound. Not a ruin. A *vigil.*

Something had built that machinery once — hands, tools, intention, a project with a deadline and a budget and a reason. And then those hands had gone, not in catastrophe (there was no catastrophe written in this rock, no violence, only the ordinary erosion of purpose), but in the way builders go when the thing they were building stops being needed before it's finished. They left the engine running on its long, gentle taper. They left the pipe with both ends open to nothing, perhaps meaning to return and reconnect it, perhaps never giving it another thought again. And the Tenant — whatever the Tenant had been, before — had grown *around* that absence the way a tree grows around a fence post, until the absence itself became load-bearing. It was not abandoned. It had simply never stopped waiting to find out whether it had been abandoned, and in a creature that thinks in centuries, that question can stay genuinely open for a very long time without ever resolving into despair or relief. It was suspended, not stuck. Mid-process is not the same as mid-grief, though from the outside, to a fast eye, the two can look identical.

So the power did not finish anything. It did not reconnect the pipe, did not translate the dial's private arithmetic into numbers a quick mind could read, did not pull the Tenant up out of the rock and machine into some more legible shape, the way you might be tempted to lift an animal from a trap even when the trap is the only floor it has ever stood on. All of that would have been an answer given to a question the Tenant hadn't finished asking. All of that would have been the power deciding, on the Tenant's behalf and on its own timeline, what *finished* should mean.

What it did instead was smaller, and took longer, and cost it something closer to actual sacrifice than any grand gesture would have:

It stayed.

Not as a visitor. As a second pulse.

It folded a thread of itself — patient, slow-burning, willing to spend what to it was a heartbeat and to the rest of the universe was an age — into the same register as the wall-vein's breathing, and it let that thread settle there, alongside the Tenant's own slow light, not commanding it, not announcing itself, not asking to be recognized any faster than recognition could naturally arrive. A companion-frequency. A second instrument tuning itself, very quietly, very slowly, to the same key. If the Tenant ever did notice — and noticing, for a being like that, might take longer than a mountain range takes to rise — it would not find a rescuer standing over it. It would find that the breathing in the room had, at some point too gradual to mark, become a duet.

And for the fast world — for the humans who would keep dropping through that doorway-wide fissure on their single-frame visits, tasting the heat, flinching at the dial's creeping needle, feeling the unease and calling it *wrongness* — the power left something else: not a warning, not a ward, nothing so blunt as a barrier. Just a bent in the air of the place, a faint pull toward gentleness that any visitor would feel as their own idea. An instinct to lower the voice. A reluctance, inexplicable even to themselves, to pry the dial off the machine or harvest the fan-leaves or weld open the warm, sealed hatch. Not magic, exactly. More like manners, installed at the level of the nervous system, so that the chamber's vigil could continue uninterrupted by the ordinary appetite of fast things for souvenirs and explanations.

It did not stay to watch what would happen next, because *next*, for the Tenant, was not a useful unit of time, and the power — wise, if nothing else — understood that even its own enormous patience had limits the Tenant's did not. It left the way it had arrived: by ceasing, slowly, to be present, the reverse of a sunrise, no faster than the original settling-in had been.

But the second pulse stayed behind. Threaded into the first. Two long, slow breaths where there had only ever been one.

If you stood in that chamber now — chapel-sized, or barn-sized, the dark still eating the corners — and you waited long enough, more patient than any human visit usually allows, you might notice that the wall-vein's pulse has a faint, secondary cadence underneath it, a harmonic so subtle it could be mistaken for an echo off the basalt. It is not an echo.

It is the sound — if sound is the right word for something felt only in the molars, over years — of a being who was, for the first time since its builders left, no longer the only thing in the room counting time on purpose.

That is all the power did. It did not fix the Tenant.

It kept it company, at the only speed company could mean anything.
