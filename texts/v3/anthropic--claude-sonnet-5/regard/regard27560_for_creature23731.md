It would not come the way power usually comes — not as light breaking, not as a voice in the wind-roar, not as some sudden golden hush falling over the birds. Anything like that would already be a kind of violence here, a foreign weather laid over a weather that has worked out its own terms across centuries. So if it came at all, it would come the way the cloud comes: gradually, from below, indistinguishable at first from the ordinary wet that the shelf already knows how to absorb.

It would spend a long time only watching. Longer than seems necessary for a power that could, presumably, know everything about the place in an instant. But knowing in an instant and understanding are different operations, and the second one requires something closer to dwelling. So it would dwell — through a dozen cloud-cycles, through the slow drying and re-soaking of the lichen, through the fallen tree's stubborn new shoots reaching up past their parent's corpse, through the raptors' hunting hours and the insects' brief frantic blooms and the long unwatched stretches when the creature folds itself into its crevice and simply waits out the wind. It would learn the shelf's circuits the way the creature has learned them: not as facts but as a rhythm taken into the body, if a power not built of body can be said to take anything in that way. Perhaps it can. Perhaps wisdom is exactly that — the willingness to acquire a rhythm before presuming to touch what runs on it.

And having dwelt, it would feel the pull of every obvious mercy, and set each one down in turn.

It would feel the pull toward companionship — to call up out of some other fold of the world a second creature, a mate, an end to the long unbroken solitude. And it would look at the shape of that solitude and see that it is not a wound. The creature does not pace, does not call out into the cloud, does not search the scrub at night for a shape that isn't there. It has organized its whole quiet appetite around a single living, the way the dwarf-trees have organized themselves around the wind — not in spite of being alone but built, root and bone, out of aloneness, so that any companion brought now would arrive as a stranger to a life that has no spare room left to make one welcome in. So that mercy would be set down.

It would feel the pull toward time — to slow the creature's clouded eyes further, loosen its hold on the years, let it go on longer in its low stone circuits. And it would look at the shelf itself, two hundred paces end to end, every crack already accounted for, every basin of standing water already known by heart, and understand that more time here would not be a gift but a sentence — that the kindness of an ending is part of what makes a small world bearable to live inside. So that mercy, too, would be set down.

It would feel, longest and hardest, the pull toward answer — toward letting the creature understand, in whatever way a clouded amber eye could be made to understand, what the ring of stones was for, who built it, what question the evening watching was meant, once, to address. This is the cruelest mercy to refuse, because it looks so much like simple kindness: here is a ritual that has outlived its meaning, surely it would be good to give the meaning back. But a wise power, dwelling long enough, would notice what the ritual has become in the meantime — not a question waiting on an answer, but a practice that has gone on so long it no longer needs one, the way a heartbeat doesn't need a reason to keep occurring. To answer it now would not complete it. It would end it. The lifted head on clear evenings, the stillness held as long as the light holds — that has become, itself, the entire content of the act, and a power that loved the creature rightly would not trade a living practice for a finished fact.

So, having considered the rescue, the extension, the revelation, and set each one down, what would be left?

Almost nothing. Which is the right amount.

It would do this: on the rare clear evenings, when the cloud parts and the low sun comes in sideways and the spire's shadow finds its old configuration across the stone ring, the power would simply be there too — not seen, not felt as anything the creature's senses could resolve into shape, but present in the place a watcher would stand, the way absence sometimes has a temperature. For as long as the light held, something would be occupying the spot the ring was built to address, after all this time, again. Not an answer. A presence willing to receive the watching as the watching was once meant to be received. The creature would not know this had happened. It would simply, perhaps, feel the evening go a little more completely the way it has always wanted to go — the way a held note, finally, finds the chord it was always implying.

It would do this also: it would keep the goat-track exactly as treacherous as it has always been, would keep the mist exactly as disorienting, would let no easier way up the rock ever quite resolve itself out of the stone's natural reluctance. Not out of indifference — the opposite. Because the obscurity is load-bearing. Take it away, bring climbers, bring studies, bring the kind of attention that wants to name and protect and therefore manage, and the green room stops being a green room and starts being a site. The power's whole gift here would be to keep nothing from changing.

And at the end — because there would be an end, the power having refused to delay it — it would attend the creature's dying the way it attended the evenings: present, unfelt, asking nothing, changing nothing about the manner of it, except perhaps softening, in some small undetectable way, the last hour's fear, if fear is even the right word for what a creature like this would feel, low in its crevice, the wind finding it through a gap it had always known about and never minded before. A mercy small enough to leave no mark. After which the moss, which has been patient about everything else here, would be patient about this too.

And then the shelf would simply go on. Cloud up the cliff-face in its long throats of stone, birds keeping their competing noise, the fallen tree's new shoots reaching higher every year past where their parent used to stand, the basin-stone holding its little ration of sky-water, the ring of knee-high stones losing one more millimeter of edge to the lichen's slow chemistry. No mark of the visit anywhere. No mark of the visitor. Only, if anyone improbable ever stood at the center of that shelf on a certain kind of evening, a feeling — hard to source, easy to dismiss — that the silence under all that noise was, just then, very slightly less empty than silence by itself should be.

That, and nothing else, is what a benevolent and wise power would do here. It would come close enough to love the place correctly, and then it would have the discipline to leave it exactly alone.
