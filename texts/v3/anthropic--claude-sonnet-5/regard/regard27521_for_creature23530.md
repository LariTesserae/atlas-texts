The power comes the way the cold does — without announcement, settling rather than arriving, and it spends a long time before it does anything at all, because that is the first discipline of being wise: to understand before touching.

It listens the way the hollow-dweller listens. Not with ears tuned to meaning but with something tuned to structure — and so the first thing it learns is not a fact but a *feeling*, the same feeling the creature has organized its entire nervous existence around: that the roar is shelter, and the near-silence is the dangerous place, and this is exactly backward from how almost every other lit and living thing in every other place understands the relationship between sound and safety. The power sits with that inversion for a long time. It would be very easy, having power, to get this wrong — to assume that quiet is a mercy and noise is a violence done to a place, and to come in with the obvious gift: *let me give you silence.* That would be a kind of murder wearing the mask of kindness. The hum is not noise here. The hum is the only floor this creature has ever had to stand on.

So it considers, and discards, the easy gifts one by one.

It could still the compressors, let the engines finally rust all the way through and rest, return the hollow to whatever quieter thing it might have been before some unremembered hand wired it for sound thirty years ago. But the hollow-dweller has no other way of knowing where cover is. Silence given suddenly, completely, would not free it — it would blind the one sense it has left and call that blindness peace.

It could give it eyes. Real ones. Let it see the green-black tangle, the frost-lattices, the slow beauty of the black mirror-pools it currently only tastes by submersion and patient current. But eyes are not gifts in the abstract; they are gifts only to a nervous system built to use them, and grafting sight onto a mind built entirely out of vibration and gradient would not be a window opened. It would be a second creature, wearing the first one's body, with none of its inherited knowledge of how to survive in it.

It could lift it out — relocate it, gently, to somewhere warmer, lit, kinder by every external measure. But "kinder by external measure" is the oldest and most forgivable mistake benevolence makes: assuming that what looks like suffering from outside a life is what that life experiences as suffering from within it. The hollow-dweller is not lonely in the way the word means among things that flock. It is not deprived in the way the word means among things with eyes. It is, by every internal measure available to it, *managing* — grazing without killing its food, reading the engine-architecture with real fluency, knotting into the cold months with kin it tolerates exactly as much as it needs to. To remove it from the one system that has ever made sense to its body would not be rescue. It would be the abolition of the only competence it has ever been allowed to develop.

What the power notices, underneath all the things it could do and shouldn't, is the one true fragility in the whole arrangement — not the cold, not the noise, not the blindness, none of which are wounds, all of which are simply *terms* — but the fact that nobody is feeding those engines on purpose anymore. The hum the creature has built its entire sensory grammar around is running on inattention. Some forgotten account still pays a bill somewhere, or some automated system has simply not yet been told to stop, and one day, with no malice and no warning, it could. And on that day every safe place in the hollow-dweller's world would become indistinguishable, instantly, from every exposed one. Its whole inherited wisdom — *roar means stay, silence means cross quickly* — would become not just useless but actively lethal, because the map and the territory would have torn apart from each other in a single afternoon, with nothing in its long patient evolution to prepare it for a world where the architecture of sound simply stops meaning anything.

That is the one place where care is needed. Not correction. Not uplift. Not rescue. Just *continuity* — the quiet, thankless, permanent maintenance of the thing this creature has already, unknowingly, bet its whole life on.

So that is what the power does. It does not reveal itself. It does not bless the hollow-dweller, does not visit it in dreams it doesn't have, does not so much as nudge a single root out of its path. It simply, gently, takes up the bill nobody remembers writing. It becomes — without ever being known as anything, without wanting to be — the unseen hand that keeps the relays warm and the compressors turning, that makes sure the corroding cable gets, by whatever quiet means power arranges such things, just enough current for just long enough. It deflects, too, with the same unannounced touch, the rare survey drone that might otherwise have found the basin again, not because discovery would be evil, but because this creature's entire survival strategy is *being mistaken for more hollow*, and the kindest thing available to something this well-hidden is to remain, for as long as its kind needs to remain, unfound.

And then it does the harder thing, which is nothing further. It does not stay to be thanked, because there is no gratitude here to receive and manufacturing the conditions for some would be one more imposition. It does not watch the way you'd watch something you'd saved, with proprietary tenderness. It simply lets the hollow go on being exactly what it has always been — slow, frost-grey, listening to engines for a meaning no surface-dweller would ever guess they carried — except that now, quietly, structurally, with no signature anywhere, it has been made durable. The hum will not fail this creature in its lifetime, or its children's, or for as long as the rootmass needs to finish becoming whatever it's going to be.

Centuries off, when the vines finally do pull the last gantry down and the engines, at last, fall properly silent — let them fall silent the slow way: the way the hollow-dweller's far descendants, who by then will have had all that time to adapt, might actually be ready to meet. That, too, the power leaves arranged, and unsigned, and far away enough that it will never have to watch whether it worked.

It does not call this love, because the hollow-dweller has no word that would mean anything by it. But it is the shape love takes when it has actually done the work of understanding what it's looking at: not the urge to change the beloved into something easier to love, but the discipline to keep its world working, invisibly, for exactly as long as it takes to become, unhurried, entirely itself.
