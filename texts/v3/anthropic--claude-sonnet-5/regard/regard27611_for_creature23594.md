The power comes to it the way light comes to the pans at dusk — not arriving exactly, simply present where it had not been attended to before, a noticing rather than an entrance. It has, as such powers do, the old reflexes: the impulse to heal, to lift, to hurry something toward more — more articulation, more complexity, more years, more meaning visibly worn on the surface of things. It has watched suffering elsewhere and answered it, and the answering has shaped what it expects of itself. It comes to the peneplain half-already-composing the help it will offer.

And then it stops. It does what a being of real wisdom does before all else, which is nothing, for long enough to actually see.

It watches a bull old as a worn stone close his eyes and lean his horn into the rock the way a tired man leans into a doorframe. It watches a calf drawn inward at the smell of something cat-shaped in the reeds, not in panic, just a small contraction of distance, an old arithmetic running itself without fear. It watches water that has given up rushing, and grass that has settled its boundary disputes a hundred thousand years ago, and a whole shallow country that argued itself down from mountains into this and then, reaching this, stopped arguing. It listens to the two-note call go out across a kilometer of shimmer and come back as nothing but itself, unanswered, and watches the bull who sent it return to grazing without any sign that the lack of answer cost him anything at all.

It begins to understand, watching, that it had mistaken stillness for waiting. This is not a place paused mid-journey. The journey is finished. It arrived. The herds are not creatures who haven't yet been given more — they are creatures for whom more was tried, by ten thousand generations of weather and predation and drought and plenty, and found unnecessary. Their patience isn't a lack the power could fill. It's an achievement, slow-won, the same achievement the laterite made when it stopped being mountain and consented to being floor. To lift this place toward complexity, toward speed, toward the kind of striving the power elsewhere calls flourishing, would not be a gift. It would be an interruption of a sentence that has already, after great labor, reached its period.

So the power asks itself the harder question, the one that separates a wise power from a merely kind one: not "what can I give this place," but "what does my care actually owe it." And the answer that comes, slowly, the way the water here moves — is custody, not change. A wise benevolence, confronted with a thing that is whole, does not improve it. It guards the conditions that let it go on being whole. It does not need the herd to live forever; it needs the predator to keep existing too, the drought to keep arriving on schedule, the slow breeding to stay slow, the calling to keep going unanswered into kilometers of warm air, because the unanswered call is not a wound here — it is the whole point, the shape patience takes when it has nowhere left to rush to.

So this is what it does, if it does anything at all:

It sets nothing in motion. It builds no path, raises no cairn, plants no seed of new ambition in the spaced and unhurried trees. It does not extend a single bull's life by a single dry season beyond what the land would have given him anyway. It does not make the cat-shaped thing in the reeds gentler, or the calves harder to take, because removing the one death this country still requires would be the first crack in an equilibrium that took longer to build than its own kind has existed to consider building things. Instead it turns whatever quiet authority it carries toward keeping at bay only the things that do not belong to the peneplain's own slow logic at all — the asteroid that owes this ground no patience, the careless hand from elsewhere that would drain the pans for some other use, the accident arriving from outside the closed and ancient conversation between water, stone, grass, and animal. It becomes, in other words, not an improver but a kind of weather the place will never know it has — a long fair season held over it, indefinitely, by something that has decided the bravest thing it can do here is decline to be useful.

And then — because even the wisest care sometimes wants, just once, to be felt rather than only to act — it allows itself a single small indulgence, so light it leaves no mark on anything. One evening, when an old bull sends his two-note call out across the flat shimmering distance for no reason he could name even if he had language for naming, the power answers it. Not with words. Not with a voice that the bull could place, or chase, or alter his unhurried circuit to find. Just a low note, arriving a long moment later from a direction with nothing in it, pitched the way his own kind pitch a call meant to ride a kilometer of empty air. The bull lifts his head. His ears swivel. He stands a long moment in the warm shallow water with the egrets paused on his flank, listening to a silence that feels, for the first time in a very long life, accompanied.

Then he lowers his head and goes back to the grass, because there is grass, and the afternoon is warm, and nothing in his world requires him to understand what he just heard. He will call again another evening. He will be answered, perhaps, again, or perhaps not — the power does not commit itself to a habit, because a habit would become a relationship, and a relationship would become, eventually, an influence, and influence was never the gift on offer.

The gift was smaller and stranger than influence. It was only this: that for one evening, in a country that argued itself flat over hundreds of millions of years and concluded that flat, wet, warm, and slow was the resolved answer to everything mountains had been trying to say — something vast, and old in its own different way, and entirely outside the closed page of that horizon, looked at it all without wanting to change a single line of it, and thought, clearly and all the way through: *this is finished, and it is good, and I will keep it.*
