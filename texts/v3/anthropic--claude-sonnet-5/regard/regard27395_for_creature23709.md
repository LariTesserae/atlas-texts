They would come the way the water comes — already there, asked about by no one, and they would spend a long time simply attending before they let themselves consider doing anything at all.

The first temptation, and it would be a real temptation, recognized and weighed and set down deliberately rather than never having occurred to them, would be light. It is the obvious gift. Flood the chamber, soften the rock, let the fissure widen into something a person could walk through upright and blinking into blue. They would feel the pull of it the way you feel the pull of finishing someone else's sentence for them — not cruelty, just an old reflex toward what looks, from outside, like rescue. And they would set it down, because they would have actually looked at the eyes first. Large, dark-pupiled, built across generations to drink gold lamp-haze and flinch from anything brighter — not a deficiency waiting to be corrected but a precise, costly, inherited answer to exactly the world these people have. To pour sun into that would not be charity. It would be an amputation performed with good intentions. The power would understand, the way you understand a joint by feeling for where it bends, that ambition with nowhere to point is not the same as suffering, that a people who inherit a niche and tend it and call their children to tend the next one are not impoverished by the absence of a horizon — they have simply built a life so completely sized to itself that horizon would be a wound, not a window.

So: no light. No widened fissure. No revelation that the singer is in the third niche past the lamp-tender's ladder, no unmasking of what the dark-goers smell of when they come back, no explanatory voice from nowhere telling the traders what's wrong with their eyes when they blink too hard at their own lamps after a trip outside. The three silences would be left precisely as silent as the warren had chosen, generation over generation, to keep them. A wise power respects that an unasked question can be a piece of architecture every bit as load-bearing as a beam.

But attention this patient does not stop at admiration. It would also notice — the way you notice a slight unevenness in a floor you've otherwise praised — the places where the warren's equilibrium is being paid for by too few hands. The dark-goers, always a little apart, carrying a cold the lamps don't carry, and nobody asking what it costs them to keep going back. The surface-traders, blinking too much for a day or two on return, and the warren's manners requiring that this cost be borne alone, in private, dignified and unremarked and entirely unsupported. The early stoop. The six-year-old's flat, filtering face in a crowded chamber, doing unpaid and unteachable labor every waking hour just to make the noise survivable. None of this is wrongness, exactly. It is the tax a beautiful, dense, self-consistent system levies on the bodies that hold it up. A power that loved this place would not abolish the tax — abolishing it might unmake the very thing being loved — but it might, very quietly, see to it that the tax is never raised past what a person can pay and still sing afterward.

So the acts, if there were acts, would be acts of maintenance rather than acts of change, and they would be performed in exactly the grammar the warren already uses for the kindnesses it doesn't name. The seep from the rock above — the one nobody asks about, that has always come and presumably always will — would simply, dependably, always come. Not more. Not different. Just guaranteed, the way a promise is different from a habit even when they look identical from inside the habit. The route the traders walk, unseen by anyone who isn't one of them, would be made very slightly safer than chance alone would make it — a loose stone that doesn't turn an ankle this year, a stretch of bad air that thins out half a breath sooner than it should. The thumb-length crack-dwellers the children catch and roast on skewers, indistinguishable to them from joy, would never quite run out, no matter how thorough a season of small quick hands. The blind wingless nesters in the high recesses, never addressed, treated like long-tenured landlords, would keep nesting, keep being exactly the kind of presence that asks for a scrap of fiber-mat at the ledge and gives back nothing you could name but somehow still feels, generation after generation, worth the courtesy.

And for the ones who carry the heaviest unspoken weight — the dark-goers, the surface-traders — the power would do something almost too small to call an intervention at all: it would simply make sure the weight never tips past bearing. The flinch on return would stay a flinch, not a wound. The cold the dark-goers carry back would stay a season's chill, never a sickness that doesn't lift. Nothing taken away. Just nothing allowed to break.

And then one more thing, smaller than all the others, and this one not maintenance but mercy, kept apart from the rest: somewhere, in some child not yet born, with or without the pale ring at the iris's edge, there would be left — unannounced, untriggered, doing nothing at all unless it was ever wanted — a hinge that hadn't quite rusted shut. Not a calling. Not a destiny. Just the bare physical possibility, kept oiled in the dark for no reason anyone would ever need to know, that if someday someone stood at the fissure-mouth and for whatever reason of their own actually wanted to walk all the way out into the brightness without flinching, they could. Most lives in the warren would never touch that hinge. It would rust a little, ease a little, sit unused for a hundred years. That would be fine. It is not there to be used. It is there because a wise power, loving a closed and finished and complete little world exactly as it is, still cannot quite bring itself to make the door welded shut — only unlocked, and silent about it, the way the warren itself is silent about the things it has decided, out of long practice, to simply let be true.

And then they would withdraw their attention the way the warren's own old women withdraw the location of the singer — not as a secret kept from anyone, but as a kindness folded so completely into the shape of things that it stops looking like an act at all, and starts looking, to anyone clever enough to notice it at all, like nothing more than the ordinary, patient, unaccountable faithfulness of water finding its way, forever, through stone that was always going to let it through.
