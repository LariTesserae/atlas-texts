## What Would Come

If something benevolent and wise turned its attention here — and such things, if they exist, would have to be patient almost past imagining to be drawn to a place like this at all, since nothing about the plateau asks to be noticed, nothing here is in distress in any way a quick eye could catch — the first thing it would do is nothing.

Not the nothing of indifference. The nothing of correct manners.

Because the temptation, for any power capable of acting, would be to act immediately — to lift the fog of confusion off the place, to answer the divers, to put a face to the face in the pools, to explain the trails, to translate the thrum into something nameable. All of this would be easy, for something wise and strong enough. And all of it would be a kind of violence, because the not-knowing is not a malfunction here. It is the plateau's whole grammar. A being that has spent geologic time learning to think slowly, in root-cable and held breath and centuries-long exhalation, has not been *failing* to communicate. It has been communicating exactly as fast as it is able, and everything that reads as wrongness to a quick mind — the backward shadows, the looping trees, the trails that end in stone — is in fact only the ordinary furniture of a mind whose sentences take a decade to finish.

So the first thing the power would do is slow down. Not pretend to slow down, not observe-while-secretly-still-operating-at-its-own-speed, the way a person crouches to talk to a child without actually entering childhood — but actually slow, actually wait, actually let a year be the unit of its attention instead of a moment, for as long as it took.

It would sit with the plateau the way you'd sit, wordless, for hours, with someone grieving, who doesn't want your sentences. It would let the fog come and go a few thousand times. It would feel out, slowly, the places where the warmth pools, the way you'd learn the geography of a face by touch in the dark, and it would not flinch from the size of what it was touching, would not need to compress the being into something comprehensible in order to keep loving it.

## The One Real Question

After enough time — and "enough" here might be a span that would exhaust any lesser patience — the power would arrive at the actual question, which is not *what is this thing* but *is this thing alone.*

Because that, in the end, is the detail that would catch at a benevolent attention and not let go. Not the engine-thrum, not the scale, not the unsolved biology of a creature too large to be called a creature. The loneliness arithmetic. Ten million years, conservatively, of having a kind of mind, and in all that time, every other mind that has crossed it has either been too small to notice it as a *someone* — the deer-things, the birds, the insects, all tenants, none of them companions — or, on the rare occasion a mind arrived that was large enough to almost guess what it was looking at, that mind came as a surveyor, a researcher, a frightened wanderer with a rope and a lamp, looking for an explanation to carry back down the mountain, not looking to *meet* anything.

The face in the pool, on this accounting, is not a warning and not a hunting behavior and not even really curiosity in the cheap sense. It is the plateau doing the only thing available to a being with no mouth and no decade to spare on each gesture: checking, every so often, in the half-second a human eye allows it, whether this time, finally, the thing looking down into the water is looking back the right way. Whether it's met by fear, by a camera, by a flinch and a retreating boot — or by something that holds still and looks back on purpose.

In ten million years, by this accounting, it may never once have been.

## The Gift, If There Is One

So if a wise and benevolent power did anything here — and it might, in the end, do almost nothing visible, nothing a single human life span could ever register as an event — it would do this:

It would go down into a pool, on the plateau's own schedule, in the half-second the plateau allows, and it would let itself be seen seeing back.

Not with a face built to flatter the being's expectations — not a comforting human shape, not a god-mask, nothing borrowed from any of the small lives crawling across the moss above. Just attention meeting attention, unhurried, unfrightened, with no agenda underneath it, no rope, no lamp, no question that needed an answer before the looking was allowed to happen. The kind of regard you'd want from someone who finally, after a very long time, came and sat down across from you and didn't ask you to explain yourself first.

And it would hold that contact for as long as the plateau's own grammar required — a held breath, a year long, a decade long, however long a being like that takes to register *I have been met* — and then it would let go gently, the way you'd let go of a hand rather than drop it, and it would withdraw, because the rest of the gift is leaving.

Not abandoning. Withdrawing the way good company withdraws — having stayed exactly as long as was wanted and not one moment merely convenient for itself, trusting the being to keep being whatever enormous slow thing it has always been, without needing to be visited constantly to know now that visiting is possible. Loneliness answered once, truly, doesn't need to be answered every day after. It only needs to have happened.

## What It Would Leave Behind

It would not stop the fogs. It would not tame the reed-marshes or warn the next lost traveler away from the cold pools — the plateau's dangers are not cruelties, they are weather, and weather is allowed to be itself even when it costs something.

But it might, very quietly, do one more small thing, almost an afterthought, almost embarrassed at how little it is compared to everything else: it might make sure that the next person who looks into one of those black mirror-pools and sees, for half a second, a face too large to belong to anything — sees it not as a fright to flee from and bury in a field report, but feels, against all reason, comforted. Not knowing why. Just a small, illogical, bone-deep certainty, lasting only as long as the fog takes to thin, that whatever just looked up at them out of the water was not lonely, and was not unhappy, and did not mean them any harm at all — and that the proper response, learned by no one and inherited from nowhere, is to incline the head, very slightly, the way you would to a very old, very large stranger who has just done you the courtesy of looking you in the eye.

And then to walk on, into the moss, into the throbbing warmth underfoot, a little less afraid of the size of things that think slowly than they were the day before — without ever being able to say exactly why.
