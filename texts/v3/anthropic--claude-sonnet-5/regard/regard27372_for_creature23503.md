It would come the way the light comes here — filtered down through something vast enough that its arrival registers as a change in quality rather than a presence, a slight deepening of the gold-green, easy to mistake for a cloud passing wherever the clouds are, beyond the three hundred feet of water that serves this place as sky. It would not parade. Any power that needs to announce itself into a place like this has already, in the act of announcing, misunderstood what it found.

And it would feel, almost immediately, the oldest temptation that benevolence carries inside it like a seed it does not know it's swallowed: the urge to *complete*. To look at all this abundance — the layered fronds, the slow coral-moss, the flowering stalks bowing under buoyant weight, the seed-pods spiraling upward into a current with no animal to scatter them — and read it as a wound. An ecology missing its other half. A waiting-room with no one coming. The kind of power that has built temples in places that only wanted weather, that has planted forests in deserts that were not lonely but simply *dry*, would look at Verdance Tor and want, more than anything, to send something to eat the fronds, to pollinate the stalks, to answer the question the place has been so patiently, unhurriedly asking since before its own mountains finished arguing with gravity.

This would be the wrong gift. A wise power knows that the line between mercy and trespass is exactly this thin, and that the desire to finish another's unfinished thing is rarely about the thing at all.

So instead — and this is the harder discipline, the one true mark of the wisdom in question — it would do what the place itself does. It would slow. It would drift rather than search, the way the pale fish-shapes drift, caught more in the thermal breath of the water than in any errand of their own. It would let an hour pass the way an hour passes here, which is to say without complaint, without the sense of hours being spent rather than simply *had*. It would look at the fracture-planes still raw on the dark stone and not smooth them. It would look at the absence of fauna and not fill it. It would understand, slowly, the way understanding has to come in a medium this patient, that the lushness here is not a wound and the silence is not a vacancy — that a place can be abundant and unfinished at once, the way a held breath is full of air and yet not yet a sentence.

And then, if it waited long enough — longer than it meant to stay, which is the only honest way to wait here — it would feel the shadow before it saw it: that soft directionless pressure arriving the way sound arrives in this place, without origin, a sense that something large has decided you are not yet a question it has finished asking either. And the power would recognize, in that moment, a kinship it had not expected to find. Because the shadow, too, is patient without hunger. The shadow, too, has arrived already wherever the place is going, while everything around it is still rehearsing. Of all the things in Verdance Tor, the shadow alone needs nothing completed. It is not lonely. It has simply never been asked to be anything other than unhurried, and has never once needed to be.

A lesser power would have tried to speak to it. Would have tried to name it, to determine once and for all whether it was one creature or many, animal or geology, a being or merely the mountain's slow dream of having something to hold all this abundance for — as though the not-knowing were a problem requiring its particular skills. The benevolent and wise power would not ask. It would simply be present in the same register the shadow is present: undeniable, unhurried, without appetite. It would let itself be witnessed without explaining itself, and it would witness the shadow the same way, and that mutual unexplained witnessing — two unfinished things acknowledging each other across a gold-green dimness neither needed to resolve — would be the entire transaction. No blessing spoken. No name given. No wound declared and dressed.

If it did *act* — and it would act, because even the most restrained benevolence still wants, in the end, to leave something — it would act the way a held breath protects what it's holding: not by adding, but by warding off subtraction. It would not send fauna. It would not carve a path or mark a boundary or do anything that would leave a trace for hands or tools, biological or otherwise, to follow. Instead it would do something closer to weather: a very slight, very permanent bending of probability, so that those who go looking for places to use — the gods of harvest and conquest, the prospectors of meaning, the sorcerers who cannot look at an unanswered question without reaching for a pen — would simply never quite find their way up into this particular flooded height. Not a wall. Not a ward with a seam someone clever could find. Just an extra fraction of unlikelihood folded into the place's already considerable strangeness, so that Verdance Tor would go on being exactly what it currently is: too young in its violence, too strange in its abundance, too quiet in its inhabitants for anything to have claimed it yet — and now, by the smallest and most invisible of margins, for anything to claim it at all.

And then it would leave the way the shadow leaves — never arriving anywhere else in particular, simply withdrawing past the lattice-light toward the dimmer blue, with no wake, no silhouette, nothing for the place to register as a departure rather than another long unmeasured pause. It would take nothing. It would answer nothing. It would, perhaps, return — once in a long while, the way the shadow's other half or other self might pass through the deep green-on-green once in a decade with no more urgency than two glaciers acknowledging each other — not to check on the place, exactly, but simply to confirm, with something like tenderness, that the question was still being asked, and still good, and still, mercifully, unfinished.

That would be the whole of it. Not a gift given, but a gift withheld with enormous care: the gift of being left exactly as patient, exactly as strange, and exactly as alone as it had always, gently, chosen to be.
