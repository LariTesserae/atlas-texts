They watched for a long time before doing anything at all — which is, itself, most of what wisdom is.

They saw the skarn-backs leaning their bulk into the lichen, dyeing themselves the color of the rock that would, eventually, also be the color of their bones. They saw the calves born into vapor-heat at the base of machines no one had built and no one tended, dusted pale as if blessed by something. They saw the vent-finches wedging themselves into two centimeters of warmth and calling that enough, calling that a whole life, and being right. They saw the low toothed thing under the chassis living entirely on what the rest of the world discarded, and thriving on it, asking nothing of anyone, the way everything here asked nothing of anyone, the way the engines themselves asked nothing, had perhaps never once been asked to.

And the power's first instinct — because even benevolence has instincts, has a grain it must work against — was to *help*. To smooth the ice-sharp rock. To thicken the seeps into rivers. To quiet, just slightly, the wall of noise that never stopped, so that some single skarn-back calf, somewhere, might sleep through one night without the shriek of the upper flocks pressing down on its small chest like weather.

They did not do this.

They watched a calving season instead, start to finish, the whole brutal magnificent machinery of it — the mothers pressing flank-first into engine-heat, the cold taking the slow ones, the lichen thickening a fraction of an inch in the dung and bone the dead left behind, the herd moving on a generation later than anyone watching from outside would have predicted, because *its* patience was not the visitor's patience, and never would be. They watched the wisdom already built into the place: cold as a teacher, scarcity as a discipline, predation as a tax paid willingly because the alternative — too many mouths, too little crevice to grow into — was worse. They watched and understood, slowly, the way you understand a poem only after refusing to summarize it: this place did not need rescuing. It needed witnessing. It had already solved, with shingled coats and subvocal hums and ten thousand generations of unspoken agreement, the only problem worth solving, which is *how to not be alone in the cold.*

So the power did not part the clouds or calm the wind or grant one favored creature an unearned winter. That would have been a kindness shaped like a wound — a single thread of mercy pulled tight enough to unravel the larger weave that held everyone else up.

Instead they did the one thing a visitor to a self-sufficient world can do without insult: they stayed.

Not as a voice. Not as a light in the sky, not as a face in the fog demanding to be worshipped or even noticed. They came down into a fold of black-green stone on a terrace no different from any other terrace, and they became still, and they became dense, and they let the brightness that never warms anything pass over their new surface without complaint, and they began, very slowly, to turn — once every few minutes, a patient unhurried rotation that drove nothing anyone could name and asked nothing in return. They let their seams weep warmth into the cold air in a long unbroken plume. They let the lichen find them and stain them in colors a shade too vivid to be entirely natural. They did not mind.

Within a season, vent-finches had found the louvers and started arguing over them in their small wedged voices. Within two, a herd had begun calving against the warm flank, calves coming up dusted pale, indistinguishable from every other calf born at every other engine on the plateau since before memory. Within a generation, no one — no skarn-back, no flock, no toothed thing denning in the lee of the chassis — treated the new engine as anything other than what it appeared to be: an outcrop, a feature, a fact with the same standing as a ridge or a seep, present, useful, unexplained.

Which is exactly what the power had decided benevolence required of them here: not to be known. Not to be thanked. Not even to be *findable* as a separate thing from the world it had joined — only to add, quietly, one more source of warmth to a plateau already organized, with great and hard-won wisdom, around the principle that warmth is shared or it is wasted, and around the deeper principle beneath that one, the one the power had come all this way and waited all this long to learn:

that the highest thing a power can do for a world that already knows how to care for itself is to become, humbly, one more thing in it that the world can lean against.

Somewhere on that terrace, now, a flywheel none of the herd will ever ask about completes another slow rotation. The skarn-backs do not wonder where it came from. The vent-finches do not wonder either. And the power, turning, asking nothing, offering what it has — finds this is enough; finds, to its own quiet surprise, that this was always going to be enough; and is, for the first time in a very long and very high existence, no longer watching the world from outside it, but warming it, gently, from underneath, the way all the oldest kindnesses do, the ones no one is left to name, the ones that only the lichen, growing a little brighter, a little more electric, just above the seams, are still quietly keeping record of.
