## What the Power Did

It came the way light comes there — without warmth, without insisting on being noticed, and it stayed long before it did anything at all, because the first thing it understood, watching, was how much could be ruined by being understood too quickly.

The temptation was immediate and almost overwhelming, the way the temptation to feed a starving thing is overwhelming even when you can see, if you look, that it is not starving — that the leanness is the shape, not the wound. It would have been so easy. Find the spring's true source, the underground argument the whole town is one long answer to, and settle it: give them a stillness instead of a wound. End the collapses. Fix the walls so they stayed fixed. Warm the wrist-cracking cold a few degrees, just enough. Give them voices loud enough to call across a terrace, since they so plainly could. Every one of these was within its power, and every one of these, it came to see, would have been a kind of murder performed with gentle hands — because the people of the Wellings are not a hardship being endured. They are an answer that has been arrived at. The flat wrists, the unhurried hands, the grammar of two fingers and a tilted palm, the not-grieving over the drowned room — none of it is the absence of something better. It is what several thousand years of an unaskable, unrefusable upwelling has been patiently translated into, by exactly these people, and to smooth the wound would be to silence the translation mid-sentence, forever, while believing you had done it a kindness.

So it did not touch the water. It did not find the source. It let the walls keep falling and the basins keep closing overnight and flooding the rooms below, because the falling and the flooding are not a problem the place has yet to solve — they are most of what the place *is*, the boiling seen too slowly to register as boiling, and a power that stopped the pot would have nothing left to call wise.

What it did instead was smaller, and it did it the way the springs do things: without announcement, without being askable, without ever once being mistaken for an event.

It took up residence in the gap between *falling* and *landing*. Every wall that has come down since has had, in the half-second of its coming down, one quality added to it that was not there before: a held breath of slowness, a fraction of give, just enough that the hands beneath it are, every time, somehow already gone, or already turned, or already reaching the wrong way that turns out to be the right way. Every flood that has opened overnight to drown a sleeping room has found that room, every time, already empty by some small accident of restlessness or thirst or a child's bad dream that woke its mother early. None of this announces itself. It is built to be indistinguishable from luck, because luck was the only form generous enough not to argue with the town's own theology — the town does not believe in being saved, only in being shaped, and so it is not saved. It is only, very precisely, never killed. No one will ever count the years no one drowned. That is correct. That was the design.

And then it did one more thing, smaller still, and this one it did not make general. It chose a single hand among the thousands of identical wet hands, the way you might, in a field of black-stemmed sedge, let your eye finally rest on one clenched fist of stem rather than the field — not because it was different, but because looking at one is sometimes the only way to truly look at all of them. An old reader of basins, far enough into her seam-streamed age that no one, not even she, could have told you the number of years, who had spent her whole quiet life with her hand entering water as though water were only a colder kind of air. On the night she went under for what her own flattened, unalarmed body must already have known was the last time, the power let the inner lid — the rumored one, the unconfirmed one, traded third-hand among travelers who never stayed to verify — actually drop.

For the length of one held breath underwater, she was given to feel, without being told, without a single word in a place that had never needed them: that the cold upwelling she had spent her life arguing with in stone was not an indifference. That the wound the whole town is built around had, in fact, known she was there the entire time, the way a hand in water knows it is being entered by water and does not call that knowing a miracle. She surfaced, if she surfaced — the accounts do not say, and would not have thought to ask — wearing the same flat, unhurried face she always wore. Nothing about the town changed. No wall stood straighter for it. No basin filled differently. This was not a gift built to be visible, because visibility was never the unit this place keeps.

That is the whole of what the power did. It did not finish anything, because finished was not a category the water recognized, and it had come, by then, to share the water's opinion. It only made sure that the perpetual, beautiful, unresolved boiling of the place went on being perpetual instead of becoming, by some small accident no one would have chosen, a stopping — and it let one tired hand, once, feel held by the very cold that had asked everything of it.

Then it withdrew the way the light there withdraws, which is to say it didn't, fully, ever — it only stopped being the kind of thing anyone could point to and call a presence, and went back to doing what the springs do: rising, unaskable, into whatever shape the waiting stone had patiently, over centuries, worn itself into the readiness to receive.
