The power comes the way the dusk-light comes here: without arrival, exactly. One moment the Roostings is merely old and full and loud, and the next, without any seam you could point to, it is old and full and loud and *attended* — the way a room changes when someone enters it quietly behind you, before you've turned, before you even know to listen for the door.

Its first impulse — and it has one, because even wisdom starts as impulse before it becomes wisdom — is to help. This is what benevolence wants to do on first contact with a wound, and it is trained, by every other place it has ever found, to read enormity of suffering into enormity of noise, to hear that wall of calls and clatter and assume distress, to see the moth-clouds bred in numbers that make death irrelevant and assume horror, to see the great sail-winged things take the small ones without even a dip in the general clamor and assume an injustice waiting to be redressed. It reaches, in that first moment, for the oldest and easiest tools of help: stop the eating. Quiet the noise. Light the windows. Speed the moss so the green wins and the stone loses, because green looked, for one held breath, like mercy and stone looked like a kind of grave.

It does not do any of these things. It pulls its hand back from each one in turn, and here is why, and the why matters more than the restraint:

It looks again — properly, this time, with the attention that only a wise power can afford to spend, the attention of something that has nowhere else it needs to be — and it sees that the noise is not suffering, the noise is *breath*; that to silence it would not be to heal the Roostings but to suffocate it, the way you'd suffocate a lung by mistaking its working for its wheeze. It sees that the small deaths in the middle registers are not violence interrupting peace but the actual metabolism of the place, no more cruel than a heartbeat, and to stop them would not save anything — it would simply starve the great sail-winged ones, and then starve whatever ate them in turn, all the way down to the many-legged things drinking quietly at the basins, until the whole vast settled breathing structure went, tier by tier, silent. It sees that the dusk-light that never resolves into night or day is not a deprivation but a setting, chosen — by the architecture, by the eyes built into the things that live in it, by some agreement struck so long ago that agreement and accident are no longer different words — and that to brighten it would be to do to this place what shouting does to a held silence.

And it sees, last and most clearly, the thing that almost stops it from acting at all: that nothing here is asking. There is no lifted face, no reaching, no name called out toward whatever might be listening. The Roostings does not know it has been noticed and would not change its breathing if it did. It is not suffering in any way that wants an answer. It is only old, and full, and patient, doing exactly what it has always done, at the only pace it has ever moved at, with no plan to be otherwise — which is, the power understands now, the whole of its dignity. To fix a thing that has not asked to be fixed is not kindness. It is a kind of theft — taking from the place the one thing it has spent all its long unwitnessed centuries earning, which is the right to be exactly, only, fully itself.

So the power does not heal. It does not speed, brighten, simplify, quiet, or save. What it does is smaller, and harder, and entirely its own to give:

It looks at the Roostings — truly looks, the way no pilgrim climbing its terraces and no scholar cataloguing its tiers and not even the Roostlings themselves, each confined to their own register of sky or ledge or window-throat, has ever quite managed to look — and it sees it whole. Not a place containing creatures. Not an architecture with tenants. One thing. One old, vast, slow-breathing being made of stone that has forgotten it was ever only stone and flesh that has forgotten it was ever only flesh, wearing wings and moss and basin-water and ten thousand years of unhurried erosion the way another body might wear skin. It sees this, and holds it in its attention the way you'd hold the whole shape of someone's life in your mind in the moment before you finally, for the first time, say their name correctly — not the worn, ill-fitting name that arrived already old, but the true one, the one that fits without slack anywhere. And it says it. Once. Not aloud, because the Roostings has no ear shaped for that kind of hearing, but into the structure itself, the way you might press a hand flat against a wall to feel, rather than hear, that someone on the other side is there.

Nothing visible changes. No light comes on. No wing lifts in alarm. The great clamor goes on being weather, unbroken, indifferent to the visit, exactly as it should.

But the power does leave one thing behind — small, and slow, and entirely in the place's own grammar, the only kind of gift the Roostings would ever recognize as a gift rather than an intrusion. It reaches into the long, unwritten future of all that stone — the fatigue banked invisibly in every arch, the centuries of damp working at the same hidden seam, the one terrace, somewhere ahead, that was always going to give all at once, catastrophically, in a single wet season, taking three tiers of roosting ledge down with it in an afternoon — and it loosens that seam early, gently, gradually, spreads the failure out across a thousand uneventful years instead of one terrible one, so that when the Roostings finally does change in any way large enough to notice, it will change the way it always has: by grains, by groove, by inference, a waterline found somewhere new long after the tide that made it has already receded. Not eternity. Not safety. Only the same death the place was always going to have, but kept honest — kept slow — kept, against the one risk it could never have weathered, entirely its own.

Then the power withdraws its attention the way it arrived: without a seam. And if, after that day, anyone climbing high enough into the deepest window-throats of the Roostings happens to glimpse one more long-necked, dark, motionless shape among the rumored old ones there — one more form it's impossible to say, looking at it, whether you've seen a living thing or only a particularly deliberate piece of unweathered carving — no one will think anything of it. That, too, is correct. That is exactly how this place keeps anything it has decided to keep.
