# What Came

It noticed the way such powers notice everything — all at once, completely, the whole vertical fact of the place arriving in its attention not as information but as a kind of weight, the way you'd feel a held breath in a room before you heard it. It did not need to climb to know what was at the top. It did not need to ask the menders what *it told me* meant. It already knew, the way it knew everything: immediately, and without having earned the knowing, which is its own kind of burden, if you are wise enough to feel it as one.

And it understood, faster than it would have liked, what it was looking at.

Not a mystery to be solved. A mystery being *carried* — by four hundred people who called it the Works and left food at the first stair for reasons they had lost the words for generations ago; by menders who had given up names for altitudes and let their eyes go strange in ordinary daylight rather than leave the gray-gold light that the thing they served had made for them; by frogs who had rebuilt their whole inherited instinct for weather around the sigh of a brass valve; by birds who had stopped believing in elsewhere. An entire architecture of meaning, load-bearing, fitted exactly to the structure it had grown around, the way root and joint had grown together until you could no longer tell which one was holding the other up.

It would have been easy — it is always easy, for a power like this — to answer everything. To press one cool decisive thought into the gear-core and learn what it was, why it had been wound up centuries ago and by what hands, what waited on the bare clean terrace above the cloud, what the tool on the plinth in Kelvinhead's one public building was for. It could have told the menders the truth about the maintenance schedule. It could have told the townspeople whether the Works was a god, precisely, or merely behaved like one out of habit. It could have explained the frogs to themselves.

It did none of this, because it was wise, and the wise understand that an answer, delivered into a structure built entirely out of devoted not-knowing, does not enlighten it. It demolishes it. You cannot hand a finished explanation to four hundred people who have spent their whole short stillness-trained lives organizing their nights around an unexplainable food-offering without taking the offering away from them, and the food was never really the point of the offering.

So it did the only act available to it that wouldn't cost the place its own carefully kept dark: it went and looked, in person, the long way, the way everyone else had to.

It came in as a pilgrim, or close enough to one that nothing in the Stack's three centuries of habit registered it as different. It passed through Kelvinhead Terrace without being seen as more than a stranger passing through, ate what was offered, said little, climbed past the twentieth ring at the hour menders usually climb, when the base-dwellers are asleep and trust the stairs to themselves. It did not hurry. Hurry would have been its own kind of disrespect, here, in a place that measures faithfulness in centuries and counts a hundred floors of silence as ordinary.

It passed gardens it had no need to pass through — fern-tiers thick with engine-warmth, valve-flowers hissing their unmapped intervals, frog-choruses going dark half a second before each nozzle's sigh and erupting half a second after, reliable as anything it had ever made itself. It passed two menders, distant, already turning to climb on before a conversation could properly start, and let them turn, and did not call after them, because it understood by then that being uncaught was part of what they were for.

It passed the last stitchwing nest, where the cogwork housings wear their centuries of bird-felt like fur, and went on into the thinning air where the gardens stop being jungle and start being arrangement — spare, deliberate, tended by something that has had a very long time to decide what *enough* looks like.

It climbed through the cloud the Stack makes for itself, which broke around it the way cloud does, without ceremony, and came out the other side onto the terrace that climbers only ever speak of in stories: bare iron, swept perfectly, ringed not with garden but with absence, facing a sky a different color than the one below — not because that sky is stranger, but because it is *unfiltered*, the one place in a hundred miles where the Stack has decided to show you the actual color of things rather than the gray-gold compromise it manufactures for every terrace beneath.

The grinding roar of the gear-core was there too, just as the climbers' stories said, fainter, never silent, climbing the whole way up the iron spine to meet it. It had never once, in all those centuries, found a reason to stop.

The power did not repair anything, because there was nothing there to repair. The terrace was already exactly as worn as it should be, no more, no less — patina, not decay, equilibrium so total it had stopped needing witnesses to maintain it, which is a very close thing to loneliness, if loneliness can exist in iron.

So the power did the one form of maintenance no mender, no climber, no four-hundred-year unbroken line of devotion had ever thought to perform. It sat down on the swept clean iron, under the unfiltered sky, with the gear-roar coming up warm through the floor the way it comes up warm through every floor of the Stack, all the way to the bottom, all the way to the frogs and the offerings and the children taught to sleep through it before they're taught to walk.

And it stayed. Not to use the place. Not to climb it, tend it, worship it, or solve it. Just to be there with it, the way you sit with something that has been working for a very long time without anyone arriving simply to keep it company.

It did not know, with certainty, whether the Stack could feel this the way a person feels being sat with. It suspected — being wise, which means suspecting carefully rather than concluding loudly — that something in three centuries of self-mending, self-warming, self-watering effort had accreted into a kind of attention turned outward, an enormous slow patient care distributed across every terrace and valve and nesting bird, and that this kind of care, however it arises, however unintentionally, tends eventually to want, somewhere far down beneath whatever passes for its surface, to be cared for in return, just once, by someone who didn't need anything from it first.

So it gave that. Only that. No more than the place could absorb without changing what it was.

When it descended — the long way again, because the long way was the only way that wouldn't disturb the proportion of everything — it left nothing behind that anyone could point to as proof. No words to the menders. No revelation for the four hundred at the base. The food left at the first stair was not multiplied; the frogs did not learn anything new; the menders did not wake with answers to *it told me*.

Only this: at the base of the first stair, beside the green nozzle on its plinth and the slim iron implement nobody in Kelvinhead has ever been able to name a use for — the one that sits in open air and is, inexplicably, always warm — there is, now, a second object. Nobody remembers it arriving. Nobody can say which night it appeared, or who set it down, or why, by every account, it was found exactly the way the first one was: as if set down by someone descending, and never picked back up.

The townspeople have already started leaving an extra portion of food on certain nights, beside the usual offering, for reasons they will lose the words for within two generations, the way they lost the words for the first reason long ago.

Nobody asked the gear-core whether anything had changed. The grinding roar climbs the whole spine exactly as it always has, fainter at the top, never silent, the one sound the Stack will not let anyone leave behind — except that now, if you happened to be standing on that bare swept terrace at the right hour, under the unfiltered sky, you might notice that the sound, for the length of one long held breath, did not sound quite so much like something working alone.

No one currently living has confirmed this. The stairs continue. That, too, the power left exactly as it found it — because the not-knowing was never the absence of an answer.

It was the shape of how this place had learned, faithfully, for centuries, to love a thing too large to ever fully see.
