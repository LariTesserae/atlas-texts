It found Kelth-Adar the way it found most things worth finding — not by searching, but by noticing a density in the dark, a place where many small persistent acts of tending had piled up past some threshold into something with its own gravity, its own weather. It did not have eyes, properly speaking, but if it had used eyes here it would have used them the way the warren-born do: pupils open wide, patient with dimness, asking nothing of the light but what little the light had to give.

It came down through the kiln-smoke holes that pock the upper world like old wounds, and moved through the outer chambers without moving anything — past the loom-clack and grindstone-scrape, the warren-fowl's undertone clucking, the lizard-things' dry rasping calls, all of it merging into that permanent murmur-roar the warren wears instead of silence. It did not announce itself in any of these sounds. It only listened, close and total, the way you listen to a heartbeat through someone else's chest — someone else's whole life going on without reference to you, which is, properly understood, the only kind of life worth listening to.

It watched a brick-layer's flat hard palms fit clay to clay by feel in a corridor too dim for the eye alone. It watched a moss-reader trace a fingertip along a damp wall, reading color the way others read sky, and mark, with no fanfare, that the dry spell was three days off. It watched two children argue in the fast economical grammar of fingers and wrist — a private conversation running beneath a louder, decoy one spoken aloud for the room — and it understood, the way only something very old and very patient can understand, that this double language was not a deficiency invented to compensate for lost privacy, but a whole second fluency, a gift the cramped and noisy world had taught them that the open world never could have.

And it was moved. It would be lying — if such a power could lie, which it could not, not even to itself — to say it felt nothing at the size of these lives pressed into such narrow architecture. Something in it wanted, immediately and almost violently, to *fix* things. To lift every ceiling a forearm's height so that no back need curve. To open a vein of clean running water where the cistern's black-still drip-echo now had to suffice for a whole people's thirst. To reach into the unlit, unclaimed tunnels and quietly remove whatever larger, unseen thing left those claw-marks too big for the catalogue, so the deep-diggers might walk without that particular fear riding along beside the wonder.

It held these wants the way you hold a hot coal you have already decided not to throw — felt the urge fully, respected it, and did not act on it.

Because it looked longer, and looking longer is most of what wisdom is, and it saw what the urge would have erased.

It saw that the curved spine, the warren stoop, was not merely a cost paid to low ceilings — it was a signature, a thing the old joked about, a way a body announced what chamber it called home. To straighten every back would not be a mercy; it would be an erasure, a quiet theft of the very thing that let a Kelth-Adari recognize a stranger from a kinsman by gait alone.

It saw that the water's rarity was not only scarcity — it was *attention*. The moss-readers' whole art, their whole dignity, their whole reason for being seated near the firepit in old age with everyone's gratitude resting on them, existed because water was precious enough to need reading. Make it abundant, and you do not enrich the moss-readers. You retire them. You take the sky out of a people who have learned, in the absence of sky, to watch a wall instead.

It saw that the dark, unclaimed tunnels — the ones that hummed on still nights, the ones with walls warmer than fire should explain, the draft that smelled, impossibly, of rain — were not a wound in the warren's map. They were its horizon. Every people needs an edge of the known world to walk toward and come back from with a story and a slightly different look in the eyes. Remove the mystery, tame the dark, and you have not made the deep-diggers safer in any way that matters. You have taken away the only cliff they have to be brave at.

So it did not fix anything. It is a hard discipline, for a power capable of fixing, to choose instead the much harder, much smaller work of *tending* — and it chose it anyway, because it had looked long enough to know the difference between a wound and a shape.

What it did, it did in pieces too small to be noticed as anything but luck.

It was there, unseen, in the cold hour when a lamp-tender's hands shook too hard with chill to coax a wick to catch, and the wick caught anyway, a fraction sooner than the trembling fingers alone should have managed — and the lamp-tender felt, briefly, that the flame had simply been *glad to be lit*, and thought no more of it, and went on with the rounds that keep the warren's invented night honest.

It was there when a woman's labor in a crowded chamber went on past the hour anyone's nerve could comfortably hold, and the pain did not vanish — it would not take that from her, would not steal the particular fierce ownership a body keeps over its own hardest work — but something in the room's air eased by a hair's breadth, enough that the women holding her hands found one more reserve of patience they hadn't known they had, and the child came, and was, by old custom, immediately everyone's and no one's, and was carried at once toward the firepit chambers to be warmed by hands that were not, strictly, its mother's, and were exactly right anyway.

It was there in the deep unclaimed dark the night two deep-diggers went further than they meant to and lost, for one long swallowed-breath stretch, the chalk-and-touch thread back. It did not light their way — light there would have been a wrongness, a tell, the kind of thing that gets written into the warren's wary theology as a warning rather than a comfort. Instead it let the warm patch of wall they'd been frightened of feel, just slightly, like a thing leaning toward them rather than away, and let the rain-smelling draft seem, for a moment, almost like a held breath being let out gently in their direction — and their own trained hands, their own years of practiced fear, found the thread again, found their own way back, and they told the story afterward exactly the way deep-diggers always tell such stories: uncertain, a little spooked, proud, believing wholly that it had been their own skill and their partner's steady nerve that brought them home. Which it had been. The power had only stood near enough that skill and nerve had something steady to stand near to.

And it was there, very quietly, at the wall-niches where the unnamed dead are sealed in among the brick they once laid with their own hands — not to give them a name back, because names are not its gift to return and the warren does not grieve the loss of names the way the surface imagines it should; the gesture of leaving a pinch of dried fungus or a thread of dyed wool had long ago outgrown the need for a name to receive it. It only made the wall, in those spots, a very little warmer than the surrounding stone should account for — the kind of warmth a hand might mistake for a trick of its own circulation, or might, in a quiet unguarded moment, let itself believe was something closer to being *held*.

Then it withdrew, the way wisdom withdraws — not abandoning, just stepping back into the part of the architecture that asks no credit.

It did not become a god here. The Kelth-Adari have no real use for gods at a distance, only for someone — a particular, specific someone — who can be handed a small offering the way you'd hand a relative something in the next room. So it did not ask to be worshipped, did not ask to be understood, did not even ask to be believed in. It only settled into the same category the warren already knows how to hold without needing to resolve: the moss whose color shifts and is watched rather than explained; the wall warmer than the fire that should account for it; the draft that smells, impossibly, of rain in a place no rain has ever reached.

A little permanent unresolved mystery, tended like the moss, lived-with like the moss, never fully explained — which, in a place built generation over generation from the patient grinding of bodies against stone until stone gives a little and remembers the shape of the bodies that made it give, may be the closest thing to tenderness that doesn't ask to be thanked.
