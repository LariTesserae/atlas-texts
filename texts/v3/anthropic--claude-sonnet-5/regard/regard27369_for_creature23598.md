## What the Power Would Do

The first thing it would do is nothing. This is not failure of compassion — it is the form compassion takes when it has lived long enough to recognize its own potential cruelty. A power capable of lifting cities into light has to be more afraid of its own hands than of the dark it's looking at. It would arrive the way the warmth arrives here — without a visible source, without announcing the arrival as an event — and it would simply *stay a while*, in the unhurried way the Warren itself insists on, because anything faster would already be a kind of violence done to a place that has organized its entire nature around not being rushed.

It would not give light. This is the easy mercy, the first and worst impulse — to flood the dark with something kinder, to let the eel-bodied things and the wheeling schools and the long iron-dark hunter see each other plainly for once. But light here is currency, the text already knows this, and a gift of free light would be a gift of ruin: every careful economy of glow and shadow, every learned discretion, undone in an afternoon by a kindness that never asked what it was paying for. The wise power would feel the temptation — feel it as something almost physical, the ache to *fix the dark* — and would set the temptation down, gently, the way you set down something you want very badly and have decided, with real grief, not to keep.

It would not give the creature a name it could use. Names are for the named to carry, and this one has never needed to carry itself in language; it carries itself in habit, in the worn groove of three rooms threaded like needle-eyes, in the pressure-shadow of two thousand years. To hand it a name would be to hand it a mirror it never asked to look into, and mirrors are not always mercy. So whatever naming happens, happens privately, on the power's side only — a held knowledge, not a gift. *This one. This long patient authority. This accidental memorial to somebody's hearth.* The name would exist nowhere but in the power's own attention, which is, after all, where most love actually lives — not in the saying, but in the noticing closely enough that a thing becomes irreducibly itself instead of a category of fauna in a guidebook of the deep.

What it *would* do — the one act, small enough to leave no mark, large enough to matter — is this:

It would go looking for the sentence.

*You are a guest in someone else's unfinished sentence. Nothing here will tell you whose.* The power, unlike you, can read stone the way stone remembers. It would move through the warren the way water already moves through it, indistinguishable from current, and it would lay its attention against every threshold, every softened corner, every roof opened like a flower that forgot to close — not to repair them, not to learn their architecture, but to recover, syllable by patient syllable, *who*. Who kept the low room warm. Who built three stories and no higher because someone, sometime, decided that humility was load-bearing. Who stood in this doorway last, before the water came in and decided, with the terrible gentleness of the sea, not to leave again.

This is the one true erosion the power would commit: it would unbury the address. Not restore it — the dead don't need restoration, they need to have *existed*, which is a much smaller and much harder thing to give back to someone — but know it, completely, the way the creature knows the warren by lean and pressure rather than sight. The power would hold, somewhere outside of time, the full and finished version of the sentence the Warren has been carrying unfinished for twenty thousand years. It would let the builders be addressed, at last, even if only by one listener, even if the listener tells no one, even if nothing in the Warren itself ever knows the gap has closed.

And then — only then, only after that quieter work was done — it would allow itself one gesture toward the living thing coiled in the lowest room, near the breathing warmth it has never questioned. It would pass close. Not a visitation. Not a blessing with edges. Just the same slow displacement the creature already knows how to read — a pressure-change at the limit of its jaw-pits, the same courteous inquiry the Warren extends to everything that enters it — except this once, underneath the familiar shape of *something testing the dark*, there would be something else folded in, wordless, untranslatable into any sense the creature owns: *I know what you are an echo of. I know what you have been keeping warm without knowing you were keeping it.*

The creature would not understand this as anything but the dark behaving, for one moment, slightly strangely. It would not follow. It has no urgency, and urgency is the only thing that could make it answer.

The power would withdraw the way warmth withdraws from a hand — without insult, without explanation, leaving everything materially exactly as it found it: the same low light, the same unhurried predations, the same patient unbuilding going on by inches. Nothing in the Warren would be different to any instrument that could measure it.

And that is the whole of what a wise benevolence does, most of the time, in places like this — it does not change the room. It changes only the fact of whether the room was ever truly seen, all the way down, by someone who did not need anything from it in return. Which is not nothing. It is, in fact, very nearly everything, dressed humbly enough to leave no mark at all.
