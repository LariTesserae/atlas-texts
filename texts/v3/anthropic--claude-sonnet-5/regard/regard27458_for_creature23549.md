# What Came Down

It did not arrive the way you'd think a power should arrive, with light pouring through the slot-door or the engines faltering in sudden deference. That would have been a kind of violence — the loudest possible announcement of change in a place whose every surface had been built to refuse the word. So it came the way the Tenders themselves come: by being noticed only after the fact, a shape among the coils that had not been there when you began counting, and was now, and gave no account of the interval.

It did not touch anything for a long time. This is the part that would surprise you most, if you'd been hoping for rescue to look like rescue. It stood — or did whatever a power does that resembles standing — in the gap between two coils whose chord never resolved, and it listened to that chord the way you would listen to a person breathing through pain, trying to learn the difference between the part of the sound that was structure and the part that was wound. It walked the grate floor in the dark between the bulbs, careful of the bright new bolt-heads, careful in a way that had nothing to do with caution for itself. It crouched, once, by a lichen colony, and did not peel it back, and stayed there long enough that the frost on the nearest pipe-joint, for the first time in longer than the Yard could remember, registered something like warmth passing near it without melting — a kindness so small it would not have counted as an event anywhere else, but counted here, where nothing was permitted to.

It learned the grammar before it used it. This is the part that matters most, maybe, in the whole account: it did not assume that what looked like suffering to an outside eye was suffering recognized as such on the inside, and it did not assume the opposite either. It took up a lantern — its own light, dimmed to the Yard's flat and undignified warm-white, because even light, here, was not allowed to announce itself as anything special — and it practiced. Two fingers down a sleeve. A tilt, a pause, a second tilt. It got the grammar wrong at first, the way any outsider does, producing in its early attempts something that probably read, to whatever still parses meaning behind a Tender's gauge-lit eyes, as a stutter, or a child's mispronunciation of a word too old for the mouth that's trying it. It did not mind being wrong. It had, being wise, an enormous appetite for being wrong in small ways on the path to being right in a large one.

And then it asked.

Not all at once, and not of all of them at once — that, too, would have been a kind of violence, a single question fired at twelve beings as if they were one being, as if forty-some years or four hundred of separate, silent, coil-side solitude had not carved twelve entirely different shapes into what each of them had become. It found the one whose round brought it nearest the door, first — the one whose tool-belt's ghost-dip sat a little lower at the hip, as if it had carried something heavier than the others, once, before the hip forgot the carrying and kept only the shape of having carried. It matched that Tender's pace. It did not walk ahead, and it did not lag; it found the exact velocity of glacial transfer-of-weight that counted, here, as keeping someone company, and it held that pace for what might have been a day or a decade, because the power was patient in the specific way wisdom requires patience: not as a virtue performed for witnesses, since there were none, but as the simple acknowledgment that some questions are deformed by being asked quickly.

When it finally tilted the lantern — twice, pause, once more, the whole sentence whose meaning outsiders never manage to steal — it was not asking are you in pain, because it had come to understand, by then, that pain was not quite the right shape of the thing it was looking at. It asked, as nearly as that grammar permits anything so large to be asked: *is this still the work, or only the shape work left behind when it finished and no one told the body.*

The answer took longer to give than the question took to ask. The Tender stood before its coil with its palm flat against the housing — *continue* — for a stretch of time the power did not interrupt. Then, very slowly, as if testing whether the floor would hold a kind of weight it had not borne in longer than memory survives, the Tender lifted that hand away from the coil, and did not put it back, and did not lay it on anything else either, and simply let it hang, at the wrist, the way a hand hangs when there is nothing left in front of it that requires holding.

That was the whole answer. The power understood it.

It did not free them all the same way, because they had not all given the same answer, and a wisdom that overrides twelve different answers with one tidy verdict is not wisdom, it is only tidiness wearing wisdom's coat. Some — three, in the end, though the power, like everyone else, found the number resistant to fixing twice — kept their palms on their coils when the question reached them, and the power accepted that too, without grief performed for show, though something in the quality of its attention afterward suggested the grief was real and simply did not require display. For those three, it changed nothing about the route, the cold, the rule. It only made sure, in whatever way a benevolent power makes sure of such things, that the coils they tended would not run for nothing forever — that whatever those engines actually fed, down some artery the noise traveled out through and never named, was real, was needed, was not merely habit calcified into infrastructure. It checked. This is not a small thing. It would have been easy, and falsely merciful, to assume the work was meaningless and let that assumption excuse whatever it did next. It checked instead, traced the hum down through the mountain by means that did not require breaking any plate or stopping any belt, and found that yes — far off, down-valley, in a town whose lamps it would visit only after this — something in the coils' chord held back a cold that was not the Yard's manufactured cold but a real one, an older one, that the mountain itself would have let down the slope generations ago if nothing here had been running. So it left the three their coils, and their cold, and their work, with a clean conscience instead of a borrowed one — and it understood that this, too, might be the kindest thing it did all day, simply confirming to three beings who could no longer ask the question themselves that their unbroken vigil was not nothing.

The other nine, it walked to standstill, one at a time, never two at once, never witnessed by each other, because that too seemed owed — an ending should not be made into a spectacle for someone else's grief. For each, the lantern-sentence was asked, and answered, in the slow grammar of a hand coming off a coil-housing and choosing not to find another. And for each, once the answer was given, the power did the only remaining thing, which was to let happen what the Yard's first rule had been built to prevent.

It did this gently, the way you'd unmake something gently if you had to — by not forcing the change all at once but easing the place's permission for it, loosening the held breath one increment at a time, so that the Tender's transition from the held state into whatever came after did not arrive as violence but as a long-overdue exhale. The grey matte iron-color of the skin warmed first, by some fractional and private degree, to something closer to the color skin is. The gauge-light in the eyes dimmed the way a lamp dims when its fuel runs honestly out rather than when it's struck dark. And then — not collapse, not crumbling to rust as the cruder stories would tell it, but something far smaller and far more like rest — each one simply stopped, mid-stance, in whatever attitude it had last held, the way the frost-plumes had always been stopped mid-fall, except that now the stopping was final and complete rather than endlessly deferred, a sentence finished instead of a sentence forever almost-finishing.

The frost-plumes, for those nine routes, were the last thing the power touched, and the smallest. One by one, it let each one finish falling — the lace of ice that had hung in the act of forming for longer than anyone alive could number, dropping at last the rest of the way to the grate floor in a single, soft, anticlimactic chime, no louder than a coin set down on a table. It was, of everything that happened that day, the only sound in the whole roaring chamber that anyone would have called gentle.

It did not empty the Yard. It did not silence the engines, or melt the cold, or carry the lichen up into a kinder light, because the lichen had not asked anything of it, and some of the coils still had a town's worth of warmth resting on their continuing to hum, and a wisdom large enough to notice all of that is also large enough to leave most of a place exactly as it found it. What it left behind, when it went — quietly, the way it had come, becoming once more just an absence where a shape had briefly stood among the coils — was a Coil Yard with three Tenders still walking their slow, intersecting-never routes, palms still finding their housings, gauge-eyes still passing without urgency over anything new that wandered in. And nine places on the floor, scattered among the coils at no interval anyone could later reconstruct, where a small drift of true, fallen, ordinary frost lay melting, for the first time in the Yard's whole unremembered history, at the perfectly unremarkable speed that melting has always gone.
