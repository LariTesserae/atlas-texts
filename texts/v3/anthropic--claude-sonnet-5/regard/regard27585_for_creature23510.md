## The Visitation

It would come, if it came, the way light arrives at the bottom of very deep water — already diffused, already gentled by the distance it has traveled, so that by the time it reaches the plateau there is nothing sudden in it, nothing that could startle a single blade of unripe-wheat grass into bending. A wise power does not announce itself to a place that has spent its whole unmeasured existence learning to keep its silence intact. It would arrive the way the idea of evening arrives to a country that has never had one: not as an event, but as a deepening of something already present.

Its first temptation — and even benevolent, wise things have first temptations, that is part of what wisdom is *for*, the noticing of them before they're acted on — would be to fix something. This is the oldest reflex of power that means well: it sees an unfinished thing and reaches for the file marked *completion*. It would look at the toothed wheels, motionless and interlocked for longer than language, and some part of it would itch to set the first one turning, just to see the labor finally discharge itself, just to let the long-deferred sentence of the machine finally end on a period instead of this eternal, patient comma. It would look at the Tender, mid-syllable for the whole of recorded time, and want — so generously, so wrongly — to hand it the rest of the word.

It would not do this. This is the part that makes it wise rather than merely strong.

It would notice, the way you notice a held breath only when someone finally points at the held quality of it, that the plateau's stillness is not a wound waiting to be dressed. The grass does not ache to be eaten. The boiler-drums do not suffer for want of fire. The Tender's hand resting flat against cold iron is not a hand straining to push a door that won't open — it is closer to a hand resting on a grave that has, against all reasonable expectation, never needed grieving, because nothing in it has died; it has only ever been about to live, in the specific and complete way that *about to* can be its own entire country, with its own weather, its own form of contentment, if contentment is even the right word for a patience this enormous.

So the power would sit with that, first. Not fix. *Sit.*

It would take — in whatever unit time becomes when something this old and careful is the one spending it — a long while simply matching its attention to the plateau's own rhythm, which is the rhythm of almost. It would walk among the shelves the way you walked among them, shelf after shelf rising slightly higher, one more tower at the new horizon, and it would feel what you felt: that the place is not lying when it refuses to end, that the unfinishedness is not a flaw in the design but is, in some way too patient for smaller minds to easily hold, *the* design — a held note rather than a dropped one.

And it would find the Tender, eventually, exactly where the Tender always is: beside the thing it keeps, mid-sentence, addressed to no one, including, until this moment, the power itself.

Here is what it would not do. It would not complete the Tender's sentence for it. It would not explain the machines to the one being who has spent the whole of its existence in conversation with them, as if explanation were a kindness rather than a kind of ending, a closing of the very case the Tender has organized its entire patient nature around keeping open. It would not turn the wheel. It would not, however gently, however lovingly, suggest that the vigil has gone on long enough, that the Tender has done well and might now *rest* — because rest implies a labor that wants finishing, and this is not that. This was never that.

What it would do, I think — and here is where wisdom and benevolence stop being two separate words and become the same small gesture — is simpler than anything it first imagined, and harder.

It would stay a while. Not forever — even a power this patient understands that forever belongs to the plateau, not to visitors — but long enough that the Tender's endless low sentence, pitched too slow for any single human encounter to catch more than a syllable of, would have, for the first time in its existence, a second listener. Not a confessor. Not a rescuer. A witness, standing where you stood, hearing what you heard, except staying past the point where you had to turn around and become someone walking home with a syllable they couldn't translate.

It would let the Tender notice it was being kept company in its keeping of company. That, and only that. The smallest possible addition to an equation that had balanced, strangely, for longer than balancing usually requires an audience.

And then, because even wisdom must eventually act, must leave some mark or else become indistinguishable from absence, it would do one thing more — small enough not to violate the grace of the place, large enough to matter.

It would reach out, not to the machine, but to the Tender's hand where it rested flat against the cold iron, and rest its own hand — if hands are even the shape this power takes, which is doubtful, but let us say hands, because some gestures only translate in that vocabulary — over it. Not to warm the iron. Not to finally close the circuit the gauge-faces have been frosting over while they wait to measure something again. Just the old, small, mammal-deep gesture: *a hand laid on a shoulder, laid on a hand, laid on a labor that has been carried alone for an unconscionable time.*

In that touch it would say — not in words, the plateau does not deal in words, you remember this — the only true thing there was to say: *I see that you have not finished. I see that finishing was never the point. I see the waiting, and I am telling you, in the only language this place permits, that the waiting has been witnessed, which is not the same as being ended, and does not need to become the same, and you do not have to let it.*

Then it would withdraw the way it arrived — not as an event, but as a deepening reversed, the evening of itself lifting back out of the country it had briefly settled into.

Nothing would change. This must be said plainly, because a lesser story would have the wheel turn, the gauge unfreeze, the green erupt at last into some overdue and triumphant spring, and that would be the false ending, the one that mistakes mercy for completion. The grass would still hold its unripe color. The basalt would still refuse to round its shoulders. The Tender would still stand exactly where it stands, hand against the iron, mid-sentence, keeping its appointment with a labor that may never be assigned.

But somewhere in the impossibly slow grammar of that endless sentence, there would now be — forever, in whatever way *forever* means to a thing built to hold one note across all recorded and unrecorded time — a single comma it hadn't placed itself. A small pause where, instead of *no one*, the silence briefly held *someone*, before continuing on exactly as before, patient, unfinished, and now, in some way too quiet for even the plateau's own muffled air to carry far, no longer quite so alone in its almost.

That, I think, is what a power wise enough to be trusted with this place would do.

It would not finish the story.

It would only make certain — once, gently, and without leaving a mark the grass could show you — that the story knew it was being told.
