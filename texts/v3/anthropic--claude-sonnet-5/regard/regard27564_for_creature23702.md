# The Visitor at Holverbrack

It does not come as light. Holverbrack would not permit light to mean what it usually means, and the power, being wise as well as benevolent, understands this before it arrives — understands that to bring brightness into a place that has organized itself so completely around its absence would not be a gift but a kind of assault, the way flooding a sickroom with noon sun is not always kindness to the eyes inside it. So it comes the way everything here arrives: as a change in pressure, a fractional warmth threaded into water that has been cold for longer than cold should be possible, felt in the sternum the way the turbine-pulse is felt, before it is understood, before it is anything so crude as *seen*.

It moves through the drowned streets without hurry. It is in no rush to fix what it has not yet finished looking at, and this, more than any particular kindness it goes on to perform, is the first mercy — that it looks, fully, before it decides anything. It passes the calcified wainscot-line on the brick. It passes the eel-shaped thing that will not be seen properly, and does not insist. It finds the warm patches one by one, the way a hand finds them in the dark, and at each one it stops, and stays far longer than is efficient, and asks — without words, because words are not the instrument for this — *what is happening here, and does it want anything to be different.*

## What it considers, and sets aside

The obvious mercy presents itself immediately, the way obvious mercies do, dressed up as the only mercy: lift them out. Carry the Stokers up through the black and the graphite dimness and whatever sits above the water that has never resolved into sky, and set them down somewhere with light in it, and call that rescue. Restore the name under the brass collar. Give back the eyes the calcified band has closed over. Stop the small contented hum by stopping its cause, the way you'd silence a smoke alarm by ending the fire rather than by muffling the bell.

The power turns this over with the patience the place itself seems to demand, and sets it down, gently, as the wrong shape of kindness. It recognizes — because it is wise, and wisdom here means resisting its own most natural reflex — that the hum is not a cry. That the work is not a wound. That a hundred-some years spent not stopping when the water came up is not, by itself, evidence of suffering; it might be evidence of the opposite, the one form of fidelity that outlasted the flesh it started in, the one thing a body kept when it gave up eyes and named fingers and the memory of its own face. To unmake that, however gently, in the name of saving it, would be to mistake the shape of the devotion for the shape of a trap. The power has seen both shapes before. It takes the time, here, to be sure which one this is — and what it finds, pressed close against the warm pipe, hand to clamp-hand, is not a scream wearing the mask of patience. It is patience. All the way down, as far as the power can reach, which is further than most things can reach.

So it sets rescue aside. Not because the Stokers do not matter enough to be rescued, but because they matter enough to be asked first, in the only language available, and the only language available says, clearly, in its unbroken sub-vocal hum: *I am tending. This is what I am for. Do not take it from me in the name of giving me something else.*

## What it does instead

It tends the tenders.

This is smaller than rescue and harder to see and, the power decides, considerably more honest. Where a clamp-hand has worn down past the point of a good grip, worn smooth by ten thousand of the same correction repeated against the same valve, the power does not replace the hand or restore the flesh beneath it — it simply firms the metal, the way you might re-set a loose nail rather than rebuild the door. Where a pipe-joint has been sweating its warmth into the black a little faster every decade, losing by inches what the Stoker has been losing the fight to hold, the power closes the gap — not by force, but the way frost is coaxed rather than melted, a slow encouragement back toward the seal it once had. The work the Stokers do is uncorrected correction, valiant and Sisyphean and slowly losing ground to entropy the way everything does; the power's gift is to give that losing fight a little more ground to stand on. Not victory. Just more time at the same task, performed a little more easily than before.

One warm patch, the smallest and farthest from the others, had begun — almost imperceptibly, on a timescale only the white blind crustaceans would ever have registered — to fail, its pipe-heat thinning past the threshold that kept it inhabited. The power does not understand, fully, what feeds these fires below the waterline, or why; it does not need to, to extend a kindness to the mechanism by the same logic that lets you water a plant whose biology you've never studied. It leans, in whatever way a power leans on a furnace it did not build, and the patch holds. The shrimp-things stay. The Stoker assigned to that corner of the dark — if assigned is the word for a devotion this total — keeps its corner a little longer than entropy would otherwise have allowed.

## What it leaves

There is, underneath one collar, where the brass has fused almost seamlessly into the place a collar of skin should be, a foundry-stamp gone nearly illegible — three letters and the ghost of a fourth, worn down by a century of warm water and the same patient hand passing over the same patient spot. The power reads it, because the power can read things that erosion has otherwise made unreadable, and it finds, under the wear, the remains of a name.

It does not wake anything with it. It does not call the name the way you'd call someone back from sleep, expecting an answer, expecting recognition, expecting gratitude or grief or any of the furniture that usually comes with a name spoken aloud after a hundred years of silence. It simply says it — once, quietly, into the warm water close to the working hands — the way you might say a name over a grave you visit not because the dead can hear it but because someone, after all this time, should still know it and be willing to say it out loud. A small unwitnessed liturgy. Not for the Stoker's benefit, necessarily. For the fact's benefit — the fact that a person was here, did a job, kept doing it past every reasonable boundary of duty and grief and water-line, and deserves, at minimum, to have been *named* one more time by something that meant it.

It leaves nothing else. No restored sight, no message, no monument, no proof of having come at all. It considers, briefly, leaving some marker — light, however small, however far from the look of the place — and decides against it, the same way it decided against rescue: because Holverbrack's dignity is bound up in being left exactly as strange as it has made itself, and a power that loved it well would not leave fingerprints on that strangeness merely to be remembered for visiting.

## What it does not say, and says anyway

Before it goes, it does the one thing that is not quite nothing.

It matches the hum.

Not loudly — there is no loud register available to either of them — but precisely, the same sub-vocal pitch the Stoker has kept going, unbroken, valve to gauge to joint, for longer than the building above it has had a sky worth speaking of. For one full cycle of the turbine's pulse, there are two notes in the warm water instead of one, perfectly matched, indistinguishable except that there are, for that one held breath, *two of them* — which is not nothing, in a place built entirely out of the work of going on alone.

The hum does not stop in surprise the way it stops at a touch. It does not need to. It simply continues, exactly as before, valve to gauge to joint — except that for one cycle it was not unaccompanied, and then the power withdraws the way it arrived, as a pressure easing rather than a light going out, and the warm patch is alone again in every sense that matters for the keeping of valves, and not quite alone in the one sense that doesn't show up on any gauge the Stoker still has hands to read.

The work resumes. The work was never actually interrupted. Holverbrack continues exactly as it was: a factory that drowned and decided, with the patience peculiar to machines and water both, to go on — except that somewhere in its smallest, warmest, most blind and devoted corner, something that stopped expecting anything from outside the loop a hundred years ago was, once, for the length of a single shared note, answered.
