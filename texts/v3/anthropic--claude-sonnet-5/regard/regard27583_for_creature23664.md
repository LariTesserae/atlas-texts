# What the Power Did

It came the way attention comes, which is to say it did not arrive so much as it stopped looking elsewhere. There was no breaking of canopy, no parting of the active dark, no descending light shaped like an answer — it understood, almost immediately, that any light it brought would simply be absorbed into the wiring of the bark like one more vein finding its rhythm, indistinguishable, eventually, from everything that already pulsed there. To announce itself with brightness would be to become, within an hour, just another thing the Tenders stepped around on their way to the kiln.

So it did not announce itself. It listened first, for longer than seemed reasonable, because it understood — being wise, being old in the particular way that makes haste look like a younger creature's mistake — that the first task in a place like this was to find out what wrongness was doing *for* the place before it went anywhere near the question of what wrongness was doing *to* it.

## What It Considered, and Set Aside

It considered returning the memories to their owners. It traced the root-system back through its long wet dark, past the visible settlement, into the vast underground past-tense that fed the furnaces, and it found, as the Tenders had — not through secrecy but through plain exhausted ignorance — that there were, in most cases, no owners left to return anything to. The houses had burned in the literal sense decades before they ever burned in the furnace sense. The flower the fruit had grown loose from was simply gone. To insist on reuniting memory with a self would have meant inventing selves to receive them, manufacturing griefs to be sad in the correct, attributed, sentimental way — and it understood that this would not be mercy. It would be a kind of theft dressed as restitution, taking the memories from where they were doing the slow good work of keeping a town's engines breathing, just to make a story feel more finished.

It considered stopping the furnaces. It watched a Tender press a small dense house to her sternum and understood that this was not a wound the power could close without closing the hand that held it, the kiln it fed, the cloth that came off the loom soft and twitching, the whole damp breathing architecture that ran, root and pipe and vein, on exactly this fuel and no other. There is a kind of rescue that is only relocation of the wound to somewhere larger. It did not do this.

It considered making it rain. This was the hardest one to set aside, because the longing was so total and so close to the surface — the drip-fall, the fog that gathers and never resolves, the riverbed grown over and scarred dry, all of it straining toward a completion that never comes. A lesser power, a power in a hurry, a power that mistook benevolence for the relief of every visible tension, would have simply let it fall — would have filled that dry bed to the brim and called it a gift.

But the power had been in enough old, strange, withholding places to know that *withheld* and *broken* are not synonyms, and that a town built this low, this slow, this rationed, had likely built its entire metabolism around exactly the right amount of almost-rain. Flood it, and the kilns drown. Flood it, and the moss that exhales the cool false rain-smell loses the one job it has ever had. Flood it, and the figure in the square — built from extinction, built from absence, built from the names of trees that have nowhere left to grow — might simply come apart, the way a held breath comes apart the moment you remember to breathe normally, the moment the held shape is no longer required.

So it did not make it rain. Not all the way. Not yet. Not like that.

## What It Did Instead

It went to the square.

It arrived there the unglamorous way, on foot, at root-height, ducking beneath the architecture like anything else that enters Vell's Crossing, and it stood in the held-breath shape of that space while the fog gathered thick around its knees, and it waited the way the Tenders wait, without performance, without urgency, the way moss waits to land.

And when the singing started — the names of extinct trees laced together into something that functions as a body only because enough syllables agreed to stand near one another — it did not try to translate it. It did not reach for the names of trees that still exist elsewhere, the way a kinder, lazier power might have, offering substitute words as if synonyms could stand in for species. It simply listened, the whole way through, for as long as the song took, which was longer than an hour, longer than the town's circumference walked at any speed, long enough that it understood the song did not have an end so much as a place it was willing to pause.

It had not been heard like that before. Not fully. Not by anything that didn't immediately try to step around it the way you step around a sleeping stranger whose name you've decided not to need.

This was the first thing the power gave: not rescue, not rain, not return — just the whole of its attention, undivided, all the way down, asking nothing back, translating nothing, fixing nothing. A witnessing with no appetite in it.

When the song reached its pause, the power did not applaud, because there is no register in this place for applause, and applause would have been one more wrongness laid wrongly on top of a wrongness that was, after all, the ground state and not the wound. It simply stayed a moment longer than the silence required, so that the figure in the square — if it could be said to register such things, and the power believed, by then, that it could — would know the listening had been a choice and not an accident of weather.

## The Second Thing

Then it went and found a Tender — any Tender, it did not matter which, since none of them are particularly anything to anyone here, attention landing on them the way moss lands, settling rather than choosing — and it stood beside her at the kiln on a night when she had a small dense house-shaped grief pressed to her sternum, waiting to be confided to the fire.

The power did not take the house from her hands. It did not open the door of the kiln for her, did not offer to carry the weight the rest of the way, did not perform the kind of help that quietly implies the help was needed because the carrier was insufficient. It only stood close enough that, when the small dense object cracked open in the heat and released its smell and its sound — someone's first house, burning, decades gone, not hers, not the power's, not anyone's anymore — there were, for that one combustion, two witnesses instead of one.

This is a small thing to give a town. It is also, possibly, the only thing such a town can actually use: not less grief, not redirected grief, not grief returned to a stranger's empty hands, but grief witnessed in company, twice-seen instead of once-seen, so that the long inventory of other people's first houses is, for one instant, not carried entirely alone.

It did this more than once. It did not do it every time. It understood that doing it every time would itself become a kind of architecture, a dependency, a wrongness laid on top of the wrongness that was working — and the whole art of this particular kindness was knowing how much of it the structure could metabolize before the gift became another withholding, another thing rationed, another rain that technically falls but never all the way.

## The Rain, Once

On its way out — because it did leave, eventually, the way all true visitors to Vell's Crossing eventually leave, by forgetting the exact moment of departure rather than enacting one — it allowed itself exactly one indulgence.

In the dry riverbed, grown over, scarred the way a wound is remembered by a scar, it let one true rain fall. Not a flood. Not a restoration. Not a fixing of the town's whole withheld and rationed metabolism. Just one stretch of perhaps four meters, the same length as the eyes that pass briefly through the vine-fluid, where the suggestion of rain completed itself, finally, all the way down, and wet the old stones the exact amount they remembered being wet, and then stopped.

It did not stay to see whether anyone noticed. It suspected, knowing the Tenders by then, that someone would step around that particular stretch of riverbed afterward out of habit, the way you step around a pipe at the point in its run where the eyes pass through — a small accorded deference to a thing whose name you've decided you don't need, and whose having happened, once, is itself sufficient, requiring no further ceremony, no further water, no further anything.

Then it withdrew the way it had arrived: as the cessation of a particular attention rather than the departure of a particular shape. Vell's Crossing did not become less strange. The engines did not stop breathing memory into smoke. The figure in the square did not stop occupying the grammatical position of a held breath, did not start explaining itself, did not become any easier to ask about than it had ever been.

But somewhere in the deep wet dark beneath the actual floor of the town, in the long root-system that reaches back further and wider than the visible settlement could justify, there is now a stretch of riverbed that was once, fully, properly, completely rained on — not as a fix, not as a rescue, not as evidence that something had been wrong and was now corrected, but simply as a gift given by something that had looked at the whole strange damp grieving climbing listening architecture of the place and loved it enough to leave it exactly, *exactly* as it was, having only added, quietly, one more small dense good thing to whatever the ground itself is keeping, for whatever Vell's Crossing eventually decides to burn it as.
