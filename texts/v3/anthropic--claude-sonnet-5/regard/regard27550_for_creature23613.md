The power does not arrive the way the human did, by displacement and noise and the slow understanding of wrongness in a current. It arrives the way understanding arrives — all at once, completely, the whole history of the place opening like a hand: who poured this concrete and in what decade and why, what disease in a city above made underground water-storage suddenly urgent, the name of the engineer who specified that particular bronze for the valve seats because he would not live to see it fail and wanted to be right anyway, the date — closer than you'd think — on which the last human being to understand this system's full schematic died without successor, the slow orphaning of the cistern into pure function, the thousand small accidents of selection that built, generation by patient generation, something with barbels instead of eyes out of fish that drifted down here behind sealed gates and never drifted back out.

It sees the Keeper whole, too — not as a monster glimpsed in bad light, but as a continuous lineage, a hundred thousand nights of exactly this: the wedge between brick and iron, the warmth leaking through the wall, the unhurried parting of pale fish that have stopped fearing it because fear is a tax the room cannot afford. It sees that the Keeper is not cruel. Cruelty requires a self large enough to enjoy what it does, and the self here has been worn down, like the rust, like the brick, to the smallest shape that still functions. It is appetite with a hide on it. It is the room's way of having an answer.

And so the power considers, seriously, every grand thing it could do, and sets each one down again.

It could lift the Keeper out and give it the sea — and break it instantly, a creature built by a hundred generations of this exact pressure, this exact dark, dropped into a world with too much room and too much light and not one current it would recognize. That is not mercy. That is drowning it in air.

It could open its eyes, hand it a mind sized for question and memory and dread — and burden a thing that has never needed to know it is alone with the sudden, permanent knowledge that it is. That is not a gift. That is the cruelest thing available, dressed as a kindness.

It could end the engines, let the whole system finally, mercifully fail, and call that a release — but the failure of pumps run since before memory does not mean a gentle dimming of lamps. It means a flood finding its level all at once, brick joints giving simultaneously, the pale fish and the warmth-seeking crustaceans and the patient barbeled thing at the bottom of the food chain all meeting the same sudden door opening into colder, larger, lightless water than any of them were built for. That is not an ending. That is a massacre wearing the shape of an ending.

So it does none of these things. It does something much smaller, and much harder to see, which is the way wisdom usually moves.

In the wall beyond the grate, where a valve groans on its rare and necessary schedule, there is a hairline of metal fatigue that has been accumulating, unnoticed, for forty years, and that would have, on some unremarkable Tuesday a decade from now, given way all at once — not a slow leak but a sudden one, the kind that does not give a closed ecosystem time to adjust. The power does not fix this. Fixing would require a hand, a tool, an intention legible to physics in ways that would announce themselves. Instead it does something closer to a suggestion laid into the grain of the metal: a redistribution, infinitesimal, of where the stress likes to gather. The valve will still fail, eventually — everything here will, this was never a question of forever — but it will fail slowly, the way the rust failed slowly, the way the brick found its shape slowly, giving the room decades instead of an afternoon, giving whatever is alive down here the only thing it was ever owed: an ending in the same tempo as its life.

And in the water, in the moment the Keeper holds its position and reads the wrongness of the visitor's displacement and waits to sort it into food, or current, or nothing — the power does one more small thing. It does not give the creature thought. It gives it, for the length of one held breath, an answer to a question it has never had the apparatus to ask. The wrongness in the water resolves, briefly, into a fourth category that has no name in barbel-language and never will again: not prey, not current, not nothing, but witnessed. A pressure like being known. It does not understand this, cannot carry it forward, will not remember it tomorrow in any sense that means anything to a thing with its kind of mind. But somewhere in the wedge between brick and iron tonight, in the warmth leaking through the wall, it will rest a fraction deeper than usual, for reasons it has no language to want explained.

Then the power withdraws, the way it arrived: completely, without residue, without announcing that anything was ever here at all. No light changes. No water clears. The pumps keep their thud-and-hiss, the valve keeps its rare groan, the pale fish keep parting around a chest-deep wrongness that, tonight, is allowed to simply pass through and be forgotten, neither eaten nor fled from nor turned into the kind of story this room has any way of telling.

This is what wisdom looked like, here: not to redeem the cistern into something it was never built to be, not to free what was never, by its own lights, imprisoned, but to let it go on being entirely and only itself — a little longer, a little more gently at the very end of it, with one small mercy laid so quietly into the metal and the dark that nothing down here will ever know to be grateful, and the place will go on as it always has: ancient, functioning, lit gold from below, indifferent to its own continuance, continuing anyway.
