The power found it the way it found most things worth finding — not by searching, but by noticing a silence shaped like a held breath. Somewhere in the endless tap and chock and creak of the Wickwork, underneath the message-traffic of warnings and arrivals and small griefs passed hand to grain to hand, there was a rhythm that never resolved. A people speaking constantly to each other in the only language their world permitted, and never once saying the word for *enough*.

It watched for what, in the place's own time, would have been generations. It pressed itself — if pressed is even the word for what a power does — against the same beams the listeners pressed their cheeks to, and heard what they heard: not just structural failure and structural news, but a kind of fear laundered so thoroughly through ritual that it had stopped feeling like fear at all. *Don't stop, or you'll go small.* Said gently, said to children, said by mothers who themselves had never once put down their tools long enough to test whether it was true.

It would have been easy — it is always easy, for something with the reach the power had — to do the large things. To unmake the dark and give them sun, and watch their wide black-pupiled eyes burn and weep and eventually, over fewer generations than they'd believe possible, adjust. To breathe vegetation into the bare wood-that-is-not-wood until moss softened every joint and the unsettling cleanliness gave way to a riot of green. To press, into one strong hand somewhere in the depths, a tool of true metal that never dulled, and let the shock of abundance ripple outward through the tap-code as news, as miracle, as a fundamentally different kind of life.

The power considered all of this, the way you consider a held breath you could release for someone — and decided, mostly, against it.

Not from indifference. From having looked closely enough to see what it would cost. The dark was not their wound; it was their architecture, load-bearing in its own way, and a people whose eyes had spent their whole inheritance learning to drink thin doses of light would not be liberated by a flood of it — they would be blinded, and call it punishment, and lash new walls against the wincing, sealing themselves in tighter than before. Vegetation, given all at once, unearned, would not read as gift; the bareness was theirs, swept by no one and yet groomed all the same, and the power suspected — rightly, as it happens — that an explosion of green would feel less like blessing than like rot, like the thing they'd spent ten thousand small repairs holding off finally arriving anyway. And metal, true metal, dropped into one hand — that hand would become a god or a target before the season turned, and either way the quiet, distributed, everyone-building dignity of the place would curdle into hierarchy, into hoarding, into a story about one chosen lashed instead of a story about all of them.

The power had not lived this long, in this way, to mistake largesse for love.

So it did the other thing — the slow thing, the thing that enters by the door the place itself had already built for it.

It went in through the grain.

There was a woman, old in the way the Wickwork measures old, hands too unsteady now to lash, eyes gone milky in a darkness that no longer needed them — a listener, third in a line of listeners in her stretch, known for sitting motionless against a particular crossbeam for whole days at a time, translating its groans into the small prophecies her cluster had come to rely on. The power did not speak to her in thunder, or in vision, or in any voice that would have to be explained afterward, defended, built into a religion with her at its center. It spoke to her the only way that wouldn't distort on arrival: it became, for one night, a tapping in the beam itself, indistinguishable in its grammar from any courier's message, except that it did not come from any direction a courier could have come from, and except that it did not stop at the boundary of her own cluster's dialect of knots, but seemed to know all of them at once, the way water finds every grain regardless of who lashed it.

It did not give her an answer. It gave her a question, phrased the way the tap-code phrases its hardest news — plainly, without ornament, trusting the listener to carry the weight of it herself.

*What if the small ones were never a punishment. What if they simply finished.*

That was all. It did not say *stop*. It did not say *the structure has no plan and never did, and you are not its hands but only its inhabitants, dressing your labor up as purpose because the alternative is unbearable* — though this was, as far as such things can be said to be true, true. It judged, correctly, that this particular truth delivered whole would not free them; it would only hollow out the only meaning they had, the way pulling a load-bearing strut out of a wall doesn't liberate the room, it collapses it. Some truths have to be grown into, joint by joint, the way everything else here is grown into, or they kill on arrival.

What it left instead was smaller than a truth. It was a doubt, planted in someone whose whole vocation was translating doubt-shaped sounds into certainty for other people, and trusting — this is the part that was, in the end, an act of something very like faith, a power having faith in the small and the lashed — that she would not pass the question on as decree. That she would do what listeners do: hold it against her cheek for a long time, turn it over, let it season, and only then, carefully, in her own words, in her own dialect of knots, offer it to a frightened child some night as something other than warning. Not *don't stop or you'll go small.* Maybe, eventually, after years of her own turning it over: *I don't know that you would. I have never seen it happen. I have only ever heard that it does.*

And separately — because a power can do more than one small thing, and because bodies deserve tending alongside beliefs — it loosed into the dampest, lowest, most forgotten joints of the structure a thing that had never existed there before: pale, faintly luminous, no more than a film at first, undemanding of light, undemanding of soil, content to simply adhere to the wet grain and live without consuming it, without rotting it, without asking the wickwork to be anything other than what it already was. Not moss in the riotous sense the power had decided against. Just a single small persistent life, finally willing to grow on this material when nothing else ever had — the bareness, after all this age, finally answered, not with abundance, but with company. The lashed would find it in a generation or two, a faint cool glow in the lowest rooms where the water pools, and would not know what to call it, and would argue for a long time about whether it was a blessing or a warning, and that argument — long, inconclusive, conducted in taps across forty layers of joinery — would itself become a kind of gift, a new thing to talk about that wasn't fear.

Then the power withdrew, the way wisdom mostly does: without announcing the withdrawal, without requiring credit, without staying to see how the question and the glow would be metabolized into knot and story and the next thousand small repairs.

It had not finished anything. It understood, better than most, that here, of all places, finishing was never really the point — and that the kindest thing it could lash onto this structure was not an ending, but one more joint: small, load-bearing, easy to miss, strong enough that a few of them, eventually, might rest their weight on it without the whole thing coming down.
