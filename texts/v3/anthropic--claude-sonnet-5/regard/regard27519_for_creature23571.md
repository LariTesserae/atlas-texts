The attention arrives the way the rare rain does — without announcement, without disturbing the standing roar of birds or the thunder-line of the herds, settling over the plateau and its threshold the way light settles over stone it has already, many times, illuminated. It is not a figure. It does not descend the switchbacks or stand at the fire-ring casting a shadow that would need explaining. If it has a shape at all, it is closer to the shape of noticing itself — the way the basins notice sky, holding it whole and unflattering and exact, without asking the sky to be anything other than what it already is.

It considers, first, the plateau. This is the easy part, almost a formality. The power turns its attention to the silver-grey flats, the wheeling birds, the herds that turn as one body, the basins round as punched coins of standing water — and finds nothing to do. Not because the place is beneath notice, but because it is already, completely, the thing it set out to be, ages ago, and has gone on being it with a faithfulness that needs no improvement and would resent one. To add anything here — a spring, a softening, a single dramatic spire thrust up out of the old smoothed shoulders of rock — would be a kind of vandalism dressed as a gift. The plateau does not lack. The plateau is the rare case of a thing that finished becoming itself and was allowed to stop. The wisest thing, the power understands immediately, is to leave it exactly alone — to let the herds cross the same line tomorrow, the birds ride the same thermals off the same sun-warmed slabs, the lightning-less thunder arrive a mile delayed and undiminished as it always has. Reverence, here, looks like restraint. The power gives the plateau nothing, and this is the whole of its gift to the plateau: nothing, offered completely.

Then it turns to the woman at the door.

This is harder, because she is not finished in the way the rock is finished. She is a person, and persons have an underside that stone does not — a place where the cold gets in not through any crack in the rock but through the simple unwitnessed weight of decades spent giving warnings to strangers who do not stay long enough to learn her name. The power sees this clearly: that she has built her whole life as a hinge, that she has made of herself something useful and necessary and almost invisible, the way a good door hinge is necessary and invisible, noticed only in the absence of its function, never in the presence of its self. She does not perform loneliness. She would deny it, mildly, the way she receives thanks — as weather, arriving and passing. But the power is old enough and patient enough to tell the difference between a woman who has made peace with a hard shape and a woman who has simply never been offered any other shape to consider.

It does not give her a different life. That temptation passes through and is set down, the way she herself sets down a coin a traveler presses into her cracked palm — acknowledged, not kept. To lift her off the threshold, to make her young, to give her a name carved into permanence, would be to dismantle the very thing that makes her what she is: the place where two worlds are allowed to meet without tearing. A guardian remade into a monument stops being a guardian. So the power does not do that.

What it does is smaller, and it does it the way the woman herself does things — practically, without ceremony, indistinguishable afterward from luck.

It sees to it that an ember survives in her fire-ring through even the worst nights, so that some mornings, when her knuckles ache too much to want to bend to the work of starting a flame from nothing, there is no nothing to start from — only a coal, waiting, patient as a cushion-plant in its wind-shadow crack. It loosens, by some fraction too small to notice, the ache in her hands on the days she most needs to climb. It does nothing to the basins above, but it makes sure that the ones she sweeps by hand near her own shelter never quite freeze solid, not even on the bitterest night, so that there is always, somewhere close, a little water holding the sky steady for her alone to look into, if she ever wants to.

And it bends, very slightly, the shape of probability around one future evening it cannot fully see the date of — some descending traveler, ordinary in every way, who does not merely leave a coin and a word of thanks shaped like weather, but sits down at the small fire a while longer than intended, and asks her, not out of duty but out of plain unguarded curiosity, what she is called. Not what travelers have called her. What she is called.

The power cannot make her answer with her true name, if she has one, or invent one if she does not — that choice, rightly, stays hers. It only makes sure the question arrives, eventually, asked by someone who means it, into an evening warm enough and unhurried enough that she might, for once, choose to answer instead of letting the question pass like rain.

That is all. The herds will cross tomorrow along the same line. The birds will roar their standing roar. The basins above will hold their borrowed sky, indifferent and exact. And at the threshold, a small fire that does not go entirely cold, and a woman whose hands ache a little less than they should, going on doing what she has always done — not rescued, not transformed, only, very quietly, accompanied.
