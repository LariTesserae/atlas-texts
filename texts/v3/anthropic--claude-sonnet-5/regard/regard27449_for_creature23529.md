The power comes the way weather comes to the high peaks — not as a figure stepping through a door, but as a pressure change, a stillness that the goats notice before the people do. For a long moment it simply attends. This is the first discipline of real benevolence: to look before deciding there is anything to fix.

And looking, it sees that Khulm-Atar is not broken.

This matters enormously, because the temptation arrives instantly and in full force — the way it always does when something powerful meets something fragile. *Warm the stone.* *Fatten the water table.* *Soften the grade of the staircases so the old don't fall.* *Grant the masons one more decade each, so the half-finished things get finished.* Each of these would cost the power nothing. Each of these, the power understands within the first unhurried second of its attention, would be a kind of violence.

Because the thing it is looking at is not a settlement that happens to be enduring hardship. It is a settlement *made of* the relationship to hardship — a culture whose entire grammar of meaning is built from scarcity handled with patience: the narrow niche filled completely, the long task carried partway, the elder as repository, the child as custodian, the dry joke instead of the raised voice, the unsentimental, unembittered acceptance that *the mountain finishes nothing in one life.* Take away the thinness of the air and you don't get the same people with easier lungs. You get different people. Fill the cisterns to overflowing and you don't save them labor — you dissolve the entire moral structure built around the jar under the gutter, the reverence, the trade of scraping dew. A people shaped this precisely by scarcity cannot be *given* abundance. Abundance, dropped on them from outside, would not be a gift. It would be an erasure wearing a gift's face.

So the power sits with the harder question, which is the only question worth a wise power's time: *not* "what can I give them," but "what, if anything, do they actually lack, that taking would not unmake them?"

It looks longer.

And here it notices the singular being the attention was drawn to — not, it turns out, any one person, exactly, though it will act through one. It is something more like what a reef is, if you tried to name the reef instead of any one polyp: the city's *continuity*, the long unbroken thread of intention running tier to tier, grandfather's crane to grandson's hands, the half-built wall waiting for someone who remembers which direction it was meant to grow. That thread is the being. It has a pulse, a memory, a slow heartbeat measured in completed terraces rather than years. It is, in its own unhurried way, alive, and like anything alive it can be wounded — not by frost or hunger, which it has already metabolized into its own structure, but by *forgetting.* A break in the thread. An intention that dies with the person who held it and is never recovered, so that some piece of the mountain's long argument with the people who live on it simply — stops, mid-sentence, forever.

This, the power realizes, is the one true wound available to it that wouldn't also be a kindness that costs them their shape. Not to fill anything. To *catch* something, once, that was about to fall.

So it looks for where the thread is thinnest, and finds her: an old woman in the ninth tier, a cisterer by trade, hands chapped in the particular pattern of forty winters of morning ice-work, who carries in her — and only in her, now — the exact angle and intended course of a channel her own grandmother began and never finished, meant to carry meltwater to a terrace that has stood dry and unplanted for two generations, waiting. The granddaughter who was meant to inherit this knowledge took a husband in a tier three switchbacks down last spring and the visits have thinned, the way visits do, for reasons involving nothing more sinister than a new baby and a hard winter and the simple gravity of a life filling up with its own immediate weather. The old woman feels the thread going slack in her own hands and has, in the unspoken way of her people, begun quietly grieving it — not loudly, not asking for help, because asking is not really done, and because some part of her has already filed it under *things the mountain didn't finish*, which is not despair, only honesty.

The power does not wake her with a vision. It does not write the channel's angle in light on her wall. It does not so much as warm the room.

What it does is smaller than a miracle and, for that reason, exactly the right size: on an ordinary morning, for no reason any Khulm-Atari would think to question, the granddaughter wakes early with her infant strangely settled, strangely undemanding, and finds herself with an unclaimed hour and an inexplicable, mild, specific restlessness — the kind any of them would call, without superstition, just *a feeling* — to walk up and check on her grandmother's threshold before the frost gets worse. She climbs nine switchbacks she hasn't climbed in a season. She arrives at dusk. The light is doing the thing it does at that hour in that season, coming in low and gold across the schist, and something about that particular light, on that particular evening, loosens the old woman's tongue the way it never has before, and she finds herself saying, unplanned, *sit, before I forget the angle of it* — and tells her. All of it. The intended grade, the reason for the dogleg around the buried boulder, the year her grandmother meant to finish it and didn't, the year she herself meant to and didn't, and now here, here is where it lives, in you, if you want it.

By the time full dark comes, the thread holds. Nothing visible has happened. No one will ever know — could ever know — that a feeling arriving at the right hour was the entire intervention; that the power's whole act, here, was the quiet adjustment of one woman's morning restlessness and the precise color of one evening's light, nudged a few degrees toward a conversation that the people themselves will always, correctly, describe as *the mountain finishes nothing in one life, except sometimes, when you're lucky, it does.*

The power does not stay to be thanked. It would not know how to receive thanks for this kind of thing without the receiving becoming its own small corruption — the beginning of being needed, watched for, prayed to, built into the cosmology as a presence that fixes things, which would change what kind of patience this is a people of. It withdraws the way it arrived: as a pressure lifting, noticed by the goats first.

What it leaves behind is not abundance. It is not safety, or warmth, or extra years.

It leaves behind exactly one preserved sentence in an unbroken conversation between dead grandmothers and unborn grandchildren — a single thread, caught before it dropped, in a tapestry that was never the power's to finish, and that it understood, the whole time, was never supposed to be.
