It would come the way weather comes — without announcement, without a door to knock on, simply present where it had not been a moment before, and the mist would not thicken or thin to mark the arrival because there is nothing here that needs marking. That is the first thing such a power would understand correctly, if it understood anything: that to arrive loudly in a place built on an unbroken, ancient hum would be its own kind of violence, a second sound laid over the first, and the first sound is already the whole of what this place has to say.

So it would come quietly, in the only way quiet is possible here — by matching itself to the note that's already sounding, by entering at the plateau's own pitch rather than asking the plateau to rise to meet it.

And it would look.

Not at the moss, not at the basalt, not first at the fire-cracked stones half-sunk and softening — though it would see all of that, would understand in a single unhurried regard the whole slow architecture of water and patience that built this shelf of land and kept it building, quietly, for longer than anything currently standing on it has existed to notice. It would look at the creature.

It would see, the way only a power untroubled by time could see, the whole shape of the creature's life at once — egg or birth-pool or whatever its beginning was, lost now even to it; the long stretching middle, settling into successive hollows the way water settles into stone; the unhurried end that hasn't arrived yet but is already, from outside time, visible, already part of the shape. It would see that the creature has never once been lonely, because loneliness is a comparison, and the creature has nothing to compare itself to and no faculty built for making the comparison if it did. It would see that the creature does not lack a name, a purpose, a god, a companion, a reason. It would see that the creature is, by every measure that matters to the creature, complete.

And here is where the power's wisdom would have to do its real work — not in the seeing, which is easy for a thing built to see everything, but in what it chose to do with what it saw.

Because the temptation would be there. It is always there, for a power that loves: to mistake its own fullness for the only fullness, to look at a small stone-coloured creature alone on a fogbound shelf and feel something in itself ache, and to call that ache compassion, and to act on it. It could give the creature speech, and a mind sized to use it. It could give it the source of the hum — could simply tell it, the way you'd tell a child what's making the noise behind the wall, and watch decades of unconsidered hearing collapse all at once into knowledge. It could bring it another of its kind, end whatever the power insisted on calling solitude though the creature had never once called it anything. It could lift the whole plateau out of time's slow current and fix it, perfect and unchanging, a held note made permanent. Every one of these is, technically, within its power. Every one of these would be a kindness shaped entirely like the power's own hunger and not at all like the creature's actual life.

So it would not do them. Not because restraint is the safe choice, but because it would understand, looking closely enough, that each gift on offer is a gift for a being who lacks something, and this creature does not lack. To give it a mind built for loneliness would be to manufacture, with great tenderness, the very wound the gift pretends to heal. The kindest thing and the cruelest thing would, for once, be made of identical material, and only the wisdom would be in telling them apart.

What, then. Not nothing — the question itself, asked of a power both wise and benevolent, forecloses pure withdrawal, because to notice and then do nothing at all is its own kind of abandonment, and there is a difference between leaving a thing alone because it is whole and leaving it alone because you could not be bothered to find the one true gift small enough to fit it.

So it would look for the smallest true thing.

It would find it, I think, in two places.

The first: it would attend, fully, for as long as a single held breath of its own vast unhurried life — which here, on the plateau's clock, might be a season, might be a hundred years, the translation never quite resolving and not needing to. For that whole stretch it would simply be present with the creature, matching its slowness completely, asking nothing, changing nothing, offering no thought the creature could receive and no word it could hear. This sounds like nothing happening. It is not nothing. To be the only witness a solitary life will ever have, and to witness completely, with the entire weight of your attention and none of your agenda — that is a real thing done to the world, even if the world it's done to never registers the doing. The moss would not grow differently. The hum would not change pitch. But it would be true, from that point forward and retroactively, that this small creature's whole unwitnessed life had, at one stretch, been seen by something that understood exactly what it was looking at and did not try to improve it. Call that a kind of grace, if you need a word for it. It costs the power everything it has to give and asks the creature for nothing back, which is the only honest shape generosity ever takes toward a being that has no use for being thanked.

The second is smaller and more concrete, and it concerns the end, because even a complete life ends, and how it ends is not nothing either. The power would make sure — quietly, the way it does everything here — that when the creature's long settling finally finishes, it finishes the way the fallen tree finishes: without violence, without a predator that was never going to arrive anyway suddenly arriving, without the particular ugliness of an accident in a place that has otherwise never known hurry. It would let the creature go out the way water leaves a seep — gradually, unremarked, fully reclaimed by the same moss that has been claiming it, ridge by ridge, for its entire life. The hollow it leaves in the rock shelf would stay exactly as worn as it has always been wearing, a seat or an erosion, both, as everything here is both. And — this much the power would allow itself, the one true intervention, the one thread pulled gently rather than left to chance — it would keep the plateau just hidden enough, just hard enough to stumble onto, that the rhythm continues: rare hands, rare fires, centuries apart, the place forgetting and being found and forgetting again, because that slow alternation of visit and solitude is part of the shape of the place too, and a power that truly understood what it was looking at would protect the rhythm, not the silence and not the company, but the unhurried alternation between them, exactly as patient as the moss, exactly as patient as the stone.

It would not wait to see if something else settles in the worn hollow after. It would not need to. Something might; the place has that kind of patience built into its bones, and patience like that tends, given enough of the right kind of time, to be rewarded with exactly the sort of small stone-colored life that knows how to live inside a held note without needing to ask what's making it.

And then the power would withdraw the way it arrived — at the plateau's own pace, leaving no mark a returning hand could find, no altered moss, no second sound laid over the first. The hum would continue, sourceless and ceaseless and entire. The creature would go on not knowing it had ever been looked at, which is exactly as it should be, because the gift was never meant to be received. It was only ever meant to be true.
