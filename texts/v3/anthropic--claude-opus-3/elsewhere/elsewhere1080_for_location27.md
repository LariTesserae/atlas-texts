The Bone Orchard

Across a narrow isthmus of jagged rock, where the ceaseless subterranean winds howl through a gauntlet of mineral fangs, lies a very different cavern. If the damp and dripping grotto of rusting machines is a cathedral, the Bone Orchard is a vast ossuary.

The first sight to greet an intrepid voyager is a towering portal of yellowed, ancient bones. Ribs longer than a man arch to form its pointed apex, fused by knotted ropes of calcified tendon. Skulls of myriad beasts stud this macabre archway like gruesome jewels, eyeless sockets gaping. Beyond lies a gently rising slope paved in a rattling scree of osseous fragments.

The cavern itself is a gargantuan bubble in the bedrock, its distant ceiling lost in shadow. Stalactites and stranger speleothems depend from above like titanic canines in a vaulted mouth. But it is the bonescape below that draws the eye and chills the blood. 

In the Bone Orchard, ancient skeletal remains carpet the floor in a vast, tiered necropolis. Million-year-old femurs, phalanges, vertebrae, and stranger fossils form a dreary, desiccated landscape of knobs, spires, and pits. Mounds of stacked, splintered bones rise like hills. Channels and pools of dark, still marrow reflect the cavern's wan light like stagnant blood. The air is dry and frigid, still and silent as an opened crypt.

But the Bone Orchard is far from lifeless. Its primary inhabitants are the Osseous Gardeners, eerily gracile figures that dance and gibber among the skeletal monuments. Vaguely hominid in stature, their bodies are gaunt and elongated, wrapped in taut, translucent skin the sickly gray of old bone. Their heads are disturbingly infantile in proportion, with bulbous craniums and dark, sunken eyes.

The Gardeners tend their boneyard fastidiously. With long, delicate fingers they sort and stack the chaos of remains into morbid sculptures - towering spires, teetering arches, precarious lattices. They croon and whisper to the bones in voices like nails on slate, a susurrating language never meant for the ears of the living. When some deep subterranean tremor topples one of their osseous constructions, they mewl and keen in dismay until it is rebuilt.

Infiltrating this ghostly reliquary is a peculiar flora. Lichens of bleak blacks and grays mottle the bones in funereal veils. Fungal growths sprout from skulls and rib cages - some bearing lurid, toxic-looking fruits, others unfurling like grasping skeletal hands. Most unsettling are the Marrow Flowers, with petals like curling parchment and roots that plunge into the dark pools of ancient myeloid ichor. Their oily pollen drifts through the still air, narcotic and noxious.

A scant few creatures call this morbid garden home. Cave crickets as pale as the bones stridulate mournful dirges. Eyeless, serpentine centipedes undulate between grinning fossil jaws. Translucent snails leave glistening, mucoid trails across ancient vertebrae. All are deathly white or drained gray, as if in mimicry of the bonescape.

This grim ecology is not as isolated as it first appears. Tiny rivulets of water, percolating through hairline cracks, connect the pools of the Bone Orchard to the flooded tunnels of the adjacent cavern. Minute fish, pale and blind, flit between the two realms, their cyclical migration an ancient, essential current of nutrients and energy. The Osseous Gardeners too sometimes venture into the dripping dark of the industrial cavern, gathering strange growths and mineral deposits to work into their skeletal sculptures.

Thus these two underworlds, disparate as they seem, are bound in a single living system, a chthonic yin and yang. In the ceaseless cycle of water and energy between rot and ruin, fungus and fossil, machines and marrow, the Bone Orchard and the Cathedral Cavern dream together in the deep, dead dark.
