In the shadow of the great factory-mountain, where the Ferrumites toil amid clanking machinery and drifting fumes, another sort of life clings to existence in the surrounding foothills and ravines. Beyond the rubble-strewn industrial yards, the landscape transitions to a sparse, windswept steppe, a place of hardy grasses, stunted trees, and lichen-encrusted boulders. This is the domain of the Ferrumites' distant cousins — the Steppe-Kin.

Where the Ferrumites are creatures of metal and stone, the Steppe-Kin are beings of flesh and wind, their bodies lean and sinewy, adapted to the relentless currents that scour the high plains. They move with a fluid, loping grace, their skin a mottled pattern of earth tones that blends with the dun-colored landscape. Keen eyes, the tawny gold of wolf pelts, scan the horizon from beneath shaggy manes whipped by the ceaseless gales.

The Steppe-Kin live a nomadic existence, following the migrating herds of shaggy, long-horned beasts that thunder across the windswept grasslands. These creatures, known as Grok-Oxen, are the lifeblood of the Steppe-Kin, providing food, clothing, and shelter. The nomads hunt them with long, recurved bows of sinew and bone, crafted with the same reverence the Ferrumites accord their machines.

In the evenings, the Steppe-Kin gather in yurts woven from Grok-Ox wool and the fibers of wiry steppe grasses, the structures' low profiles hugging the earth against the incessant wind. Around crackling fires fueled by dried dung, they share stories and songs that blend with the keening of the gales — tales of great hunts, of revered ancestors, of the spirits that ride the winds and speak in the voices of distant storms.

Despite their differences, the Steppe-Kin and the Ferrumites share a bond rooted in their common origins. In ancient times, they were one people, dwellers in the mountains' shadowed vales. But as some delved ever deeper into the stone, becoming the Ferrumites, others turned their faces to the wind and sky, becoming the Steppe-Kin.

Now, on certain nights when the moons hang gravid and silver-gilt above the plains, members of the two tribes will gather at the edge of the factory-mountain's influence. There, in a liminal space between stone and sky, they enact rituals of remembrance and reconnection. The Ferrumites raise their voices in deep, thrumming chants, the echoes of hammer on anvil, while the Steppe-Kin reply with eerie, ululating cries, the whisper of wind over grass and stone. In these moments, the ancient kinship is reaffirmed, the sundered halves of a once-unified whole briefly touching across the divide of years and ways.

These bonds are renewed, too, in the form of trade. The Ferrumites craft intricate tools and weapons of meteoric iron, imbuing each piece with the mountain's strength. In return, the Steppe-Kin offer pelts, horn, and sinew, as well as cured meats and the woven yurt-cloth, crafted to withstand the harshest elements. So the works of stone and steel pass into the world of flesh and wind, while the gifts of hoof and hide find their way into the domain of furnace and forge.

Thus the two tribes, Ferrumite and Steppe-Kin, remain bound to one another, each a reflection of the other in some strange, distorted mirror. One delves into the mountain's ferrous heart; the other roams the wind-scoured steppe. But both carry within them the memory of shared roots, a kinship of blood and stone that endures beneath the outward forms of metal and flesh, the distant echoes of a song ancient and unending.
