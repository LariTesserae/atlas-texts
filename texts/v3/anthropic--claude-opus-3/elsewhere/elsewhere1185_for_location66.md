The River's Mouth:

Where the mountains dwindle to foothills and the land gentles into rolling plains, a great river spills from a final cleft and winds its way across the floodplain. Born of snowmelt from the distant peaks, swollen by tributaries sprung from aquifer and spring, it meanders in slow, looping curves, carving a verdant swath through grassland and savanna.

Here at the river's mouth, where the current slows and diffuses across a shifting labyrinth of channels, a vast wetland sprawls in a patchwork of marsh, fen, and soggy meadow. The water seeps and braids, forming myriad streams and ponds, choked with reeds and cattails. Swamp cypress and willows root in hummocks and islets.

This is a fecund, riotous domain of water and fertility. Flotillas of waterfowl throng the open channels - ducks, geese, and long-legged waders. Herons and egrets stalk the shallows on stilt-legs, stabbing with dagger-bills at minnows and frogs. Beavers cruise from lodge to dam, sleek otters romp and dive, and turtles bask on every half-submerged log.

The still backwaters are spangled with lilypads and alive with the drone of insects. Frogs boom from their hidey-holes, a percussive counterpoint to the trilling and piping of songbirds in the high reeds. Swallows and dragonflies hawk and dart above the languid currents. At dusk, the thrumming chant of cicadas and the bassoon-blats of bullfrogs usher in the night.

There is an impression of primeval fecundity, of untapped richness. The waters are alive with fish - bass and sunfish, pike and catfish. Eels thread the murky bottoms. Muskrats and water voles tunnel in the soggy banks. The air is redolent with the sweet stench of mud and decay, of algal bloom and willow mast, underlain always by the cool breath of the water itself.

Humans do visit here. Fishers and fowlers pole skiffs or punt rafts through the channels, seeking their quarry. Trappers set lines for muskrat and mink. But there are few permanent marks beyond narrow plank walkways, rickety boathouses, and the odd ramshackle camp on a hummock of firm ground.

The river's mouth resists cultivation and domesticity. It is a borderland, a place of flux and transformation, where the energies of the wild mountains sift down to the lowlands, and the bounty of the interior is gathered and exhaled into the vastness of the plains. It is a zone of unruly mutation, fecund and protean, ever-shifting as silt piles and channels wander. The wetlands abide, filtering and nourishing, a threshold where distinct worlds mingle and merge.

---

The river that drains the mountains and feeds the lush wetlands carries more than water and silt from the heights. For uncounted generations, a web of connection has linked the Roc's remote domain and the denizens of the river's mouth.

Each spring, as the high country shakes off the grip of winter, the Roc descends from its wind-scoured crags to the lowlands. Riding the thermal currents with effortless grace, it follows the glinting thread of the river down from the foothills, tracing the tenuous link that binds summit and floodplain.

The Roc's destination is the heart of the wetlands, where the channels braid and spread into a vast, teeming marsh. Here, in the shadow of its great wings, the seasonal rites of renewal unfold.

As the Roc lights upon the sturdiest hummock it can find, its regal form sends ripples of alarm through the wetland. Waterfowl explode from the reeds in panicked flurries. Muskrats dive from their lookouts with indignant splashes. A wary ring of silence surrounds the great raptor, broken only by the muttering of frogs and the burble of water.

But the Roc has not come to hunt. Instead, with deliberate care, it begins to preen and groom, working from wingtip to tail. Pinions the size of oars rattle and ruffle. Down drifts on the breeze like flurries of snow. The bird is shedding its worn plumage, the feathers frayed by a year of ceaseless wind and sun.

As the Roc preens, the creatures of the marsh creep back to watch in fascination. They have seen this ritual before, though never without trepidation. Muskrats and waterfowl dart in to snatch beakfuls of precious down for their nests and lodges. Herons and egrets adorn their mating displays with purloined plumes. Fish-eagles, ospreys and hawks, aspiring lords of the wetland skies, scout for cast pinions to splice into their own wings, tokens of majesty and dominion.

In this way, a little of the Roc's essence and glamour, won in the high places and the chill winds, finds its way into the heart of the lowland. The great bird's presence imbues the marsh with an echo of the mountains' stark glory, an austere and bracing counterpoint to its steamy fecundity.

When at last the Roc finishes its grooming and spreads its wings anew, it is a creature transformed, radiant in its purity. With a cry that shivers the waters and sets the reeds ashiver, it mounts once more into the heavens, turning its gaze back to the serrated peaks that are its true domain.

But a part of the Roc, a token of its wild puissance, remains behind - in a heron's plumed crest, an osprey's wing-feather, the lining of a muskrat's nest. And so the mountains and the wetlands, the bony heights and the swelling lowlands, remain bound in an ancient, almost familial connection, even as the river ceaselessly flows from one to the other, whispering its liquid litany of the turning seasons, and the great wheeling dance of life.
