In the dim light, the landscape reveals itself gradually — a vast, shallow marsh stretching to the horizon, interrupted only by the silhouettes of primitive structures rising from the still waters. These buildings, if one can call them that, are woven together from the ubiquitous reeds and grasses that sprout from the marsh bed, lashed together with vines and sealed with mud. Some take the form of huts on stilts, others are low, sprawling platforms barely above the waterline. None stand taller than a person.

The denizens of this strange place move about their business, splashing through the shallows from structure to structure. They are a peculiar folk, small in stature with mottled green-gray skin that blends into the marsh. Oversized eyes glow softly in the gloom. Webbed hands and feet make them swift, silent swimmers. They communicate in a language of clicks, chirps and lilting calls that echo across the wetland.

The air hangs heavy and cool, saturated with moisture and alive with the sounds of the marsh. A constant chorus of amphibious creatures provides an undercurrent — trills, croaks, grunts and splashes. Larger animals bellow from the reeds. Strange insectile buzzing and clicking fills the air. The cacophony is punctuated now and then by the unearthly cries of the marsh-folk as they call to one another.

Everywhere, life teems. Fish dart through the shallows, scooped up in woven reed baskets by the agile locals. Eels slither through the grass. Crustaceans scuttle along the silty bottom. Overhead, iridescent dragonflies the size of hawks zip and hover while bat-like creatures with translucent wings flutter in pursuit of them. Bioluminescent amphibians cling to the structure posts, pulsing gently.

The ecosystem exists in a primeval balance, everything interwoven in a complex, dynamic web. The marsh-folk are part of this, building their lives in harmony with the ebb and flow of water, the cycles of their prey and predators. Their structures are extensions of the living marsh, grown and woven rather than built, recycled back into the environment as they outlive their usefulness to sprout anew later.

Time seems to move strangely here, the dim light unchanging, the ancient rhythms of life proceeding as they have for eons. The strange, simple lives of the marsh-folk are woven into the fabric of this place, as much a part of it as the reeds and the mud and the still, dark water. In the cool, moisture-laden air, their clicks and cries and splashing feet form a primal music, the song of this eerie, twilight world.
