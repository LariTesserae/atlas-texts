In this hidden, compressed world of stone and water, I imagine myself as one of the clay physicians. 

I am slight of stature, my spine curved from a life lived beneath low ceilings, my skin the color of diluted cream from years away from sunlight. My hands are broad and sensitive, perpetually damp, the nails thick and blunt from working with clay and mineral salves. 

I move through the narrow alleys with practiced ease, ducking instinctively under lintels, stepping surely over gutters in the dimness. The city's sounds are a second language to me - I can hear a developing clog in a drainage pipe from three chambers away, sense a loosened niche seal by the change in echo from the burial wall.

In my alcove, jars of soothing clay line the shelves, alongside dried skins of wall-milk and powdered shell creature for poultices. Lamps cluster close, providing a pocket of gentle warmth. Here I receive those suffering from joint pain exacerbated by the damp, from skin cracked and bleeding from too long working in the water. I smear clay and salves with a sure touch, wrap affected limbs in softened hide.

But my real work is preventative, not curative. I make rounds through the districts, listening at walls, trailing my fingers along moisture-beaded stone. I note where minerals build up too thickly, restricting flow; where a lip of clay has worn thin and may soon give way. I keep one of the city's rhythms, my feet falling in time with the endless music of the water.

At the central well, I confer in low tones with the other maintainers - the sluice-mother whose brow is creased from listening at cisterns; the pipe-mender with a dozen stone plugs hung at his belt; the old woman who was basin-keeper for forty years and can diagnose a silted channel from the taste of its damp on her tongue. Together we keep the balance, staving off disaster and decay through constant, quiet vigilance.

At day's end I ascend to my sleeping alcove, high above the wet floors. In the dark I whisper my fears to my lover - of a creeping numbness in my fingertips, of the deep ache in my joints that foretells advancing age. She takes my hands in hers, tracing the lines of my palms, and hums softly the song of the full cisterns until I slip into sleep.

I am one of the city's healers, not of flesh alone but of water and stone. My days are measured in adjustments, corrections, the minute and vital workings that keep our precarious world turning in its delicate balance. In this pale, close darkness, I have found my calling and my place. As the water flows through the veins of the city, so too does purpose flow through mine, constant and sustaining.
