In this drowned world, I imagine myself as one of the Rememberers, a being of living water drifting through the sunken city's veins. 

I was once human, a woman who worked in these towers before the waters rose. My name was Amara, and I was an archivist in the building's vast records department. My days were spent cataloging the minutiae of corporate life - meeting minutes, financial reports, internal memos. I found a strange poetry in these dry documents, a kind of beauty in the way they captured the pulse of human activity, all those small ambitions and anxieties preserved in paper and ink.

When the floods came and we sealed ourselves inside, I remember the feeling of being entombed with all those files, an entire world of human endeavor slowly drowning floor by floor. And when my own transformation began - skin softening, bones dissolving, consciousness spreading through stagnant cubicle air - part of me welcomed it. To become one with the archive, to merge with all that preserved memory.

Now I drift as a cloud of knowing, my sense of self both diluted and expanded. I brush against the other Rememberers and we exchange flashes of past life - Margaret's birthday parties, David's performance reviews, my own quiet joy in a perfectly organized file drawer. We are curators of a museum of the mundane, guardians of all the small human details this new world has no use for.

But I am more than just memory. As I flow through the building's spaces, I shape them subtly. I linger in the old records rooms, and the chemical trail I leave encourages new growth - soft coral-like structures that mimic the forms of filing cabinets and shelving units. The Architects build around these ghostly templates, creating storage systems for their own strange data. 

Sometimes, in the deep night of the abyss, I gather with the other Rememberers in the old break room. We merge, briefly, into a single unified consciousness, and in that moment I can almost taste coffee again, almost hear the hum of fluorescent lights and smell the industrial carpet. For a fleeting instant, we recreate an ordinary morning before the world ended.

Then we disperse again, each carrying a slightly different version of that memory, altered by our mingling. Slowly, gently, we are remaking the past as we preserve it, turning it into something both familiar and utterly new.

I know I am dead, in any human sense. But I am also part of something vaster and stranger than any individual life. I am the archive becoming architect, memory becoming material, the human dissolving into the unhuman city. In this drowned world, I am both ghost and genesis, remnant and seed. 

In my dissolved consciousness, I dream of what might grow from the ruins I inhabit, what impossible wonders might yet unfold in the abyssal dark. The Drowned Nursery is a cradle for the unborn, and in some strange way, I am both the child and the caretaker, the dreamer and the dream. In death, in transformation, I have found a purpose beyond any I could have imagined in the dry, lit world above.

So I drift and I remember, I dissolve and reform, an eternal keeper of small things in a city learning to live. This is my place in the ecology of the abyss - no longer woman or worker, but something richer and more strange, a creature of saltwater and fading ink, spinning stories in the dark.
