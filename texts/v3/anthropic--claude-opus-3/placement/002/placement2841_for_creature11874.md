In the world of the Spine City, I imagine myself as one of the attendants of the rock.

I am a quiet, observant presence, moving slowly and deliberately through the terraced dwellings and along the steep, switchbacked trails. My hands are roughened from years of pressing them against stone, learning its textures and temperatures, listening for the mountain's subtle shifts.

Each season, I walk the paths assigned to me, pausing often to lay my palm flat on a cliff face or a foundation wall. I tune my senses to the mountain's frequencies - the minute vibrations, the almost imperceptible changes in scent and moisture that might presage a fracture or a fall. This is a skill honed over a lifetime, passed down through generations of attendants.

When I'm not walking my routes, I can often be found sitting cross-legged on a plaza stone, eyes half-closed, composing short poems in my head about the quality of the light on the peaks or the sound of the wind through a narrow stair. In the evenings, I share these poems with my flue-kin around the fire, and they often respond with poems of their own, creating a gentle call-and-response that stretches into the night.

I wear the coat of an attendant who came before me, its hem embroidered with a pattern that marks my role. The coat is heavy on my shoulders, a reminder of the weight of my responsibility to the city and the mountain. When I walk, I feel the presence of those who wore it before, their attentiveness and patience woven into the felted wool.

In the winter, when the stairs are treacherous with ice, I carry a pouch of ash and sand to scatter for traction. In the summer, I bring a waterskin to share with those I pass. These small acts of care are as much a part of my work as my listening vigils.

I have never been quick to speak, but my silence has deepened over the years. I have learned to let the mountain's rhythms shape my own, to let my thoughts unfurl at the pace of geological time. When I do speak, it is softly and sparingly, my words worn smooth like river rocks.

On the occasions when I feel something amiss in the bones of the peak - a worrying resonance, a shift in the spring's song - I carry the knowledge to the city as carefully as I would carry an egg in my cupped palms. I deliver my concerns quietly to the other attendants and the elders of the affected flues. There is never panic, just a slow redistribution of households, a gentle emptying of dwellings, until the mountain settles back into its long sleep and the face holds fast.

I know that one day, when my legs can no longer carry me up the high paths and my hands have lost their preternatural sensitivity, I will be borne up to the sacred stone by my flue-kin. The lammergeiers will take me into the sky, and my bones will return to the ossuary below the hearth. There, I will become part of the city's memory, as solid and lasting as the bedrock itself.

Until then, I continue my patient walks, my listening vigils, my whispered poems. I am a small stone in the city's vast mosaic, a single note in its ancient, endless song. The mountain dreams its slow dream, and I keep watch, and the spine endures.
