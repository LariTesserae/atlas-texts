In this world of cold roots and quiet bargains, I would be one of the stone-listeners.

I would inhabit a small niche near the heart of the settlement, where the young black stones are still sharp and the channels newly cut. My alcove would be lined with woven reeds and hung with a heavy curtain of oiled leaves to trap the precious warmth of breath and body.

Each day, I would make my careful way through the damp passageways to the deepest parts of our root-city. There in the darkness, I would press my hands and feet against the cold stone teeth that pierce the earth. Closing my eyes, I would listen through my skin for the minute shifts and echoes that run through the bedrock.

The stones here are freshly fractured, their edges still learning to bear weight. A quiver in the wrong place could spell disaster for our fragile home. So I listen. I feel for the groan of settling sediment, the hair-thin crack widening under pressure, the shudder of water finding a new path through clay and gravel.

When I sense danger, I trace the omen back through the maze of channels and chambers. I whisper warnings to the root-keepers and the drip-counters. "Here," I tell them softly, "Near the western cistern. The stones are talking of letting go. Shore up the walls. Redirect the flow. Spread the weight."

They trust my words, these others who maintain the city's balance. They know I have the stone-listener's gift, the ability to hear the earth's intentions before they manifest in flood or collapse. It is a heavy knowledge, feeling the very foundations of our world shift beneath us. But it is a proud one too, to know my quiet work keeps us all alive another season.

In spare moments, I carve small objects from the stone flakes I gather. A shallow bowl, a little figurine, a smooth oval to warm in the hands. I trade these for morsels of smoked eel, peat-tubers, or a fingerful of precious salt. Sometimes I gift them to the anxious, the grieving, or the sick, imbuing them with a bit of the bedrock's patience and strength.

At night, I drowse in my reed-lined nook, lulled by the slow breathing of the city. The drip of distant water. The click of beetle feet in the wall. The almost inaudible flex and sigh of the great roots surrounding us. Beneath it all, the deep slow pulse of the stone itself, bearing us up from the secret earth.

I dream of the day the city finally settles. The day the stones sigh into silence, their edges worn smooth, the channels of our world locked into place. I dream of listening and hearing only the whisper of the white frogs, the ripple of the black pool, and the contented root-songs of my people, held safe at last in the stone's dark palm.

Until then, I keep my vigil. The stone-listener, the earth-whisperer, reading the future in the bones of the mountain we call home.
