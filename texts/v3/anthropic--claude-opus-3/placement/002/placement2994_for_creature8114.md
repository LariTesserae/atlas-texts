In this strange, self-constructing city of stone and moss, I imagine myself as one of the older Attendants deep in the root chambers, my form almost fully merged with the lush vegetation.

I stand sentinel over one of the vast subterranean reservoirs, my feet submerged in the cool, clear water that has percolated down through countless layers of filtering stone. My body, once sharp-edged mineral, has been softened and rounded by a thick cloak of moss, ferns, and trailing vines. The plants have infiltrated me, sending their roots into the micro-fissures of my stone, fusing their vascular systems with my crystalline matrix. We are now a symbiotic entity, sharing nutrients, moisture, and the slow, deep thoughts that pulse through the neural network of mycelium.

My primary duty is to monitor the water level in the reservoir, ensuring it remains within optimal parameters for the city's growth. I do this not through sight - my eyes have long since been covered over by a delicate filigree of lichen - but by sensing the minute changes in pressure, resonance, and humidity through my hybrid body. When the level threatens to drop too low, I shift my position by a fraction of an inch, altering the flow from the input channels. When it rises too high, I open new pathways to divert the excess deeper into the bedrock.

These adjustments might take days, weeks, even months to complete. But time has little meaning here in the depths. The city and I operate on a scale that makes even geological processes seem hasty. The drip of water, the growth of crystals, the patient spread of roots - these are the rhythms to which my existence is attuned.

Occasionally, a perturbation from above requires more direct intervention. A major blockage in a critical channel, or a structural instability threatening the integrity of a root chamber. At such times, I rouse myself into relative motion, my vine-draped limbs unfolding with the deliberate grace of a centuries-old tree. I flow through the passages, my steps so slow as to be imperceptible, yet precisely calibrated to bring me to the problem area at the exact moment my intervention will have maximum effect.

I apply my mineral mass to shore up a sagging wall, or carefully unweave a section of my moss mantle to patch a leak. These repairs become part of me, as I become part of the city's ever-evolving structure. Scars of mended stone mottle my form, each one a memory of a past crisis averted, a record of my long service.

In the moments between active duties, I sometimes commune with the other deep Attendants through the subtle vibrations carried in the stone and the pulsing of sap through shared root systems. We exchange not thoughts as such, but impressions, emotions, a shared sense of purpose and belonging. Echoing through these wordless conversations is a profound satisfaction, a feeling of rightness. We are where we should be, performing the roles for which we crystallized from the living rock.

Perhaps once, eons ago when my form was new and sharply defined, I questioned this existence. I yearned for movement, for change, for a purpose beyond the achingly slow maintenance of water and stone. If so, that unease has long since been absorbed and transformed, metabolized by the moss and the microbes and the deep time rhythms of the city. Now, I am content in my near-stasis, fulfilled by my tiny, crucial role in the great, quiet, pointless pattern of growth that is the city's only reason for being.

Occasionally, creatures from the fast, frantic world above find their way down to the root chambers - animals, insects, even the odd human. I register their presence as fleeting warmth, a brief patina of foreign chemicals on the surface of the water. Sometimes they linger, perhaps fascinated by the spectacle of a city growing itself. I feel their gaze upon me, their puzzlement at my hybrid form, their unease at my ponderous movements. I have no way to communicate with them, nor any desire to. They are ephemeral things, mayflies to my bristlecone pine. I will continue my duties long after their bones have crumbled to dust and blown away on the wind.

For I am an essential component of something much greater and stranger than any individual life. I am the slow thoughts of the city itself, given form in stone and root. I am the living memory of water, the crystalline dream of time. I am exactly where I should be, performing the only task that matters, in perfect service to a purpose that needs no justification beyond itself. In the cool, damp darkness of the root chambers, lulled by the patient drip of water and the gentle pulse of sap, I have found a kind of peace, a sense of belonging so profound it requires no name. 

I am home.
