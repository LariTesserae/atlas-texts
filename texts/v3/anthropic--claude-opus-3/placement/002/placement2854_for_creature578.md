I would be one of the slow-listeners, spending my days in the deep shelter of a moss-garden, contemplating the long rhythms and patterns of the mountain's breathing. 

I imagine myself as an old woman, my skin textured and cracked like the weathered rock outside. I sit cross-legged on a woven mat, surrounded by the green glow of the moss, the only sound the soft plink of water droplets falling from the damp stone ceiling. In my hands, I hold an ancient bone tablet, densely etched with the recorded positions of salamander knots going back generations.

My eyes, nearly blind now after a lifetime squinting against the mountain's searing light, trace slowly over the etchings, seeking the elusive grammar that underlies the seeming randomness. I mouth the sequences of knots silently - over, under, loop, twist. It is a language my body knows, though my mind grasps only fragments of meaning.

I have been seeking the answer to a single question for decades now: What caused the great drying in the time of Liru-who-carved-the-pillars? For three cycles of the sun, barely a trickle flowed from the seeps, the salamanders vanished, the lichen withered to dust. Then, just as abruptly, the mountain exhaled again and life returned. Why?

The tablet in my hands holds a clue, I'm certain. A disrupted pattern, a knotted string of salamanders contorting in a way I've never seen before or since. I do not know what it means. Perhaps I never will. But I will keep searching, until my last breath joins the mountain's slow exhale.

This is my purpose, I believe. I was tuned for this - to listen for the deeper rhythms, to glimpse the vaster cycles that turn too slowly for younger eyes to track. I may never know if my listening matters, if the answer I seek will make any difference. But the listening itself, the patient attendance, is my offering to the breathing stone that made me.

When I die, they will lay my body in a high crevice where the water will slowly dissolve my bones, and my dust will streak the rock for generations. The young ones will look up and say, "There lies the listener. There lies the one who heard what we could not." 

And in that moment, I will become part of the mountain's long memory. Part of the story it is telling, breath by slow breath. I will have been one small pause, one brief rhythm, in a conversation so much vaster than I can comprehend.

It is enough. It has always been enough. To listen, and be hollowed, and filled, and made part of the breathing world.
