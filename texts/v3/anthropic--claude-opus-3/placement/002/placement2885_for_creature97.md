In the world of the Long Shallows, I would be one of the Pacers.

I would stand for hours in the warm, knee-deep waters, becoming part of the landscape itself. My long, low body would be the color of wet reeds, blending into the tans and ochres of the marshland. Fish would gather in the shade beneath my belly, insects would alight on my flanks, and small birds would walk between my splayed toes, as if I were no more than another tree or hummock. 

When I moved, it would be with immense slowness and deliberation. Each step would lift carefully from the water, disturbing the surface as little as possible before settling into the ancient bronze-colored clay. I would have no need to hurry - no predators to flee, no prey to chase. My kind would have long ago forgotten urgency.

I would feed on the soft growth along the channel bottoms, browsing for minutes at a time with my head submerged. From a distance, only the still hump of my back would show above the waterline. My meals would not be hunts but slow, thorough harvests taken at the pace of the landscape itself.

My mind would be a vast, patient eye, trained by millennia of stillness to attend to the smallest details - a beetle's progress up a reed, the play of light on far-off trees, the movement of a single silver fish. I would remember everything - every channel bend, every hummock, every angle of shadow. The memory would live not just in my thoughts but in my bones, an accrued knowing vaster than recollection. 

When I spoke, it would be in slow, resonant hums that pulsed beneath the choruses of frogs and insects. Across the distances of the marsh, my hums would find the others of my loose constellation. We would converse in these deep thrummings, acknowledging presence, sharing states of being. Rarely would we gather, but when we did - to attend the slow sinking of our dead into the accepting mud - it would be with a ceremonial patience, an ancient knowing of the way of things.

My life would span perhaps a century, short only in comparison to the land itself. I would be the middle term between the quick, bright flares of the frogs and the ponderous enduring of the clay. In my slowness, in my long, thorough attention, I would be the consciousness of the marsh, the part of the landscape that knew itself. 

For what is a Pacer but the land itself grown aware? We would be what arose when the terrain, having made its mountains and rivers, had nothing more to shape than warm water, soft earth, and time. In our heft, our patience, our long stillness and deep memory, we would echo the marsh. To stand in the shallows, letting the hours pass, noticing everything and needing nothing, would be prayer and fulfillment all in one.

A Pacer I would be, and gladly - a living monument to the long, slow work of water and clay and light, born of the landscape's desire to know itself. In the Long Shallows I would abide, as much a part of that ancient, warm, patient world as the mud, the reeds, and the eternal braid of the channels under the heavy sun.
