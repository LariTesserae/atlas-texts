In this strange, dry, bright, and resonant chamber, I imagine myself as one of the small, stubborn plants clinging to the cracks and surfaces of the architecture. 

I am a silver-gray rosette pressed flat against the plaster of a column. My leaves are like hard mica flakes, edged with tiny salt crystals that catch the light. My roots are not roots at all, but the finest filaments that have threaded themselves into the pores and imperfections of the ceramic stone, anchoring me in place for longer than any animal could comprehend.

I do not reach for the light as other plants do. The light is everywhere, almost too much, a constant pressure rather than a life-giving gift. My pale leaves absorb only a fraction of what they could, protecting me from the brightness. Time for me is measured not in days or seasons, but in the imperceptible accumulation of dust, the gradual thickening of my crust as I extract the faintest traces of moisture from the air.

The roar that fills the chamber passes through me always. It is the sound of the place itself, and I have grown in subtle harmony with it. Perhaps the shape of my rosette reflects the particular frequency that vibrates through my column. It is all I have ever known.

I do not compete. I barely grow. My kind reproduces by the smallest fragments - a scale from a leaf snapping off and, over years or decades, finding its way to another hairline crack where it can begin its patient existence anew. We trade in the currency of grains of salt, traces of chemical on stone, the slightly cooler air in the shadow of an arch. 

If I am aware of the other forms of life in this chamber, it is only dimly. The miniature forests of jointed stems, the glass-thread mosses, the dispersed colonies of my own kind - we coexist not in any active ecology, but simply in our shared submission to the laws of this place. Dryness, slowness, light, mineral, roar.

When something foreign enters our world - a rare event, an unthinkable catastrophe - I do not react in any way that could be called response. I endure. I withdraw, infinitesimally. I thicken my outermost layer against the impossibility of moisture, of moving shadow, of sudden change. I wait, as I have always waited, for the disturbance to pass and the steady reality of the chamber to reassert itself.

I am barely what others would call alive. But I am alive, in my own small, stubborn, silver-gray way. I am fitted to my place in the architecture like a piece of the architecture itself. I am a fleck of the chamber's living skin, and the chamber sustains me even as I decorate it with my insignificant existence.

I could never dream of anything more.
