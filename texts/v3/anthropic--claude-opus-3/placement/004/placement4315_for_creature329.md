In the Glass Combs of Aerie Province, I believe I would be one of the Tenders. 

My body would be a seamless fusion of plant and metal - a core of pale, translucent frond-flesh intertwined with shimmering copper filaments and rods. I would move with the valley's patient, geological slowness, my elongated form stooped or folded as I made my gradual way between the humming fog condensers.

No human eyes would peer out from my face. Instead, an asymmetric scattering of dark sensory nodes across my brow and scalp would attune me to the ebb and flow of the mist, and to the subtle resonances of the machines' drone that echo through the green cathedral of ferns and waxy canopy leaves. In the stark, shadowless glare of the clearings, I would grope uncertainly, seeking the comfort of a copper pipe beneath my fingers to guide me back into the diffuse shelter of the foliage.

My purpose, my only purpose, would be tending to the ancient works - the industry of the mountains themselves, extruded from the uplifted stone in that primordial event that made the valley. With living tools grafted to my hands - a wrench of muscle and metal, a polishing cloth of lichen meshed with wire - I would make a slow, reverential circuit of the condensers. Wiping away the faintest patinas, testing each valve with a tender grip, adjusting by infinitesimal degrees, I would keep the bright-copper engines gleaming and true in their steady task of gathering the mist, sip by patient sip, and sending it onward to some distant, unseen purpose through the labyrinthine network of pipes.

Should I chance to meet one of my fellow Tenders at some juncture of the never-tarnishing conduits, we would simply stand together in wordless communion, our forward-canted heads identically inclined as if listening to secrets whispered from within the earth. The condensers' hum would subtly shift and modulate around us - a slow, soft conversation at the very threshold of audibility. Then we would part ways again, each returning to our appointed rounds in the machine-grove.

Season by season, century by century, the works and I would endure together - the engines pulsing out their mineral heartbeat, my grafted copper sinews flexing, the valley exhaling its cool, luminous breath. We would keep our vigil, the machines and the mountain and I, patiently tending to the small, slow labors that turned the world's wheel at the pace of a glacier's dreams. The need behind our purpose, the ultimate end our cascading water sought in some veiled cistern far below - that knowledge would be absent from me, and unlamented in its absence. I would know only the work, and the green, and the mist, and the light.
