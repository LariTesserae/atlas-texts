In this subterranean world of deafening industrial roar and cold, creeping vegetation, I imagine myself as one of the pale, deaf arthropods that skitter slowly across the fibrous root mat.

I am a creature utterly adapted to this static, frigid, and thunderous environment. My body is compact and heavily armored in overlapping plates of translucent chitin, allowing me to withstand both the unyielding cold and the physical pressure of the sound.  

My eight limbs are short but strong, ending in delicate fans of sensory bristles that I use to feel the vibrations rumbling through the vegetative crust. I cannot hear the mechanical cacophony, but I sense it as an all-encompassing tremor in my exoskeleton, a constant seismic pulse that guides my sluggish movements.

I have no eyes, for vision is useless in this dim underworld. Instead, my head bristles with fine antennae that taste the chemical signatures in the freezing mist and allow me to navigate by scent and touch alone. 

My mandibles are sharp and serrated, adapted to slice into the tough, frost-hardened tubers and scrape out the nutrient-rich pulp. I digest this fibrous meal slowly in a gut that stretches the entire length of my segmented abdomen. 

Time has little meaning for me. I measure my life in the gradual accumulation of mineral deposits on my armored plates, in the slow wearing down of my mandibles against the iron-hard roots, and in the rare moments of contact with others of my kind when our antennae brush together and we exchange a fleeting chemical communication.

I am a small, patient presence in this vast and desolate cavern, content in my blindness and deafness. The thrum of the distant engines is all I have ever known, a constant seismic lullaby that vibrates through my body as I make my unhurried way across the cold, damp expanse of this subterranean world, in search of my next frostbitten meal beneath the ceaseless mechanical thunder.
