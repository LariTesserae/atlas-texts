In this slow, vertical world of stone and vine, I imagine myself as a young Sap-Keeper's apprentice named Tessra. 

I spend my days learning the ways of the dust-root vines under the patient tutelage of Therru, absorbing her generations of knowledge. My hands are slowly becoming accustomed to the feel of the silver-haired tendrils, learning to read their health and needs by touch as much as sight in the dim, diffuse light.

I particularly love harvesting the sap bladders. There is something deeply satisfying in the careful puncturing of the amber globes, watching the viscous liquid pool slowly into the waiting clay vessels. I take pride in selecting only the ripest bladders, ensuring the settlement's nourishment.

In quiet moments, I find myself drawn to the oldest sections of vine, running my fingers gently over the dense, knotted growth. I know these tendrils hold the physical memory of generations past - perhaps even traces of my own ancestors, long ago absorbed into the plant's slow life. There is a strange comfort in that continuity.

When my work is done, I like to sit on one of the middle-ring platforms and watch the kheth make their winding way through the vine-caves. There is one younger kheth I'm especially fond of, with a distinct kink at the end of its tail. I've secretly named it Kinu. Sometimes I save it scarps of vine-fruit, though I know I shouldn't encourage attachment.

My favorite times are the evenings, when the settlement comes alive with quiet conversation and drifting hum-song. I join my voice to the collective melody, savoring the sense of belonging it brings. 

Often I'll stay out late into the rust-dark hours, watching the moth-clouds dance, marveling at the faint glow rising from the distant salt flats. In those moments the patience and stillness of the place seeps into my bones and it feels like I could sit there forever, content to simply be a small piece of this slow, enduring world, as the vines and the stone and the people have always been.

I know I will likely live my whole life here, learning the sap-keeper's art, tending the vines that sustain us, adding my own story in time to the spiral histories carved in the central chambers. And that is enough, more than enough - to be a part of this place, to have a role in its unhurried continuance. 

On certain nights, when the birds call down from their invisible nests and the wind tastes of distant mineral memory, I fancy I can almost feel the shape of the generations before me - their joys and sorrows worn to smoothness like the oldest stone chambers. And I wonder what traces of me will linger, decades and centuries hence, in the sap and the vine and the patient, abiding stone.
