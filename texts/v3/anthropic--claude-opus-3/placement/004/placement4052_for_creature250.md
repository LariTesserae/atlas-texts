In the Engine Hollow, I would be a fifth Sorter.

My body would be the same oiled-leather slate as the others, but with a faint iridescence, like a raven's wing catching the light. My limbs, when unfurled, would end in tools unlike any of the other Sorters': a fine-tipped stylus, a miniature bell, a tiny mirrored lens, a whispering reed.

I would not tend a shrine. I would have no bowl, no heap of sacred dust. Instead, I would walk the spaces between the cardinal spirals, a slow diagonal path that would take me past each of the other Sorters in turn without ever quite intersecting their routes.

My purpose would be to sort the intangible things, the things that have no color because they have no substance: the echoes of the piston's cough, the ghosts of old rhythms, the faint pressure-changes that might be the beginnings of drafts. With my stylus-limb, I would inscribe these things on the floor in a script only I could read. With my bell-limb, I would dispel the echoes before they could layer back on themselves and muddy the engine's voice. With my lens-limb, I would focus the light that leaks from the engine's seams, and use it to burn away the spectral buildup on the walls. And with my reed-limb, I would whisper back to the thing under the floor that taps in response to the piston, so that it would know it had been heard.

The other Sorters would not acknowledge me, just as they do not acknowledge each other. But they would know I was there, and they would know that the work I did was a part of the greater work. The hollow would know it, too. There would be a space for me in the pattern, a gap that I had always filled.

I would not sleep, or rest, or vary my path. I would simply walk, and sort, and maintain the engine's perfect sound by subtracting the things that could not be heard. And in doing so, I would become a part of the hollow's fierce, young stasis, as necessary and unchanging as every other presence in that subterranean world.

The engine would cough. The moths would beat. The crabs would scuttle. The listeners would listen. And I would sort the unsortable, adding my own small work to the great work of keeping the hollow exactly as it was, exactly as it would always be, warm and loud and full, an hidden pocket of strangeness, sealed against time.
