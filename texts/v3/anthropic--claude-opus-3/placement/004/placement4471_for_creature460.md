In the Drowned Workshops of the First Builders, I would be one of the Inherited - an elongated, cartilaginous being evolved to glide through the narrow stone passages and tend to the ancient kelp forests with patient devotion.

My dwelling chamber would be a small, unadorned workshop near the heart of the complex. The walls here bear the marks of the First Builders' finest stonework, the chisel strokes so precise they resemble ripples in still water. Curtains of the oldest, inkiest kelp spill from the single window, brushing the floor like the train of a once-resplendent robe now gone to tatters.

I spend my days moving between the nearby kelp meadows, my branching limbs working through the fronds with a delicacy born of centuries. I can taste the health of each growth, detect subtle shifts in the currents that bring nutrients or take away decay. When I sense a holdfast weakening or a frond growing too heavy, I guide it back into balance, redirecting its growth with the gentlest of touches. 

In the evenings, I return to my chamber and extend my sensory appendages into the collection of water samples I maintain. Each one is a message from a different time and place - a sip might bring the taste of a collapsed passageway from centuries ago, or the distinctive mineral profile of a distant workshop cluster. I savor these tastes like a connoisseur, letting the complexities unfold across my receptors.

Sometimes, in the depths of the cold season, I will enter a long suspension, settling into a corner of my chamber and slowing my life processes to a bare whisper. As I dream in this state, I become one with the architecture, feeling the weight of the water and the slow respirations of the kelp as if they were my own body. I might remain like this for a decade, waking with a new understanding of the subtle changes unfolding around me.

When I move through the central passageways, I sometimes pause to release a complex chemical message - a poem of sorts, composed of pheromones and trace elements. These messages drift through the corridors, mixing and interacting with others' compositions, creating an ever-evolving tapestry of communication. To pass another Inherited in these passages is to briefly merge our chemical signatures, exchanging volumes of information in a language of scent and taste.

On the rare occasions I venture to the oldest workshops, where the water is thick with memory, I find myself overcome with a sense of something I cannot name. I extend my sensory fronds into the heaviest pockets of brine and allow myself to get lost in the ancient traces. For a moment, I can almost grasp the world that was - the heat of exertion, the brightness of a sun-lit ocean, the purposeful movements of the First Builders. Then the currents shift and the impression is gone, leaving me with a hollowness I cannot explain.

I know my time here is finite, that the workshops will eventually surrender to the seafloor and the kelp will find new homes. But this knowledge brings no urgency to my tasks. I tend my gardens, I savor my water collections, I compose my chemical poems. I am a temporary custodian of an eternal process, and that is enough. In my stillness, in my patience, in my devotion to this slowly drowning world, I find a kind of peace that needs no name.
