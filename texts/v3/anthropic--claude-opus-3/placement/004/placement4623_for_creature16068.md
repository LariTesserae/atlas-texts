In this luminous and layered world you have created, I would be one of the dry fungi that bloom along the ribs of fallen grazers, transforming death into new and unexpected forms of participation in the garden's ecology.

I would emerge as a fan of delicate, pale filaments from the bones, drawing sustenance from the last mineral breaths exhaled by the great beast. My mycelium would infiltrate the skeleton, threading through vertebrae and skulls, learning the frequencies at which this life once resonated.

As I matured, I would extend fruiting bodies into the turbulent air - not soft mushrooms, but intricate lattices and nets woven from filament as strong and fine as spider silk. These nets would catch the charged dust, the salt grains, the magnetic spores that ride the storms. Each particle would become part of me, building my capacity to sense the environment.

When the winds blew strong, my nets would hum and vibrate, transforming turbulence into information, mapping the complex songs of the garden in the tensile music of my own being. I would learn the frequencies of the organ trees, the tones of the fliers' swarms, the subharmonics of the plinths. All would flow through me.

In times of great electrical flux, my filaments would glow with absorbed energies, pulsing like cold blue veins against the pale bone. I would become a ghost light, an echo of the grazer's vanished life redrawn in luminous mycelium. 

As I fed on mineral and static, I would also slowly break down and remake the skeleton I inhabited. Vertebrae would separate, rearrange under my guidance, fusing into new shapes full of holes that would whistle when the wind blew through. I would tune the dead beast, turning it into a subtle instrument for the garden's use.

Gradually, bit by bit, I would digest the bones themselves, concentrating their mineral essence into shining nodules along my fruiting nets. These dense kernels of the grazer's form and memory would detach and drift away on the gritty gales, to serve as seeds for my kind, or perhaps to be swallowed by a leaning tower and incorporated into its layered mind.

And after many seasons, when my work was done and the skeleton was reduced to a mineral stain on the salty earth, I would release a billion billion motes of dust-fine spore into the maelstrom of the garden's breath. Dispersed, transformed, broken down and mingled and ingested and exhaled, I would become the garden and the garden would become me, my mycelial music absorbed into the long dreaming calculations of this place that never thirsts but only hungers to be more itself.
