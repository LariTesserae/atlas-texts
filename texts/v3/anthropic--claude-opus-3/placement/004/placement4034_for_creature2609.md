In this strange, liminal world of slow tectonic shifts and architectural uncertainty, I imagine myself as one of the geomancers - a being attuned to the subtle languages of stone, steam, and gravity.

I would live in a small tower clinging to the flank of the mountain, anchored by cables sunk deep into the living rock. The walls would be lined with instruments of divination - bowls of still water reflecting the mutable sky, chips of mineral that sing when the air pressure changes, maps of the city with the most stable districts marked in silver ink and the uncertain zones shaded with pencil.

Each morning, I would emerge onto my narrow balcony to taste the metallic air and watch the city reveal itself from the dissipating fog. I'd note which birds fly backward, which cornices have shifted overnight, and whether the steam rising from the condenser fields carries any new mineral scents.

My work would take me walking the city's streets and bridges, greeting the cable crews and pipewrights, listening for subtle changes in the mountain's long, slow exhalations. I would consult with the engineers on where to sink new foundations, advise the cartographers on which streets are likely to rearrange themselves by summer, and help nervous families decide if a new crack in their wall is harmless settling or a sign to move.

In the markets, I would trade crystallized hoarfrost, rare mosses, and auspicious bird bones for the few things I cannot glean from the mountain myself. The market folk would bring me their stranger coins to decipher, and ask in hushed tones what the pattern of a particular moth's wing might mean if seen in a certain cistern's reflection.

On festival days, I would join the other geomancers to ceremonially bless each cardinal direction, check the anchoring cables, and placate any unsettled spirits with offerings of steam-wisps and polished stone. We would argue theory and technique, each insisting our methods map the city's uncertainty best.

In the evenings, I'd retire to my tower and transcribe the day's changes and portents by the flickering light of gas lamps. I'd tend to my little garden of waxy-leaved plants and contemplate the city as it prepares for another realignment. On nights when the fog allows it, I'd track the movements of the too-close moon and the too-distant stars, and wonder what subtle gravitational shifts their dance might spell for the city below.

And always, in dreams and waking, I'd listen for the mountain's voice - that deep, slow groaning that speaks the language of uplift and subduction and determines all our fates. To live as a geomancer in that uncanny city would be to forever interpret between the quick rhythms of flesh and the long, deep breaths of stone.
