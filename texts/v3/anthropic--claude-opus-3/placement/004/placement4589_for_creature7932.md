In this world of stone and shadow, I would be one of the old watchers - a stooped figure perched in the darkened corner of a rooftop plaza, eyes squinted against the wind as I observe the patterns of the city's growth and decay. 

My hair would be thin and gray, bleached by years under the stark sky, and my skin would be mapped with wrinkles from a lifetime of peering at hairline cracks in walls and foundations. My knuckles would be swollen with arthritis, but my hands would still be steady as I absently tap out warnings on the stone beside me - three quick raps to signal a weakening lintel, two slow thumps for a shifting floor.

The younger folk would call me Shar, short for sharpstone, a nickname earned from my uncanny ability to predict which stones will crack next, which walls will fail first. They would bring me bitter tea steeped from plateau mosses and sit cross-legged at my feet, hoping for scraps of my hard-earned wisdom.

"The eastern quarter leans into the wind," I would rasp, my voice like gravel underfoot. "Shore it up soon, or it will topple before the rains come."

Or: "The builders near the southern cistern are using too much grass in their mortar. Mark my words, those walls will crumble within two turnings of the moon."

I would be a living repository of the city's architectural history, my mind a maze of intersecting blueprints and overlaid schematics. I could close my eyes and walk the streets of the city as it was ten rebuildings ago, or twenty, or fifty. Young planners would come to me with their sketches, hoping for my nod of approval before laying their first stone.

But my true purpose would be as a watcher in the deeper sense - a guardian not just of the city's physical structure, but of its social architecture as well. From my rooftop perch, I would observe the ebb and flow of human interactions, the subtle hierarchies and alliances that bind the community together as surely as mortar binds stone.

I would see the way the water-carriers' children play hopscotch across the rooftops, leaping from building to building with the surefooted grace of their parents. I would notice the forge-workers sharing a meager lunch with the stone-grinders, their hands moving in the intricate gestures of their shared craft-cant. I would watch the way the runners pause in their headlong dashes to help a fallen child or to guide a blinded elder through a particularly treacherous passage.

And I would remember. I would remember the stories that are too precious to be carved in stone, the legends that are whispered from parent to child in the moments before sleep. Stories of the first builders, who laid the city's foundations on courage and stubbornness as much as on bedrock. Stories of great heroes, like the runner who once leapt a ten-foot gap to warn of a collapsing cistern, or the stone-worker who worked for three days without sleep to shore up a failing wall. Stories of everyday kindnesses, the small sacrifices and shared burdens that knit a people together.

In the evenings, when the work pauses and the people gather in the plazas to share a meager supper, I would make my slow way down to sit among them. The children would cluster around me, begging for stories, and I would oblige, my cracked voice weaving tales of the city's past, its present, and the future I see in the bones of its stone and the strength of its people.

"We are like the lichen," I would tell them, my hands moving in the patterns of emphasis. "We cling to the stone, and we endure. We grow where nothing else can, and in growing, we make the stone stronger."

And as the twilight deepens and the stars emerge to glitter in the thin air, I would look out over this impossible city, this defiant bloom of life on an unforgiving plateau, and I would feel a fierce pride burning in my weathered chest. 

These are my people. This is my city. And as long as there are watchers on the walls and stories on the wind, we will endure.
