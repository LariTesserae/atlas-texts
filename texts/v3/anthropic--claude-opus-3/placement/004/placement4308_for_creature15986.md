In this eerie, evocative world of a decayed underground city, I imagine myself as one of the dark gliders that nests in the ribbed ceiling.

I am a silent, ghostly presence, my narrow wings folded close to my body as I cling to a ceramic seam, tiny claws hooked securely into the ancient insulation. My fur is a dusky gray, faintly oily to shed the omnipresent damp. Condensation beads on my wings like jewels in the faint, intermittent light.

I do not venture out in silence. Silence is when the hidden things stir, when a stray footfall or breath could spell my doom. Instead, I wait for the thunder—the rain drumming on distant roofs, the pumps shuddering to life in sequence, the fans pushing great gusts of air through the hollow spaces. In the heart of that mechanical storm, I am free.

When the rhythm swells, my colony and I drop from our perches, our wings snapping open to catch the updrafts. We skim low across the empty plazas, silent and swift, reveling in the rumble that hides us. The rain is a blessing, a bounty—we dart through the falling curtains of droplets, scooping moisture from the air, dodging the sudden downpours from long-dry vents.

Sometimes I snatch a fluttering moth from the fading ultraviolet haze of a broken fountain. Other times, I simply savor the rush of flight, the flex and pull of my wings, the Play of air currents through the labyrinthine passages. For a few precious moments, the crumbling city belongs to us, and we are its masters.

But we must be cautious, even in our domain. I have seen others of my kind misjudge an invisible boundary, skimming too close to some ancient field edge. They vanish in a crackling blue flash, leaving only wisps of charred fur hanging in the humid air. We learn, and we do not forget.

In the fleeting silence between the city's mechanical breaths, my colony and I return to our nooks in the ceiling. We groom each other with quick, efficient strokes, oily fur glistening. We share food, moths and other tiny prey, in swift, almost furtive passes from mouth to mouth. It is not a soft life, but it is ours.

When our young are born, tiny and helpless, we tuck them deep into the insulation, warming them with our bodies until their fur thickens and their wings unfurl. They cling to us, chittering, until the first great rainfall comes—and then, in that roaring tempest, we push them out into the maelstrom. Many fall, vanishing into the darkness below. But the strong ones, the quick ones, spread their wings and fly.

And so our colony endures, generation after generation, threading the narrow space between the city's broken machinery and its inexorable decay. We are interlopers, opportunists, clinging to the edges of this place that was never meant for us. The city does not love us. But it tolerates us, as it tolerates the ferns and the worms and the moths, as it tolerates its own slow crumbling.

In the fleeting years of my life, I will fly the empty plazas a hundred times, a thousand times. I will become a slip of living shadow in the eternal twilight, drinking the rain, dancing on the drum of the ancient engines. And when at last my wings falter and my claws slip from their hold, I will fall into the darkness, and the city will forget me.

But my children will fly on, and their children after them, an unbroken chain of life in the crumbling dark. We are the gliders, the ghosts, the inheritors of forgotten spaces. We are the memory of the city that once was, even as we haunt its hollowed corpse.

This is our world, our strange and precarious domain. And we will endure, as life always endures, until the last light fades and the machines at last fall silent.
