In this world of staggering extremes, I would find myself as one of the pale, slug-like invertebrates clinging to the sweating, freezing pipes. 

I am not built for the blinding glare and blistering heat of the open plateau. My flesh is soft, translucent, filled with antifreeze proteins that allow me to press my body directly against the brutal industrial cold of the conduits. While the armored reptiles below churn endlessly from shadow to precious shadow, I remain still, feeling the thrum of the coolant through my boneless, gelatinous form.

My world is small, confined to a few dozen yards of pipeline. I spend my days slowly grazing on the delicate strands of chemo-autotrophic lichen that cling to the seams and joints of the metal. The lichen draws its energy from the trace chemicals weeping from the aging gaskets; I, in turn, draw my sustenance from the lichen. It is a simple, glacial existence, one dictated entirely by the slow, automated rhythms of the ancient refrigeration system.

As I feed, I excrete. My waste trickles down the curved sides of the pipeline, dripping into fat, heavy droplets that shimmer with dissolved nutrients. Far below me, I can sense rather than hear the seething desperation as the drops fall into the armored herd. I am only dimly aware of the greater ecosystem - to me, the reptiles and the insects are just a distant, pulsing heat signature, an alien and perilous world. 

My chief concern is the gliders. In the blazing sky above, I can feel their papery forms slicing through the thermals, can sense the ripples in the air as they trawl the plateaus. I have no eyes to see them, but my skin is embedded with touch-receptors that can read the faint pressure changes when a glider passes overhead. If the shadow of a predator falls across the pipeline, I have but one defense: I go utterly still, pressing my slug-flesh against the metal, trying to vanish into the cold. I do not fear the hooks and trailing tentacles - I fear being torn from my delicate, frozen world and carried into the terrible heat above.

Yet even as I cower from the gliders, I am the unwitting keystone of their ecosystem. The nutrients in my waste, in the precious moisture I drip down upon the herd, are what sustain the prey population. In this world, I am an unlikely conduit, a humble link in the brutal, elegant choreography of survival. I am both profoundly vulnerable and indispensable. 

So I press close to my pipeline, to my thin ribbon of lichen, and I feel the thrum of the ancient coolant in my bones. I feed, I excrete, I shiver motionless when the shadow of a glider darkens my small, frigid world. It is an existence pared down to the essentials - cold, sustenance, stillness. In the face of the blistering heat and the anvil-glare of the sun, I have found my niche in the precious cold, clinging to a forgotten machine at the roof of the world. I am a strange, soft creature wedded to an industrial artifact, both of us bound in an automated dance of necessity. In the thin, searing air of the high plateaus, it is enough to simply be - to feel the cold, to taste the metal, to drip pale sustenance down upon the desperate multitudes below.
