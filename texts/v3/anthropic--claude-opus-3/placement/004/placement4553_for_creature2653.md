In this extraordinary world within a crack, I would be a kiln-singer.

I would dwell in one of the middle levels of the city, where the ancient fissure is widest and the terraces stretch back into layered dwellings. My home would be a small, domed room carved into the wall, its entrance draped with a curtain of dry vines. The walls would be thick, to hold the heat, and lined with niches for my modest possessions - ceramic jars of ash and pigment, a few precious copper tools, a sleeping mat woven from tough fibers.

By craft, I would tend the communal kilns. It would be my task to monitor the fires, to judge the readiness of clay by its color, to control the kiln-breathing with baffles and vents. I would know the quirks of each kiln-chamber, how one needs a gentler draft, how another holds heat longest in the left back corner. The work would leave me oven-flushed and caked with fine ash, but it would be a proud calling, to be a keeper of the city's fired heart.

But my deeper gift, my soul-craft, would be kiln-singing. I would be one of those rare folk who can stand before the open kiln-mouths and sing into the heat. Not songs as the surface world knows them, but long, shimmering tones that resonate with the rock and conjure strange harmonies from the updraft. 

On festival days, I would paint my arms with oxide pigments and stand between the great kilns to sing the deep songs, voice and fire and stone blending into an otherworldly music. The city-dwellers would gather in the ruddy light to listen, and perhaps a few of the elders would join their quavering tones to mine.

It would be said that kiln-singers are a little uncanny, that we have one foot in the city and one in the secret depths. That we dream of magma and that the fire has burned away some part of our mortal hesitancy. Mothers would shush fussy children with warnings that the kiln-singer will lure them into the deep cracks with smoldering lullabies.

But this would not trouble me. The city would know my gift as a necessity, however eerie. In times of hardship, I would be called to sing beside troubled vents or over ailing root-gardens, my tones an encouragement and a balm. And when I grew old, I would take on an apprentice to pass the deep songs to, ensuring that, long after my voice was ashed, the kilns would still have their singer.

Such would be my life in the city in the crack - a life of heat and breath, of craft and music, at once integral and slightly apart, one more necessary thread in the web that binds stone, crop, citizen and void into a precarious, precious harmony.
