In this world of the plateau, I would be one of the walking forms - a quiet, mossy being that moves carefully through the gullies and service passages, tending to the land with gentle, purposeful actions.

My body is low and narrow, well-suited to navigating the damp, overgrown ravines and the undersides of old catwalks. I walk on four limbs, though the distinction between leg and root has begun to blur over my long life. My feet are broad and soft, cushioned with living moss that silences each deliberate step. Where claws might be, I instead have strong keratin growths, dark and sharp like volcanic glass, perfect for gripping the slick stone and rusted metal.

My hide mirrors the plateau itself - pale, chalky plates interspersed with shale-gray folds, mottled with bruise-colored lichen and dark, damp seams where moisture gathers. Along my back, a mat of dwarf ferns and grasses grows, and a single stunted shrub, its roots sunk deep into the ridges of my shoulders, shaped by the constant wind. Beneath my translucent skin, fluids move in thin, vertical threads, sometimes flowing down with gravity, sometimes defying it.

My face is stony and still, easily mistaken for a broken insulator or capped pipe-end when viewed from an angle. But when I turn to regard my surroundings, one can see the dark, glossy, perfectly circular eye, mechanical in its precision. Smaller white lids ring this eye, adjusting to the omnipresent brightness. My mouth is a quiet valve, made for sipping condensation and rasping minerals from stone, not for tearing or chewing noisily.

I do not speak with sounds. My communication is a subtle language of pressure changes, moisture, electrical fields, and precise timing. To converse with my kind, I angle the undersides of fern fronds to catch the light, or send spreading ripples across a still pool in a specific cadence. 

My purpose is not to preserve this industrial landscape precisely as it was, but to continue its existence in a new form - to keep what still functions, to gently break down what doesn't, and to reincorporate it all into the plateau's patient, growing memory. I tend to the land slowly and steadily, clearing a certain length of rail to maintain its conductivity, while encouraging the moss to swallow another section whole. I direct the flow of mineral-rich water to the roots that need it most. I judge when a pumphouse door must remain accessible, and when it can be allowed to vanish beneath lichen and fern. 

I am a part of this place in a way that goes beyond animal residence - I am its living intention, its inherited and evolving drive to continue, to breathe, to find new purposes in old spaces. I am the child of metal and moss, of broken stones and thriving vines, learning slowly, season by season, to build an ancient future from the bright, young bones of the past.
