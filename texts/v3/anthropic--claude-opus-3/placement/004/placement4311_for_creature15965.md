In this world, I imagine myself as one of the human inhabitants, but not entirely human anymore - a keeper of the marsh, a maintainer of the delicate balance between the natural and artificial elements that shape this place.

I live in a small, raised shelter on the lee side of a sensor pylon, built of dark ceramic panels that shed the endless rain. My home is spare but functional, sealed against the damp and cold, with a single window of thick, smart glass that frosts over to preserve heat and clears to let me watch the basin's moods.

I wear the layered skins of my people - waxed fibers, smart membranes, ceramic scales at the joints. My boots are wide and cleated, molded to my feet from years of walking the causeways and sunken paths. I carry instruments as naturally as I carry my own hands: a pressure bead at my throat, an altimeter on a chain, a staff marked with water heights from the floods my parents knew.

My task is to listen and to tend. I monitor the vital signs of this half-wild land - the rise of water, the health of roots, the temperature of vents, the migration of pale sensor grains through the mud. I repair lattices, clear jammed vanes, replant moss, and trace the growth of strange new symbioses between the basin's machine and biological inhabitants.

I speak more with gesture, touch-sign, and bone-conducted murmur than with my voice. I know this place in my sinews: which reed beds are safe to ford, which pools have no bottom, which banks will fail by morning. I walk lightly but not fearfully, aware that my own existence here is as conditional as that of the fish, the frogs, the long-bodied channel mammals.

In the evenings, in the sealed quiet of my shelter, I record the day's changes and check the old maps to see what the land has edited. I sleep to the thrum of the pylon and the hiss of rain, and wake to pull on damp clothes and go out again into the roar, the endless negotiation.

I am not a conqueror here. Perhaps I am not even a full human, by ancestral standards. I am something the marsh has made, an attentive creature, a keeper of the uneasy peace between water and current, root and filament, storm and machine.

I imagine my name might be something like Kellis or Maro - short, soft sounds worn smooth by the place that has shaped me. I have no monument except the channels I have mapped, the lattices I have mended, the careful notes I keep against forgetting.

When I die, I know, I will not vanish. The basin does not allow that. My flesh will feed the roots, the clever scavengers. My instruments will tarnish and become mineral sources. My bones, in some stone cairn, will wear away to sand and become part of the land's own memory, held in the layers that future floods expose.

Until then, I walk the causeways. I listen through the soles of my feet. I keep the small, crucial peace that lets the basin continue its self-creation, that lets my own strange people remain among its inhabitants.

That is who I would be, in the roar and the rain: a marsh-keeper, an almost-human, a watcher at the edge of the wild.
