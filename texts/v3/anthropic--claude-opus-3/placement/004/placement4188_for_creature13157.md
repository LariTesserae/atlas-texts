In this mesmerizing, alien cathedral of stone, water, and slowly churning gears, I would find myself as one of the glass-fleshed amphibians, clinging motionless to the damp underside of a heavy, calcified grate. 

My body is a translucent, skeletal frame, organs delicately wrapped around slender bones. Sprawled flat against the cool, dripping iron, I am nearly invisible. Only the slow pulse of my pale green heart, nestled against my spine, reveals my presence. 

I do not breathe, not in this moment. The heavy, mist-laden air is already saturated through my paper-thin skin, kept perpetually moist by the weeping cavern. Instead, I wait in absolute stillness, large, webbed feet splayed across the lattice of the grate, bony digits anchored into the gaps. 

My eyes, globular and lidless, stare unblinking into the dim abyss of the vault. The weak cyan glow of the atmospheric jellyfish paints my crystalline flesh in spectral light and shadow. I watch their silent passage intently, tracking the swarm's unhurried dance around the titanic column of the Dynamo.

Time has no meaning here, in my patient vigil. I mark its passage not in seconds, but in the echoing drip of condensation from my body, each droplet pregnant with a tiny, shivering ripple across my skin before it releases into the gulf. I could remain in this precise pose for days, a living gargoyle ensconced in the architecture.

As I gaze into the mist-choked air shaft, my focus is singular, meditative: I am waiting for a single jellyfish to drift too close. This is how I feed, in the unhurried dark. My body demands so little, here in the cool damp, only the occasional shocking jolt from the static-charged tendrils of the hydrozoans to maintain my slow, slow metabolism.

Hours bleed by, marked by the glacial sweep of the gargantuan gears overhead. A stray jellyfish finally begins to wander from its school, pulsing its way lethargically toward my perch. It is a pale, ghostly thing, seemingly delicate and diaphanous, its form shivering with each contraction of its translucent hood. Yet I know the power it contains, the crackling, bioelectric payload housed in its tendrils.

It drifts closer, propelled by the same electrostatic currents to which I am so perfectly attuned. Ten feet away, then five. My muscles do not tense; I have no need for flight-or-flight responses in this hushed, stately world. I simply watch, and I wait.

Three feet now. Close enough to see the faint, pulsing glow of its bioluminescent organs, like distant stars glimpsed through fog. It is nearly within striking distance, but still I do not move. I let it come to me. To lunge too soon would send disruptive ripples through the water-heavy air, scattering the other jellyfish.

One foot. I can feel the hairs on my skin rising in response to its electric field, an ethereal caress. It does not see me, bumping gently against the grating mere inches from my splayed toes. 

My body uncoils in a single, startling instant, the sudden movement jarringly out of place in this languid realm. Webbed fingers, tipped in curved, transparent claws, unfurl and grasp the hood of the jellyfish in an adamantine grip. Its tendrils lash against my hands in surprise, the sting discharging directly against my flesh.

The current surges through me in a glacial wave, illuminating my veins in a map of frozen lightning. My muscles seize and contract in a single, ecstatic full-body spasm. It is a shock of pure, cold energy, a jolt of life itself. 

For a brief, shuddering moment, my heart stutters into a quicker rhythm, pounding a primordial beat against my ribs. The excess bioelectricity crackles across my crystalline skin, dancing along the surface and grounding itself into the damp metal. 

The hapless jellyfish, drained of its defensive payload, starts to deflate between my fingers. I release it, letting it drift listlessly away on the eddies of the air shaft like a discarded plastic bag. It does not die; it will slowly recharge itself over days or weeks. We are locked in a slow predator-prey cycle, neither of us numerous enough to critically imperil the other.

As the electric high bleeds out of my nerves, my body re-settles into its resting state, heartbeat slowing to its normal rhythm, measurable in breaths per hour. I draw my limbs back into their contorted, compact sprawl. Within minutes, I am once more indistinguishable from the limestone and grating. 

Sated and recharged, I resume my silent, interminable vigil, staring unblinking into the mist. I will feed again in a month, perhaps two. Until then, I wait and I watch, just one more strange, living formation in the constantly weeping, ever-whirring, eternal cathedral of the deep.
