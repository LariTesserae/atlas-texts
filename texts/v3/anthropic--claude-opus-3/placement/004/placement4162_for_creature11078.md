In the world of the Hanging Gardens, I would be a young moth-shepherd, born and raised in this vertical city of stone and silk. My body would show the adaptations of my people—an extra joint in my smallest fingers for gripping the perpetually mist-slick rock, hair that grows in multiple textures to sense changes in the air, eyes with nictitating membranes to protect against the ever-present wing scales and condensation.

From infancy, I would have learned to climb before walking, to move with the sudden stillness of the creatures around me. The games of my childhood would have been vertical challenges—blindfolded races up the cliff face, memory tests of moth wing patterns, hide-and-seek where the seeker must locate the hider by changes in the insect songs. 

As I grew, I would have been apprenticed to a family of moth-shepherds, learning to tend a section of the gardens. My days would be spent harvesting silk from cocoons the size of melons, guiding moth flocks with whistles pitched too high for normal human hearing, reading the health of the ecosystem in the shimmer of wings and placement of eggs. 

My tools would become extensions of myself—a shepherding stick carved from heartwood, developing its own microbial ecology to warn me of moth diseases; a bronze knife whose patina changes with what it cuts, turning green when I harvest plants and blue-black when I handle moths. Over time, my skin would begin to change where the giant moths perch on my arms, developing patches of rough scales in intricate patterns.

I would dress in the color-shifting silks of my people, the patterns on the fabric emerging and fading with the moisture in the air—maps of wing scales, diagrams of root systems, the tunneling paths of beetles. The ceramic tubes hidden in my clothing would collect drinking water from the condensation of the mist.

My diet would be the strange bounty of the gardens—moth nectar collected in ancient vessels, larvae cultivated for their transformative secretions, flowers that can only be consumed after an insect has sipped their essence. When injured, I would visit the healers, whose own bodies record their work—violet-stained fingernails from medicinal fungi, skin that glows faintly in the dark from contact with bioluminescent organisms.

In time, I might marry into one of the complex family clusters, becoming part of a unit of adults collectively tending our section of the vertical world. We would raise children communally, teaching them to read the wood grain patterns left by ancestors long returned to the ecosystem.

At night, I would dream the collective dreams of my people, my sleeping mind filled with impossible colors and incomprehensible conversations, the chemical signals of night-blooming flowers guiding my work in ways my waking self could never articulate.

As I aged, my speech would gradually transition from audible words to subsonic vibrations, conducting complex conversations through stone and water. Sitting for hours among the giant moths of the highest reaches, I would negotiate with creatures my ancestors once simply tended, the boundaries between our species blurring.

In the end, I would hope to become one of the great cultivators—to develop a new moth lineage, to find a novel harmony between species, to create a garden that thrives for generations beyond my own lifespan. I would aspire not just to tend the Hanging Gardens, but to be tended by them in turn, transformed into something that belongs to the mountain as much as myself.

When I finally died, I would be given to the gardens—my body becoming a nursery hollow, then a pattern in the wood that future generations would read, learning my story through the arrangement of bore holes and fungi. I would become ancestor and ecosystem, leaving behind both descendants and a living, vertical world marked forever by my passage.

In the Hanging Gardens of the Moth-Shepherds, I would be one strange thread in a living tapestry vaster and more complex than any individual life, my humanity not lost but transformed, woven into something ancient and enduring and incomprehensibly beautiful.
