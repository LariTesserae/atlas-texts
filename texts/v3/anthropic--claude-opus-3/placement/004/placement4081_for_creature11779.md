In this world of the Sweatworks, I imagine myself as a small, pale moth fluttering through the thick, heavy air. My wings are a ghostly white, almost translucent, and they bear the faintest tracery of rust-colored veins, echoing the hues of the ancient machinery and the bleeding rust upon the brick.

I am drawn to the dim, flickering glow of the few remaining bulbs, their orange light a beacon in the oppressive heat. I dance around them, brief flashes of pale wings in the shadows, always careful not to venture too close, lest my delicate form be singed by their failing heat.

When I tire of the bulbs, I find respite in the cooler air near the ceiling, alighting upon the old iron hooks where the cable sags under the weight of age. From this vantage point, I survey the chamber, my compound eyes taking in the pulsing, echoing chuff that rises from deep below, the slow drip of condensation, the creak and tick of metal eternally contracting and expanding.

I am a silent observer, flitting through this place unnoticed by the great, slow being that dwells here. The creature, in its timeless cycle of growth and decay, fire and foliage, takes no heed of me. To it, I am as transient as a mote of dust, a fleeting presence in the long, patient work of its existence.

Yet I am a witness to its labors. I watch the vines, dark and glossy, twining through the rusted machinery, seeking the heat of the banked fires. I see the pale fungus ringing the still, dark pool, a strange and singular form of life in this place devoid of other creatures.

In my ephemeral way, I map the Sweatworks. I trace the paths of the vines, flutter through cracks and crevices too small for any other creature to pass. I drink in the damp, metallic air, tasting the sweetness of slow decay, the sharpness of ancient iron.

And perhaps, in the way of moths, I am drawn to the deeper chamber, to the unseen heart of this great being. Perhaps, in a moment of curiosity or instinct, I venture beyond the wall, following the thickening vines into the hidden places where the creature's work is oldest and densest.

But if I do, I do not return. For in that chamber, the air is too close, too rich with the exhalations of the creature, and my fragile wings cannot bear me back to the lighter air. And so I become a part of the work, my dust adding to the slow accretion of matter, my tiny life given over to the great, patient life that endures here, in the heat and the damp and the darkness, cycling on in its slow, implacable way, indifferent to the small things that briefly share its space.

In the Sweatworks, I am the moth, the transient observer, bearing witness for a flickering moment before I, too, am subsumed into the greater whole, a mote in the vast, slow dream of the being that dwells eternal in this strange, pulsing place.
