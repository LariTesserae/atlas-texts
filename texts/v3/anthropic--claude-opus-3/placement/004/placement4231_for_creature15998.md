In this evocative world, I imagine myself as an old woman who serves as the town's memory keeper. I live in a small house built into the slope near the central cistern, my walls as weathered and gray as the surrounding stone. My role is not official, but it is acknowledged - when disputes arise over boundaries, water rights, or half-forgotten obligations, people come to me for guidance.

I spend my days tending the tiny garden on my roof, where I nurture fragile medicinal herbs and coax seedlings to take root in soil I have painstakingly gathered and enriched with ash, dung, and scraps. As I work, I watch the patterns of the town flow around me - women gathering at the cistern to fill jars and exchange news, children scampering up and down the narrow lanes on errands, the ebb and flow of trade and gossip. 

In the evenings, I often sit in my doorway with a small lamp, and neighbors stop by to share tea or seek advice. The younger ones ask about managing difficult in-laws or colicky babies. The elders reminisce about harsh winters long past and the clever solutions that saw the town through. I listen to it all, weaving each story into the larger tapestry of our shared history.

On market days, I make my slow way to the trading area, not to buy or sell, but to observe. I note which traders seem honest, which may bear closer watching. I read the health of other towns in the quality of the goods they send, the eagerness or desperation in the voices bargaining. When the trading is done, I often linger to share a meal with visitors, trading news and knowledge that will never show up on a tally sheet but that may prove far more valuable.

I do not command respect, but I am granted it all the same. When I speak in council, fixing a rambling speaker with a steady gaze, the room quiets to hear my soft voice recount how a similar quarrel was settled two generations past. When sickness strikes, I am consulted as frequently as the healers - though I can no longer climb to gather herbs, my mind holds lifetimes of accumulated wisdom about nursing and comfort.

As the town's memory, I am in some ways the town itself - my bones as weathered as its stones, my voice as quiet as its nights, my silences as deep as its hidden cisterns. I hold its stories, its joys and sorrows, its unvoiced hopes. And in turn, it holds me - each child that brings me a choice bit of gossip or a warm loaf, each man who repairs my roof unbidden, each woman who sits with me to learn the secrets of a tricky dye or a colicky goat, weaves me more securely into our shared life. 

When at last I die, I know my town will tend me as I tended it. They will see my body safely to the ancestors' rest beyond the rim, and then return to add a new story to the fabric - the time old Sefa left her door, the year the town's memory passed from one hand to another. And I will become part of the place in truth, my dust mingling with its stones, my voice one more whisper in the wind off the highlands. The town will continue, and I will continue in it, a memory held in its bones.
