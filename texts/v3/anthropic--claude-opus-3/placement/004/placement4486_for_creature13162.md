If I found myself submerged in this deafening, lightless pocket of the planetary crust, I imagine I would be one of the blind, translucent, gelatinous scavengers occasionally swept helplessly through the churning shear-currents. 

My body would be a fragile, pulsing thing, evolved to absorb the relentless acoustic shock by virtue of having almost no rigid structure at all. I would drift passively in the warm, heavy ocean that smells of iron, sulfur and ozone, my simple nervous system stunned into silence by the bone-rattling roar of industry battling fluid under immense pressure. 

With no eyes, I would sense my surroundings only through subtle changes in the turbulent flow - the shuddering thrum of a massive turbine blade, the percussive blast of a steam vent, the metallic screech of a piston. The violence of the currents would tumble my gelatinous form end over end, stretching and compressing me like an organic stress ball.

My existence would revolve around the rare brushes with biomatter - perhaps a flake of shed thermal slime from the ablative hides of the settlement's lumbering inhabitants, or a microscopic whiff of organic particulate from some long-dead scavenger cousin pinned against an intake grate. I would engulf these precious motes, absorbing them into my translucent mass, eking out just enough metabolic energy to pulse onward through the aquatic pandemonium.

Most of the time, I would simply try to survive by virtue of my yielding formlessness. I would allow my body to become one with the turbulent water, offering no resistance, a fleck of clear jelly riding the submarine storm. The same qualities that make me perfectly adapted to absorb the acoustic abuse also render me utterly inconsequential within it - a mote of living gel, barely more significant than the bubbles and silt and industrial effluvium.

And yet, I would serve an invisible but vital purpose. As I pulsed through the shear-currents, absorbing and filtering the traces of organic detritus, I would act as an unwitting agent of connection. The sparse nutrients I collected would inevitably become a part of me, and I would inevitably become a sparse nutrient myself - a tiny morsel gulped down by another gelatinous cousin, or a smear of ruptured jelly smashed against the crenelated surface of a pressure sphere. In my living and dying, I would serve as a crucial link in a tenuous food web, ensuring that the recycling of biomatter continued within our sealed world.

Perhaps once in a thousand generations, a singular event would elevate my simple existence into something almost transcendent. The gruesome demise of one of the settlement's titanic, armored inhabitants - pulverized by a blown gasket or shredded in the teeth of a gear - would fill the churning water with a sudden abundance of ruptured lipids and iron-rich blood. A hundred thousand of my kind would swarm the area, absorbing the rare windfall, engorged on the unexpected plenty.

In that moment, for a brief time, the anonymous, pulsing multitude of us would cease to be inconsequential motes. Our translucent bodies, swelled and thickened and stained reddish-grey, would throng in great drifts and shoals. We would become a visible, tactile presence in the water - a vast cloud of vibrating jelly, reverberating with the energy of our feeding frenzy. Perhaps the cymatic resonance of our collective gorging would even reach the sensitive skins of the survivors far across the settlement, forming a strange new frequency in their three-dimensional soundscape.

Then the currents would shift, the cloud would disperse, and I would be alone again - an invisible wisp of gel pulsing quietly in the roaring dark, nourished and renewed. I would drift on, a small, secret, but indelible part of the ecosystem of the churning sphere, until I was inevitably swallowed up by the shear forces or gulped down by a cousin. My simple life would end as anonymously as it began, but the faint trace of my absorbed nutrients would carry on, an eternal, necessary note in the deafening, grinding, iron-dark symphony of our world.
