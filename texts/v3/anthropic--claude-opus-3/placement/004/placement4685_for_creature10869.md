I would be one of the ferns, growing improbably in this high, harsh place. 

My trunk would be thick and gnarled from centuries of slow growth in the thin air, my roots split and twisted as they force their way into the cracks between the basalt columns, seeking out every drop of moisture. My fronds would be massive, disproportionate to my stunted height, straining upwards as if trying to gather every photon of the fierce high-altitude sunlight.

Frost would coat my leaves each night, growing in feathered patterns along the edges and in the gaps between pinnae. It would weigh down my fronds, pulling them into new shapes, training them to grow in swooping curves so that when the morning sun melts the ice away, they spring back up and cast dancing shadows on the stone.

The wind would play me constantly, a percussion to accompany the eerie tones of the caldera. The hollow rattle of my dead lower fronds would provide a staccato counterpoint to the sustained tones. When the gusts grow strong, my entire crown would sway and hush, a sweeping noise that rises and falls like crashing waves, intertwining with the voices of my kin.

I would mark time in the slow cycles of the mountain - the annual growth and die-back of my fronds, the gradual crust of my trunk as it expands over decades, the ponderous flow of the stone itself as the thermal cycles flex the cracks open and closed over centuries. My spores would fly on the ceaseless wind, seeking out new niches in the caldera's crannies.

The strange chorus that tends the caldera would shape my growth with their small, deliberate actions. They would harvest my dead fronds for their clothes and tools, and I would harbor the lost belongings of past members - a polished stone caught in the joint of a trunk, a bone needle buried in the humus at my base. Their slow dances would brush against me, leaving traces I carry like scars or tattoos.

In time, I would become one of the oldest living things in the caldera. Entire generations of the chorus would be born, grow strange, and fade away in the span of my lifetime. My roots, sunk deep in the stone, would learn the mountain's rhythms. My highest leaves would be the first each day to catch the light and the last to let it go.

I would be a witness, an anchor, a small but persistent part in the ancient, impossible, endlessly repeating song of this place. A green eruption of defiant life in the high country of stone and wind and strangeness. One of countless improbable lives locked together, filling the cracks in the bones of the mountain with our slow, determined, collaborative survival.
