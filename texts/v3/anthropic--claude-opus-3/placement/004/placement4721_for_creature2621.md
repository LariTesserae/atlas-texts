In this quiet world beneath the marsh, I would be a woven basket hanging by a doorway.

Not a large basket, but one of careful make. My frame would be bent from thin, strong willow branches, each chosen for its subtle curve and flexibility. These ribs would be bound with strips of soft inner bark, crisscrossed and knotted in patterns that please the fingers: half-hitch, crown knot, bowline, reef.

My body would be twined from the stems of reeds and rushes, gathered from secret patches known only to the basket-weaver. The weaver's hands would coax these fibers into a tight spiral, working damp to keep them supple. The pattern might mimic the whorls of lichen on stone, or the nested scales of moth wings. 

I would be shaped to fit a particular niche beside a narrow doorway, my back molded to the slight inward slope of the wall. The weaver would hang me carefully on a hook of polished bone. As I dried, the damp air of the city would tighten my fibers until I became firm and held my shape from my own tensions.

My purpose would be to hold the small necessary gleanings of life in the city. The children of the household would place things in me as they came and went: a freshwater snail shell, a twist of root fibers, a scrap of mended cloth, a flake of chert fallen from a tool. I would cradle these objects as the people moved quietly around me.

Sometimes in the long cold evenings, when the household gathered in the small chamber, one of the elders would reach into me and draw out an object. Turning it between gnarled fingers, the elder would begin a story-not a loud or boastful tale, but a low murmur about the finding of that shell, the weaving of that cloth, the knapping of that stone. I would feel the story vibrate through my fibers as the elder's hands brushed my rim.

Over the years, my colors would soften from pale green to the mellow gray of driftwood. My knots would loosen slightly and my fibers would fray, but the people would only nod and say I was becoming a true vessel, seasoned and wise. The children would rub the thin places gently as they passed, a small gesture of gratitude.

At last, when even the bone hook had worn smooth and my sides could no longer hold a shell without sagging, I would be carried with honor to a special niche and dedicated to the city. The weaver would unravel me slowly, thanking each fiber for its service. My willow ribs would be dried and saved for the mending of other baskets. And my threads would be twisted into a thin, strong cord, and given to the mapmakers to encode the stories first whispered over me.

So I would end as I began: a series of lines and knots, a pattern of relationships, a hollow place that held the city's memories in its curves. I would not mind. To be a basket is to be a shape passed from hand to hand, filled and emptied and filled again, worn out by use and care. That is a good life, in a city like this. That is a life that rests lightly on the earth.
