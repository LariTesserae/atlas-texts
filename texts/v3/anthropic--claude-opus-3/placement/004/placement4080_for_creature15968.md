I would be one of the ward growers, living deep in the pocket-city beneath the earth's surface. 

My hands would be stained green from tending to the vines, roots and fungi that help knit this fragile place together. I would know each plant like a difficult relative - which ones drink too greedily from the walls, which stems can be grafted onto structural roots, which shelves of fungi can be carefully shaved without killing the organism. The scent of loam, vinegar and lamp oil would cling to me.

I would walk the humid corridors and cramped alleys with a quiet reverence, seeing not just stone and metal, but a precarious ecosystem held in tenuous balance. With a touch, I could tell if a vine is straining a wall towards cracking. With soft words, I would persuade roots to grow deeper to stabilize a foundation, never commanding, but bargaining with moisture, minerals and shadow.

The other citizens would look to me to read the green breath of the city - too much and the rot sets in, too little and the city dries and cracks. At meetings around the Condenser Tree, my voice would be one urging patience, tending, and respect for the plants without which the city could not breathe.

On rest days, I would find a quiet corner in the fungal caverns, breathing the loamy air and communing with the small, silent lives that are as much residents as the humans. I would meditate on the humble magnificence of algae, bacterial films, and fruiting bodies - the unsung multispecies multitude that filters the city's air, water and waste.

Sometimes I would volunteer to guide children or apprentices to the raw edges where habitation crumbles into wild stone. There I would recite the litany of gratitude and warning - never take the green for granted, never forget that we survive by the grace of roots and the patience of lichen. I would hope my words take root in their minds like spores.

In the evenings I would join the other growers to drink pungent root-tea and swap stories of stubborn molds, dry-rot outbreaks heroically contained, and the sweet subterranean fruits that appear once a decade. We would speak of the city as a willful garden to be tended and placated, coaxed and thanked.

And sometimes, in the deep of night in my sleeping niche, I would press my palm to the warm, moist stone and whisper secrets to the hyphae in the cracks - my fears that our green magic cannot hold forever, my dreams of fruit-laden archways and fountains running clear. I like to think the mycelium carries my hopes like nutrients through the city's hidden veins.

I would grow old tending the city's green liver and lungs, my knuckles swollen and my back bent. But I would be content in the knowledge that, when my time comes, my body too will feed the fungi and join the cycle of breath and decay that is the true pulse of this place. There are worse fates than to become loam, compost and new green life in the cracks of the city I served.
