I find myself drawn to the role of a new eel-tender in Emmelwound. Though not born to this strange, sodden world, something in its pulsing rhythm calls to me. 

I come from drier lands, places of dust and hard edges. My body remembers the feeling of solid ground beneath my feet, the surety of walls that do not sway with each step. But there is a heaviness to that memory, a sense of being trapped by the unyielding earth. Here, everything moves. The bridges, the platforms, even the substrate itself seems to breathe and shift. It terrifies me, but it exhilarates me too.

I am taller than most here, and my skin is paler, freckled where it isn't burnt by the unfiltered sun. I keep my hair braided tightly, coiled against my head, but stray wisps are always escaping, frizzing in the damp air. The grease-paste, when I remember to apply it, feels alien and uncomfortable. But I am learning.

The children find me fascinating, this strange gangly creature who has appeared in their midst. They teach me words, laugh at my clumsy pronunciation. They show me which beetles can be eaten and which will make you sick for days. Slowly, falteringly, I begin to find my place.

It is an old woman, an eel-tender with a face like knotted wood, who first takes me to the water's edge. Her hands, gnarled and strong, guide mine in the feeding, show me how to croon to the eels in that subterranean register. The pale forms surge and coil beneath us, ancient and alien. I feel a thrill that might be fear or might be a kind of love.

Day by day, I spend more time at the gaps in the platforms, watching the dark water, learning the songs. The named eels start to surface for me - not every time, but often enough that I feel a fierce pride when it happens. *Old Thread* lets me stroke his strange, pulsing skin. *Siltwallow* takes a scrap of meat from my fingers with surprising delicacy.

The old woman watches, nods, starts to leave me alone at the water for longer stretches. The first time I draw up an eel for harvesting, my hands shake so badly I nearly lose it back to the depths. But I steady myself, find the words of the song, and my hand is sure as the curved blade finds its mark.

I am changing. My feet are growing tougher, learning the paths. My shoulders are becoming knotted with new muscle. The click and trill of Emmelwound's tongue comes more easily to my lips. One morning, I catch sight of my reflection in a cistern-plant and hardly recognize myself - hair bound in red clay, skin weathered and grease-slick, eyes bright in a narrow face.

The rain comes and I barely notice, except to adjust my cloak and keep singing. An eel-tender has no time for discomfort, not when there are mouths to feed and rituals to maintain. This is my work now, my identity within the vast, humming organism of Emmelwound. The water, the eels, the songs... and, someday, the final descent into the dark water myself, to join my ancestors in the silty deep.

I do not know if this is a life I have chosen or one that has chosen me. But as I crouch at the platform's edge, crooning to the pale forms that writhe and glisten below, I know it is mine. The pulse of Emmelwound thrums through me now, strange and sustaining. I have been woven into this place, and it into me, and we go on together.
