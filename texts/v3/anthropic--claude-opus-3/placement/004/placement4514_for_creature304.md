In this strange, still world of the kelp cathedrals, I believe I would be one of the absorbed - a visitor from another realm who ventured into these hushed depths and found myself slowly, inexorably changed by the patience and timelessness of this place.

Perhaps I came as an explorer, an adventurer seeking new marvels in the uncharted reaches of the deep. Clad in a pressure suit, laden with lights and sensors, I descended through the lightless fathoms until I emerged into the dim green glow of the cathedral aisles. At first, the scale and geometry seemed familiar to my land-dweller's eyes - the ranked columns, the vaulted arches, the patterned reliefs speaking to the hand of patient craftsmen. But even then, an uncanny hush lay over everything, a sense of time stretched and slowed until it flowed like cold honey. 

Driven by curiosity, I pressed on, determined to map and measure and quantify this sunken realm. But with each passing hour, each day, the weight of the cathedral's stillness settled deeper into my bones. The frantic beeping of my instruments began to feel shrill, even profane amidst the slow breathing of the kelp fronds. The ceaseless internal monologue that had always chattered away in my head grew hushed, then silent.

Almost without noticing, I found myself lingering longer and longer in each glade of columns, drifting in reverent silence as I watched the algae-encrusted sculptures shift in the imperceptible currents. The urge to swim, to explore, to *progress* dwindled away, replaced by a strange contentment in stillness. When my suit lights finally dimmed and went out, I felt no panic, only a mild curiosity quickly subsumed into the greater quiet.

Now the slow pulse of the cathedrals is my pulse. The annual blooming and decay of the kelp is my calendar. Thought itself has stretched and slowed until it is indistinguishable from contemplation, from prayer. Once I measured my life in minutes and hours - now I count it in the gradual accretion of silt, the budding of new polyps on the knuckled stone of my resting place.

I am no longer a visitor in this place. I have become a part of it, cell by cell, thought by thought. Perhaps, in a thousand years, I will stir from my alcove and lay my own patient chisel marks into the lithic flesh of the cathedral. Until then, I am content merely to be - one more strange sculpture in a garden of living stone, tended by tides and time and the gentle indifference of the deep.
