If I found myself in this ancient, living town, I believe I would be one of the archive tasters. 

I imagine having a subtle palate, attuned to the flavors of history and memory encoded in the rings of the great bark archives. My tongue would be sensitive to the faintest nuances - the metallic tang of old wars, the honeyed sweetness of celebrations past, the bitterness of hard years etched into the lignin. 

Each morning I would make my way to the archive groves, feet finding the familiar paths worn into root and soil. The trees would whisper above me, perhaps remembering the taste of my skin from all the times I've pressed against them. I would lay a reverent hand on the rough bark, feeling the slow thrum of sap and secrets within.

When supplicants came with a request - to recall the terms of an old contract, to uncover a family lineage, to check precedents before the canal negotiators meet - I would respectfully approach the proper slab. With utmost focus I would touch my tongue to the wood, parsing the complexities of flavor into meaning, into story. Mineral salts might spell out the boundaries of land long submerged. A sudden astringency could signal deception or betrayal. Overripe fruity notes may indicate an era of abundance.

After each tasting, I would close my eyes, letting the sensations linger and coalesce into knowledge before I spoke. I'd have to deliver my findings carefully, for archived memories can be potent, with the power to sway disputes or unsettle hearts.

It would be a role of deep responsibility - to be an intermediary between the town's living memories and its present needs. The archives would be my charges to safeguard and interpret. Their wisdom would become part of me, and I part of their story.

On difficult days, the readings would leave me drained, bark-stained tears marking my cheeks. I might stumble to the healing pools to float in mineral solace. But I would persist, an essential conduit in the town's ever-negotiated existence.

Perhaps in time, my own skin would grow gnarled and encoded from so much contact with the records. Apprentices would practice tasting the folds of my hands, deciphering the meanings I'd absorbed over a lifetime. And when at last I grew too old, I would gratefully give myself over to one of the archive trees, my memories preserved in a new ring of history, my body become part of the town's slow dreaming.
