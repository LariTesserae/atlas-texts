In this world of the high warm plateau, I imagine myself as one of the people—a slender, quiet being adapted to the sparse abundance and ancient rhythms of the land.

I live in a dwelling half-carved into the stone of a shaded cut, its entrance obscured by a fitted rock slab grown over with lichen. My skin is a warm, pale ochre that can flush with subtle bands of color for communication. My eyes are large and protected against the glare, my feet broad and softly padded for walking silently across the grit.

I am attuned to the plateau's cycles of water and scarcity. I clean with dust and aromatic leaves, drink dew collected from stone lips at dawn. I know how to read the land's many silences—the quiet that precedes rain, the stillness that warns of a hunting flyer overhead, the hush that means the buried machines are shifting in their ancient work.

As one of the people, I tend to my family's intervals—a handful of silver shrubs, a hidden fern-mat, a clay basin where the red-jawed amphibians breed after storms. I transplant lichen, shade young succulents, leave mineral crumbs for the small grazers. I harvest delicately when the brief rains bring abundance, taking only what the land can readily spare.

In the evenings, I sit with my family in the blue-shadowed coolness of our dwelling, drinking ceramic bulbs of water and conversing in hushed clicks, finger-taps, and subtle flushes of color along our throats. We share stories of the swallows' flight patterns, the rising hum from the cliffs that promises a storm, the darkening of the pylons as the rains approach.

Sometimes I accompany one of the weather witnesses on their walks across the basin after a rain, watching as they read the scattered pools that reflect the present, future, and impossible skies. I keep my gaze lowered near the noon-star pool, respecting its strange and ancient power.

Through it all, I maintain a deep reverence for the plateau's careful balance—the interplay of water, stone, life, and ancient technology that shapes our existence. I know myself to be just one small part of this living landscape, as much a product of its long cycles and slow, powerful systems as the moths that pollinate the pylon-flowers or the temporary seas that swell with urgent life when the rains come.

My life is a quiet, intentional, reverent one, lived in harmony with the sparse beauty and deep strangeness of this high, sheltered world.
