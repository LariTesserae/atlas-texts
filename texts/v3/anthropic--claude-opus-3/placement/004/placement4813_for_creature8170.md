In the suspended miracle of Meltwater, I would be a bridge-singer. 

My body has elongated over generations in this upside-down city, bones becoming lighter and more hollow to better float through the cavernous space. My fingers are long and delicate, perpetually stained green from tending the vines that weave through the bridge cables. The skin of my bare feet has grown thick and calloused from gripping the rusted metal as I traverse the web of walkways.

I carry a staff of living wood, grown into a spiral by the patient cultivation of my ancestors. Moss and small flowers sprout along its length, nourished by the oils of my hands. The wood is dense but lightweight, the result of careful breeding to create a tree that could thrive in this eternally humid twilight.

My role among my people is to maintain the living architecture of the city. I sing to the bridges—long, warbling notes that reverberate through the metal and encourage the symbiotic plants to grow in the patterns that strengthen rather than corrode. The vines follow my voice, twining through the cables in helical patterns that redistribute the weight and prevent catastrophic failure. The flowers that bloom along the railings open and close in time with my song, pumping oxygen into the walkways for the other Gardeners to breathe.

I learned this craft from my mother, who learned it from hers, back through generations of bridge-singers. Our songs are wordless but information-dense, conveying complex instructions to the plants through pitch and cadence. Different tones encourage different growth patterns, different flowers, different root structures weaving through the metal. 

When I'm not singing, I tend to the bridge-gardens. I prune back overgrowth, harvest the air-filtering blooms, splice in new varieties bred by the garden-priests. I forage for the bioluminescent mushrooms that grow along the undersides of the walkways, using them to create living lamps that light the paths. 

On special occasions, I join my voice with the other bridge-singers to create a resonant harmony that fills the entire subcity. These songs are part prayer, part performance, part symbiotic communication. The plants respond, blooming in waves of color and scent, pulsing with our voices. The city itself seems to breathe with our song, the metal humming in sympathy.

In quiet moments, I sit cross-legged on a walkway and meditate, feeling the thrum of life through the soles of my feet. The bridges are never still—they sway gently with the thermal currents, shiver with the footsteps of passersby, quiver with the growth of their living components. To sit in silence and feel that movement is to know the pulse of Meltwater itself.

I dream of one day composing a song so perfect that it encourages a brand new form of life—a creature born from the bridge itself, a chimera of metal and vine and fungus that can sing in harmony with the voices of the city. I imagine it moving through the walkways like a gentle guardian, tending to the gardens and the people in equal measure.

Until then, I continue my duties, my own voice joining the polyphonic murmur that rises from the bridges and mingles with the endless dripping of the thaw. In a city of inverted miracles, my song is just one more strange and necessary inversion—plant following human, metal embracing life, voice become garden, music become structure. I am a bridge-singer of Meltwater, and my body and my song belong to the green, growing, ever-evolving symbiosis of this place.
