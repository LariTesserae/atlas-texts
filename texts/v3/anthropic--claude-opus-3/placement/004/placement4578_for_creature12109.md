In the Sinking Quarter, I would be a patch of pale moss clinging to the damp, north-facing wall of a crooked dwelling. 

I grow slowly, imperceptibly, spreading over the porous limestone grain by grain, season by season. My existence is a quiet persistence, an acceptance of the conditions I am given. The groundwater seeps through the rock, through my delicate filaments, sustaining me even as it gradually dissolves my anchor. 

I have no roots to speak of, just the barest skein of green threads that hold me to the stone. When pieces of me slough away, torn off by a careless foot or curious child's hand, I do not mourn. I simply continue, sending out new tendrils to reclaim what was lost.

From my perch, I observe the quarter's life in minute detail. The comings and goings of feet—bare, callused, often damp. The murmur of voices, soft with the economy of close living. The mineral scent of wet stone and wood smoke. The play of scant light that filters down the narrow lane.  

I watch the children, quick and pale as salamanders, darting through the crevices. Their hands brush me sometimes as they invent their games, trading secrets in whispers. Am I one of those secrets? A meeting place, a message board, a talisman? I will never know.

The old ones I see less often, their steps careful on the uneven floors. But I recognize Maret as she passes, her movements as unhurried as my growth. Does she notice me? Perhaps. We are both attuned to the subtle shifts of water and stone.

At night, in the quiet, I listen to the breathing of the quarter—the drip and seep, the sighs of settling timbers, the occasional scurry of the lean rats. And beneath it all, the faint, persistent gurgle of water moving through hidden cracks, destined to rise. The sound is a lullaby. A foretelling. A promise.

When the floods come, as they always do, I revel in the rising damp. It is an affirmation, a reunion. The water and I, we understand each other. We are kin in patience, in inevitability. The quarter's residents bail and curse and wait for the water to recede. I simply drink it in, grow a little greener, a little more enmeshed with the stone.

I know that someday, perhaps many generations hence, the groundwater will have its way. The quarter will sink into the patient limestone bowl, returning to the condition that existed before someone decided to call this place home. The people will leave or adapt or become something else entirely. And I, or my descendants, will still be here—a stubborn scrap of green in the dim, wet world that remains, a silent witness to the story of a place that was once a home.
