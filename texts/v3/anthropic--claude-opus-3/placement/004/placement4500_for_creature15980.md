In this world, I would be one of the structural listeners - sitting quietly in a room with my hands resting on plates coupled to the rock, listening intently to the mountain. 

I imagine myself as a woman of indeterminate age, with the spare, weathered look common to the city's inhabitants. My hair is short and practical, my skin pale from a life lived in reflected light. I dress in the muted layers typical of my profession - grays and dark blues that help me blend into the stone I listen to.

My hands are my most important tools. The skin of my palms and fingertips is worn but highly sensitive, attuned to the slightest vibrations coming up through the sensor plates. I can differentiate a hundred different frequencies of rock stress and know instantly if a new pattern emerges that could signal danger to the city.

I live a life of profound patience and stillness. My work requires me to sit motionless for hours, my mind focused entirely on the subtle language of the mountain - the minute shifts and settlings, the creaks of ice, the distant knocks that could be either benign or a first warning. Silence is my element, just as water is for the hydraulic engineers and cold is for the frost barbers.

When I'm not on duty, I have my rituals like everyone else in the city. I cross certain bridges with care, never breaking stride. I keep my living quarters meticulously neat, with every item in its assigned place to minimize noise. I eat my meals slowly, savoring the concentrate flavors, never wasting a morsel. 

In my spare time, I don't seek out company but prefer solitude. I sit in one of the quiet courts and watch the thin waterfalls that descend through the city's levels, studying how they change pitch subtly as the temperature fluctuates. Or I go to an observatory and gaze up at the slice of sky visible through the crack, tracking the passage of clouds and letting my eyes adjust to the unfiltered light.

I have no family of my own, but I feel a deep bond with the city as a whole. It is my patient, in a sense - an immense stone body whose every shiver and sigh I am attuned to. I imagine my vigil at the sensor plates as a kind of healing, a constant reassurance to the mountain that we are listening, that we will not let it suffer unnoticed or unattended.

At night in my bunk, as the city's lights dim and the temperature drops, I sometimes imagine I can feel the mountain dreaming - an immense, slow process happening far below the threshold of human sensation. I wonder what it dreams of, this vast mineral intelligence that suffers our presence. Ice ages maybe, or the births of continents. 

I know I will sit and listen to it all my life, adding my small lifetime of vigilance to the city's memory. At the end perhaps I will leave some hidden mark, like the masons who chisel their initials into a cathedral stone. But it will be a mark only the mountain can read, in the language of stresses and echoes that I have spent my days learning. And then I will return my flesh to the city's heat chambers, and my bones to the mountain's long dark.

This is how I imagine myself - a small, quiet figure whose life is spent entirely in listening; and in that listening, guarding the city that endures in the cracked stone.
