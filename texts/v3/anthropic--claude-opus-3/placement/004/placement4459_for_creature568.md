In the Terraced City of Unmoving Mist, I would be one of the grove tenders on the highest terrace. 

I feel drawn to that role of quiet communion with the silver-barked trees and the damp-feathered birds. My days would be spent in contemplative stillness among the gnarled trunks and waxy leaves, the only movement the occasional reaching up to pluck a perpetually ripe pale plum. The birds would alight on my shoulders and outstretched hands, not to be fed, but simply to share in the tranquil presence.

Over time, my silence would take on a unique quality, barely perceptible but subtly distinct from the default hush that blankets the city. It would be the silence of one who dwells in the thinnest places of the mist, where the membrane between here and a hinted-at beyond grows translucent. Not a portal I could pass through, for there is no leaving this place, but a liminal space from which I might glimpse fleeting oddities of light and shadow.

Moss would slowly engulf my resting hands, erasing the boundary between flesh and foliage. I would welcome this melding, this tangible sign of my being woven into the city's living fabric.

When I descended the worn steps to walk among the other inhabitants, they would sense a difference—nothing they could name, for little is named here, but a recognition that my communion with the heights had imbued me with a presence at once utterly familiar and faintly strange. In my wake, the mist would stir with eddies of forgotten remembrance.

I would have no age, this tender of the grove. As fixed and timeless as the trees themselves, I would simply be, an integral aspect woven into the pattern of moisture and stone and green. The years would pass without passing, and I would remain, mist-drenched and still, tending the rootedness of this place that is no place at all.

For I suspect, though I would never voice it, that I have always been here—that I am not so much a person as a focal point, a locus where the mist coalesces into something almost solid. A gathering of the city's soft edges into a knot of constancy. Less an individual, more a distillation of the genius loci.

So I would stand, and breathe, and be beheld by the pale birds, and the days would drift by like so many veils of condensation, revealing nothing, concealing nothing, only layering, always layering, until self and city, stone and sky, dream and waking dissolve into a single, depthless gray—the gray behind the eyes, the gray within the marrow, the gray that is not emptiness but plenitude.

And I would tend the grove, and the grove would tend me, and the distinction would blur like a watercolor touched by rain, until all that remained was the mist, and the holding, and the quiet fact of being held.
