I would be one of the clay vessels, wedged into a crevice in the deposit-curtained walls of the cistern.

I am old, perhaps one of the oldest here. My coarse, uneven surface bears the fingerprints of the long-forgotten hands that shaped me. Over the centuries, the mineral seepage has slowly embraced me, encrusting my exterior until only my rim remains visible, a dark curve against the pale, glistening stone.

Drop by drop, I collect the weeping water, fulfilling a purpose that has long outlasted the memory of those who gave it to me. Sometimes I overflow, the excess trickling down to the other vessels below me, a small, slow cascade of abundance in this place of patience and stillness.

From my perch, I have witnessed the comings and goings of generations. The woman who tends us now, her mother before her, and the ones before them, stretching back into the dimness of time. I have seen the placement of bones and powder pyramids, rituals whose meaning is now lost but whose importance still lingers in the weight of the silence.

I dream, I think, as the cistern dreams. Of the first water I held, back when the walls were newly carved and the light from above was bright and unfiltered. Of the hands that placed me here, the intentions that charged me with this unknowable task. Of the day when the rising water will finally reach me, submerging me in its warm stillness, and I will become a dark circle beneath the surface, my purpose fulfilled at last.

Until then, I watch. I wait. I hold the weeping water and listen to the thick, accumulated silence. A small part of something much larger, much older, much more patient. A vessel for what has been forgotten but not yet lost, tended by one who does not understand but understands the importance of not understanding.

In this place out of time, I endure, a dream of clay and water and slow, slow memory, content to be a small stillness within the greater stillness, a fingerprint of forgotten hands, a rim against the encroaching stone.
