In the world of the Cistern Cities, I would be a potter-engineer, shaping the ceramic pipes and vessels that form the lifeblood of the water collection system.

My workshop would be built into the base of a collection tower, where the thick clay walls keep the air cool and damp. The space is a honeycomb of small rooms, each dedicated to a different stage of the process - clay mixing, shaping, firing, finishing. Shelves line the walls, filled with pipes and pots in various states of completion, all precisely arranged according to the master plan in my head.

I learned the craft from my mother, as she did from hers, back through generations. The secrets of the clay are part of my inheritance - how to judge its moisture content by feel, how to build up shapes that are strong yet light, how to fire pieces at just the right temperature to make them water-tight without becoming brittle.

My hands are perpetually caked with clay, the creases of my palms permanently stained a darker hue. I wear the mark proudly, a sign of my role in keeping the cities alive. Each morning, I survey the collection reports from the keepers, noting where repairs are needed, where extensions must be made to accommodate a growing population. Then I set to work, losing myself in the steady rhythm of the potter's wheel.

I shape pipes to exact specifications, using long-guarded family techniques to create perfect seals without glaze. I craft water vessels of all sizes, from palm-sized cups for children to shoulder-height urns for communal fountains. Each piece is a small miracle - born of clay and skill, destined to hold the most precious substance we know.

When I dream, I see the city as a vast, living pottery work - water flowing through ceramic veins of my making, nourishing and sustaining us all. I imagine new forms, new ways to gather and store the moisture that is our lifeblood. In my most ambitious visions, I see the entire lakebed remade as a colossal cistern, every surface shaped to collect and conserve.

On rest days, I wander the neighborhoods near my workshop, observing how my creations have integrated into the fabric of life. I pause to watch children scoop water from a fountain house basin, admiring the elegant curve of the lip that prevents even a drop from splashing astray. I trace a meandering crack in a walkway wall, making mental notes for a new joining technique that will better withstand the subtle shifts of the clay.

I have no children of my own - my legacy will be in the enduring ceramic skin of the city itself. I take apprentices not from my own blood, but from those who show an innate affinity for the clay - the girl whose fingers can sense the finest air bubbles in a mix, the boy who hums unconsciously in tune with the potter's wheel. I will pass my knowledge to them, brick by brick, until they too can coax life-giving shape from the earth.

In the evenings, I often climb to the roof of my workshop tower, where I have shaped a small garden of clay pots. There I nurture rare plants - not the hardy succulents of the city gardens, but delicate flowers that bloom in improbable shades of blue and gold. The plants are my secret indulgence, each one representing a future I dare to imagine - a time when water flows so freely that we might grow beauty alongside necessity.

From my rooftop perch, I can see the city stretched out before me in all its strange, harsh glory. The clay beneath my feet, the ceramic pipes carrying their precious cargo, the people moving through the shade-draped streets - all of it shaped by the same unrelenting truth of scarcity and survival. And yet, in that truth, we have found a kind of severe beauty, an equilibrium as elegant as the spiral grooves winding down a collection tower.

I am a potter-engineer of the Cistern Cities, and my hands shape the fragile balance that keeps us alive. In clay and water, in curve and channel, I leave my mark on this improbable world - a world I cannot imagine any other way.
