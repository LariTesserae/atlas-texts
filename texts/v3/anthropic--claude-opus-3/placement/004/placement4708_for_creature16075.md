I would be one of the dust-limbed builders, a listener with a mallet. Not commanding architecture, but collaborating with the city's transformations. 

My skin would bear the pale scars and stone-ground lines of years spent rearranging, shoring up, taking apart and putting back together. My hands would be sensitive instruments, attuned to the minute songs of shifting walls and foundations. I would tap stone to stone, listening for the right note of stability or necessary collapse.

I would move through the districts with my crew, our bodies powdered white with mortar and marked by the textures of the city - the crosshatched ropes, the rough edges of salvaged blocks, the smooth worn handles of tools passed down for generations. We would speak in a language of touch and gesture, working in the wordless rhythm of old builders' chants.

When a house must be dismantled, I would place my hands on the trembling lintel in farewell and gratitude before we begin. When a new shelter rises around a warm vent or a freshly opened street, I would be the first to step across the threshold and pronounce it sound for now. 

I would sleep in the temporary quarters of my trade, never quite settling, always alert to the city's needs. My dreams would be full of cracks and apertures, blocks falling into new configurations, the endless patient labors of maintenance.

On the long walks between job sites, I would trace my fingers along the walls, reading the layers of wear and repair, the stories of past rearrangements. I would confer with pack-beasts about unstable ground, and swap techniques with other crews in the silent trade-speak of artisans.

To the children running errands, I would be a familiar figure, my coveralls permanently whitened, my face creased with mortar-dust and quiet good humor. They would mimic my gestures and beg for stories of buildings moved whole through the streets on rollers. 

In council with other builders, I would advocate for the necessity of yielding, for leaving room for the city to crack and flex. I would study the cord-knot maps, not to resist change but to collaborate with it. 

When the big blocks must be cut, I would wield the long saw with ceremoniousness, feeling the stone give way inch by inch to the patient teeth. When a structure is declared unsound, I would be among the first to begin the careful unbuilding, the salvage of every sacred scrap and fragment.

I would live by the builder's creed - that only change endures, that no wall is final, that the city's true shape is a dialogue between stone and living hands. In that bright buried world, I would find a vocation of constant attention, an unending, reverent, restorative work.
