I imagine myself as a Listener, pressing my ear to the damp stone, feeling the vibrations of the shifting rock through the cartilage that has grown thick and cup-shaped around my ears from years of attentive listening. My skin is a living map of the plateau's minerals - swirls of green copper, streaks of rust orange, patches of limestone white - each marking a different place, a different stone I have communed with.

I dwell in one of the deep chambers, where the rock is oldest and most stable, though even here the moisture seeps through hairline cracks, the air always cool and slightly metallic on my tongue. My chamber is small and simple - a pallet of woven moss, a few stone jars holding water and dried snailmeat, a shelf where I keep my most prized possessions: a piece of glowing fungus that I cultivated into the shape of a twisting spiral, a perfect snail shell the size of my head, translucent and shimmering, and a chunk of stone veined with a mineral that glows softly blue in the perpetual dimness.

My days are spent in a kind of wandering meditation, moving slowly across the plateau from one listening spot to the next. I know every depression where the water pools, every boulder that chimes with a clear note when struck, every crevice where the steam vents hiss and sigh. I hear the stone's intentions in these voices, and it's my role to interpret them, to warn the Foundling Folk of changes to come.

When I press my ear to the rock, the rest of the world falls away. The thunder of moth wings, the eerie songs of the climbing beasts, the constant trickle and drip of water - all of it fades into the background as I sink into the deeper frequencies of the stone. I feel the slow stretch and compression of ancient layers, the minute shudders that precede a shift. Sometimes, in moments of profound connection, I swear I can almost sense the stone's dreams - slow, tectonic things, heavy with mineral memories.

As I walk, I often hum to myself, weaving my own melodies into the plateau's constant music. The tunes change from day to day, shaped by the particular song of the stone beneath my feet at any given moment. Sometimes, when I pass another Listener on my rounds, we pause and exchange a snatch of melody, a bit of rock-whispered wisdom.

When I return to my chamber at night, I often find small gifts left by my people - a particularly resonant piece of stone, a morsel of sweet fungus, a shell with an iridescent interior. They honor my service, my willingness to immerse myself so deeply in the plateau's voice that my own has become strange, my speech patterned with long pauses and sudden, guttural inflections.

Sometimes, as I lie in the damp darkness, I wonder what I would be if I had been born in the lowlands, in a place of stability and permanence. The thought seems as alien as the idea of a sky without clouds, a night without the moth-thunder. I am a creature of this becoming-place, shaped by its constant changes as surely as the water-catchers are shaped by the ceaseless condensation.

In my deepest dreams, I imagine myself becoming stone - my flesh slowly mineralizing, my blood replaced by seeping groundwater, my thoughts slowing to the tempo of geological time. I see myself as a living stalagmite, a testament to the transformative power of this place, my consciousness diffused through layers of sediment and memory.

But always, I wake to the sound of water, to the stone's ever-changing song, and I know my purpose anew. I am a Listener, a reader of the plateau's intentions, and for as long as the Foundling Folk need my skills, I will wander this shifting landscape, my ear always pressed to the damp and living rock, my strange, mineral-mapped body a bridge between the human and the geological, a living embodiment of the adaptation and beauty that defines life on Keening Shelf.
