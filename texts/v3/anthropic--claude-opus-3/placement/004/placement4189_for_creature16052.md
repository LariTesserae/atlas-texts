In this city, I would be one of the legal engineers who maintain the brass calibration lines and argue with overambitious streets. 

I would move through the damp, cold air wrapped in layers of felt, foil and translucent resin, my breath fogging the inside of my condensation mask. My gloved hands would be thickened at the fingertips from years of reading the textures of the city - the temperature of walls, the subtle variations in the metal of railings that indicate their age and stability.

Each morning, I would collect my tools - angle combs, spirit levels that measure more than the horizontal, maps engraved on thin sheets of stone - and set out to walk my assigned districts. I would run my hands along the seams where one locality's physics abut another's, feeling for slippages, for boundaries overstepping their assigned positions.

Where a street has migrated too far, impinging on its neighbors, I would unroll my maps, consult the brass lines set into the paving, and begin a slow negotiation. Reasoning with the very stones, citing precedent and charter, I would coax the willful paving back into its proper configuration. Where persuasion failed, I would resort to more concrete measures - boundary stones repositioned, survey stakes driven deep, flows of water and memory redirected to erode the street's ambition.

It is delicate work, balancing the competing demands of place and populace. A street must be stable enough to walk, but not so rigid that it stifles the city's careful, continual growth. The avenues and alleys have their own stubborn aspirations that must be respected even as they are reined in.

In the slow-sound square, I would pause after each argument, waiting as my words crossed the intervening space. The patience the city has taught me serves well here. I am used to delays between cause and effect, to actions that ripple out across time as well as space.

On the days when the tree in the central plaza drops one of its rare, hard seeds, I would be among those who gathered to witness, then carry the seed to the cistern where its fellows wait for their enigmatic purpose to be revealed. Touching my hand to the tree's bark, I would feel a resonance, as if the city's past and future were a slow current moving through its roots and branches.

When my work was done, I would stop at a tea room built into the warm heart of a wall, sip the steam from a bowl of pale, puckering tea and listen to the old people tell stories of the city's earlier days, before its laws were as entangled as they are now, when reality was a fractionally simpler thing. Or perhaps the telling is just simpler with the memory of youth.

On my way home, I would nod to the auditors as they made their rounds, hands outstretched to question the structures. I would step carefully over the white crustaceans clicking along the gutters in their ritualistic dozens. At the door to my modest rooms, I would turn the lock that still remembers the pattern of my grandfather's hand.

This is where I would make my careful, considerate life - in a place that demanded constant attention but repaid it with a strange, cold, condensed beauty. A city to be lived in slowly, negotiated with daily, understood as an organism that I was both part of and tasked with tending. A home that would never stop teaching me to walk softly, think conditionally, and listen always for the quiet, vital pulse of infrastructure.
