In this strange, symbiotic world of bio-mechanical fusion, I imagine myself as one of the Graft-tenders. 

I would be an older woman, with skin weathered and creased like bark, stained permanently with chlorophyll and grease. My hands would be strong and sure from decades of coaxing metal and wood to merge.

I'd wear a vest with countless pockets, each containing some specialized tool or enzyme or growth medium. Spools of organic polymer tape would hang from my belt, alongside pouches of metal shavings that could be pressed into compromised bark to seal leaks. My boots would be studded with iron cleats for scaling the larger trunks.

Most days, I'd make my rounds through the district, listening with a practiced ear for the discordant notes that indicated a troubled graft. I might pause by a steam pipe, laying my palm against it to feel the rhythm of the vapor pumping through, then trace it back to the valve-branch juncture to check for overgrowth. 

Or I'd spot a telltale streak of rust on bark and know that a beetle colony needed to be introduced there to chew away the oxidation and excrete their protective oils. I'd reach into a pocket for a vial of the insects, whispering encouragement as I dabbed them onto the affected area.

The most challenging cases would be when a graft rejected entirely, the mechanical and biological components unable to find their balance. Then I would have to open my kit of salves and unguents, carefully trimming away the necrotic tissue and soothing the angry metal until the two could be eased back into symbiosis. It might take days of patient attention, singing the old working songs to the failed junction until it began to knit back together.

On rare occasions, I'd be called to attend a birth—those delicate moments when a new strain of bio-mechanical hybrid was being attempted. Perhaps a new type of photosynthetic panel was being grafted onto a young factory-tree, or an experimental alloy was being introduced to replace a disease-vulnerable wood. I'd oversee the process, making minute adjustments to the hormonal balance, the nutrient mix in the sap, the temperature of the growth chamber. 

And if successful, I'd be the first to see the strange new leaf unfurl, half-green and half-copper, or to hear the new branch-valve shudder into life. I'd place my stethoscope against the bark and listen to the hybrid heartbeat, learning its tempo. 

In the evenings, I'd lecture to the apprentices, my voice rasping with the same steam-roughness as the pipes'. I'd make them recite the Ten Thousand Grafts, the encyclopedia of every bio-mechanical fusion known to our kind. I'd drill them on the warning signs of rejection, the subtle markers of healthy symbiosis. "The graft is everything," I'd tell them, "the graft is what allows the district to breathe."

On my rest days, I'd tend to my own small section of the district, a grove of delicate copper-leafed trees that produced a particularly pure distillate for the boilers. I'd prune them with my oldest, most delicate tools, collecting the filings to be pressed into medallions for the next generation of Graft-tenders. In quiet moments, I'd lean my forehead against their trunks and whisper the stories of my own mentor, and her mentor before her, a chain of knowledge grafted down through the generations.

And at night, in my moss-lined bed chamber pulsing gently with the factory-tree's sap-flow, I'd dream of new hybrids yet to be attempted, my mind spinning with visions of alloys and enzymes and sap, until sleep took me and I drifted off to the distant clanking lullaby of the district's iron lungs, content in my purpose, another organ in the breathing whole.
