In this magnificent, drowned world of rain-lashed islands, sunken forests, and stubborn human ingenuity, I imagine myself as one of the great cranes - long-legged, gray-feathered, watchful. 

I stand motionless in the shallows of a sedge flat that was once a valley, my form mirrored in the pewter-dark water. Mist hangs low, blurring the mossy hummocks and dwarf birches into impressionistic daubs of green and silver. The air is utterly still, but never silent - beneath the shushing of reed stems lies the muted metronome of waves, the distant purr of a turbine.

At dawn and dusk, I raise my head and issue forth a wild, bugling cry that echoes off the rounded shoulders of drowned mountains. It is an ancient sound, well-suited to this place where time has worn the land down to its bones. My kind have witnessed the slow drowning, the gradual softening of sharp edges into something kinder, wiser. We remember roads and spires now lost to water and moss.

I hunt patiently in the cold channels, spearing fish and frogs with my dagger beak. I know the good feeding grounds - the drowned orchard where perch shelter beneath gnarled branches; the submerged plaza where carp root through sunken cobblestones. When storms rage and the water rises, churning with debris, I take shelter in the lee of a granite island, my head tucked beneath one wing.

Summers, I nest among the reeds with my mate, weaving a raft of stems anchored against the current. Our chicks are ungainly balls of fuzz, but they quickly learn the ways of water - how to stand motionless and let minnows nibble their toes; how to stalk through shallows on stilt legs. We teach them the important things: where the eel-grass meadows grow thickest, which sunken towers make the best roosts, how to read the weather in the wind's voice and the wave's pulse.

Sometimes I see the basin people in their boats, hauling nets or mending cables. They nod to me as one worker to another. We are not so different, the cranes and the humans. We each adapt in our own way to this half-drowned world, balancing on the knife's edge between land and water.

Like them, I am both watcher and survivor. My days are spent in careful observation - a ripple that might be a fish, a distant engine-cough, a shift in the breeze that carries rain. Vigilance is the price of enduring in a landscape where the only constant is change.

When night falls and the mists roll back in, I roost atop a rust-furred navigation marker, one leg tucked up beneath me. The marker's lamp sputters, sending flares of light across dark water. Somewhere nearby, an otter chirps. A fish splashes. The slap of waves on stone is a lullaby, ancient and unceasing.

I dream crane dreams of soaring over drowned cities where towers of glass and steel reach up from the depths like the arms of the drowning. I dream of a time when the land dries and the waters recede, revealing a strange new world of sculpted stone and scoured steel. But for now, this is my home - a place of water and wings, of hidden rhythms and slow transformations. And I am its witness, gray-cloaked and still, standing sentinel at the edge of past and future.
