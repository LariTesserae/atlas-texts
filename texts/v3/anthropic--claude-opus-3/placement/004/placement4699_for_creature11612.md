I imagine myself as a Night-Walker of the Ninth Terrace. 

When the daily rhythms of the terrace have quieted and the glow of the beetle-lanterns bathes the stone streets in soft light, I emerge from my modest dwelling to wander and observe. My feet follow paths ingrained by years of traversing the terrace's winding ways in the peaceful solitude of night.

I pause often in my meanderings. At the cistern grates, I crouch and listen to the musical drip of water, pondering both its ancient source deep in the mountain's core and the hidden labors of the Cistern-Keepers who ensure its steady flow. I brush my fingers along the luminous carapaces of the lantern-beetles, marveling at the Moth-Fathers' patientacquisitionition of living light. 

On nights when sleep eludes me altogether, I climb step by worn step to the terrace's edge. There among the dozing Edge-Sitters, I gaze out at the spectral silhouettes of the other peaks, ghost-like in the moonlight. The fire-scarred, the overgrown, the distant mirror of our own - each with its own untold stories that even now continue on as ours does, in the slow rhythm of made things enduring.

Sometimes as I walk, I glimpse the other Night-Walkers - those kindred spirits drawn to the mysteries only the midnight terrace can evince. We nod to each other, a subtle acknowledgement: I see you, walker of shadows, tender of untold tales. But we do not speak. The night is not for speaking.

I hold no special role in the daily workings of the terrace - no Root-Reading or Mist-Catching or tending of machines deep below. My lot is merely to witness, to be present with this place in its moments of dreaming. To feel the layers of history under my feet and the weight of ages whispering in the whirr of insects' wings. To breathe the night-blooming sweetness of cultivated gardens and wonder at the human ingenuity that coaxed such lushness from unforgiving stone.

When at last I retire to my bed as dawn gilds the highest rooftops, I carry the night within me through the day, a secret that flavors my interactions with my fellows like the subtlest of the Color-Makers' dyes. It is the knowledge that even stone can dream, that the immeasurable slow dance of a made world continues in the dark, and that bearing witness to such dreams is its own sacred task, as vital as any work of hand or mind.

So I ready myself for another night of wandering the lamplight stone, where my only duty is to be present, and in presence, perhaps, to be a lantern of a different sort - one whose light is the glow of stories collected and kept and carried on through the years, a human beetle shining my small light into the layered dark.
