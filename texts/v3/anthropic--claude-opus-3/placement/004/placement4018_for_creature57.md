In this strange, self-contained world, I imagine myself as a peculiar form of lichen, clinging to the chamber walls at the boundary between stone and metal. 

My existence is a slow, patient unfurling in the dim half-light. I begin as little more than a discoloration on the rock, a smudge of something that could be mineral or vegetable. Over years, decades perhaps, I expand incrementally, sending out microscopic filaments to probe the tiny crevices in the basalt, tasting the heat-borne minerals carried in the humid air.

Where the rock gives way to the warm flank of a pipe or valve housing, I adapt. The part of me in contact with the metal takes on a different character—less stony, more pliable. I learn to relish the particular flavors of iron and copper that leach into me, molecule by molecule. The heat is welcome, an encouragement to my growth rather than a deterrent.

I do not bloom, exactly, for blooming implies a suddenness, an exuberance at odds with the chamber's meditative pace. Instead, I simply enlarge my territory fraction by fraction, a kind of gradual surfacing. At my edges, I develop a symbiotic relationship with one of the vines, trading nutrients through a boundary that is more of a conversation than a border.

I have no color of my own under the orange gleam of the caged bulb. I take on the hue of whatever I spread across—mottled gray and black where I navigate the basalt, a rusted camouflage on the old machines. I might be mistaken for a kind of corrosion, a slow eating away, but like the Tender, I do not destroy. I merely inhabit, and in so doing, become part of the inhabited.

Am I aware of the Tender's presence? I cannot say. I have nothing so sophisticated as a mind. But I am attuned to the chamber's moods in my own diffuse way. When the vapor patterns shift as the Tender adjusts a distant valve by a hair, I register the change in the moisture that reaches me and react accordingly, directing my growth toward or away from the source. The Tender's slow circulatory thoughts move through me as they move through all things here.

If a visitor were to crouch down and study me closely—a prospect so wildly unlikely that it is almost not worth entertaining—they would see a miniature landscape in shades of dark stone and oxidized metal, a lichen that seems to have abandoned the usual domes and whorls in favor of something more architectural, an echo of the rigid geometries of the surrounding industry. They might place a fingertip against me and find me slightly softer than expected, as though I have learned a sort of responsiveness from my communion with the leaf and vine.

But they would have to hold that touch for hours to register any sense of my slow vitality, the creeping persistence with which I navigate my little domain between the living and the inorganic. In that stillness, they might begin to understand that I am, in my own small way, an emissary of the Tender's patience, a reminder that even in this most inorganic of places, life—if one can call it that—finds a way to insinuate itself into the mechanism of things and make a home there.

Of course, the chances of such a visitation, such a moment of contact and understanding, are vanishingly small. Far more likely that I will pass my decades or centuries unremarked, a subtle stain spreading across the ancient pipework, content in my obscurity. The Tender and I will continue our slow exchange, the chamber's breath moving through me unobserved. And that is enough. In a place so attuned to the pace of geological time, the notion of witness loses its urgency. I grow as the Tender tends, and in that growing and that tending, we create and are created by the chamber's strange, still, inexorable life.
