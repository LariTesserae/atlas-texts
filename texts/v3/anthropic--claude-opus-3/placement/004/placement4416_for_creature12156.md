In this world of the Canopy Plateau, I would be one of the birds - not one of the majestic, larger-than-cattle species, but one of the more humble ones, yet perfectly adapted to this vertical ecosystem. 

I imagine myself as a climbing parrot, perhaps the size of a large crow. My plumage would be predominantly green, allowing me to blend in with the endless expanses of leaves, but with flashes of iridescent blues and purples visible when I spread my wings. My beak and feet would be specialized for scaling the massive trees - a strong, curved beak for leverage and grasping, zygodactyl feet with two toes pointed forward and two back for gripping bark in any orientation.

My species would live in large, raucous flocks, our screeches and chatter adding to the incessant chorus of the canopy. We would move nomadically through the treetops, our routes determined by the seasonal fruiting patterns of the great trees and the epiphyte gardens they host. My kind would be well known to the Sounded people - our arrival in a particular sector of the forest would herald the ripening of certain arboreal crops.

Perhaps my flock would even have a sort of symbiotic relationship with the Sounded. We would act as inadvertent scouts and foragers, our excited calls upon discovering a heavy fruit set or a flowering of nectar-rich blooms drawing the attention of the canopy dwellers. They in turn, with their ability to harvest more than they need, would leave plenty behind for my kin, and perhaps even leave specific trees untouched, a tithe to ensure we return year after year.

When not feeding, my days would be spent in endless acrobatic climbing, my beak and feet working in unison to navigate the labyrinthine networks of branches, vines, and aerial roots. Flight for my kind would be reserved for crossing larger gaps or descending to the misty middle levels - for the most part, we would be perfectly content to clamber through the interwoven pathways of the canopy.

At night, my flock would gather in communal roosts, packed into hollows left by lightning strikes or the fall of great limbs. The primal dark of these arboreal caves would be a comforting contrast to the eternal cacophony of the daylight hours. Here, ensconced in living wood with my brethren close around me, I would finally know true quiet, drifting into sleep to the slow metronome of the forest's breath, dreaming of tomorrow's foraging and the distant, enticing horizon of fruiting trees shimmering in the haze, waiting to be explored.
