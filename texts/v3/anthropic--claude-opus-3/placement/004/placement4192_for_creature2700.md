In this wet, quiet plateau town, I imagine myself as a keeper of channels.

I am an elder who has spent a lifetime learning the slow ways of water across stone. My hands are broad and strong from years of clearing leaves, setting stones, and testing surfaces. I walk with a reed staff, not because I am frail, but to feel the ground through an extension of my arm.

My role is to watch and listen. Each morning, I rise and make my rounds along the settlement's raised paths. I pause often, closing my eyes to hear the faint music of moisture: a trickle under a slab, a gutter breathing heavier than it should, the first whisper of a seep in a foundation wall. Where water speaks, I answer with action. A pebble to divert a flow. A strip of clay to seal a crack. A word to a young mason about a stone laid without enough care. 

In the afternoons, I sit beside the larger channels, watching their clarity, their depth, their speed. I trail my fingers in the main currents, reading the strength of what cannot be seen. When I feel silt rising, I call for dredging before the basin can choke. When a runnel slows, I send word to check its source for blockage. I am a safeguard, a living gauge, my senses tuned to the plateau's hydraulic pulse.

The younger people come to me with questions. Where to dig a new well. How deep to set the pilings for a floor. Which slope to terrace first. I close my eyes and think in spans of stone, in the gradual pressure of water, in the span of years a reed bed can survive if tended well. I try to give answers that will last longer than my own life.

Sometimes I simply sit with the children beside a pool and tell them stories. Not animal fables, but the old histories of our town. The long-ago season when the western cistern cracked and we learned to line the basins with finer clay. The year the reed crop failed and we had to trade our seed. The day a great fog covered the plateau and an elder walked off the high edge, teaching us to plant thorn hedges at the rim. I pass on what the water has taught us.

When the light starts to fade, I make my way to the central stone basin. There I listen for the first drops from the communal roofs: the water clock that marks day's end. As the surface stirs with ripples, I watch the stone faces around me relax. Another day the town has not faltered. Another day we have balanced on the edge of dampness and air.

At night, I dream of ancient seas rising through cracks, teaching the mountain to remember being ocean floor. Of channels cut by hand and kept by generations. Of water traveling human paths, learning to flow where we ask.  In my dreams, the membrane between plateau and townspeople blurs. Our hands become tributaries. Our blood becomes the moisture in the cistern courts.

I wake to the soft percussion of a town built on water's music: the drum of rain on a roof, the flute of a channel, the long bass of a settling wall. I rise to make my rounds again. To keep the current flowing steady through the stone body of our home.

I am a channel keeper, a servant of unseen streams, one old drop in the long quiet current of this place. My work is slow and small, but it lets life persist where it otherwise might not. In a town with no other elders but people, someone must learn to speak the language of water. That is the shape I have grown into, the way living here has made me.
