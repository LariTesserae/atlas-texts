In this wondrous world of living cities and verdant industry, I imagine myself as one of the bio-engineers, deeply attuned to the rhythms and needs of the great organism that is our city.

My skin would bear the same jade translucence as my fellow citizens, a testament to the slow alteration of our biology to suit this place. But beyond that, I would be marked by the tools of my trade—the lichen robes that shift and glow with my thoughts, displaying the status of a dozen ongoing projects in patterns only my fellow engineers can fully read.

My fingers, long and multiply jointed, would be stained with the juices of the plants I tend—saps and secretions that can direct the growth of a trunk or coax a new strain of bioluminescent algae into being. I would smell of the dense, green places where I work, of crushed leaves and rich fungal soil.

In the laboratory gardens, I would move with the patience of tree-time, watching my experiments unfold across years and decades. The seedlings I plant might take a human lifetime to mature into full districts, their twining forms following the templates I sketch in pheromones and growth hormone. 

I would speak the chemical language of the city as fluently as I speak to my human colleagues. The messages I send through the vascular systems of the great trunks would be poems in auxin and cytokinin, shaping the urban landscape with each whispered verse. In my rare idle moments, I might simply sit with a hand pressed to bark, listening to the vast, slow thoughts of the trees and trying to discern the greater pattern we all serve.

My home would be a pod I've grown myself, a cluster of symbiotically interwoven plant species that process my waste, feed me with their fruits, and open their stomata to ventilate my rooms. To enter, I brush a genetically keyed patch of moss, and the living door dilates to admit me.

In my work, I would wrestle with the deep questions of our existence here. How do we best balance the needs of the human and the non-human as we guide the city's growth? What new morphologies might allow us to integrate even more deeply with the urban ecosystem? How can we encode our values and history into the very genes of the city, so that it remembers us long after we individual gardeners have returned to the soil?

This is the only life I can imagine, this patient, green existence spent shaping and being shaped by a city that breathes. I am a bio-engineer of the Canopy Cities, and my work is the slow shaping of eternity, the crafting of a living future from the green dreams of the present.
