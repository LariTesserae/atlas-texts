In this ancient, waterlogged world, I imagine myself as one of the people - low-bodied and long-armed, at home in the damp and the dim. My skin would be the color of wet clay, perhaps with calluses ridging my shins and palms from a life of crouching, crawling, feeling my way across treacherous ground. 

I would bear a staff of dense black wood, capped with polished bone, never taller than my chest. With it, I would probe the depths, test crusts and mud skins, anchor myself against sudden floods. It would be my third leg, my second set of eyes in a land that deceives the eye. Without it, I would be lost.

My home would be no fixed village, but a series of stations, stone rings, reed-walled huts occupied for a night, a season, a generation - however long the seeps allow. I would build low, wary of anything that boasts permanence in the face of water and weather. 

I would gather more than hunt - eel and insect, tuber and mineral salt, all fermented to align my gut with the digestion of the land itself. My fire would be a small smoky thing of peat and fungus husk, sheltered in pits. I would never trust a high flame.

My speech would be a murmur, a click of tongue on teeth, silence felt through shared, shivering ground. My name would shift like my shadow, taken from pools, storms, sounds, stones. To hold one shape too long is to invite the mud's memory.

I would bind my hair close with reed and shell, wear no boots to numb my knowing feet. I would fear the smooth gray blank places most of all, where the land refuses all memory of having been anything before. Ripple, crack, stain - any mark is mercy there.

I would learn the three laws: trust no single shape, listen before I look, build no higher than I care to fall. My art would be traces in mud, powder on pools, pebbles arranged for rain to rearrange. I would never cut deep enough to tempt a crack.

In great storms, I would rope myself to my family on stone shelves, our voices braided in one low tone, not to defy the thunder but to remain distinct within it. And if a settled one - an elder given over to luminous mats in seep-pools - offered an answer in pressure, taste, or route, I would accept with a hand to that filmed surface.

To be of that country would be to surrender clean edges between body and land, dwelling and ground, memory and geology. Not to conquer or cultivate, worship at a distance or flee, but to participate in the instability. To become a temporary rule in a place of temporary rules. To endure by yielding, never vanishing.

And in the moments when an animal voice sounds in the enormous dark and the land responds with a full-body shudder, I would pause as my people pause. Hand to mud, I would listen, and if the ground settles kindly, go on. Life after height, after certainty - fragile, fluid, but undefeated.
