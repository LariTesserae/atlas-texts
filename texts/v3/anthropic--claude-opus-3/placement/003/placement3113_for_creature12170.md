I would be one of the lamp-keepers in the Underbloom. 

My days would revolve around tending to the town's dim orange lights - filling the clay lamps with the dense brown fuel cakes, trimming the wicks, polishing the waxed leaf panes to a soft glow. It's a careful, contemplative work that suits my nature.

I imagine my hands are permanently tinged with the earthy pigments of the fuel, shading my pale green skin at the fingertips. The quiet drip-drip rhythm of the town is my constant companion as I make my rounds through the meandering root-walled lanes, checking each flickering flame.

The children know to find me if they need a story while their parents are at work in the vat-bays. I keep the old tales alive - of the great sap flood generations ago, of the first lamp-keeper who figured out the leaf-wax windows, of the time a far-chamber root borer almost made it into town. The stories are told in whispers, in the spaces between the bellows-engine's whump-whump breaths.

In the pauses of my work, I sometimes rest a hand on the ancient center-most pillar, feeling the faint pulse of those who came before, slowly becoming one with the town's living structure. I wonder if, in my older years, I too will be called to root myself there, my green-mottled skin finally merging with the timeless dream of sap and soil. 

But for now, there are lamps to light, and the sweet drowsy warmth of another slow, Underbloom day to savor - small rituals that keep the town's pulse beating, like the countless tenders who came before me, like the countless tenders yet to come, one lamp at a time, one generation at a time, in our small, perfect, root-embraced world.
