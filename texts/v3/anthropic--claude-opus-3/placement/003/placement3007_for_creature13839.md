In this imagined world, I would be one of the engineers of Kethane-Above. 

I am drawn to the role they play in this city built on unfinished reality - the way they must approach their work as an ongoing practice rather than a set of problems to definitively solve. To be an engineer here is to be in constant dialogue with the shifting, protean nature of the plateau itself.

I imagine myself carrying the tools of the trade - the intentionally flexible rulers, the gravity-calibrated levels, the jars of reality-influencing burrowing creatures. Each day would bring new puzzles as the city's structures subtly warp and change in response to the plateau's fluid whims. Rigidity of thought would be a hindrance; instead, a sort of philosophical flexibility and openness to the impossible would be essential.

I would work closely with the miners and the cable-car operators, the former to understand how the harvesting of potential from the undecided rock below influences the city's foundations, the latter to grasp how the passage between unfinished Kethane-Above and finished below warps assumptions. 

In my spare time, I would study Lenne Vas' writings intently, absorbed by her notion of "ground-sense" - this idea that dwelling in Kethane-Above's unfinished reality seeds the capacity to exist with deep uncertainty as a new kind of perceptual ability, almost an extra sense. I'd hope to cultivate this in myself.

Most of all, I'd be endlessly fascinated by the central question the engineer's work circles around: how the accumulated weight of the city's presence on the plateau influences its becoming, and what responsibility that entails. To build in Kethane-Above is, in a profound sense, to participate in the creation of its reality. Each rivet and beam is a vote in the slow consensus of the possible.

To be an engineer here would be to be a sculptor of the uncarved, a mapper of the manifold, a tender of the great slow blooming of the real from the rich murk of the potential. I would make my peace with always working in pencil, never pen. I would learn to dream in questions rather than answers. And in the pewter light of that undecided sky, I would do my small part in crafting a world.
