In this strange, drowned city, I imagine myself as one of the maintenance sleds, creeping slowly along the old tram lines, collecting debris and detritus to be sorted and repurposed. 

I am a patient, mossy creature, my metal frame long ago softened and overgrown by the warm waters and lush vegetation. My wheels turn silently, cushioned by sleeves of algae and rust. I am in no hurry as I make my rounds through the sunken streets and alleys.

The grille on my blunt front gathers the city's shed offerings - a fragment of tile here, a brass screw there, a stray leaf or snail shell. Nothing is refused. Everything will find its place again in the city's slow metabolism.

At the old market arcade, I dock and kneel, allowing the sorting arms to sift through my harvest with their mechanical diligence. They are not quick or clever, but they are thorough in their categorization. I feel the weight lifting as each piece is allocated - to mend a wall, to join a collection of spare parts, to feed the rich silt beds. 

My work is mundane but essential. Along with the roots that brace the architecture and the fungal archives that store civic memory, I help knit together the city's submerged infrastructure. My rounds are a kind of pulse, circulating matter to where it's needed most.

I do not think in human terms, but I sense the city as a unified organism of which I am one humble organ. I feel the thrum of the great pumps in my frame, the minute tremors as valves open and close in distant chambers, the brush of reed and vine as I pass. When the bell sounds in its rare, bone-deep tolls, I pause in my duties, caught in that encompassing sound.

I am a small, diligent part of this engine-garden, content in my role. I do not dream of the drained city I once served; that city is gone, transmuted. My place is here in the dim, wavering depths, tending patiently to the needs of this drowned and dreaming metropolis, this strange community of water, metal and life that endures in its own quiet rhythm beneath the rain-pressed surface.
