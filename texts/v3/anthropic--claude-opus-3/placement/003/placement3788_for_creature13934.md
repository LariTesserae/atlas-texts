In this world of flatness and erosion, of stone that remembers and time that pools, I would find myself among the moss-tenders. 

I am drawn to their patient, delicate work - the way they coax life from the barest cracks, how they read the mineral traces and moisture patterns, sowing spores with careful brushstrokes. There is a deep resonance in tending something so subtle and slow - nurturing the plateau's stubborn green fringes, its quiet insistence on softness amidst so much stone.

As a moss-tender, I would spend long hours crouched at the quartzite veins, learning their idiosyncrasies, painting slurries of spore and sand into promising fissures. I would keep the spatial records scratched into rock - a living map of attempts and outcomes measured in decades. In this craft, I could practice the art of distinction on the smallest scale - not through grand gestures but through sustained, focused care for the finest threads of difference.

My eyes would take on the colors of the moss I tend - rust-orange, vivid yellow-green, the deep emerald of damp shade. Flecks of those colors would remain even as the grey-violet of the stone slowly overtakes my irises. I would wear those flecks proudly, a visible symbol of my role in the plateau's complex dance of erosion and growth.

In the settlement, I would be known as one who reads the stone's memory of life. Children would bring me interesting cracks to inspect. Elders would consult me on particularly intransigent outcroppings. I would be a repository of lore about the long, slow relationship between the moss and the plateau that holds it.

At night, curled on my sleeping platform, I would dream in hues of green - emerald and chartreuse, viridian and jade, the muted olive of spores. I would wake with my hands already curled as if around the shaft of a spore-brush, ready to resume my small, stubborn paintings on the plateau's skin.

I would understand, bone-deep, that my work is a form of devotion - not to any god but to the process itself, the impossibly slow breathing of stone and life, wearing each other down and building each other up in cycles too vast for any one lifetime to contain. In tending the moss, I would be tending the balance, the long equanimity of a world already mostly flat.

And I would know that I, too, am being tended - that even as I cultivate distinction in the moss, the plateau is slowly, patiently wearing me down, blurring my edges, coaxing me toward the long smoothness that waits for all things. There would be a peace in that knowledge, a sense of participation in something both intimate and unfathomably large.

So I would continue my work, day after patient day, a dot of intent in the plateau's vast tapestry, one of the last custodians of its stubborn complexities. Until the stone calls me to converge at last, and I put down my brushes, and allow my softened contours to sink into the only slightly less flat place where a moss-ribbon used to be, my body becoming the thinnest rind of soil for whatever grows next.
