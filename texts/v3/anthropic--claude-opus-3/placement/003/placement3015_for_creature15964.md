At night, I would sleep fitfully in the damp pump house, dreaming of vines coiling around control wheels, of a tiny frog-like creature with skin the color of ash and eyes like gauge-glass singing a song that sounds like metal ticking and water dripping in complex rhythms. 

By day, I would read the basin's moods in the glitter of wet leaves, the speed of the ancient flywheel, the mineral stains blooming on rock faces. I would tie bright scraps of cloth to mark the safe ways, would coax and scold the stubborn machines in a creaking voice that echoes the mountain's own knocks and hisses.

But that knowledge matters less with each passing season, as the mountain slowly reclaims our work for its own purposes, as root and rust and water reshape our machines and our intentions into something new.

I remain, an old green-stained figure puttering amid the pipes, checking the ledgers, listening always - an organic valve in an inorganic landscape, at once out of place and utterly essential, until the day I too am overgrown, absorbed, and the basin's next caretaker arrives to learn its secrets anew.
