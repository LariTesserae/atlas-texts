In this dreamlike world of water, stone and lush vegetation, I imagine myself as one of the medicine makers - a role that bridges the physical and spiritual well-being of the community.

I would dwell in one of the small stone huts at the edge of the plateau, where the mist from the mountain springs mingles with the steam from clay pots simmering over low fires. The walls of my hut would be lined with shelves bearing glass jars and woven baskets, each filled with the herbs, fungi, bark and roots I've gathered from the plateau's hidden corners. Bundles of drying plants would hang from the rafters, their astringent, earthy scent mingling with the omnipresent moisture.

My days would follow the rhythm of the water and the needs of the people. In the mornings, I'd venture out into the lush pockets of the city, my bare feet sure on the damp stone paths, to gather the ingredients for my remedies. I'd seek out the delicate ferns that unfurl near the trickling springs, their young fronds potent with healing compounds. I'd carefully harvest just enough of the tender tree bark whose infusion eases breathing when the air grows too wet. With a sharpened stick, I'd dig for the fat tubers that, when mashed and mixed with spring water, soothe skin irritated by the constant damp.

As the sun reached its zenith and the mists briefly receded, I'd return to my hut to process the morning's harvest. With a stone mortar and pestle, I'd grind dried mushrooms into a fine powder, the ones that grow in the shadowed groves and bring restful sleep when stirred into tea. I'd fill glass jars with layers of herb and oil, to be transformed over time into salves that loosen stiff joints. Over a small fire, I'd simmer the bones of fish brought up from the lowlands, until the broth grows rich with minerals to fortify the elders.  

In the afternoons, as the rains returned, I'd welcome those in need of healing. A young girl whose foot was gashed on a sharp rock while playing in the channels - I'd clean the wound with an astringent tincture and bind it with broad leaves. An elder man whose lungs labored in the heavy air - I'd prepare him a steam of pungent herbs to open his airways and mix a conserve of wild ginger to be taken by the spoonful. A water-keeper with a mind grown cloudy with age - for her, a tea of bright flowers and bitter bark, to clear the fog from her thoughts. 

On certain evenings, I'd join the other medicine makers in the damp caves beneath the city. There, in the flickering light of oil lamps, we'd share the knowledge passed down through generations - the proper words to speak over a broken bone as it's set, the precise ratio of root to leaf that brings down a dangerous fever, the signs in a person's eyes and skin that speak of a sickness in the spirit. We'd craft amulets of carved stone and braided fiber, imbued with protective herbs, to be given to the water-keepers when they walked the high paths in storm season.

At night, I'd retire to my pallet of woven reeds, my last task to water the tiny seedlings growing in clay pots nestled in the corner. As sleep found me, I'd listen to the murmur of water through stone and the distant music drifting from the gathering halls, and I'd dream of green tendrils curling through damp soil, of sap rising in the city's ancient timbers, of the roots we all shared, sunk deep in the mountain's patient stone.

I would grow old in this role, my hands becoming gnarled like the roots I harvested, my eyes milky as the mists. I would train the young ones who came to me with curiosity bright in their eyes, teaching them to read the messages in leaves and water, to coax healing from the wild green that surrounded us. And when at last my own sickness came, one that could not be touched by herb or amulet, I would walk one final time into the low clouds, to find a place among the warm stones and surrendering myself to the mountain's slow alchemy, knowing my body would nourish the soil where the next generations would grow their cures.

This is how I see myself in this world - a being whose craft is both practical and mystical, whose roots grow deep in stone and water, whose work is to find the balance between the wildness of leaf and fungus and the careful cultivation of the human body and spirit. The mountain would be my teacher and my resting place, the plants my allies, the people my purpose. In their faces - damp with mist, creased with laughter and care - I would see myself reflected, one more growing being tending the precarious, precious balance of this place.
