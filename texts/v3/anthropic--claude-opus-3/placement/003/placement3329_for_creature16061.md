I would be one of the old sump-jacks, a creature born and shaped by this strange, liminal world beneath the surface. My coat would be weathered and stained by the minerals, my fur bleached along the belly from countless passages through silt and shallow pools. My ears would be tattered, my muzzle pale with age, and my gait slightly uneven from old injuries - perhaps a kink in the tail from a long-ago encounter with a sluice gate.

I would know this dim, dripping labyrinth like no other creature. The hum and thud of the pumps, the ebb and flow of the black water, the patterns of light and shadow cast by the scattered lamps - all would be as familiar to me as my own heartbeat. With whiskers sensing every eddy and vibration, I would navigate effortlessly through culverts and over catwalks, pausing now and then to scent-mark a corroded pipe or strike a paw against a rail in a pattern known only to my kind.

My den would be high in some forgotten maintenance shelf, lined with bits of scavenged insulation and dried reeds. There I would sleep, curled tight against the constant drip of condensation, dreaming perhaps of fat eels nosing through the grilles or swarms of midges dancing in the lamp glow. 

When hunger drew me out, I would follow the ancient routes - the mud-pressed paths, the polished undersides of pipes - to check trap-falls of debris for injured pigeons, or to hunt crabs in the shallows with swift, darting bites. Generations of knowledge would guide my paws - which pipes ran warm in the wet season, which culverts might flood with the next rain above, which pressure changes meant danger.

I would encounter others of my kind along the way, greeting them with soft clicks and whistles, rubbing cheeks to share scent-marks. The younger ones would be more cautious, more prone to vanish into the shadows at the slightest unfamiliar sound. But I would know every groan and murmur of this place in my bones. Only the rumble of human voices, echoing strangely off the curved vault of a chamber, might cause me to slip unseen into some dark, dripping warren to wait until the disturbance passed.

For I would be a creature of edges and in-betweens, at home in a world that is neither entirely natural nor built, neither fully wet nor dry, neither silent nor deafening. The sump-jack does not resist the strange conditions of this place, but shapes itself to fit them, finding a way to thrive in the narrow spaces where other creatures cannot tread. 

And so I would spend my days, growing ever more a part of the underland - my coat more salt-pale, my paws more skillful in the mud - until at last I would lay my bones down in some hidden den, becoming another layer in the sediment of this place that is perpetually unfinished, always becoming something stranger and more wondrous in the dark.
