In this breathtaking, otherworldly place, I imagine myself becoming one of the Weavers, but not as they are now - rather, as one of the first generation to arrive and begin the long process of adaptation and symbiosis with the Crown Canopy.

I see myself as a young woman, part of a small band of refugees who found themselves awestruck and humbled by this cathedral of ancient trees. Unlike some who wanted to press on, I felt an immediate sense of belonging, an intuitive pull to stay and learn the ways of this place that felt almost like a homecoming.

In those early years, life would have been a constant struggle - learning which plants were safe to eat, how to find shelter from the ceaseless rains, how to navigate the branch-floor without falling. Many times I would have despaired, wondering if we had made a terrible mistake. But always, the majestic silence of the trees and the towering green light brought me back to a sense of purpose.

Slowly, through trial and much error, I would begin to understand the patterns - when the beetle-birds migrated, where the gossamer cats denned, which crown elk were most vulnerable. I would have been among the first to realize we could influence the trees' growth, shaping our homes to match their natural forms. My hands, not yet elongated but already growing nimble, would have woven those first breath cloths.

As my own children were born and I watched their feet begin to change, I would have felt a mix of wonder and trepidation. Were we becoming something no longer quite human? But seeing how easily they moved across the bark, how attuned they were to the canopy's rhythms, I would have known it was right. We weren't losing ourselves; we were becoming part of something greater.

In quiet moments, I would find myself talking to the trees - not with words, but with touches, with the breath from my lungs. I imagined I could feel them responding, though I told no one for fear of being thought mad. When we first dared try the hunters' dance, shadowing the crown elk for days, I was certain the branches moved just slightly to conceal us. That first blood spilled on the bark seemed an offering and a covenant.

I like to think I would have lived to be old by the standards of that time - perhaps seeing my grandchildren born, telling them the stories of the world below that was already fading into myth. I would have been among the first to feel my skin begin to mottle green, to notice that the sun now fed me as much as any meat. The day I finally rooted, high on a promontory of still-bare stone, I would have felt no fear - only a great sense of welcoming, of finally being fully embraced by the world I had spent my life learning to love.

As the bark closed over my eyes and my consciousness began to expand into the vastness of the canopy, my last thought would have been a prayer of gratitude. Not for myself, but for the generations I knew would come after - the true Weavers, who would move through these branches as easily as breathing, and who would be, in ways I could only begin to imagine, as much a part of this place as the trees themselves. I had only been a fleeting pioneer, but they would be the true children of the Crown Canopy.

And now, ages later, some part of me would still be there - watching over them, delighting in their lithe forms darting through the foliage, feeling the patterns of their touch-language thrumming through my ancient wood. The trees and I would be one, rooted and watching, as the centuries scrolled by in a great susurrus of leaves. The humans who had become part of the forest, and the forest that had become part of the humans, forever entwined in this place at the roof of the world, this place of green eternity, this place where stone and wood and flesh had all become one great, slow, wondrous dream.
