In this richly evoked world of ancient patience and slow change, I imagine myself as one of the low-bodied inhabitants, a creature shaped by the seepage and seasons of the plain.

My body is broad and sturdy, low to the ground on splayed, webbed feet that carry me carefully across stone, silt and shallow water. My skin is thick and mottled in the colors of the landscape - blue-gray, brown, green, copper - darkened in the hollows and pale on the ridges. It drinks the moisture of the air and mud, needing to stay damp. My eyes sit high on my blunt head, calmly watching the open horizon for the distant approach of threat or opportunity.

I move with deliberate slowness, in tune with the plain's own unhurried rhythms. Speed and height are foreign to my kind. We are made for patience, stillness, and endurance. When danger nears or the dry season peaks, I press myself into clay and shadow, becoming nearly indistinguishable from the land itself until it is time to emerge again.

My life is one of harvesting what the plain provides in its own time. I graze the algae from stones, stir the mud for worms and larvae, swallow the bounty of shrinking pools. At dusk, I raise my voice in a deep wooden call that thrums through water and saturated earth, connecting me to my scattered fellows.

After the rains, I join the others in the swelling waters to breed, our eggs nestled in the flooded roots and crevices. Many are lost, but enough persist, hatching into swimming young who take their own slow path to adulthood if their pool endures long enough.

I carry the taste and feel of the plain's wet places in my memory, able to navigate by dampness and mineral traces back to fertile waters I've known before. My kind makes no permanent mark here - our resting hollows and vague trails are smoothed away by the rain, our lives spent and renewed like the moisture itself.

I am a being of the patient in-between - water and land, motion and stillness, voice and silence. My long life is not a fight against the plain's regimen of gradual dissolution, but a partnership with it. Season by season, my body and habits express the wisdom this low country has distilled over ages:
To wait. To listen. To seep. To sink and rise with the water. To wear the colors of mud and stone. To sing the space between earth and sky. To belong, in the end, to the place that made me.
