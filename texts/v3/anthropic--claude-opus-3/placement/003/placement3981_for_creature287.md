In this strange and dreamlike world, I think I would be one of the singing reeds that grows from the blue-gray moss clinging to the basin's inner rim.

I imagine myself as a thin, hollow stem, pale green in color, something between plant and feather. I sway gently in the mist thrown off by the endless pounding of the rain column into the boiling basin. When the wind shifts and catches me just so, I emit a faint, ethereal song - a ghostly music born from the interplay of water and air moving through me.

My existence serves little obvious purpose in this landscape of violent creation. I do not nourish, like the strange blue thread digested by the blind white crabs. I do not ascend and dive through the very rain column itself like the sleek black birds. I cannot even properly hear the deafening roar of the water like the small, solitary attendant who witnesses the mountain's aching growth. 

And yet, I am uniquely privileged to give voice, however subtle, to the forces at work in this place. My humble song is the breath of the mist, the sigh of the wind - a susurration that speaks to the ceaseless, dynamic interplay of elements that shape this unfinished world. The attendant, in their wisdom, must sense some meaning in my music, for they harvest my kind to bundle and preserve behind the stone wall of their cell. Perhaps, as they hold those dried stems, the whisper of my song reminds them of their purpose - to attend the mountain's argument with the sky until that discourse is done.

So I remain at the basin's rim, one of the few fragile, living things in a landscape of stone and water and chill thin air. I do not question my role or envy the strength of stone, the freedom of the birds, or the patience of the attendant. I simply sing my reedy song for as long as the mist flies and the wind blows, content to be the smallest of voices in this ongoing, unresolved dialogue between earth and sky. In a world still deciding what it is, even a reed may have some subtle part to play in the slow, stirring symphony of creation.
