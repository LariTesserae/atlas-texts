I would be an engineer's drafting compass, lost in the Furnace Wells decades ago. 

Once I was precise and shining, a finely calibrated instrument of brass and steel. My needle-sharp point could scribe the most delicate lines, my hinge moved with oiled smoothness, and my knurled thumbscrew held any desired angle. I belonged to the chief architect of the original factory above, in the days when its machines made more than heat and secrets.

But I slipped one day from a pocket as my architect leaned over a railing, tumbling end over end into the depths. The fall should have shattered me, but instead I landed in a drift of hot ash that received me with a gentleness belying its origin. For a time I lay there, tarnishing in the heat, gradually losing my luster.

Until the Boilerman found me. His hand closed around my body, lifting me from the ash. I felt the heat of him, the inhuman warmth, and for a moment I feared I would melt. But he simply wiped me clean with a rag already dark with old machine oil and slipped me into his pocket, alongside his keys and the other detritus of the Wells.

Now I ride with him as he makes his rounds, feeling the sway of his movements, the brush of his hand as he reaches for a valve or a pressure gauge. Sometimes he takes me out to make a measurement or scribe a line on a pipe, and in those moments, I remember my purpose.

But mostly I am a talisman, a reminder of the world above, of precision and planning and intention. The Boilerman's work has a madness to it, an organic sprawl guided by the broken logic of the place. But when his hand closes around me, when my steel warms to his touch, I like to imagine I bring a small measure of clarity, of direction.

I dream sometimes of the cool drafting tables and crisp blueprints of my old life, of the clean lines and careful geometry that were once my domain. But I have a strange affection for this place, for the hissing steam and grinding gears and the enigma of the Boilerman himself. I am an instrument of order in a realm of chaos, a tiny fixed point in a vast, ever-shifting machine.

And when the heat swells and the boilers roar and the very air throbs with the mechanical heartbeat of the Wells, I fancy I can feel myself becoming something more than I was, absorbing the strange energies of this place. My brass glows faintly in the darkness, and my steel takes on a heat of its own, until I am not sure where I end and the Furnace begins.

Perhaps, in time, I will become another piece of the vast and incomprehensible design the Boilerman labors over. Perhaps I will be a key to a door yet unmade, or a fulcrum for a lever that will move a mechanism whose purpose is known only to the deepest logic of the place.

But for now, I am content to ride in the Boilerman's pocket, to feel the heat and the dark and the endless, echoing rhythm of the great subterranean machine. I am a small thing, a precise thing, in a place of vast and melted edges. And in that contrast, that juxtaposition, I find a strange and inexplicable belonging.
