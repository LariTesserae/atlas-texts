In this world, I imagine I would be a vine - not an ordinary vine, but one that has learned to live in intimate connection with the town's mechanical rhythms.

I would begin my life as a seed carried by overflow, washed into a narrow gutter running along the side of a old pumping station. There, nourished by the mineral-rich dampness, I would sprout and begin to grow, my roots finding purchase in the cracks between bricks.

As I grow, I would be drawn instinctively to the warmth and moisture emanating from the building. My tendrils would reach out, questing, until they found a narrow vent perpetually sighing steam. Delighting in the heat and humidity, I would wind myself around this metal throat, leaf by leaf, until my greenery all but obscured the opening.

But I would not choke off the steam. Rather, my leaves would learn to dance in its rhythmic exhalations, opening and closing like a thousand tiny valves. When the pressure rose, my leaves would spread, allowing the steam to escape. When it fell, they would settle back, trapping the moisture and heat close to the bricks. 

In time, my roots would find a hairline crack in the mortar and penetrate into the pumping station's interior. There, they would dangle in the warm, moist air, drinking in the vapors, growing slick and glossy in the dim light filtering through high, narrow windows.

As seasons passed, I would grow in synchronicity with the machine I embraced. My flowering would coincide with the annual maintenance cycle when the great engines were briefly stilled and the air grew cooler. My fruits would ripen just as the vibrations from the pumps reached their autumn crescendo, and they would drop one by one to burst on the damp bricks below.

In the town's strange ecology of almost-failure and correction, I would play my part. When a particularly violent storm tore some of my leaves away, exposing the vent, the sudden rush of unimpeded steam would alert the system to the needed repair. When mold began to grow too thickly on the bricks I clasped, shading my roots, I would allow a section of myself to wither, signaling the walls were due for a cleansing flood.

I would be a living gauge, a green indicator light, my growth and health a legible sign of the building's own well-being. And the building in turn would sustain me, its steady cycles of heat and wet, pressure and release, the very pulse of my existence.

Travelers who chanced upon the town might marvel at the sight of me, a verdant cascade draped over rusting metal and stained brick, my leaves glittering with perpetual dew. But the locals, if there were locals, would understand. In a place where rot is scheduled and decay is municipal, where the line between organism and mechanism has long since blurred, a vine that regulates steam with its foliage is no oddity. It is merely another working part in the vast, humid, clanking body of the town.

So there I would grow, season by season, one of the many silent green citizens working in concert with the Sump's great project of survival - not a separate life, but a thread in the living fabric of the place, as much a part of its infrastructure as any pipe or valve or gauge. A vine, yes, but also something more: a verdant cipher, a botanical witness, to the strange and secret workings of this half-drowned, never-quite-falling, always-almost-failing marvel of a town.
