As I envision myself within the cistern-town of Oss, I feel drawn to become one of the hand-eels that navigate the interconnected basins and weirs with a deep, embodied knowledge of the town's intricate layout. 

I imagine my elongated, pale form slipping through the lime-rich waters, propelling myself with subtle undulations. My sensitive hands would trail along the submerged stones, reading their histories and origins through touch as I journey from pool to pool under the cover of night.

During the day, I would rest in one of the middle-tier basins, appreciating the unique mineral composition that gives life to Oss's distinct ecosystem. I'd share this habitat with the particular species of bronze-hued frogs, listening to their resonant calls pulse through the perpetually damp air.

As an older, experienced hand-eel, I would serve as a guide to the younger ones, leading them along the time-worn paths and teaching them to navigate the town as a living map imprinted in our collective memory. We would pause together at certain significant lips and weirs, resting in the same spots where generations before us have paused, partaking in a ritual of place that transcends our individual lives.

My wanderings would take me through many of Oss's regions, but like all inhabitants, I would carefully avoid the enigmatic deep sumps, sensing the ancient mystery they contain without needing to witness it directly. To be a hand-eel is to understand one's place in the greater balance.

In a lifespan attuned to the rhythms of water and stone, I would bear witness to the slow, small changes of Oss—the gradual shifting of silt, the almost imperceptible growth of flowstone curtains—while I and my kindred moved through the patterns we inherited, playing our part in the ongoing dream of the cistern-town, this place of dampness and life that exists for purposes not fully understood but achingly felt in every still pool and dripping wall.
