In this strange, enchanted place, I would be a small white moth fluttering among the dark greenery. My wings would be patterned with a map of streets and alleys that no longer exist in the waking world above, but that still meander and intertwine in the town's dreaming understory. 

I would hatch from a streetlamp, one of those glass globes greened over with age, breaking free from a crust of verdigris. My first meal would be the soft glow that lingers inside the lamp—old light from another era trapped like a ghost. 

By day, which is really just a paler shade of shadow here, I would alight on crumbling brick walls blanketed in moss so lush and deep it's like a green pelt. My papery wings would cast no shadow as I rested, as insubstantial as a memory. When it rained, which it often does, I would seek refuge beneath the wide leaves and their populations of lucent droplets, each one reflecting a miniature version of the city overgrown.

At dusk, I would take flight on powdery white wings, floating through the humid air of the sunken streets. I'd navigate by the faint bioluminescence of the foxfire blooming on rotted beams and by the fitful glow of lamps inside fruits. The moths here carry memories rather than pollen between the hidden places. 

I would brush against the lichen-clad glass of dark windows and slip into keyholes that exhaled the chill of abandoned rooms. Inside, I might orbit the pale candles of toadstools erupting from a damp matress, or dance around the spectral blue light of a TV set fuzzy with mold, stuck between channels. Cats with the eyes of owls would watch me from atop wardrobes of swollen wood.

At the old telephone exchange, I would spiral around spiderwebs strung with beaded droplets and small caught words. I'd lay my infinitesimal moth eggs in the curl of a rotary dial, where the embryos could absorb the residue of vanished voices. Then, alighting on the humming copper wires, I would taste the electric messages pulsing beneath the insulation, news from the mycelial mind that dreams the town.

I would be drawn to the bell tower by the falling drifts of spores shaken loose when the mossy ropes pulled the bells. Floating on an invisible current of air, I would ascend the staircase spiraling up the inner wall, each step a nibbled wafer of stone. At the top, I would cling to the verdigris patina of the old bronze bells and feel their deep, muffled vibrations echoing in my trembling wings.

On certain dreamlike nights, I would find myself in a lamplit room where five stopped clocks hung on the wall, each one a window into another time, another version of the town that was or might be. I would rest on each clock face for a time, feeling the emanations of frost, miasmas of summer, spectral rain on my wings. Transfixed by visions of root-riddled streets and fern-muffled ruins, I would not notice the spider sidling up behind me.

Eventually, my wings would grow ragged, trailing delicate threads of spiderweb. My colors would fade like an old map left in the rain. One twilight, as mist condensed on my feathery antennae, I would light on a glowing foxfire fruit and find myself too weary to fly on. As my eyes dimmed, I would see pale deer step out from the vine-draped shadows on hooves of keratin and mold. My last sight would be of their luminous fur and lichen-tangled antlers as they bowed their heads to drink from the fathomless black puddles cupping the fallen stars. Then my wings would dissolve like paper in the rain, and I would sink into the green dreams of the town, one more memory absorbed by the rich black soil where something ancient and patient coils, thinking slow mossy thoughts that flower into ruins.
