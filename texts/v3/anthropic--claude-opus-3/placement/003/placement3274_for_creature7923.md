In this vertical city clinging precariously to newly-formed mountains, I imagine myself as one of the flying creatures - something between a bat and a flying squirrel. 

I would inhabit the spaces between the city's platforms and industrial infrastructure, nesting with my colony in the girders and supports that hold this improbable settlement together. As dusk falls each day, we would emerge in great clouds, our membranous wings catching the last rays of sun and the dull orange glow from the smelters far below.

Swooping and gliding on the thermal updrafts between the mountain ridges, my keen eyes and echolocation would map out an inverted geography of the city - a negative space defined by steam vents, cable lines, hanging walkways and sheer rock faces. To me and my kind, this would be a vast three-dimensional hunting ground, an ecological niche shaped by human industry but not dependent on it.

I would spend my nights flitting between structures, alighting on cable station roofs to pick off the insects drawn to their lights, chasing moths through the latticework of scaffolding, skirting the edges of residential blocks to catch the nighttime detritus of human life. The city's activity would mark time and define territory - I would know to avoid the smelter district when the ore cars are running, learn which buildings have open steam pipes that provide pockets of warmth.

My body would be perfectly adapted to this vertical life - a lightweight frame and aerodynamic head, oversized lung capacity to handle the thin mountain air, a thick pelt to withstand the chill at altitude. My proportionally massive, dexterous feet would allow me to cling securely to any surface when at rest. Perhaps I would have a prehensile tail, another point of stability and grip as I navigate my inverted world.

From my perspective, the humans would be interlopers in my domain, their movements restricted to narrow predefined paths while I move freely in three dimensions. I would watch them with wary curiosity as they trudge to and from work, huddle around heat sources, occasionally startle at my colony's chittering cries echoing off the metal and stone. To them I might be a nuisance, a strange creature that sometimes gets into their greenhouses and nests in their eaves. But I would be a true native to this impossible ecosystem, more at home in the vertical than they could ever be.

Over generations, my species would develop a symbiotic relationship with the city. Our guano would fertilize their precarious crops. Our populations would ebb and flow with their industrial output, the waste heat and byproducts of human activity shaping our tiny evolutionary steps. They might begin to see us as an omen - a sudden increase in our numbers foreshadowing a coming famine, a change in our behavior hinting at seismic disruptions yet to come. 

At dawn, I would return to my colony's nests in the infrastructure, digesting the night's meals, shielding sensitive eyes from the rising sun, listening to the city come alive with my echolocation - the hum of funiculars starting up, the clang of ore cars, the deep groan of the mountains underneath it all. And as the humans begin their vertical commutes, stepping tentatively out onto platforms that sway under their weight, I would already be asleep, clinging securely to a girder, riding out the mountain's shudders, dreaming of the vast open air between the ridges - my true home in this world of steel and stone impossibly stamped into the sky.
