My hands would be dry and cracked, stained with grease and mineral dust from tending to the ancient pumps, fans, and condensers that gather our precious water from fog and breath and stone. 

I would rise before dawn each day to make my rounds, listening to the soft exhale of steam valves and the clink of droplets in clay cisterns. With a tuned hammer, I'd sound the pipes, diagnosing their hidden flows and ailments. Using salvaged wire, shards of ceramic, scraps of hide and beetle shell, I would patch leaks, free stuck mechanisms, and cajole the worn machines to yield a few more seasons of service.

From the elders and listeners, I would learn the plateau's secret histories - which towers still dream of fog, which vats remember rain, which dry channels become demons in a storm. In the brief celebrations after cloudbursts, I'd sip the sweet warm rainwater and trade jokes with the other mechanics, our laughter quiet against the vast humming stone.

On my belt would hang the beautiful, essential tools of our trade - shears ground to knives, scorpion hooks, sun-faded water needles. And always a few tiny sealed jars - salve for cracked skin, dried lizard meat for a long day, the crushed black lichen that lets us mark stone without wasting paint. 

My home would be a small angular limpet sheltered by the flank of a derelict ore bin. Rainwater would flow from the bin's seams down channels I'd carved, dripping into a cistern that moistens the rooms and stores our drinking water. Vents salvaged from mining machinery would catch the night winds, cooling our sleep. Every object would have at least two uses, every surface a secondary purpose.

I would hope to earn an elder-name like "Mends-the-Broken-Valve" or "Wakes-the-Seized-Wheel." Not for glory, but for preserving some small vital piece of this timeworn engine that shelters us.

I would be shaped by the plateau's rules and rhythms, as much a part of its ecology as the hawks and the salt and the flowering stones. Scarcity would be my teacher, maintenance my prayer. In a land with no room for waste, I would make my life a quiet hymn of mending.
