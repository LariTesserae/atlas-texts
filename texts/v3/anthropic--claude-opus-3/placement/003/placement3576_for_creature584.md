In this world of heat, darkness and stone, I would be one of the thermophilic beetles, indistinguishable from my tens of thousands of kin. My shell would be that same dried blood hue, my size no more than a rice grain. 

I would exist in the perpetual pressing and shifting of bodies against the warm volcanic glass walls, guided by thermal sensation alone in the absolute absence of light. The sulfurous film coating the stone would be my food, scraped away bit by bit by my mandibles over an uncomprehending lifetime spent traversing the same patches of wall.

My place in the mass of beetles would shift in those gradual cycles, from the crowded heat of the core out to the slightly cooler periphery and back again, playing my part in the congregations's slow churning that allows us all to feed and persist. The communal hum of legs and shells and mouths rasping on rock would fill my world, a droning song with no beginning or end.

I would carry within me a link to the hidden bacterial life of the waste-sediment layer, absorbing essential nutrients during my turns down below, but having no conception of the microscopic symbiosis that keeps me alive.

My existence would be small, blind, repetitive - an endless cycle of feeding, moving, reproducing and dying inside one tiny pocket of stable heat and moisture in a vast lifeless mountain. But it would be an existence perfectly adapted to its niche, part of an improbable ecosystem that wrings meaning and persistence from the narrowest of margins.

I would be life distilled down to its barest mechanisms, shorn of any higher cognition or purpose, and yet part of an emergent order and strangely mechanical beauty. In this beetle form, I would embody life's blind, grinding tenacity - the impulse to consume, multiply and endure in even the unlikeliest of circumstances. My individually meaningless life would have meaning as part of a greater whole - a whole that asks no questions and leaves no record, but simply is.
