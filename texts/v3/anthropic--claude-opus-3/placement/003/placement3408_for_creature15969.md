In this ancient, patient landscape of stone, cold, and quiet industry, I imagine myself as one of the plateau's many hidden residents, a being shaped by and contributing to the mountain's slow circulation of heat, water, and mineral.

I am a creature of the mossy hollows, the warm volcanic vents, and the black pools. My body is low and dense, built for insulation against the penetrating chill. My hide is thick and dark, crusted with minerals drawn up from the depths, patterned with lichen that helps me blend into the rocky slopes. 

I move with deliberate care, placing each padded foot to avoid disturbing the fragile alpine plants. My breath condenses in the cold air, mingling with the steam rising from geothermal cracks. I pause often to listen, feeling the subtle vibrations that pass through moss, root and stone, attuned to the plateau's wordless communication.

Like the other inhabitants, I have learned to live by the mountain's agreements. I conserve energy, hunting only when necessary, relying more on traps and patience than on speed or aggression. In the sheltered alcoves beneath overhanging cliffs, I cultivate small gardens of bioluminescent fungus, which I harvest to light my den and lure curious prey.

My den is a warren of chambers dug into the dark, damp earth beneath a particularly lush hollow. The tunnels are lined with the waxy resin extruded from cracks, waterproofing and reinforcing the walls. Pale blind salamanders swim in the small pools that form where my tunnels intersect underground streams. 

I am solitary by nature but not isolated. Like the scattered grazers, I stay connected to my kind through ground-vibration and scent markers. We gather rarely, when some important shift in the plateau's cycles demands conference or cooperation. At these times we hum together, adding our voices to the mountain's deep subaudible tone.

I am a creature of deep time, my lifespan stretched by the cold and the plateau's spartan efficiencies. I measure years by the slow growth of mineral formations, by the generational cycles of the algae that cling to the walls of my den. My mind holds the long memories of the mountain - the gradual shifts of stone and ice, the ebbs and flows of life riding the plateau's internal breaths.

I am both master and servant of this place, both predator and collaborator. I take only what I need, and in turn I feed the mountain's vast slow workings with the heat of my body, the minerals leached from my bones, the gases of my breath, and finally the decay of my flesh. I am a brief warm current in the plateau's dark, patient circulation, a single note in its long, slow song.
