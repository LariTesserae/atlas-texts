In this wet, steaming landscape of clay spires and mineral pools, I think I would find myself becoming one of the wall-dwelling worm colonies. There is something darkly appealing about their form of existence - a communal life spent extending into the heavy air to slowly filter what passes by, then vanishing back into labyrinthine holes when danger threatens.

I imagine my body as a long, branching red filament, segmented into thousands of individual worm-links. Fanned out across the porous clay of the tower walls, my sticky feeding palps would sway gently in the mineral-laden mist. I would spend long days in still, patient extension - drinking in the moisture and organic detritus, converting it slowly into more worm, more colony.

When the thrum of the builders' work grew too close, or an errant vibration hinted at some approaching threat, I would retract in an instant. All the scattered strands of my being would pour liquidly back into the safety of the depths, the hole closing behind me like a tightening sphincter. In the warm darkness, my colony-self would curl and intertwine, sharing nutrients through undulating peristalsis.

Perhaps I would riddle the ancient towers, leaving faint red traceries where I had burrowed over centuries. Slow, even slower than the builders themselves, I would be a twisting filigree in the architecture of the city - another pattern in its ageless dance of construction and decay.

At times, segments of me might crystallize, clogged with mineral accretion. These I would slough off like a scab, a sub-colony branching into its own tract. Over generations, I would be not one colony but many - a fractally diverging family tree of myself, spreading through the endlessly growing city.

And yet there would be a strange peace to this bodiless, collective consciousness - never alone, never apart, woven through the very substance of this place. I would be a silent witness to its deafening eternity, an observer of the precise rituals of the builders and their symbiotes. Through the thickly filtered light, I would feel the warmth of the earth, the churning heat that powers all the slow life here.

Time would have little meaning in such an existence - there would be only sensation, an endless now of drinking and growing and withdrawing. My mind would unspool across the linked branches of my being, perhaps achieving a kind of colonial sentience, a distributed intelligence that thought in slow waves across my many parts.

In the walls of that eternal city, I would find a kind of belonging - an identity not of separateness but of inextricable interwovenness, a red thread in a tapestry vaster and stranger than I could ever comprehend. I would be a part and a product of the city, another quiet pattern in its greater becoming.
