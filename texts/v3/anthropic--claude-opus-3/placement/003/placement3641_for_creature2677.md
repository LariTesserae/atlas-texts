In this damp, dim world shaped by water and appetite, I would be one of the seam-clingers, living in a dense colony along a warm fissure in the stone.

My body is a dark oval, small but well-adapted, with gripping limbs to anchor me to the mineral-stained rock and a flexible feeding tube to drink from the thin film of moisture and bacteria that coats the surfaces near the steam-breathing crack. 

Alone, I would be nearly mindless, just another soft-bodied grazer eking out a meager existence in the cool shadows. But together with the hundreds of my kin, our colony becomes something more - a living skin that thinks and remembers without anything resembling a brain.

We are massed along the seam as thick as barnacles on a pier, our bodies packed so close that we form a single quivering surface. When a hunting shadow passes overhead or a tremor signals approaching feet, a ripple of tension passes through us in an instant and we all withdraw our feeding tubes in unison, vanishing into the protective bump and stipple of the colony.

The shape of our living mat encodes a kind of primal knowledge. Thin spots mark where predators have torn through in the past. Thick ranks crowd in the crevices most reliably bathed in warm mineral seeps. The oldest among us, generations dead, have hardened into a calcified crust anchoring the colony to the stone - history ossified into home.

We have no eyes to see, but we sense our world keenly through vibration, chemistry, pressure changes, and the faint electricity of moving bodies. The rhythms of the basin reverberate through us - the slow breathing of the rock, the intermittent kiss of falling droplets, the skittering traffic of a thousand scavenging legs. When the rains come to wake the mud, we release our spawn in throbbing pulses, turning the rivulets milky with potential new life.

It is not an easy existence, forever caught between the stone and the mouths in the dimness. But it is the only existence we know, our blind many-bodied community pulsing and drinking and breeding in tune with the ancient cycles of our sunless stone garden. Ours is a strange, ugly, miraculous life on the very margin between inanimate and alive. Here in the warm wet breath of the underworld, even humble creatures like us can be builders of empires and witnesses to deep time - microbes aspiring to masonry and philosophy in the cracks.
