If I found myself in the Singing Gorges of Keth-Marai, I imagine I would be a Listener-in-training, still new to the strange ways of this ancient, wind-carved labyrinth. 

At first, the ceaseless noise would overwhelm me, a cacophony that drowns out thought. But gradually, with the patient guidance of the elder Listeners, I would learn to parse the canyon's songs. My ears would adapt, elongating and thickening until I could modulate the frequencies I took in, sifting signal from noise. I would shed my shoes, learning to read the stone's vibrations through my bare soles, feeling the subtle shifts that presage floods or rockfalls.

I would learn the circuit of stone chambers, the places of refuge carved by ancient hands. Following the examples of my teachers, I would begin to cache supplies in these hushed spaces, learning to anticipate the canyon's moods, to know when to move on and when to hunker down and let the acoustic storms rage overhead.

The Listeners' tonal language would be slow to come to me. At first my throat would ache with the effort of shaping sounds that slip between the gorges' frequencies. But in time, the intervals would come more naturally, the pitches bending to my will. I would learn to throw my voice, to whisper across vast distances, to let my words ride the currents of sound like eagles on thermals.

As my skills grew, the elder Listeners would take me deeper into the maze, showing me the strange lifeforms that call this place home. I would marvel at the Hush Beetles' pockets of engineered silence, strain my eyes to catch a glimpse of the transparent wings of the Membrane Bats. I would learn to read the patterns in the wind-polished stone, to see where the Resonance had passed by the new formations in its wake.

In quiet moments, the elders might share their stories of the Resonance - never a clear description, but a patchwork of impressions: a shimmer of heat like a mirage, a sense of vast wings unfurling, a sudden absence of sound that made the ears ring. I would strain to glimpse it, always at the periphery, always just out of clear sight.

My own skin would slowly shift, taking on the hues of the gorges - amber, rust, dusky rose. The patterns would move subtly with my pulse, an unconscious camouflage. My packs would be woven of canyon grass and cotton, muffling my movement, letting me blend into the acoustic landscape.

Perhaps, in a moment of rare silence, an elder would take me aside and whisper of the Echo Prophets - those who had listened too long and too deep. They would caution me of the danger, even as they acknowledged the temptation. To hear the future in the stone, to know what will be... but at the cost of losing the present, of forgetting human connection. I would shiver, and resolve to walk that line carefully, to not lose myself in the canyon's endless song.

In time, perhaps I would become an elder myself, leading a new generation of Listeners. I would teach them as I was taught: to listen, to adapt, to find the spaces between sound and silence. To leave offerings for the Resonance, and to read its gifts in return. To sustain our small, strange society in this place that was never meant for humans, but which we had nonetheless made a kind of home.

And always, the canyons would sing on, indifferent and eternal, wearing us all away bit by bit, until we became just another set of notes in their vast composition, our lives a brief interlude in a symphony that has been playing since the dawn of time, and will continue long after our bones have turned to dust on the canyon floor.
