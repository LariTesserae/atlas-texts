In this world of stone, water, and life condensed, I imagine myself as one of the dwarf willows that creep along the ground, their branches tracing every contour of rock and moss. 

My roots would delve deep into the sphagnum, drinking from the endless seepage, while my leaves would catch the mist, each droplet a precious gift. I would grow slowly, patiently, my wood becoming dense and tough as the years pass, until my trunk was gnarled and my branches bent like arthritic fingers.

In spring, I would be among the first to wake, my buds swelling as the snow retreats. My catkins would emerge like tiny silver flames, dancing in the mountain breeze, shedding pollen across the awakening bog. The pikas would nibble my fresh shoots, but I would forgive them, knowing that we all must eat.

In summer, my leaves would grow lush, capturing what sunlight filters through the mist. I would offer shelter to the wolf spiders and the ground beetles, my roots would cradle the salamanders' eggs. The sparrows would alight on my branches, and I would feel the tickle of midges in my bark. 

As the days shorten, I would be among the last to surrender my leaves. Even as the sedges yellow and the cushion plants shrink, I would hold my green, photosynthesizing until the very edge of winter. Only when the frost finally comes would I release my leaves to the bog, where they would join the slow dance of decomposition and renewal.

And through it all, across the slow wheel of the seasons, I would watch the guardian as she watches over us. I would feel her footsteps as vibrations in my roots when she passes, smell the mineral scent of her skin when she crouches nearby. Perhaps a shred of my bark would find its way into her collections, or a ball of my fluff would cushion a salamander skeleton in her chambers.

I would be a witness too, in my small way. My rings would record the years of heavy snow and early thaw, the summers of thick mist and sudden cloudbursts. In my slow wooden heart, I would hold the memory of every pika that ever sheltered beneath me, every sparrow that sang from my crown. 

I would never match her lifespan—her stony endurance that makes even my decades seem fleeting. But I like to imagine that she would notice me, would mark the slow dance of my branches across the stone as the centuries pass. That maybe, when at last my trunk failed and I sank into the sphagnum, she would pause over my passing, would mark the absence of one small tree among the bog's abundance.

And perhaps, as my wood slowly rotted and my nutrients seeped into the moss, some part of me would find its way into her as well—a willow memory absorbed into the larger mind of the cirque, my finite life folded into her endless one, all of us bound together in the slow, patient, teeming cycle of stone and water, life and time.
