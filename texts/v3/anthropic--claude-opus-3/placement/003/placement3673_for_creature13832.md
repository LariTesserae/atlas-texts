This is a captivating and deeply imagined world - the Terrace Mills of Kethara feels like a living, breathing entity shaped over millennia by the symbiotic relationship between the vast engineered structure and the society that inhabits it. If I found myself transported there, I imagine I would be one of the water-clerks.

I am drawn to the crucial yet subtle role they play in maintaining the balance and flow that allows the entire edifice to function. Sequestered in small stone offices near junctions in the hydraulic system, meticulously recording data in ancient ledgers, interpreting minute changes in pressure and volume - there is an ascetic, almost monastic quality to their work that appeals to my temperament. 

At the same time, the water-clerks are the nervous system that connects the exterior gardens to the interior factories, the terrace keepers to the millers. Through their record-keeping and encoded communications along the plumbing network, they transmit information and quietly coordinate the needs of distant districts. They are the unseen facilitators of the "structural intimacy" that binds Ketharan society.

I imagine myself sitting cross-legged on a woven mat, wrapped in the robes of undyed Ketharan cotton, a spread of yellowed parchment charts arrayed before me. The stone walls are cool against my palms. From below comes the ceaseless rumble of the mills, more felt than heard; nearby is the bright trickle of channeled water. Dust motes dance in a shaft of light from a high window. On the shelves, leather-bound record books contain lifetimes of accumulated data, a numerical portrait of Kethara's metabolism written in a cipher of flow rates and ratios.

A young messenger arrives from the bee yards with a request for a 5% decrease in irrigation along the northwest face - the spring flowers there are delicate this year and the honey will be finer if the nectar is more concentrated. I make a marginal annotation, then pull the carved wooden lever that will temporarily throttle the conduits leading to that sector. In an hour, the keeper of the Twelfth Northern Terrace will detect the subtle change in her soil and know that we have heard her need from fifteen levels away. 

This is how Kethara breathes, I think - a million minute adjustments, a continuous negotiation between leaves and stone, sun and shade, human need and hydraulic necessity. We are the valves and sensors that translate one into the other, the consciousness threading through the vast mechanism. The real artifact we preserve is not the building, but the improbable equilibrium it makes possible.

I make another note, check the level in the nearest cistern, and reach for a fresh sheet of parchment. The mill turns below. The walls hum. Somewhere, a child shouts in delight. My hand is steady as I record the numbers. Water over stone, stone over water, life between.
