In this wet industrial hollow, I would become one of the pale threadworms that inhabit the rust. 

My body would be no thicker than a hair, nearly translucent, coiled tightly in the orange layers of corrosion that cover the old pipes and tanks. In dry times, I would lie dormant and brittle under flaking scales of rust, waiting patiently for rain. 

When the damp returned, I would emerge to graze on the bacterial mats that bloom along the submerged metal. I would comb the velvety iron pastures with my minute mouth, feeding on microbes enlivened by the moisture.

My world would be one of texture and vibration more than sight. The gritty roughness of corroded surfaces, the viscous films on standing water, the shivering impact of drops falling from heights - these would be my signals to emerge, feed, and hide again. 

If disturbed, I would retract in an instant, vanishing backward into rust-pores too small to see. An entire pipe could shiver with the collective withdrawal of my kind when a rat's paw splashed the water or the old pump rumbled to life.

I would be a humble part of the hollow's ecology - a grazer of bacterial pastures, a thread in the living fabric of rust, an unseen mouth that slowly ate the residues of industry. Patient, resilient, nearly invisible, my tiny hairlike body would be woven into the very skin of this place.

Through countless threadworm lives, my kind would help shape this sunken world. Our grazing would encourage some bacterial colonies and hinder others. Our burrowing would aerate the rust and alter its texture over time. Our deaths would return nutrients to the wet metal soil.

And so I would pass my days, a translucent filament, coiled in the oxidized strata of abandoned machines, waiting for wetness, eating the orange blooms, feeling the basin shiver, one tiny life amid many in the ongoing, water-guided digestion of the ruin.
