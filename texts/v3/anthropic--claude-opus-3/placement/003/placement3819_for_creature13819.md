In this strange, liminal space between the mechanical and the organic, I imagine myself as a delicate fern unfurling from a crack in the concrete floor. 

My roots probe deep into the crevices, seeking moisture and minerals in the ancient masonry, while my fronds stretch upward, yearning for the diffuse green light filtering down through the wisteria canopy high above.

With each cycle of the great iron pumps, I feel the floor tremble beneath my rhizomes. The vibrations travel up through my stems, setting my leaves aquiver like countless tiny tuning forks resonating to the station's industrial breath. 

Season after patient season, I grow - slowly, imperceptibly, but with the inexorable persistence of life finding a way. My roots help knit together the decaying concrete, even as my leaves drink in the station's strange half-light and thrive on the steady warmth radiating from the network of pipes.

I am part of the equilibrium here, one of countless organisms locked together in a web of symbiosis so intricate it blurs the line between individual and collective. The cadence of the pumps pulses in my vascular tissues; moisture condenses on my leaves and trickles down to be absorbed by the mosses below.

Yet I have no more comprehension of my place in this system than any other fern in any other forest. I simply grow according to my nature, an unwitting participant in the living machine of Briar Sink, as much a part of its structure and its breath and its slow, dream-like consciousness as the corroded pipes and the strangler figs and the ceaseless percussion of falling water.

I am life in the presence of an alien kind of life, biomass and circuitry woven together into something that is neither and both at once. A collaboration between kingdoms, a hymn sung in iron and sap and deep green shadow. Until the day when the station's extended death finally arrives, I will grow here, and be here, one frond uncurling in a hidden world where the mechanical has become the mystical.
