In this stark, meditative world of heat and scarcity, I imagine myself as one of the solitary shrubs eking out an existence in the cracks of the vast constructed plain. 

My pale, flaking bark blends into the weathered ceramic and plaster stretching endlessly in all directions. My small waxy leaves turn edgewise to the relentless sun, conserving what little moisture I can draw up through roots that probe ever deeper into hairline fractures and forgotten voids in the architecture below.

Season after dry season, I stand alone, a mere speck against the shimmering horizons. My only companions are my own shadow shifting slowly across the paving stones and perhaps a distant sibling shrub, itself an island in the expanse.

When the rains finally come, I rouse from dormancy, my leaves taking on the faintest sheen of green - a rare hint of color in this washed out landscape. And for a brief moment, I flower, delicate cream-colored blossoms opening to the morning light. There are no insects to pollinate me, no eyes but the sun's to witness my fragile beauty. By afternoon, my petals begin to wither. 

Yet even as my blooms fade, my deeper self stirs to life. From my roots, ever so slowly, I exert patient pressure on the wall beside me. Mortar crumbles. Cracks widen infinitesimally. I am in no hurry - I have the luxury of geologic time. Over years, decades, centuries, I will reshape my little section of this constructed continent, becoming part of its weathering, its gradual return to dust and earth.

I am both resident and process here, both life and the slow unmaking of the very surface that sustains me. In a world pared down to mineral austerity, to water's fleeting gifts, I endure in near stasis, measuring out my days grain by grain, drop by precious drop. Dreaming in my own slow time of the reckoning between root and stone, leaf and light, life's impulse and the weight of ages pressing down upon this strangely tiled ground.
