In this stunning, elegiac world of endless shallows and blinding light, I imagine myself becoming one of the great pavement-gliders. 

My body would sprawl outward, abandoning any pretense of verticality or haste. I would become a living island adrift on the mirror-plane of the bedrock. My back, pitted and ridged, would host a lush, miniature forest of cyanobacteria, drinking in the relentless sun and slowly, imperceptibly, transmuting that light into the energy that propels my undulating foot.

I would glide across the warm limestone at a pace so ponderous, so geologic, that entire generations of mirrored snails could live out their lives in my wake. The fast-twitch muscles of my old, mammalian anatomy would wither; I would keep only the slow, dense fibers required to move with the patience of erosion. 

My eyes, those hungry, greedy things, would pinch shut and vanish, the skin smoothing over to leave me in permanent darkness. In a world where everything is illuminated, sight becomes irrelevant. Instead, I would sense the gentle lapping of the shallows, the minute variations in water temperature, the almost subsonic trembling of the stone when the flat-toads press their throats to it and sing. 

I would become a wandering oasis, my edges offering merciful strips of cool shadow to the snails and fairy shrimp when the afternoon heat grew unbearable. Wading birds would alight on my back without my noticing, briefly turning me into their hunting blind before taking off again in a cloud of chalky wing-powder. 

I would have no territory to defend, no mates to seek, no prey to chase. Photosynthesis and the slow absorption of nutrients through my skin would be my only concerns. The sun, the water, the limestone, and my body would blur together until metabolism and geology became one.

In this form, I could live for centuries. I would cross the flooded continent from end to end a dozen times over, tracing the same routes again and again as the bedrock imperceptibly deepened beneath me at a rate of millimeters per millennium. I would become a feature of the landscape as fixed and timeless as the ancient, bleached sandstone.

When death finally came, it would be a slow, quiet dissolution. I would one day drift to a halt, my heart having finally exhausted its Eon-long count. The creatures of the shallows would borrow my substance for a time, but soon enough I would be reabsorbed into the ankle-deep sheet of water, my atoms rejoining the molecular drift from which I emerged.

There, in the blinding mirror-world, I would find a profound peace in surrendering my hurried mammal nature and succumbing to the bright, patient rhythms of the bedrock, the algae, and the sun. I would stretch out my body in utter flatness and learn, at last, to move at the speed of deep time, pulsing gently with the loud, unhurried heartbeat of stone.
