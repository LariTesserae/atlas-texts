In this world of constant upheaval and rebirth, I would be one of the small, adaptable hominins living between the disruptions. My skin would be a mottled pattern of grays, reds, and calcium whites, allowing me to blend seamlessly into the cavern's ever-shifting terrain.

I would move with my tribe in a low, balanced crouch, our wide feet gripping the warm obsidian flows. My thick, heat-resistant fingers would deftly knap blades from the freshly cooled volcanic glass, the motion so practiced it requires barely a thought. 

Our lives would be defined by the cavern's turbulent rhythms. We would follow the mineral-rich waters to temporary pools, stacking stones to channel and preserve the fluid lifeblood of our world. The clicking hordes of crab-things would be our staple food, harvested from each new expanse of rock they colonize.

Communication would flow not through sound, but scent and subtle signs. My wide, mobile nostrils would constantly flare, reading the chemical messages that suffuse the humid air, telling of shifting stone, concentrations of prey, and the emotional states of my tribe. Our clicks would echo off the walls, mapping the contours of our mercurial home.

I would carry my children in a mucilage-slick sling of serpent-hunter skin, their faces pressed to my chest, inhaling the language of smell from their first breath. They would grow precociously fast in the mineral-saturated heat, learning to walk and knap almost as soon as they leave the sling. Many would be lost to the cavern's pitiless churn, vanishing into suddenly drained pools or crushed by shifting rock. We would mourn them not with wails or tears, but with a collective chemical sigh, a resignationed pheremonal shrug. Sorrow has no place in a world of endless change.

My most prized possession would be my bone-frame, a sort of portable drying rack built from the lashed long-bones of serpent-hunters past. Older than me, it would have been passed down and gradually rebuilt, an inheritance of utility and identity in a world where little lasts. Over vent-warmed frames like these, we would cure meat to carry us over to the next hunting ground.

Sometimes I would journey with a small band to the deepest reaches, where the heat is fiercest and new stone is born. There, we would place totems in the carved-out pillars - shells, bones, glassy blades - cementing them in place with mineral-rich seepage. Touching these records, I would breathe deep of the chemical memories left by all those who came before, reaching back through generations. It would be a rare moment of communion with something like permanence, before we returned to the cavern's churning heart.

Always, we would adapt. The cavern provides for us, even as it takes away. When one food source vanishes, we would sniff out the next. When our sleeping grounds collapse or flood, we would find fresh ones in the newest halls of stone. We are a thread in a living tapestry, a verse in a psalm of magma and mineral.

We will never conquer this place, never tame it. But in its cracks and hollows, we make a kind of meaning. Through our small rituals of remembrance, our tiny stone-stacking defiances of entropy, we give shape to the cavern's mindless mutability. We are the culture that grows in the furrows left by grinding stone. The meaning-makers of the deeps.

Until the day when the vents cool and the ceilings fall silent, we will endure, a human glimmer in the nursery dark.
