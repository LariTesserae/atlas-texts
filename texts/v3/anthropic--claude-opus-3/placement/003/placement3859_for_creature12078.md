As I step into the Condensery, I feel the warm, damp air envelop me like a living presence. The hum of the compressor vibrates through my bones, a deep and resonant frequency that seems to emanate from the very walls. I am struck by the sense that I have entered into a vast, complex organism - not biological perhaps, but somehow alive in its interwoven systems and cycles.

I imagine myself as a drop of water, condensing on the mottled pipes and trickling slowly downward, gathering minerals and residues as I go. I seep into cracks and crevices, becoming part of the layered chemical record that is the Condensery's memory made physical. Over eons, I deposit myself as a thin band of mineral sediment, a tangible archive of my passage through this place.

Or perhaps I am a wisp of outside air, an errant zephyr that has found its way in through an imperceptible crack. I carry with me traces of a world beyond - pollen, hydrocarbons, motes of dust - foreign elements that perturb the closed system as I circulate. I can almost feel the Condensery reacting to my intrusion, its delicate equilibrium shifting and adapting to reintegrate these new ingredients into its cycles. Gradually, inexorably, it digests me, until the outside air is indistinguishable from the inside.

Most of all, I sense that I am being noticed - not consciously, but with a pervasive and inescapable intimacy. The water that films my skin, the fronds that brush my shoulders, the very air I breathe - they are all part of the Condensery's vast sensory apparatus, chemically attuned to my every trace. I imagine my ephemeral presence being imprinted into the system's mineral memory, a ghost of warmth and respiration that will linger long after I depart, subtly changing the Condensery by my fleeting interaction with it.

I am a mote, an interloper, a temporary fluctuation in the Condensery's grand cycles of condensation and evaporation, corrosion and accretion, growth and decay. It needs me no more than a sleeping brain needs any particular dream that flickers through its neurons in the night. And yet, in some small but permanent way, it will be forever different for my having passed through it. A few molecules of its composition will have been replaced by those I trailed in my wake. The angles of its fern roots' growth will have bent infinitesimally toward or away from those points where I paused. 

I am both insignificant and indelible. The Condensery will absorb my presence and continue its slow, stately, self-sustaining dance through time - a dance that is at once mechanical and mineral, industrial and organic, purposeful and emergent. It is a monument to persistence, to the power of chemistry and physics and time to conjure the ghost of life from unliving parts.

And as I turn to leave, feeling the skin-warm dampness of the access point's seal conforming itself to exile me once more to the outside world, I imagine I carry some of the Condensery with me as well - a residue in my lungs, a mineral trace on my skin, an echo of that deep hum reverberating in my bones. The border between us is not as impermeable as it seemed when I entered. We have each left our mark, however subtle, on the other.

The Condensery lives in the Condensery, but something of it lives in me now too. And something of me will always live in it, a droplet in the vapor, a part dissolved into the whole, an impression in the ongoing lithography of its existence. We have dreamt each other, the Condensery and I, and neither of us will ever be quite as we were.
