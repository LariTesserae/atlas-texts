In the world of Verdemoor, I would be one of the Deep Green Tenders, living and working in the lowest, warmest parts of the hanging city where the rarest and most delicate plants are cultivated.

I imagine myself moving slowly through the humid, misty chambers near the thermal springs, my skin tinged with the same greenish cast as the plants I tend. The air here is heavy and still, broken only by the rhythmic exhalations of the valley's great steam vents. Sound is muted, movement deliberate. To speak is to disrupt the fragile ecosystem.

My hands are nimble and precise, trained through years of experience to detect the subtlest signs of stress or illness in the orchids, medicinal herbs, and spice plants that are my charge. I know each one intimately - the particular twist of a leaf that indicates thirst, the faint yellowing that precedes root rot. In this warm, womblike space, the plants are not merely crops but wards, dependent on my skill and devotion.

I have lived most of my life in this verdant twilight, leaving the upper levels only rarely. The dry recycled air and harsh electric lights of the Ledger-Keepers' archives are as foreign to me as the sun-scarred surface world. My place is here, among growing things, participating in Verdemoor's vast and complex system of sustenance.

When I communicate with my fellow Tenders, it is through a language of gesture, touch, and silence - a brushing of hands as we pass each other on suspended walkways, a quirk of eyebrow to indicate a task completed or a problem noticed. We are a quiet folk, attuned to the wordless communication of our charges. To care for that which cannot speak, one must first learn to listen without sound.

During rest shifts, I don't leave the Deep Green. I sleep in a woven hammock stretched between the warm pipes, letting their vibration lull me into slumber. I eat the strange and precious fruits of my labor - a wrinkled citrus, a peppery rhizome. In my scant leisure time, I pore over ancient horticultural manuals, seeking forgotten wisdom to apply to the needs of the now.

I know, with the certainty of a seed that knows only darkness and moisture, that I will live and die here in Verdemoor's humid heart. When my hands finally stiffen with age and my eyes dim even to bioluminescent glow, I will welcome the mulch pits. It is a fitting end, to nourish that which I have spent a lifetime nourishing.

This is my place in the breathwork of the valley - tending the rarest roots of the deep green, cultivating light in the dampened shadows of the hanging city. I need nothing more than this: the patience of plants, the company of my fellow green-stained Tenders, and the constant, cycling breath of Verdemoor itself. It is enough. It is everything.
