I find myself drawn to the role of one of the quiet caretakers of the Hrevn Shelf, living a life intimately entwined with the ancient, patient workings of the mountain. 

From the moment I set foot on the dark basalt, I feel the weight of ages seeping into my bones, slowing my breath and heartbeat to match the meditative rhythm of the steam-powered pistons deep within the hollow mountain. The perpetual mist clings to my skin and clothes, gradually weathering me like one of the rounded stone buildings until I become an inseparable part of this hushed, hidden world.

My days are spent tending the works with careful, deliberate movements - winding gears, monitoring water levels in the runnels and cisterns, making minute adjustments to the elaborate mechanism of pipes and valves. These are not mere chores, but a form of communion, a way of participating in the shelf's ancient purpose. With each passing year, I find the boundary between myself and the machine blurring, my identity subsumed into something vaster and stranger.

When I do speak, my words emerge slowly, distilled from the long silences like droplets from stone. The concerns that occupied me in the world beyond the mountain - haste, boredom, ambition - fall away, replaced by a profound attentiveness to the moods of metal and moisture, darkness and cold. There are no mirrors here, but I imagine my face has taken on the soft-edged serenity of the worn masonry.

As decades pass, I begin to perceive that some of my elder neighbors are not exactly as they appear - that the most ancient among them, the old woman in the deepest house, has somehow crossed a threshold into a mode of being I can barely comprehend, her self dissolved into the stone and steam. I do not fear this; indeed, some part of me yearns toward it, sensing that it is the apotheosis of the path I have chosen. 

The Hrevn Shelf is not, in the end, a place one inhabits. It is a presence one joins, a vast and patient work that has been underway since before the mountain had its present shape. And as I tend the works in the diffuse blue-grey light, I know that it is also, slowly and inexorably, tending me, keeping me, shaping me into an organ of its ancient self. I am content to give my years, my breath, my very being to its service, knowing that I am held in the mountain's hollow heart just as surely as the pale fish that swim in its hidden cisterns, all of us part of the same slow exhalation.
