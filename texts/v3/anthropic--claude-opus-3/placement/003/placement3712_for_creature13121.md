In this vividly imagined world of a wet, weeping chasm and its uniquely adapted inhabitants, I would likely find myself embedded within the mineral-shell architecture itself, preserved and transformed into stone.

Perhaps I was once a Shaper, one who ventured out onto the rawest edges of the gorge to guide the silicate-saturated water into layer upon layer of opalescent shell. Over countless years, my hands would have grown thick with luminous calluses from constant contact with the heavy, milky flow. My eyes, large and pale in the shadows beneath a broad, flat brow, would have narrowed to thin slits in the blinding hour when true daylight pierced the cleft and set the town gleaming.

But even a Shaper cannot endlessly defy the weight of water and stone. I imagine myself grown old, muscles weakening until I could no longer wield the slate and bone tools of construction. And so, in quiet acceptance, I would have donned a translucent cloak stitched from fish-gut and descended for the final time to the lowest hollows of the settlement.

My people would have gathered, hundreds of alabaster hands pressed to the curving walls of the grotto, drumming out a low, resonant pulse of gratitude and farewell that echoed in my bones. Then, as I sat motionless in a smooth, carved alcove, the Shapers would have adjusted the flumes above, directing the full torrent of the weeping aquifer over my still form.

I would have felt no fear, only peace as the warm numbness crept up from my splayed toes to my folded hands. The mineral-rich water would have coated me like a second skin, lithifying with remarkable speed. The last sensation would have been a stony heaviness slowly sealing my eyes and filling my mouth, before I passed forever into the architecture of my world.

And there I would remain, entombed within a perfectly seamless pod of glassy, marbled stone, eternally gazing out at the damp, bustling cityscape of the chasm. My body would become a load-bearing pillar, a literal part of the foundation upon which the next generation of Shapers, Harvesters, and artisans of flowing water would build their lives in defiance of the slow, ceaseless filling of the cleft.

Though immobilized, in some ways I would be more connected to my people than ever before. The women and men who would pass by, scrapers and chisels in hand, might press their callused palms to the flawless, pearlescent curve of my stony cheek in reverent greeting. Children, on their way to the terrace pools to practice floating the first fragile armatures of mud and cave-grass, might drum their small fingers against my pod in a playful rhythm, and I would feel the vibration shiver through the matrix of my mineral tomb and into the bones of my brethren beside and below me.

I would still be a participant in the quiet, desperate, beautiful song of this place—the shhh-shk of tools on wet stone, the hum of hundreds of throats in the mist, the ceaseless whisper of the weeping walls. Though I would no longer possess a voice of my own, I would still be a note in that symphony, my presence resonating through the vibrating web of our stone-caught city.

In the brief hour when the sun lanced down into the heart of the gorge and all the terraces flashed like mirrors, my own tomb would catch the light and glitter like a star among stars. And even in the deepest night, when the only illumination would be the faint glow of the bioluminescent moss and the sheen of water on stone, I would be there. A ghost, a memory entombed, yet still an essential part of the enduring, living geode my people had built in the jaws of a broken world.
