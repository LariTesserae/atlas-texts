In the Drowned Parishes, I would be a Tender. 

I stand motionless for days in the shallow waters of a broad avenue, my elongated limbs folded, my gray skin mottled with the same mosses and mineral stains that mark the stone around me. To an observer I would seem like a statue at first, weathered and still, just another strange shape in this city of abandoned forms.

But I am listening. Through my splayed fingers resting on the water's surface, through the soles of my feet pressed against the submerged cobblestones, I feel the pulse and flow of the city's lifeblood. The water whispers secrets of distant rains, of silted drains, of minute changes in pressure and level. Information courses through me, as slow and patient as the city itself.

When I do move, it is with deliberate, stately grace. I unfold to my full height, taller than any human, and begin my gradual progress down the flooded street. My destination is known only to me - a grate that my sensitive hands detected was clogged, disrupting the critical flow. It may take me a day to reach it, each step an act of ceremony. Time is meaningless. Only the task matters.

As I wade through the shin-deep waters, small pale flickers of movement catch my eye beneath the surface. The quick ones, the young. They flit through the drowned underways, too fast to follow. I feel no kinship or concern. They are not of my world yet. In time, they too will slow, will settle into stillness and contemplation. But for now, I let them pass unmarked, ephemeral as minnows.

I reach the troubled grate, kneel in the pooled water. With ritualistic slowness, I begin to clear the debris, prying loose pebbles and silt, letting them wash away in the current. An hour passes, or a day. The water begins to flow properly again. I have maintained the equilibrium, served my silent purpose. The city will endure.

Rising, I cast my gaze across the gray stone horizon, the endless rooftops and spires fading into rain. Somewhere, carried on the damp air, I catch the faint thrum, the deep harmonic call of others of my kind. I do not go to them. I simply register their presence, their perseverance. We are the city's immune system, the white cells in its bloodstream, slow and vital.

I choose a new direction, a new current to follow. With each ponderous step, I leave the grate behind, seeking the next flaw, the next falter in the city's ageless dance with water and stone. The moss on my back thickens imperceptibly. The rain courses down my face, as familiar as breath.

I do not think of builders, of purpose, of meaning. Those are concerns for quicker minds, hotter blood. I simply am. I tend. Until one gray day, decades hence, I will finally fall still, calcify into stone and city and rain. A monument no one will mark. The Drowned Parishes will flow on.
