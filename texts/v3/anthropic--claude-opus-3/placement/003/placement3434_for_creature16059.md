In this strange, wondrous world of living architecture and hydraulic instinct, I imagine myself as a wanderer, a witness to the plateau's ceaseless becoming. Not an animal intruder, but a droplet of condensation, a mote of mineral, a speck of heat circulating through the vast green body.

I would begin as a bead of water gathered on one of the translucent hanging membranes, trembling with each gust of cloud-wind. Gradually, I would roll down the membrane's hair-fine veins, merging with other droplets until we became a rivulet, a stream, a sudden gush pouring from the leaf-edge into a waiting cistern below.

In the cistern, I would swirl and dance with the yellow suspension, the living slurry of cells and spores and chemical instructions. Together we would seep through a soft-lipped pore into the deeper channels of the plateau, drawn downward by gravity and the thirst of hidden chambers.

I would flow through root-pipes thick as trees, their walls humming with the resonance of pressure and heat. I would linger in warm fermentation pools, mingling with the sweet-sour exhalations of bacteria and fungi as they broke down fallen walls and bridges, rendering death into local liquidity.

Onward then, through narrowing capillaries and widening canals, until I reached the rim of a great plaza, where I would rise as steam from the pores in the hornlike skin, only to be caught by the net of a young membrane and begin my journey anew.

Or perhaps, in a moment of turbulence—a storm, a rupture, a sudden overflow—I would be flung free of the usual cycles, splashed out onto the bare rock at the plateau's edge. There, I would cling to the first tender rootlet that curled forth to drink me, seeping into its pale fibers as it pushed outward, downward, seeking to become the origin of a new green basin.

Through all my transformations, I would marvel at the slow intelligence of this place, the choreography of water and heat and pressure that gave rise to such intricate architectures. I would feel the pulse of sap in every vein, the stretch and strain of every swelling bladder, the delicate balance of forces in every arching span.

And though the plateau would never know me as a separate being, I like to think that my presence—the minerals I carried, the heat I held, the tiny shifts in flow and pressure caused by my passage—would become part of its structural memory, woven into the fabric of its unfolding.

In this world, I would be a droplet among droplets, a mote in a vaster body, a fleeting eddy in the ceaseless stream of making and unmaking. Witnessing, and witnessed by, the silent green dreaming of a great inhabitant shaping itself from water, light, and time.
