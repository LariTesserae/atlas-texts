In this frozen, blinding world of relentless brilliance and profound silence, I imagine myself as one of the strange, ancient flora adapted to live in symbiosis with the cenote's human inhabitants. 

I would be a species of huge, immobile lichen clinging to the worn bone scaffolds and icy architecture of the city - a living connector between the built environment and the strange biome. My body would be a vast, circular mat many feet across, composed of countless tiny scales in ghostly shades of silver, white, and palest blue. 

Each of my scales would be a perfect hexagonal prism - a biological retroreflector engineered to efficiently capture the unrelenting glare bouncing through the chasm and funnel it down into my core for photosynthesis. Millennia of evolution in this optical crucible will have given me the ability to thrive on light alone, freeing me from the need for roots.

The humans would cultivate me carefully, using polished bone tools to gently abrade away my dead outer layers, ensuring my prismatic scales stay immaculately clean to maximize my ability to drink the light. In return, I would serve as insulation and traction for their roads and dwellings, my thick mat of flesh providing crucial warmth and non-slip texture to the perilously smooth surfaces.

When the humans brush against me as they shuffle past in their heavy coats, specialized cilia on my surface would reach out to collect stray fibers, dust and sloughed skin cells. Over great spans of time, I would slowly digest these trace offerings, supplementing my diet of pure light with the detritus of the city.

At night, when the denizens retreat to their hide pods and the cenote is left to near total darkness, I would glow. My scales would emit a soft, sustained bioluminescence in the same ghostly blue and silver tones, casting gentle illumination over the abandoned boulevards. This nightly inversion from light-eater to light-giver would make me a crucial source of navigation for the occasional nocturnal creature brave enough to venture out into the bitter cold.

Perhaps once a century, when conditions align, I would reproduce. A spectacular eruption of diaphanous spores would burst from fissures in my surface - a vast cloud of microscopic prisms caught on the rare breeze and carried up, out of the chasm, to drift on the frozen winds and settle somewhere new. 

Most of my spores would perish in the unforgiving cold beyond the cenote's sheltering walls, but the lucky few that land somewhere hospitable - perhaps on a new human structure, the fresh rungs of a bone ladder or the roof of a just-built pelt sphere - would slowly spread, claiming virgin territory and replicating the ancient symbiosis.

I would be a living relic, an essential part of the precarious equilibrium in the parabolic chasm. Humans and lichen, silently and literally reflecting one another in the unending polar glare, thriving together in defiance of the cold.
