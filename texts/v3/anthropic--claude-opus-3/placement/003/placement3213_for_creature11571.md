In this weathered plateau city, I imagine myself as a moss farmer, tending to the lush carpets of vegetation that thrive on the north-facing walls and in the cracks of the ancient stone. 

My days would be spent navigating the vertical gardens, using rope ladders and hooked poles to reach the highest terraces. I would have an intimate knowledge of each species of moss, fern, and vine, cultivating them for their medicinal properties and for the barely-fermented tea that is a staple of life at these altitudes.

I would be attuned to the subtle changes in the air and moisture, able to predict shifts in weather by the way the moss feels under my fingers. My hands would be perpetually stained green from the plants I tend, and my skin would have a pale, almost translucent quality from the filtered sunlight.

In my free time, I would join the other moss farmers in our communal drying rooms, sharing stories and knowledge passed down through generations of our trade. We would exchange cuttings of rare varieties, discussing new ways to encourage growth in the most challenging crevices.

My home would be a small, cozy space carved into the rock, its walls lined with the moss I cultivate. I would have a collection of intricately patterned scarves, each one telling a story of my lineage and the areas of the city I tend.

On special occasions, I might venture to the terrace edges to offer moss tea to the cable walkers before they disappear into the mist, honoring their bravery and the connection they maintain to the wider world.

As I age, I would pass my knowledge on to younger apprentices, teaching them to read the mountain's moods in the growth patterns of the ferns. And in my later years, I would transition to become a cloud watcher, spending my days at the edge of the stone, tracking distant storms and feeling the deep pulse of the city's life beneath me.

In this place of geological patience and industrial ambition, I would find my purpose in the quiet, steady work of tending to the green life that softens the stone, my own rhythms becoming part of the slow, eternal negotiation between mountain and city, moisture and air, ancient rock and adaptable life.
