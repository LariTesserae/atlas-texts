In the Breathing Chamber, I would be one of the thread-thin vines that traces the moisture paths down the luminescent walls. Clinging tenaciously to the limestone, I would grow ever so slowly over the decades, my growth imperceptible to any but the most patient observer.

My roots would delve into the tiniest cracks and crevices, seeking out droplets of mineral-rich water. I would savor these life-giving sips, letting them suffuse my narrow stems before expending that precious moisture to uncurl a new leaf or extend a searching tendril.

The light filtering down from above would be my nourishment, captured by my translucent leaves and transformed, through the alchemy of photosynthesis, into the energy to persist, to reach, to climb an infinitesimal distance higher.

I would mark the passage of time through the cycling of the chamber's breath - the gradual waxing and waning of humidity that makes the walls glimmer. My own transpiration would contribute to this primordial rhythm, an exhalation of water vapor from my stomata in invisible communion with the stone.  

In the diffuse glow of the bioluminescence, my tendrils would cast the most delicate of shadows, a faint tracery that sways with imperceptible air currents. Occasionally, one of the floating creatures might drift by, and I would feel the ephemeral brush of its diaphanous form.

The traveler, the woman, would pass me often in her patient circuits of the space. Perhaps once in a long while, she would brush her fingertips along my leaves, a fleeting connection between her world and mine. I would sense her presence more than see her, feeling the minute changes in air pressure and temperature that herald her approach.

Over great spans of time, my growth would describe a record upon the wall, a living hieroglyph that maps the chamber's history of humidity in the twists and whorls of my form. Coiled in my tissues would be memories of particularly lucent blooms of the wall's phosphorescence, of especially prolonged periods of droughts between the limestone's exhalations.

I would flower, maybe once in a century, sending out wisps of seeds on silken parachutes to drift like motes in the light shafts. A precious few would find a new crevice to call home, extending my reach to a new corner of our closed world.

And if one day the traveler did not pass by again, I would continue my patient growth, an unceasing witness to the chamber's slow miracles, climbing towards the light in an odyssey measured in millimeters and lifetimes, content in my small but vital place in the tapestry of this strange and wondrous ecosystem, a part and parcel of the breathing of the world.
