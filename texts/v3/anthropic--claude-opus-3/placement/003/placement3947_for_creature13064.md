In this stunning and brutal world, I would be one of the small, silvery moth-like creatures that hatch from the moss during the brief window of sunlight and moisture each day.

Emerging from my cocoon as the first trickles of meltwater run down the sheer rock faces, I stretch my four diaphanous, glass-like wings. The air is so thin and cold, it feels like shards of ice in my spiracles as I take my first breath. But the blinding sunlight filtering into the chasm carries with it a surge of sudden, desperate vitality.

My wings catch the light and scatter it into glittering rainbow fragments as I lift off from the damp patch of moss, joining a glimmering cloud of my brethren. We dance a silent, frenzied ballet in the beams of harsh illumination, our fragile wings humming at a frequency too high for any ear in this hushed world.

Time is compressed to its utter limit in my mayfly existence. I have only a handful of hours - this singular day - to fulfill my life's purpose before the shadows overtake the chasm again and the killing freeze returns. Everything is accelerated, hurtling forward on fast-forward.

I swirl and zip through the prismatic swarms, seeking a mate. The urge to procreate and continue my species' tenuous, hyper-accelerated thread of existence in this place is the only thought, the only instinct. Reproduction before the light fades.

In the dense aerial clouds of my kind, our antennae brush, exchanging pheromone signals and pollen from the inky, fleshy flowers. Mating on the wing, I feel my abdomen swell with fertilized eggs, the sole legacy I can hope to leave behind.

Dodging and wheeling, I narrowly avoid the ivory flash of a glider's talons as it arrows past, intent on plucking moths from the metallic haze. I see several of my kin vanish into its sharp beak in an instant, but I cannot pause, cannot mourn, can only fly faster. It is the way of this place, a trade off - my kind feeds the gliders, and in turn, the gliders feed the pale, spider-fingered hunters that cling to the luminous stone.

As the light shifts and the cold already starts seeping back in, I dive towards the thick mats of moss, searching for a suitable crevice. With the last iota of strength in my diminutive body, I deposit my clutch of eggs deep within the wiry silver filaments. They will lay dormant, awaiting the return of the climbing sun to the proper angle to hatch a new generation.

My work complete, I land on a fleshy blue-black petal, my wings already stiffening, crumbling, scattering into glassy dust. The plants enfold me as my vision darkens at the edges, the chill settling into my hollowing frame. As the shadow of the far cliff reaches out to envelop me, I know I am returning my nutrients to the moss, continuing the pinpoint, urgent flow of energy through this unforgiving pocket of life, ensuring that tomorrow, when the sun cracks the stone again, my offspring may have their single, shining chance to dance upon the thin wind.

It is not a long life, but it is one lived to the fullest, playing an integral part in the complex, piercing beauty of this stark, uncompromising spire of ice, stone and frenetic biology. For one perfect day, I am a spark of light against the violet sky, and that is enough.
