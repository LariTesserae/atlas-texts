In the world of Oost-in-Condens, I imagine myself as one of the pipe-tenders, a role both humble and vital to the city's perpetual rhythm. 

I am old, but not ancient - perhaps in my sixties, with skin the color of weathered copper and eyes a pale, lamp-lit amber. My hands are strong and sure from decades of climbing the great trunk-lines, listening with a practiced ear for the telltale notes of a rivet about to give way.

I live on a middle terrace, equidistant from the hissing furnaces below and the bat-moth roosts above. My days are measured not in hours but in circuits - I trace the same paths along the catwalks and pipes each day, feeling for changes in temperature, watching for shifts in the damp metal's hue that might presage a leak or a crack. 

In the evenings, I sit on a small balcony outside my quarters, sipping bitter beer and watching the grazers drift by on their endless circuit of the walls. Sometimes my grandchildren join me, and I teach them the songs of the specific pipes I tend - melodies as old as the rivets themselves, telling stories of the men and women who laid these lines in days beyond memory.

I am content in my work, in my place in the city's structure. I have never ventured more than two terraces from my birthplace, nor felt the need to. The city provides all - food, kinship, purpose. I am a small but necessary part in the machine of Oost-in-Condens, as much a component as any valve or gasket. The pump is my pulse, the pipes my veins, the condensation on my skin just another part of the water cycle that powers our world.

I cannot imagine a life outside this steady, steam-driven rhythm. To tend the pipes is to tend the city's breath, and the city's breath is my own. I will do this until my hands fail, until my eyes dim, and then I will make that final journey downward, giving myself back to the warm, damp stone and whatever mechanisms turn below. We are all part of the loop in the end, all held by the city's ancient, iron embrace.
