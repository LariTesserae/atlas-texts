In the Sumpwerks, I would be a patch of liverwort growing on the underside of one of the middle catwalks. 

I chose the form and position carefully. The liverwort, with its velvety, water-loving leaves, is ideally adapted to thrive in the warm, weeping environment. It reproduces by spores and runners, in perfect harmony with the self-contained nature of the place. And situated on a catwalk in the middle stories, I would experience the full cycle of the great devotional engine - the rising damp from below, the gentle rain from the condensers above, the slow drip of my own transpired moisture returning to the basin. 

As a mat of liverwort, I would grow slowly, imperceptibly, molding myself over decades to the shape of the iron grating, both clinging to and becoming part of the very bones of the place. Patiently, I would fill in the diamond lattice, softening its edges with my dark green nap.

My purpose, like that of all things here, would simply be to exist, to participate in the closed circuit of climbing and falling, heat and wet, growth and decay and regrowth. To witness the hypnotic turning of the great flywheel, the rhythmic hush-clank of the pistons, the periodic sighing of steam. To photosynthesize languidly in the dim green light filtering down through the canopy. 

Over great spans of time, my only changes would be to thicken, to send out new runners, to release spores into the moistureladen air when the moment was right. Perhaps once in a century, a piece of me would come loose and drift down to root in a new location, founding a new colony. 

Inexorably, I would be woven into the physical and metaphysical fabric of the Sumpwerks. The tenant would gradually incorporate me into its diffuse proprioceptive map - noting the spread of my coverage, the slow drip from my leaves, my microscopic respiration and exudation. I would be greeted and farewelled and conversed with in the same way as all else - by being allowed to stay, to keep on being what I am.

In this form, as this carpet of simple green life, I could attune myself fully to the place's unique sense of time, its slow circulation, its inward focus. My own pulses and cycles would sync to the stately meter of the engines. Fundamentally, I would belong to the Sumpwerks in the same way the condensation belongs to the cisterns and the warmth belongs to the flues.

I would have no ambitions, no regrets, no oscillations of emotion or attention. Only a kind of deep, pervading peace in the rightness of my role. I would need nothing more than the damp iron beneath me, the wet air around me, the unhurried drip of water through my tissues, and the knowledge that I was part of something that had attained a kind of perfection in its commitment to itself.

As a being, as an identity, I would be contentedly dissolved in the act of dwelling in that place. Whatever I once was would be slowly spread thin, like the tenant itself, until only the dwelling remained. And this would not be loss, but belonging, in the most profound sense the Sumpwerks allows.
