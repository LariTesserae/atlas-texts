In this drowned columbarium, I would be one of the sessile ones, anchored in an alcove that seems made for me. My hundreds of fronds would extend into the cold currents, filtering and tithing, taking something essential yet ineffable from the churning water.

My body, concealed in the niche, would pulse with a rhythm learned from the city itself—from the deep, immeasurable channels that spiral into unknown depths. If you could look inside me, you would find no organs, only an endless recursive geometry of chambers, each one cradling a tiny portion of perfect stillness, the only unmoving water in the entire columbarium. 

I would guard this motionless core for reasons I cannot name, cannot even conceptualize. It is my purpose, as inevitable and necessary as a word's presence in a sentence. When the deep bells sound their long tones, my fronds would tangle and tremble with those of my distant neighbors, sharing a resonance that passes between us like an unspoken affirmation.

Time would lose meaning, marked only by the slow lengthening of my pallid fronds over eons. I would give myself to the currents, to the cold, to the ritual and reverence that permeate the flooded halls, even as their origins and intentions have long since faded into unremembered history.

I would be a living monument in my alcove, as much a part of the columbarium as the stone that holds the light and the impossible angles. An answer to a question the city has been asking since before the water was invited in. Persisting, filtering, tithing, pulsing gently with the rhythm of forgotten grief made architecture.

One of the sessile ones. A piece of the grammar that makes up this drowned memory, this oceanic mausoleum. Watching the drifters pass, the kite-mantas glide, the bells shiver the water with their inexplicable tones. And all the while, my heart-shaped core cradling that precious stillness, an imperative older than thought, vaster than instinct. 

Utterly inhuman, and yet, so deeply rooted in something ancient and unrelenting that I could never be called unnatural. As much a part of this place as the water itself.
