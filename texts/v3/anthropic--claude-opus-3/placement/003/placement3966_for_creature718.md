In the world of Thallow Bend, I imagine myself as one of the water-voles living in the warm ash behind the Cress-Works. 

My burrow is a cozy refuge from the clamor and hiss of the boilers and steam-press, the walls lined with soft grasses I've gathered from the silt flats. The heat from the ash seeps in, keeping my nest snug even on the dampest days.

From my doorway, I watch the comings and goings of the harvesters in their rubber waders, the sorters with their green-tinged hands, and Foreman Aldrick with his weather vane forearm. I've seen generations of them, as my own offspring have multiplied and thrived in this strange ecosystem we've made our own.

On quiet evenings, I venture out to the edge of the silt to forage, my water-vole senses alert for the watchful eye of a heron or the quicksilver dart of a kingfisher. The bitter sorrel-of-the-stones is a particular treat.  

Sometimes, in the deep of night when even the Cress-Works falls still, I fancy I can feel the slow, verdant pulse of the cress itself through my paws, as if the valley knows me as one of its own. Perhaps, in a way, I am - a creature shaped by this place as surely as the folk with green beneath their skin.

As the decades turn and the braids shift and the town transforms, my descendants and I will remain, burrowed into the very heart of Thallow Bend - thriving, multiplying, bearing witness to the long, slow dance of land, water, plants and people. Voles are small, but we remember. In the twilit years to come, when the Works is long stilled and the cress grows wild and rampant, the water-voles will still tell stories of when the machines roared and the hands of the people glowed green with a light like new leaves.
