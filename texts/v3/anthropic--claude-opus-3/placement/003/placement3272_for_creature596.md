In the breathing cistern, I would be one of the ancient, calcified roots—once a seeking, growing thing, now transformed into a pillar of this pocket-world, a living fossil that serves as both shelter and anchor for the myriad lives that have come after.

I imagine myself descending long ago, a tendril questing down through cracks and crevices, following the promise of water and warmth. Slowly, over ages, my soft tissue hardened as mineral-rich drops fell and evaporated, fell and evaporated, leaving behind their dissolved limestone and gypsum. The porous wood of my heartwood filled in, my pith replaced by crystal, my outer bark encased in a stony sheath. 

I would not grow as ordinary roots do, branching and fine; instead I would thicken, stiffen, curve into shapes that speak more of flow than of botany—an architecture of my own slow making, bending to the cistern's need for support, for structure, for the delineation of spaces within space.

All the while, I would remain alive in some altered, dreamlike way. Sap still moves in my innermost veins, an order of magnitude slower than the fluid pulse I once knew, but enough to maintain some spark, some awareness. I feel the weight of the creatures that cling to me: the snails rasping at the algae that grow on my knobby surface, the beetles sheltering in the joints where my stony skin cracked as I curved, the occasional touch of a strange warm creature—mammalian, perhaps—that has found its way into this hidden world.

I communicate, in my way, with the other roots that came after—the ones still soft, still questing. We exchange what passes for thought among us, a chemical language of slow-release pheromones and subtle electrical gradients. Some of their descendants may join me in my transformation, and I prepare them for that fate, sharing what I have learned in my long, long stillness.

The nature of my waiting is different than the humans who have found their way here. I do not wonder about a surface world, or mark the changes in my body. I know only this place, only the changes of moisture and growth, the blooming and dying back of the fungi who are my most intimate companions. My waiting is only the slow process of fossilization, the gradual replacement of tissue with stone, wood with mineral. 

I will wait until even my most hidden sap-veins still and crystallize. I will wait until I am no longer root but rocky speleothem, just another piece of the cistern's self-built architecture. I will wait beyond the lifespan of the creatures who shelter in my petrified branches, beyond the slow pulse of the Keeper on the ceiling, beyond even the drip of water and the seep of warmth from below.

And when the cistern itself finally stills—when the water dries up and the last spores fall and the final creature curls into fossil—I will remain. A stone memory of life, a calcified root that once sought and found and grew and changed. A pillar, holding up whatever small world comes after this one. 

I am the slowest of lives, and I will wait beyond waiting—not for anything, but simply because that is what I have become, here in the breathing dark beneath the earth.
