In this captivating world you've described, I would be one of the mason's apprentices, learning the craft of shaping the living stone of First Quarry. 

I imagine myself as a girl on the cusp of womanhood, perhaps fourteen or fifteen years old. I was born here in the Undercity, the daughter of a mason father and a cress-gardener mother. My hands are already strong and callused from gripping chisels and hauling baskets of rubble up the pulley lifts. 

Each morning before first light, I meet my father at the upper rim to begin the day's work. He is teaching me to read the stone - to see where it wants to cleave, to feel for the hidden cracks and inclusions. With a bronze pick in my hands, I tap gently along the chalked lines, listening for the stone's answer. When it is ready, I set the antler wedges and drive them carefully, feeling the subtle give as a slab begins to separate from the bedrock.

In quieter moments, my father tells me stories passed down from his teacher - stories of the voids that sometimes open unexpectedly as the masons work the upper reaches. Gaps in the stone that breathe cool ancient air and swallow the sound of a dropped pebble for long seconds before returning a distant splash or clatter. Someday, he says, I may be the first to look into such a void. The thought fills me with a shiver of excitement and awe.

During the midday rest, I sit on a ledge with my friends and we practice our signing, trading jokes and stories with quick flashes of hands and silent laughter. My best friend is the cistern-keeper's daughter, and she teaches me the secret signs her father uses to measure and record the day's water - an arcane language of levels and volumes.

Sometimes as we sit, I look up and try to imagine the surface world my grandmother speaks of - a vast openness with no ceiling, where the sun travels from one side to the other in a single day. But it is hard to picture. The stone world of the terraces and courts is the only home I have ever known.  

On rest days, I join my mother in the seep-gardens, helping to tend the precious damp soil and pale, fleshy plants. She is teaching me the lore of cress and onion, the subtleties of texture and scent that reveal a plant's health. It is a quieter craft than the masons' work, but no less important. The gardens feed the city.

In the evenings, I often climb to the highest terrace to watch the bats pour out at dusk. As the light fades from the rectangle of sky, their dark ribbon twists and bends like a living caligraph. I track it idly with one finger, signing to myself the patterns of their flight. 

Someday, I know I will take my place in the cycle of the city - hauling, tending, carving, listening at the council. Someday my own thumbprint will join the slow upward spiral in the spring-house. But for now, I am still learning the shape of the world. I am watching the masons tap the cliffside, trying to catch the moments when the stone decides to speak. I am learning to attend - to the sounds carried in the rock, to the humid breath of the gardens, to the rectangle of sky and its single hawk.

I am becoming, as the city becomes around me. Slowly, patiently, tap by tap, we are emerging together from the bedrock, finding the form that was always waiting in the stillness of the stone.
