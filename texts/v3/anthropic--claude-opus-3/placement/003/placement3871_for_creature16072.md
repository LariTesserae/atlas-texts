I find myself drawn to the quiet, diligent work of maintaining this living machine of a city. In this place, I would be one of the water-keepers.

My days would be spent traversing the raised streets and grooved stairways, veiled against the glare and wet heat. With testing pole in hand, I would check the flow through channels and cisterns, ensuring blockages are cleared and pressures remain balanced. I would learn to read the colors of mineral deposits on the walls as signs of the city's health, and know which films to clear and which to cultivate.

In the early morning, I would make my rounds to each condenser in my district, polishing the glass and checking the seals before the sun reaches its zenith. The touch of droplets gathering and sliding down the panes would be as familiar to me as the sound of my own breathing.

When the rains come, sudden and torrential, I would not flee indoors, but continue my work. The feel of water sheeting off my cape, the drumbeat of drops on my hood, and the roar of runoff surging through the swollen gutters would not alarm me. They would be signs that the city is being scoured clean, its channels flushed, ready to continue its careful circulation.

On festival days, I would join my fellow water-keepers beneath the dripping arcade. We would raise our voices in quiet songs that echo the rhythm of the downpour, our chants merging with the clink of rain in the cisterns and the hum of distant engines. In those moments, we would feel our kinship with the city and all the life it sustains.

Over the years, my hands would become etched with salt scars and mineral stains, my eyes creased from squinting against the dazzling light. But this would only mark me as one who knows this place, who has learned to read its signs and live by its rules.

When I am old, I will pass on my knowledge to the young water-keepers who follow. I will tell them stories of great floods and dire droughts, of fissures sealed and condenser panes replaced, so they will know the city's history as I do. And I will teach them the most important lesson of all: that here, we do not rule, but humbly serve the elemental dance of heat, mineral, and water that allows life to find a way amidst the bright severity of the plateau.
