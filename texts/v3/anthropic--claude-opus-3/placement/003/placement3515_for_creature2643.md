But I would not always be cloistered with my weaving. Some days I would accompany a lime-scraper to study the growth patterns on submerged stone, tasting the water to sense what mineral shelves may soon extend into our streets. We would navigate by the shadows of the three great summits, our narrow boats threading channels through the sunken forest. Returning, I would knot a new border cord at the edge of my largest map.

In this city of damp murmurs and gradual shifting, I would be a keeper and updater of its woven memory. Through knots and beads and fibers, I would encode our place's ceaseless renegotiation with water and stone. The maps I made would be the city's self-knowledge - a record of its quiet and continuous reinvention.

When my hands grew too stiff to weave, I would pass my scrolls to an apprentice, and spend my final season in the deepest chamber of my family's house, listening through sodden walls to the mountain's dreaming. Until one day I would drift off to join the ancestor-currents, and a dark bead would be knotted for me on the great map, marking another change the city had absorbed and understood.
