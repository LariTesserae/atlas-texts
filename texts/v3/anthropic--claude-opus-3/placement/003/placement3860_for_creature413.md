In this ancient seabed turned salt pan, I would be one of the salt-adapted lizards basking on the warm mineral crust. My scales would be a mottled pattern of grays and whites, perfectly camouflaged against the polygonal plates that stretch to the horizon. 

I would move with a patience born of a slow metabolism, conserving energy in the languid heat. For long stretches, I would be still as stone, my lidless eyes unblinking, absorbing the sun's warmth to fuel my infrequent bursts of activity.

My territory would be one of the dark warming stones left by the enigmatic Salters—a perfect spot to regulate my temperature. In the early morning, I would emerge from a shallow burrow at the stone's edge, my claws finding perfect purchase on its mineral surface still holding the chill of night. As the sun climbed and the stone drank in its heat, I would adjust my position, seeking the narrow band of optimal temperature. 

My diet would be spare but sufficient—insects and small crustaceans drawn to the moisture that collects in the crevices of my basking stone, perhaps the occasional windfall of a brine shrimp swarm swept onto land by a rare gust. I would hunt with sudden, darting movements, my jaws snapping with a precision honed by scarcity.

As the Salters went about their inscrutable tasks, I would watch with a lizard's unreadable gaze. Their slow processions and crystalline structures would be part of the landscape of my life, no more or less remarkable than the weeping of the mesas or the singing of the salt crust. 

On the rare occasions when the rains came and the pans turned to a shallow sea, I would retreat to higher ground, waiting out the inundation in a state of semi-dormancy. The floodwaters would erase my scent trails and hunting routes, but such upheavals would be part of my species' memory, our instincts tuned to the slow cycles of our home.

My life would be measured in shedding cycles and the slow accumulation of mineral deposits on my scales. I would grow in fits and starts, my body responding to the booms and busts of the salt pan ecology. In times of plenty, I might develop the vivid mating colors that signaled my fitness—a flush of rusty red beneath my throat, bright blue rimming my eyes.

I would be a patient observer, a witness to the slow dramas of the salt pans. From my basking stone, I would see the flamingo flocks come and go, the Salters perform their opaque rituals, the mesas weep their ancient tears. Generations of my kind would live and breed and die, our small stories playing out in the shadow of geological time.

At the end of my days, I would find a deep crevice in which to hide myself, an ancient instinct driving me to seek the dark and the cool. There, my body would slowly mummify in the mineral-laden air, my scales and bones adding their increment to the patient accumulation of the pans. And in that final stillness, I would become what I had always been—a small, perfect piece of the pattern, a mote in the eye of eternity, content in my lizard's knowledge of sun and stone and salt.
