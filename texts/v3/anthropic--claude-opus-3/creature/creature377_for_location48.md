In this harsh and unforgiving landscape, only the hardiest and most well-adapted forms of life can hope to survive. The primary inhabitants are the Kryll, a species of small, six-legged creatures distantly related to insects. 

Kryll have stocky, segmented bodies covered in tough, dark scales that help retain moisture and protect against the scouring winds. Their compound eyes are large and sensitive, able to detect the faintest movements and see into the ultraviolet spectrum. This allows them to navigate the dim crevices where they shelter during the coldest parts of the day.

The Kryll survive by scavenging the meager resources of their environment. Their strong, spiky mandibles can crush seeds, burrow into desiccated plant matter, and scrape lichen from the rocks. A few species cultivate fungal growths in carefully tended tunnels and chambers beneath the permafrost.  

The largest varieties of Kryll grow up to 30 centimeters long. These are generally solitary opportunists, ranging far and wide in search of food. Smaller species live in loose colonies, cooperating to gather resources and raise their young in communal warrens dug into the mountainsides.

All Kryll can generate small amounts of electricity, which they use to navigate, communicate, and manipulate their environment. The most adept can sense and interact with the strange energies permeating the landscape, drawing on them to power esoteric technologies. Some ancient ruins show signs of long-ago Kryll modification and inhabitation.

Kryll reproduction is cyclical, tied to the subtle seasonal changes of their world. In a brief, frenetic mating season, winged males take to the skies, riding the updrafts in search of receptive females. Fertilized eggs are laid in secure crèches, where they lie dormant until conditions are right for hatching.

These creatures are patient, unhurried, and unrelentingly practical. They have evolved to exploit every niche and opportunity their world has to offer. The Kryll do not dream of leaving their mountain home; it is the only life they have ever known. And yet, over countless generations, they have shaped and been shaped by it, finding a stark, spare beauty in its challenges and privations.

Kryll and their world exist in dynamic equilibrium, each a small but vital part of something ancient and incomprehensible. To the Kryll, the crumbling ruins are as eternal and inexplicable as the mountains themselves. They scuttle through the long-abandoned halls, unaware of the lingering presence of their makers, as distant and unfathomable as the stars wheeling overhead in the thin, cold night.
