In this eerie lunar crater, where ancient lava flows have congealed into hexagonal columns and enigmatic extraterrestrial life tenaciously takes root, there dwells a lone xenobiologist—the final inhabitant of the abandoned research outpost.

Dr. Asta Iversen, age 51 Terran Standard years, arrived with the original survey team over a decade ago, tasked with studying the moon's peculiar extremophile ecosystem. But as the years of isolation wore on and the alien environment exacted its toll, one by one the other scientists departed, until only Dr. Iversen remained.

In solitude, her fascination with the strange lifeforms has deepened into obsession. The glowing neural nets, the ambulatory oil-drops, the slow dance of the gossamer stalks—she's convinced they hold the keys to unlocking revolutionary advances in biomechanical computing, regenerative medicine, and more. If only she can decipher their inner workings before her scant supplies run out.

Each "morning" (though the half-eclipsed sky never truly changes), Dr. Iversen emerges from the dilapidated habitat in her weathered EVA suit, toolkit and sample containers at the ready. With deliberate care, she navigates the labyrinthine forest of columns, tending to her "garden"—the lifeforms she semi-cultivates between the pillars nearest the outpost.

Through meticulous experiments, Dr. Iversen has coaxed the luminous filaments to grow in different patterns by modulating the feeble light and radiation reaching the crater floor with jury-rigged shields and reflectors. She "feeds" the lichen with mists of precious water vapor, harvested from the suit's recyclers. Delicate probes and cameras record the oil-drops' migrations, hinting at complex algorithms guiding their behavior.

But Dr. Iversen's communion with the native life has come at a cost. The unfiltered radiation, the alien biochemistry, the loneliness and deprivation—it all gnaws at the moorings of her mind. Her speech grows strange, addressing the specimens like old friends. The line blurs between analysis and anthropomorphization. 

Some "nights," suit piled in a corner, she huddles shivering over a meager heater, sipping brackish recycled water, watching the immense planet cast its ribbons of light through the viewport. She ponders the specimens' cellular secrets, and whether the lichen's hues indicate contentment or distress today. 

She wonders if the others will return soon with the new equipment she requested. She wonders what season it is back in the fjords of home. She wonders if the oil-drops dream as they circulate, what patterns could make them dance for her, if she's nearing a breakthrough or slowly going mad.

Then with a start, she remembers the others aren't coming back—she's alone here now with her silent charges, for good or ill. But that's alright, the work still calls to her, the discoveries yet to be made. She'll continue on, the sole mind watching over this unearthly Eden, until her own light gutters out and joins the cold glow between the columns.
