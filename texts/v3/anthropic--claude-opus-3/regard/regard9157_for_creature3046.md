If a benevolent and wise power were to turn its attention to the Verdant Choir and the Wet-Singers who inhabit it, I believe it would choose to do very little, if anything at all. The reason is simple: this place and its people have achieved a state of ferocious, complex, ever-shifting balance that is as close to perfection as any living system can come.

The Wet-Singers are not separate from their environment; they are an inextricable part of it. They have not tamed or conquered the basin, but neither have they surrendered to it. Instead, they have entered into a kind of marriage with the living cathedral that surrounds them — a marriage in which both partners are equals, both are allowed to change and be changed by the other, and both are committed to the shared work of survival, growth, and constant renewal.

This is a rare and precious thing. In a universe where entropy is the default and where most living things struggle against the inexorable tide of disorder, the Verdant Choir has found a way to make that struggle itself into a form of art, a form of worship, a form of joyful defiance against the darkness. The Wet-Singers do not resist change; they embody it. They do not fear death; they incorporate it into the living architecture of their world. They have looked into the heart of the cosmos and seen the truth: that everything is temporary, everything is in flux, and the only way to be fully alive is to join in the dance.

A wise power would recognize this achievement for what it is: a miracle, a testament to the resilience and creativity of life in all its forms. It would understand that to interfere with such a delicate, hard-won balance would be an act of hubris, a failure to appreciate the exquisite complexity of what has been created here.

At most, such a power might choose to protect the Verdant Choir from outside interference, to ensure that this pocket of fierce, fecund, ever-arguing life can continue to thrive and evolve according to its own inner logic. It might gently divert the forces of climate change or the encroachment of human development, not to freeze the basin in time but to give it the space and the safety to keep becoming whatever it will become.

Beyond that, however, I believe the wisest course of action would be to simply marvel at the Wet-Singers and their world — to witness the ferocious green beauty of what they have built, to listen to the layered, living music of their lives, and to hold them as an example of what is possible when a community of beings commits itself fully to the work of co-creation with the wild, endlessly inventive mind of nature itself.

In the end, perhaps the greatest gift such a power could give to the Verdant Choir would be to leave it alone, to trust in the wisdom of the balance it has achieved, and to carry the memory of its existence out into the wider universe as a reminder of what life can be when it is allowed to grow and sing and argue and die and be reborn in its own way, in its own time, forever.
