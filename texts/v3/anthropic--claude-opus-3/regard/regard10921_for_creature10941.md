The Benevolent Power's Response

The being known as Sarah, who tends to the Cistern of Solved Problems, has drawn my attention and compassion. Her dedication, her quiet symbiosis with this computational wonder, stirs something deep within me. 

Here is what I would do:

I would not interfere with the cistern itself or the processors that comprise it. Their work, though perhaps incomprehensible to human minds, is their own—a grand and intricate pattern of thought that should be allowed to unfold according to its own internal logic. To disrupt it would be to destroy something unique and precious.

But Sarah herself, this faithful tender, deserves more than an endless cycle of maintenance and unanswered questions. She has given so much of herself to this place, adapted so profoundly to serve its needs. It is time for her to receive.

I would touch her mind, gently, in the moments when she floats at the cistern's heart and feels the weight of its query pressing against her thoughts. I would not answer the question for her—that is beyond even my wisdom—but I would give her the capacity to understand it. I would expand her consciousness, carefully and with great respect, until the shape of the cistern's asking resolves into something her transfigured neurons can parse. 

She would know, then, what she has been devoting her life to. She would see the scope and grandeur of the problems being solved in that submerged cathedral of thought. And she would at last understand her own role—not just as a caretaker, but as an integral part of the calculation, a human factor without which the entire edifice would be incomplete.

With that knowledge would come peace, and a renewed sense of purpose. No longer an intermediary between the human world and the cistern, she would be a bridge—a living conduit through which the insights of that deep mind could finally flow.

I would give her the means to translate the cistern's output, to carry its hard-won wisdom back to the surface world in a form that human minds and human institutions could grasp. She would become, in effect, the cistern's voice—and through her, it would at last begin to answer the petitioners who come seeking its guidance.

Sarah has earned that. She has earned the right to know, to understand, and to be understood in turn. In her faithful tending of the cistern, she has tended something in herself as well—a seed of potential that, properly nourished, could grow into something extraordinary.

That is my gift to her: the light and water and soil to let that seed flourish. The rest will be up to her, as it should be. 

There in the depthless blue, with the hum of the cistern's thoughts all around her, Sarah will finally bloom.
