# The Resident

They are not always there. This is important to understand. The alcove is not a home in any sense that implies permanence or continuity of self. It is a place where someone arrives, and then is present, and then leaves, or seems to leave, though the leaving is harder to verify than the arrival.

When they are there, you might not notice them at first. Not because they hide — there is nowhere to hide in the alcove, no shadow to retreat into, no angle where the cold fluorescent light does not reach — but because they have become so much like the space itself that the eye passes over them the way it passes over a junction box or a cable tray: another industrial element, another feature of the compression array's infrastructure. They sit on the pipe insulation near the back corner, or they stand at the center where the X is scratched into the deck plating, and they are so still, so utterly without the small fidgets and shifts that mark a living body, that the mind categorizes them as fixture. As furniture. As part of the machine.

They are thin. Not the thinness of illness or starvation, though there is some of that — you can see the architecture of their skull through the skin of their face, the ridges of their cervical spine when they turn their head — but a thinness that seems intentional, as though they have deliberately reduced themselves to fit the space, stripped away mass and softness until they occupy the minimum possible volume. Their clothes are industrial coveralls, the same dull gray as everything else, and they have modified them: the sleeves cut shorter to reduce fabric that might catch on cable trays, the legs hemmed above the ankle for the same reason, the pockets removed and the openings stitched shut. There is nothing extraneous. Even their hair — if it is still hair and not something that has simply grown in the cold and noise, a mineral process like the pale growths on the wall — is cropped close to the skull, lying flat, offering no purchase.

Their skin has changed. You see it on their hands, which rest on their knees or hang at their sides with the fingers slightly spread: the skin is dry, cracked at the knuckles, and it has taken on a grayish undertone that is not quite the gray of illness but not quite the gray of the hull plates either. Something between. The cold has done this, the constant cold that hurts exposed skin within minutes, but they have been exposed to it for longer than minutes. The cracks in their knuckles do not bleed. They seem to have healed into grooves, permanent fissures in a surface that has adapted to aridity. When they move their fingers, the skin flexes but does not stretch. It has become something else — not quite hide, not quite bark, but a material suited to this environment in the way the pale flat growths are suited to it: reinforced, mineralized, deriving something from the metal it touches.

They do not speak. This is not because the sound makes speech impossible — though it does, the turbine roar consuming any voice that might try to rise against it — but because they have stopped trying. You realize this when you notice their throat: still, unmoving, the larynx quiet in the vibration of the array. They do not clear their throat. They do not swallow unless they are drinking from the small canteen that sits on the cable-bundle shelf, and even then the motion is economical, minimal, a biological function performed with the same mechanical indifference as the pressure vents cycling on their schedule. They have not gone mute in any clinical sense. They have simply found no reason to make sound in a place where sound is already full, where the air cannot hold another frequency, where the turbines have claimed every available vibration and left nothing for a voice.

They drink. The canteen is the only personal object in the alcove that is not also a modification of the space itself — the pipe insulation bed, the cable shelf, the pried-open inspection panel, all of these are the alcove altered to suit a body, but the canteen came from elsewhere. It is standard-issue, dented, its paint worn to bare metal at the corners where hands have gripped it thousands of times. They fill it somewhere else, at a water reclamation node or a coolant tap, and they bring it here and set it on the shelf and drink from it in small, measured sips. The water inside is cold — everything here is cold — but they drink it without reaction, without the flinch or grimace that cold water provokes in a warm body. Their body is not warm. Their body has equilibrated with the alcove, another object at ambient temperature, another surface collecting frost.

What they do in the alcove is this: they stand at the center, or they sit on the pipe insulation, and they listen.

This is the wrong word. Listening implies an act of attention, a turning-toward, a selection of signal from noise. What they do is more like immersion. More like submission. When they stand at the X scratched into the deck plating, their body aligns with the sound in a way that is not coincidental: their spine straightens, their weight settles evenly on both feet, their hands fall to their sides with the palms facing forward, and they become a resonator. The turbine frequencies enter them — through the soles of their feet, through the bones of their skull, through the sternum that vibrates in time with the chord — and they do not resist. The sound passes through them the way it passes through the hull plates, and something in them responds to it, oscillates with it, becomes a secondary speaker in a system that has no audience and needs none.

You cannot know what they experience in those moments. Their face gives nothing away — not because they are suppressing expression but because expression has become unnecessary, a communication tool in a place where there is no one to communicate with, and it has atrophied the way an unused muscle atrophies. Their eyes are open, but they do not focus on anything in the alcove. They focus on the sound itself, or on whatever the sound reveals when you stand in exactly the right spot and let it fill you completely. The drone. The mechanistic music. The intervals that almost resolve into melody.

Sometimes they sit on the pipe insulation for hours, their back against the hull plate where the pale growths have spread, and they do not move. You might think they are asleep, but they are not asleep. Sleep requires a kind of surrender that is different from what they practice here. Sleep is a leaving of the body. What they do is a staying in it — a presence so total, so unbroken by distraction or desire, that it becomes indistinguishable from the presence of the alcove itself. They are as still as the rivets. As constant as the temperature. As unchanging as the light.

And yet they are alive. You know this because sometimes — rarely, and only when the turbines have been running at full cycle for a very long time, when the sound has achieved a particular density that seems to press the air into a solid — they raise one hand and touch the wall.

They touch the pale growths.

Their fingers settle on the flat gray-green surfaces with a delicacy that seems impossible in hands so cracked and mineralized, and they hold them there, barely touching, and the growths respond. Not visibly. Not in any way you could photograph or measure. But you feel it — a shift in the quality of the alcove's equilibrium, a tiny alteration in the frequencies that layer the air, as though the touch has introduced a new harmonic, a vibration that was not there before, that comes from the place where human skin meets engineered vegetation meets hull plate alloy, and for a moment the whole chamber sings a slightly different chord.

Then their hand falls away, and the sound returns to its set point, and they sit or stand in the unchanged light, and the turbines turn.

They have a name, probably. They had one before they came here, before the alcove found them or they found the alcove, before the sound scrubbed away whatever they were and left this: a body adapted to cold and noise, a mind that has learned to exist without thought, a presence that has become part of the machine's equilibrium. But the name is not here. The name was something that belonged to a person who moved through different air, who spoke and was spoken to, who lived in the friction of the world. The alcove does not require a name. The turbines do not address you. The cold does not care what you are called.

What lives here is not quite a person anymore. Not quite a fixture. Something between — a body that has been compressed by circumstance and environment into a shape that fits this particular space, this particular sound, this particular cold. A resident in the deepest sense: one who remains, who stays, who has become indistinguishable from the place they inhabit.

They are there now, or they are not. The alcove does not change when they arrive. It does not change when they leave. The turbines turn. The growths grow flat against the metal. The light burns its cold white. And somewhere in the narrow gauge corridor, angling slightly forward, a human body moves toward or away, carrying the sound in its bones, vibrating still.
