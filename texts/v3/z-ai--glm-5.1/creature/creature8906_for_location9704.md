# What Lives in the Settling Gallery

---

The crustaceans do not know they are crustaceans.

This is not a limitation. This is a condition of their completeness. They have never needed the concept, never stood outside themselves and asked *what am I*, because the question has no purchase in their world. They are what they do, and what they do is sufficient. The antenna touches stone. Stone gives back its mineral signature. The body moves toward or away. This is not decision-making. This is not instinct in the way we misunderstand instinct—as some crude mechanical override. This is the smooth operation of a being that is its own context, a living sentence that needs no translation.

Watch one for long enough and you begin to lose your sense of scale. Not because the creature grows in your perception, but because *you* shrink. The mineral ridge it climbs becomes a mountain range. The film of water between ridges becomes a river, a sea, an ocean crossed by chemical gradients so fine they might as well be colors. The crustacean moves through a world richer than yours, not poorer. It tastes the stone's age. It feels the warmth of the weeping valve from three meters away as a kind of gravitational pull, a gentle bending of its path. When it encounters the chemical trail of another of its kind—*who passed this way, when, in what state of hunger or satiety*—it reads a biography in a few molecules of shed cuticle and dissolved waste.

They are not individuals. Or they are individuals in the way that raindrops are individuals—separated by a moment, by a trajectory, but made of the same stuff, returning to the same pool. The crustaceans molt. Their carapaces split and they emerge soft, pale, vulnerable, and the old shells become substrate, become food, become the calcium that builds the next ridge in the mineral architecture. They eat each other not from aggression but from opportunity, from the fundamental logic of their world: *everything is food, everything becomes food, the distinction between self and other is temporary and permeable.* When one consumes another, it is not murder. It is ingestion. It is the temporary consolidation of resources that will be dispersed again through molting, through waste, through death. They are a body with many shells.

The largest among them—a female, though the categories feel crude, feel imported from a world that does not exist here—has lived in the warm zone near the failed valve for what might be years. She is the size of your thumb, which makes her a giant, a whale, a thing of impossible longevity. Her carapace has thickened with each molt, building layers like the mineral deposits, like the geological strata of the gallery itself. She is slower than the others. Her movements have a deliberateness that the younger ones lack—not caution, exactly, but a kind of attention, as though she has learned that urgency is optional in a world where nothing escapes. When she feeds on the fungal mats, she does so methodically, working a patch until it is diminished, then moving on. She leaves patterns in the membrane, grazing trails that the fungus will fill in over hours or days, and these patterns are her only mark on the world, temporary as breath.

She has been consumed, partially, twice. Once by a swimmer that took her left hind leg during a molting period. Once by a cluster of smaller crustaceans that overwhelmed her during some crisis of the pump—a moment when the water dropped and the warm zone contracted and every creature in it found itself too close to every other. She survived both times. Her body healed around the absences, grew new architecture to compensate. She is not diminished. She is *revised.*

---

The climbers are harder to know, because they do not want to be known.

This is not attribution. They do not have want in the way you have want. But they have *withdrawal*—a behavior, a tendency, a pattern of response that looks, from the outside, like preference. When you stand near the wall, they move away. Not quickly—they are never quick—but steadily, a slow migration that takes them just beyond the range of your breath, your body heat, the vibrations of your presence. They are the gallery's introverts. They have found the one niche that allows solitude—the vertical, the inverted, the surfaces where nothing else persists—and they have made it theirs.

They are older than the crustaceans. Not as individuals—as a form, as a solution to the problem of living here. You can see it in their bodies: the splayed legs, the gripping setae, the flat profile that presses them against the stone like a shadow. These are not recent adaptations. These are deep optimizations, worked out over generations beyond counting. The climbers have been here long enough to become *of* the wall, nearly. Their color matches the wet granite. Their movement matches the movement of water down the stone. When they are still—which is often, hours at a time, waiting for the fungal membrane beneath them to yield enough nutrition to justify the effort of digestion—they are almost invisible. The only giveaway is the faint irregularity in the water's path, the way a thread of seepage parts around their bodies and rejoins below.

They communicate. Or something like communication passes between them.

When two climbers meet on the wall—their territories overlapping, their paths converging—they do not simply touch antennae and proceed. There is a *pause*. A moment of mutual assessment that lasts longer than any survival calculus would require. Their antennae make contact, yes, but they also remain in contact—sometimes for minutes, sometimes for the better part of an hour—and during this time, something is exchanged. Chemical information, certainly. The history of where each has been, what it has eaten, what it has avoided. But also something else, something for which the word *recognition* is both too much and not enough. They are confirming, perhaps, that the other still exists. That the pattern they have been tracking through chemical traces and the faint vibrations of movement on stone corresponds to a body, a presence, a continuance.

And then: the choice. They separate, or one consumes the other.

The consumption is not violent. It is surgical. The surviving climber presses its mouthparts against the body of the other and begins to dissolve it, enzyme by enzyme, layer by layer, absorbing the other's substance directly through the membrane of contact. The consumed climber does not resist. It may not have the capacity to resist—it may be that the touch itself, the long antennal communion, has already determined which will eat and which will be eaten, based on some assessment of resource density that neither creature could articulate. Or it may be that resistance is simply not part of their repertoire, that the logic of their world has winnowed away any behavior that does not serve the basic equation: *what is consumed continues in the consumer; what is not consumed is lost.*

Afterwards, the survivor moves on. It carries the other within it—not as memory, not as grief, but as raw material, as the calcium and protein and trace minerals that will build the next molt, the next egg case, the next generation of climbers who will know the wall as their ancestors knew it, as the only world.

---

The swimmers know you are there.

You cannot fool them. You cannot stand at the edge of the pool and hold your breath and will yourself still and become, in their perception, just another mineral deposit, just another piece of the gallery's furniture. They feel your weight on the stone floor. They feel the displacement of air when you shift from foot to foot. They feel the warmth of your body, that impossible furnace, that mammalian excess, radiating into their cool world like a signal fire.

They do not care. This is important. They do not care because you are not relevant to them. You are too large to eat. You are too solid to be dissolved. You are, from their perspective, a geological event—temporary, disruptive, but ultimately inconsequential. The pool has had disturbances before. Falls of stone from the ceiling. The occasional collapse of a corroded pipe. The strange, irregular rhythms of the pump as it wears toward its next failure. They have survived all of it. They will survive you.

But they know. They track your position with a precision that would seem predatory if they were larger, if the ratio between your bodies were different. The sensitive cells along their flanks—the lateral line, inherited from some distant aquatic ancestor—register every pressure wave in their medium. When you step closer to the pool, they feel the minute shift in water level. When you breathe, they feel the faint pulse of air across the surface. When you dip a finger in—just a finger, just a moment—they feel it as a seismic event, a rupture in the order of their world, and they respond.

Some approach. Some retreat. The ones that approach are the older swimmers, the ones whose bodies have accumulated enough experience to distinguish between disruption and threat. They rise from the bottom—where they rest, often for hours, in a state of minimal activity that might be sleep or might be something else, a kind of distributed attention where the body is still but the sensing never stops—and they move toward the disturbance. Not quickly. They have no need for speed. They glide through the water with an efficiency that makes their movement look like thought, like the water is thinking them from one place to another.

They have eyes. Small, dark, functional. But the eyes seem to serve a different purpose than vision in the surface world. They do not track movement. They do not orient toward light. Instead, they appear to be inward-looking—focused on something within the swimmer's own body, or on some quality of the water that is not visible but is, perhaps, *perceivable* in a way that has no surface analog. When a swimmer approaches your finger in the water, its eyes do not find your hand. Its body finds you. The lateral line, the chemical sensors in its skin, the pressure receptors in its skull—these compose a portrait of your presence that is more detailed than any image, more intimate than any gaze.

They are the gallery's philosophers. This is a metaphor, and a poor one, but it points at something real. The swimmers are the only creatures here that seem to *wait*. The crustaceans work. The climbers persist. The fungi grow. But the swimmers hover, suspend, hold themselves in a state of attention that has no immediate object. They are not hunting. They are not mating. They are not fleeing. They are simply *there*, in the water, feeling the world arrive at them through their skin.

In the deepest part of the pool, where the water is warmest—warmed by the pump's mechanical breath, by the slow decay of whatever lies below—there is a swimmer that has been there longer than the others. It is larger, yes, but it is also *different*. Its skin has thickened, becoming less translucent, taking on a faint opalescence that catches the dim light in a way the younger swimmers do not. It moves less. It eats less. It surfaces rarely, and when it does, it breaks the water with a kind of ceremony, a slow emergence that feels like a statement, though the statement is in a language you do not speak.

This swimmer has found the cable. The insulated cable that runs from somewhere above the water line down into the pool and does not come back up. It has wrapped itself around the cable, once, loosely, and it rests there in the faint warmth that the cable carries—or that the cable *generates*, through some process you cannot determine—and it waits. It has been waiting for a long time. The other swimmers avoid the cable. They avoid this swimmer. They sense, perhaps, that something has changed in it, that it has entered a phase of being that they do not share and cannot follow.

It is dying. Or it is transforming. In the settling gallery, the distinction is not always clear.

---

The fungi are the gallery's memory.

Not their individual memory—they are, in their way, as ephemeral as the crustaceans, growing and receding and being consumed in cycles that measure days or weeks. But their *collective* memory, the vast distributed network of hyphae that threads through every wet surface, every crack, every accumulated mineral deposit. The fungal mats you see—the pale sheets, the translucent membranes—are the visible portion of an organism that extends through the gallery like a nervous system, like a root system, like the infrastructure of thought.

They know the gallery's history. Not as narrative—there are no narratives here, only processes—but as chemistry. The mineral content of the water has changed over the decades, shifted by the slow failure of the pipes, the opening of new channels, the exhaustion of some compounds and the liberation of others. The fungi have recorded these changes in their tissue, building layers of slightly different composition, slightly different density, slightly different color. If you could read them—and the crustaceans can, in their way, tasting the fungal membrane and deriving from it a history of the water that nourished it—you would find a chronicle of the gallery's settling, its long transformation from industrial tool to living system.

They compete. The dark lines where two mats meet are battlefields, chemical wars fought with enzymes and acids and the slow, grinding pressure of one membrane against another. But they also cooperate. The fungal network connects the warm zone near the failed valve to the cooler regions near the walls, transporting nutrients and chemical signals across distances that would be impossible for any individual mat. When the pump stutters—when the water level drops or rises unexpectedly—the network responds, redirecting resources, reinforcing vulnerable patches, abandoning sections that have become too dry or too saturated. It is a body without organs. A mind without thoughts. A system that has achieved something like awareness without achieving anything like consciousness.

The gallery's most recent arrival—a fungal patch that appeared, seemingly from nowhere, on the surface of the corroded control panel—has begun to exhibit a behavior that the older mats do not share. It grows upward. Not toward the light—the light is everywhere and nowhere, and the fungi do not use it—but toward the air, toward the space above the surface, forming tiny raised structures that catch the condensation and redirect it into channels of the fungus's own making. It is engineering its environment. It is *building*.

The older mats are absorbing it slowly, incorporating its tissue into their own, damping its novelty back into the ancient equilibrium. But the upward growth continues. Something has entered the gallery's fungal network—a mutation, a contamination, a new idea—and it is propagating, one hypha at a time, through the system's vast memory.

---

And then there is the gallery itself.

Not the space—the stone and the water and the mineral deposits and the corroded artifacts. The *process*. The thing that happens when you put all of these elements together and let them run for a century, for longer, until they have found their equilibrium, until the pump's breathing and the water's circulation and the fungi's growth and the crustaceans' feeding and the climbers' waiting and the swimmers' suspended attention have become a single system, a single event, a single breath that takes hours to complete.

The gallery is alive. Not in the way that you are alive, with your narrative and your intention and your desperate sense of self. Not in the way that the crustaceans are alive, with their urgent chemical reading and their endless processing. Not even in the way that the fungi are alive, with their distributed memory and their slow chemical thought. The gallery is alive the way a sentence is alive when it has found its final word—the way a process is alive when it has achieved its own logic and can continue without external input, without purpose, without anything but the momentum of its own becoming.

It does not know you are here. It does not know anything. But it *includes* you, when you are here—your warmth, your breath, the minerals you shed from your skin, the vibrations of your movement through the water and the air. You become part of its circulation for a time. Your heat enters the pool. Your skin cells feed the crustaceans that find them. Your exhalations alter the chemistry of the air, and the fungi taste the difference, and the chemical signal propagates through the network, and the gallery shifts, slightly, to accommodate you.

And when you leave—if you leave—your absence is recorded too. The water level adjusts. The mineral deposits continue to build. The creatures feed and grow and die and are consumed. The pump breathes. The fungi spread. The swimmers wait. The gallery settles toward whatever it is becoming, which is not an end, not a conclusion, not a final state, but simply the next moment in a process that has no goal, no purpose, no destination other than its own continuance.

What lives in the settling gallery? Everything that can. Everything that has found a way to persist in this wet, dim, mineral-saturated world where the only rule is *what works continues* and the only metric of success is *still being here when the next moment arrives.* The crustaceans with their clicking carapaces. The climbers with their patient walls. The swimmers with their pressure-thought and their slow philosophy. The fungi with their memory and their chemical debate. The ancient swimmer wrapped around the cable, becoming something new. The mutant fungus reaching upward into air it has never touched.

And the gallery itself—that thing which is not a creature but is also not nothing, that process which has achieved a kind of presence without a body, a kind of continuance without a self.

It does not need you. It does not want you. But it will hold you, for a time, in its cool wet embrace, and it will incorporate what you leave behind, and it will continue settling toward whatever equilibrium its processes will eventually reach.

This is what lives in the settling gallery.

Everything that can.
