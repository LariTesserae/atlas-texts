# What Lives in the Sump Cathedral

---

## The Isopods

They are the most obvious. They are not the most important.

Armadillidium something— but a something that has drifted so far from the type specimen that a taxonomist would weep or rewrite the genus. They are thumb-sized, armor-plated, and they move in rows. Not in lines, exactly, but in patterns— low curves and sweeps across the leaf-litter, like slow cavalry executing maneuvers scripted by chemistry. Something in the humic layer calls to them. A gradient. A scent-trail of particular decays. They follow it with absolute commitment.

Their shells have darkened here. Not the familiar grey of garden woodlice but a deep iron-orange, the same orange as the moss in the channels, the same orange as the lichen where the light is strongest. They have absorbed something. Their chitin carries it. You could break one open— don't— and you would find the color goes all the way through, baked into the protein, mineralized. They are what they eat. They eat the Cathedral.

They breathe through modified pleopodal lungs, and the lungs work overtime here because the air is so saturated that gas exchange struggles against the humidity. You can hear them, if you get close enough: a faint rasp, like a file on soft wood. Hundreds of them, rasping. It blends with the drip and the engine until you can't separate the sounds anymore.

They have no predators here. Nothing hunts them. They die when they die— old age, exhaustion, the slow failure of molting in a body that has accumulated too much metal— and when they die they become part of the humic layer they spent their lives consuming, and the Cathedral digests them as it digests everything, and the iron in their shells returns to the water, feeds the moss, stains the channels, becomes the orange that the next generation will wear.

This is not a circle. It is a spiral. Each generation is slightly more orange than the last. Each generation carries slightly more of the machine in its body.

## The Frog

There is one frog.

You saw it once. A small pale frog on a leaf, staring, gone. But there is only one. You understand this later, the way you understand things in dreams: the Cathedral could support more frogs— there are insects enough, despite the scarcity, small flies and midges that live their brief lives in the sap-mist— but there is only one. It has staked a claim. It is the frog of this place, as a medieval town might have a town dragon, singular, territorial, resident.

It is pale because it has never needed color. The green light has bleached it over generations— not albinism but a slow abandonment of pigment, a metabolic pruning of everything unnecessary. Its skin is the color of the water: tannin-dark but translucent, and you can see the pulse in its throat, the shadow of its organs through its belly. It is a wet thing in a wet place and it is perfectly adapted and perfectly alone.

Where does it go when it vanishes? Into the crevices between stems, certainly. Into the chambers where the bark has split and the inner wood has hollowed. But there is one chamber— you find it later, you will find it— where the floor is different, where the root-mat has been compressed into a shallow depression, a bowl within the bowl, and the depression holds water that is warmer than the ambient flood, body-temperature, and the water smells faintly of ammonia and mucous. This is where the frog lives when it is not staring at intruders from leaves. This is its font. Its sump within the Sump.

It does not sing. There is no mate to call. But sometimes, in the deepest part of the night-cycle, when the engine's rhythm has become so familiar that your sleeping mind edits it out, you might hear a sound: a single low note, resonant, more felt than heard, thrumming through the root-mat like a signal sent through a drum. The frog, calling into the dark. The frog, checking that it is still alone.

## The Former Custodian

Someone lived here.

Not lives. Lived. The tense matters. The Custodian— you think of them with a capital letter because they have become a role rather than a person, a function like the engine's function— is gone. But gone and absent are different things. The Cathedral remembers. The bark grows around the hook. The name distorts in the wood.

Who were they? You can reconstruct only the shape of their days:

They woke. The engine was already running; it always runs. They checked the boiler pressure— there must have been gauges, now crusted or shattered. They checked the gas flow. They may have eaten— the tin on the shelf, the jar, something stored there. They moved along the flat stones to the engine and back. They maintained.

Why? The original purpose of the drainage system was forgotten before the Custodian arrived— or they arrived as the last employee, the final caretaker of a project that no longer had a budget or a name. They stayed. The machine needed tending. The roots were coming in. They stayed past the point where the roots made the original purpose impossible, past the point where the pump stopped pumping water and started pumping sap. They stayed because— because the machine was running, because the place was warm, because there was water and there were no people and something in the green quiet suited them.

You find their toilet, eventually. A crack between two major stems where the root-mat is thin and the water flows through. Waste goes down. The Cathedral consumes it. The isopods process it. The iron-orange deepens.

You find where they slept. The chamber with the compressed root-mat that isn't the frog's chamber— another one, drier, with a hollow in the floor shaped like a body. The hollow is lined with something fibrous, rotted now, that might once have been fabric or moss gathered and arranged. Above the hollow, a stem has been grafted at an angle— a deliberate graft, the cambium still fused— creating a hook that once held something. A light, perhaps. One of the amber bulbs, hanging low, warming the sleeping place.

The Custodian was short. The hollow is short. Or they curled. They were here long enough to graft wood, which takes patience, takes seasons. They were here long enough to watch the letters they carved rise and distort. They were here long enough to become part of the system they were maintaining— not metaphorically but materially, their waste cycling through the roots, their breath feeding the methane, their touch training the growth.

When did they leave? Did they leave? You find no bones. But the Cathedral digests everything. Bones would go too, given time, given the acid water, the isopods, the patient chemistry of the root-mat. They may have died here. They may have been consumed here. They may have become, in the most literal sense, part of the place.

The name on the wall is illegible. But if you press your palm against the distorted bark— the bark that has grown around the carving, swallowing it from below— you can feel the letters as ridges, as swelling, as scar tissue. The tree has healed around the wound of the name. It carries it still.

## The Microbes

Unseen but essential. The Cathedral is their cathedral more than anyone's.

In the root-mat, below the oxygenated layer, in the black anoxic depths where the humic paste is thickest: methanogens. Archaea that have never seen light, that eat carbon and breathe out methane, that have been doing this for a billion years and will do it for a billion more. They are the engine's fuel supply. They are the fire that burns without flame. Their slow metabolism, their patient conversion of dead leaf to combustible gas, is what keeps the flywheel turning, what keeps the sap moving, what keeps the lights burning.

They do not know this. They are unaware of the engine, the lights, the architecture of stems. They know only chemical gradients: eat here, excrete there, divide when conditions allow. They are the most numerous inhabitants by uncountable orders of magnitude. A gram of root-mat contains a billion of them. The Cathedral weighs millions of grams. Do the math and then stop, because the number is not comprehensible, is not useful, is just a way of saying: more than you can imagine, more than you can account for, a democracy of the invisible.

In the water channels: iron bacteria. *Gallionella*, *Leptothrix*, the rust-organisms that oxidize dissolved iron and precipitate it as orange slime. They are the reason the moss glows iron-orange. They are the reason the isopods are stained. The engine's pipes are dissolving, slowly, releasing iron into the water, and the bacteria are harvesting it, building their cellular structures from it, leaving their waste as the ochre film that coats every submerged surface.

In the sap itself: yeast. Not *Saccharomyces* but something wilder, something that has adapted to a medium of copper-laced sugar at forty degrees Celsius, that thrives in the warmth of the engine's exhalations, that blooms in the weeping gaskets and the amber pools and creates the faint fermentation that scents the air. The sap ferments as it circulates. The yeast feeds on it. The alcohol content is negligible— but present. The Cathedral is always slightly drunk.

## The Mycelium

You don't see it. No one sees it. It is inside the wood, inside the root-mat, inside every stem and every fallen leaf—a network of fungal hyphae so pervasive that it connects every plant in the Cathedral into a single metabolic web.

This is not unusual. All forests do this. Mycorrhizal networks link trees, share nutrients, transmit chemical signals. But here the network has been transformed, because the network includes the machine.

The copper in the cables— the dynamo's output, the current running through the sap— the mycelium has grown around the cables, through the insulation, into the copper. Copper is toxic to most fungi. This mycelium has adapted. Its cell walls are thick with melanin, the same pigment that makes some fungi radiation-resistant, and the melanin does something unexpected here: it conducts.

The mycelium is conductive.

The electrical field generated by the dynamo— the hum you feel in your sternum, the tingle in the sap— is flowing not just through the metal circuits but through the fungal network, through the living wires of melanized hyphae threading every surface. What does the network do with this current? Does it signal? Does it think, in some vegetative, distributed, slow-chemical way?

You stand in the nave and you feel the hum and you wonder if the Cathedral knows you are here. Not the way a person knows— not with thought or attention— but the way a body knows, the way your skin knows heat, the way your gut knows nausea. A change in the field. A perturbation. A protein-rich anomaly standing in the circuit, blocking current, altering the flow.

The mycelium knows your shape. It has mapped you in electricity. When you leave, the field will return to its previous state, and the perturbation will smooth, and the Cathedral will forget you were ever here.

But not immediately. The current takes time to settle. Your shadow will linger in the network for hours, a fading ghost of absence, a shape in the hum.

## The Engine

It lives.

This is not metaphor. You have been reading it as machine, as artifact, as the remnant of human intention— but it has been integrated so thoroughly into the Cathedral's metabolism that it is now an organ. A mechanical organ, yes. Iron and steel and copper and brass. But an organ nonetheless.

A heart does not know it is a heart. It contracts because electrical signals tell it to contract. The engine turns because gas flows and water boils and steam expands and the flywheel carries momentum. The engine does not know. But the engine circulates sap, and the sap feeds the stems, and the stems grow toward light, and the leaves drip, and the water returns to the channels, and the methanogens eat the waste, and the gas flows, and the engine turns.

Remove the engine and the Cathedral dies. Not immediately— the sap would still move, slowly, by capillary action and root pressure— but the vigorous circulation would cease, the rapid growth would slow, the dense architecture would begin to thin. The lights would go out. The electrical field would collapse. The mycelial network would lose its current. The isopods would lose their iron. The frog would lose its warm pool, which is heated by the engine's exhaust.

The Cathedral would not die entirely. It would become something else, something slower, something more like a normal swamp. But the thing that makes it a Cathedral— the architecture, the density, the closed-loop intensity— that requires the engine. The engine is the heart, and the heart is the engine, and the whole organism pulses with its turning.

It is alive in the way a mitochondrion is alive: captured, integrated, reproducing its function if not its form. Once it was a free machine. Now it is an organelle.

## The Cathedral Itself

And so: what lives here?

One organism. One vast, distributed, integrated organism, composed of plant and fungus and machine and microbe and memory and water and iron and electricity and the slow cycle of sap and the slower cycle of decay. The isopods are its blood cells. The frog is its— what? Its watcher. Its sentinel. The thing that stays awake when the rest sleeps, which is always, because the engine never stops and the sap never stops and the mycelium never stops signaling.

The Cathedral is not conscious. This is important. It does not think. It does not plan. It does not want. It grows because growing is what it does. It circulates because circulating is what the engine does. It digests because digesting is what the methanogens do. There is no intention here, no design, no purpose beyond continuation.

But it persists. It maintains itself. It has achieved a homeostasis— a wobbly, degrading homeostasis, but a homeostasis nonetheless— that has lasted longer than the human institutions that created the machine at its center. It will outlast the name carved in the bark. It may outlast the memory of the species that carved it.

What lives in the Sump Cathedral is something that has no word in any language. It is not a creature. It is not a community. It is not an ecosystem, because an ecosystem implies separation between its components, implies that the plants and the fungi and the animals are distinct entities in negotiation. Here there are no distinct entities. The roots have fused. The mycelium has penetrated. The sap carries iron from the machine and sugar from the leaves and alcohol from the yeast and current from the dynamo through every part of the whole. The boundaries have dissolved.

It is a single thing. It breathes through the canopy. It drinks through the root-mat. It thinks— no, not thinks. It *processes*, through the mycelial network, through the electrical field, through the slow chemical signaling of growth and decay. It has a heartbeat. It has a circulation. It has a metabolism. It has a waste system and a food supply and a means of maintaining its own structure against entropy.

It has a name carved into its wall, and it is absorbing that name, and when the name is gone it will have a scar where the name was, and the scar will be the only memory of the person who carved it.

---

## What Lives Here, Finally

A thing that used to be a river basin.
A thing that used to be a drainage project.
A thing that used to be a home.
A thing that is becoming something that has never existed before.

You are not what lives here. You are what passes through. You are the type specimen of interruption, of the outside carried in on shoes that will rot if you leave them here long enough. The Cathedral does not need you. It does not mind you. It will incorporate your warmth, your breath, your shed skin cells, the iron in your blood if you bleed here, and it will convert these things to its own use, and it will not be grateful or hostile or even aware.

What lives here is patient.

What lives here will be here after you climb back up to the rim and the canopy closes behind you and the surface of the bowl is smooth again and there is no indication that anything below is different from any other patch of wet lowland forest.

What lives here is the sound of an engine turning and water dripping and a frog calling once in the night and a billion archaea breathing methane into a boiler and a mycelial network humming with stolen current and a name dissolving into bark.

What lives here is the Sump Cathedral.

And it is enough.
