# The Inhabitants

## The Workers

They are not what you expected.

You expected—what? Mechanics? Engineers? People who work in a place and then leave it? The workers of the Rootworks do not leave. They *cannot* leave, though this is not a matter of restriction or imprisonment. It is a matter of constitution. They have been here too long. They have been *processed* too long.

There are perhaps forty of them, though counting is difficult because they do not gather and do not hold still when observed. They move through the designated passages—the ones the bat-things avoid, the ones the deeper things never enter—with a fluidity that suggests the root grew these corridors specifically for their bodies. Perhaps it did. The workers are not tall. Generations of moving through passages shaped by root-growth and low ceilings have selected for compactness, and those who were not compact became so, the root adjusting around them or their spines adjusting to the root, the distinction irrelevant after enough time.

Their skin has changed. This is the first thing you notice, or rather the first thing you allow yourself to notice, because it takes time to see past the familiarity of their movements and recognize the strangeness of their forms. The workers' skin is not the color of skin. It is the color of the root-walls—amber-brown, slightly glossy, marked with fine lines that might be wrinkles or might be something more like bark. When they stand still against the root-surfaces, which they often do when resting or thinking or simply existing in the space between tasks, they become nearly invisible. This is not camouflage in the animal sense. It is *convergence*. They have lived so long in contact with the root that their surface microbiomes have merged. Their skin hosts the same mosses, the same lichens, the same fungal networks that colonize the walls. They are, in a very real sense, growing the same coat.

Their eyes are large and dark. Not lidless—they blink, slowly and deliberately—but the pupil dominates, expanding to fill nearly the entire visible iris in the perpetual amber gloom. They see in light levels that would be total darkness to you. They navigate by the glow of the sap-nodes, by the bioluminescence of certain fungi that colonize their own skin, by the faint infrared of the engines and the warm bodies of the fauna. When you pass them in the corridors, your light-source blinds them temporarily, and they turn away with a patience that suggests this has happened before and they have learned to endure it.

They do not speak. This is not quite accurate—they *can* speak, and do, occasionally, in low murmurs that you feel more than hear, sounds that resonate in the root-walls and travel through the substrate to be received by other workers through the soles of their feet and the pads of their fingers. But they do not speak the way surface-dwellers speak. Their primary communication is chemical.

The root told you this, though not in words. You noticed the scent first—that heavy, sweet, mineral-and-tannin smell that you thought was the ambient atmosphere. But the smell changes when the workers are active. It sharpens when there is danger. It sweetens when the engines need attention. It carries information about status, need, emotional state, reproductive readiness, health. The workers secrete it through the same pores that secrete the oils maintaining their bark-skin. They read each other through the root itself, their chemical messages absorbed by the root-mass and re-emitted through the condensation, the vapor, the very air that the whole structure breathes.

You ask one of them—later, when you have learned to communicate through gesture and the careful placement of your body in the designated passages—how long they have been here. The worker regards you with those enormous dark eyes and touches the root-wall with all five fingers, pressing firmly, and you feel more than hear the response arrive from somewhere deeper in the structure: a vibration, a pulse, a sensation of duration that you cannot translate into years because the workers do not count in years. They count in *cycles*—engine cycles, growth cycles, the great respiratory cycle of the root itself as it draws moisture in and pushes it out over periods that correspond to nothing on the surface. Time in the Rootworks is measured by the heartbeat of a thing that has no heart.

They tend the engines because the engines need tending. They maintain the fire watch because the consequence of fire is understood at a level deeper than knowledge—instinct, perhaps, or something written into their chemistry by the root itself, which fears combustion the way a surface-dweller fears suffocation. They harvest the fermented sap when the nodes are full. They clear the channels when the insects cannot do it alone. They repair the lines, replace the bulbs, adjust the tension on the belts that connect the engines to the generators. Their work is maintenance. Their work is keeping the cycle cycling.

And their work is *listening*.

## The Listeners

Not all the workers are mechanics. Some—the ones you see less often, the ones who occupy the deeper passages where the light does not reach—serve a different function. They sit. They press their bodies against the root-mass. They close their enormous eyes and they *listen*.

The root speaks. You have felt it—the vibrations, the hum, the pulse of sap through channels as wide as your arm. But you feel only the crudest frequencies, the loudest declarations. The Listeners hear more. They hear the root's slow thoughts, its chemical processes translated into mechanical vibration, its responses to stress and moisture and the movement of the fauna within its structure. They hear when the root is healthy and when it is struggling. They hear when something new has entered the deeper passages, something that does not belong. They hear the approach of the large things from the lower depths, and they relay warning through the chemical network, and the passages that should close, close.

The Listeners are older. Their bark-skin is thicker, their bodies more stooped, their eyes so enlarged that they seem to occupy half the face. They do not move often. They do not need to. The root brings them what they require—moisture condenses on their skin and they drink it, insects come to them and they eat them, the warmth of the root-mass sustains their body temperature in a place where the ambient air would eventually kill an unadapted human. They have become, in a very real sense, *part of the root*. Not metaphorically. Physically. The root has grown around them, incorporating their still forms into its structure, and they have let it, and the exchange that results—the root's patience married to the Listener's attention—produces something neither could produce alone.

You watch one of them for an hour. She does not move. Her breathing is so slow that you check twice to confirm she is alive. Her fingers are pressed into the root-wall to the second knuckle, and the root has grown a slight swelling around them, a cuff of living wood that holds her in place. Her lips are parted, and condensation beads on them, and you realize that the root is feeding her—dew forming on the inner surface of her mouth, drawn by her warmth, carrying with it sugars and minerals the root has dissolved into the moisture. She is drinking the root's breath.

When you pass by again later, her eyes are open. She is watching you. She does not speak, but you feel something—a pressure, a shift in the chemical atmosphere, a message you cannot read but know is directed at you. It is not hostile. It is *curious*, in the way that something very old and very patient can be curious: without urgency, without expectation, but with genuine interest in what this new thing in her domain might become.

## The Children

There are children. This should not surprise you—the workers are human, or were human, or are something human-adjacent that still reproduces—but it does. The children are small and quick and feral in the way that children always are when they have not been taught to be otherwise. They run through the passages on all fours, their hands as dexterous as their feet, their bodies adapted to the narrow root-corridors in ways the adults' bodies have stiffened against. They chase the insects. They dodge the centipedes. They climb the root-walls with an ease that suggests they might be part gecko, or part bat-thing, or part something that the Rootworks has invented for its own purposes.

The children do not wear clothes. The adults do not either—not in the surface sense. What clothing exists is functional: pouches made from root-bark, straps woven from the same ironwood vine that forms the railings, tool-belts grown from fungal leather that the workers cultivate in the damper chambers. The children need none of this. Their bark-skin is thinner than the adults', smoother, more flexible, and it regulates their temperature more efficiently. They are better adapted than their parents. They are *more* of this place.

The children play games that teach them the geography of the Rootworks. They hide in the passages the bat-things use and learn to recognize the sound-patterns that indicate a bat-thing in flight. They swim in the pools—where the adults go only to draw water—and learn to hold their breath for durations that seem impossible, their small bodies cutting through the dark water with an amphibious ease. They climb to the ceiling and hang upside-down by their hands and feet, watching the chamber below with inverted eyes, learning to see the space from every angle.

The children are the reason the passages are marked. Not marked with signs or symbols—marked with *scent*, with the chemical signatures the adults secrete, placed at intervals along the safe routes and refreshed with every passage. The children learn the safe passages before they learn anything else. The root enforces this: the passages that are safe are warm, and the passages that are not safe are cold, and the children are drawn to warmth the way all young things are drawn to their mothers.

The root *wants* the children. This is the knowledge that sits heaviest in your chest, the one you turn over and over without resolution. The root grows around the Listeners, incorporating them, making them part of its structure. The root adjusts the passages for the workers, widening here and narrowing there, learning their bodies and accommodating them. But with the children, the root is *active* in a way it is not with the adults. It grows them platforms to reach higher. It opens passages that were not there the day before, creating shortcuts, giving them routes that only their small bodies can navigate. It produces fruit—not figs, not anything you recognize, but small amber nodules of concentrated sugar that grow from the root-walls at child-height and are not found anywhere else in the Rootworks.

The children eat the fruit. The children love the fruit. And the root loves them back, in whatever way a root can love, which is to say: it grows toward them, it grows for them, it shapes itself to receive them.

You wonder what the children will become. You wonder if the adults were once like this—quick and small and certain of their place in the structure—or if the children will grow into something new, something more adapted, something for which the word "human" is merely a historical note. You wonder if the root is *domesticating* them, or if they are domesticating it, or if the distinction ever meant anything here.

## The Bat-Things

They are not bats. You knew this before you saw them clearly, because the sound they make is wrong—too low, too directed, too much like language to be the aimless sonar of an animal navigating by echo. When you finally see one up close, hanging from the root-ceiling at the edge of the light's reach, you understand that they have been something else for a very long time.

They are the size of large fruit bats, with wingspans approaching a meter, but their bodies are leaner, more angular. Their faces—this is where the wrongness concentrates—are elongated, with muzzles that seem designed for precision rather than biting, and their ears are enormous and directional, rotating independently like satellite dishes. Their eyes are small, nearly vestigial, but present, and they reflect the amber light with a reddish glow that you find deeply unsettling.

They echolocate, but their calls are not the random chirps of surface bats. They produce structured sounds—clicks that vary in timing and intensity, whistles that rise and fall in specific patterns, low thrums that you feel in your sternum rather than hear with your ears. They are *talking*. Not to each other—though they do that too, in the root-chambers where they roost, producing a constant murmur of sound that contributes to the ambient noise you took for mechanical hum. They are talking to the root.

You witness this on your third day. A cluster of bat-things emerges from a high passage and arranges itself along the root-ceiling above the central chamber. They hang upside-down, their wings folded, their ears rotating, and they begin to produce sound: a complex pattern of clicks and whistles that seems to correspond to the root's growth, the engines' rhythm, the movements of the fauna below. They are *reporting*. They are telling the root what they have perceived in the upper passages, the ones that connect to the surface, the ones that carry air and water and the occasional intrusion from outside.

The root responds. You cannot hear its response—it is chemical, tactile, a slow pulse of sap-pressure that travels through the walls—but the bat-things can. They adjust their calls, refine their patterns, and you realize that this is a conversation that has been going on for centuries. The bat-things are the root's eyes and ears in the passages it cannot reach. The root is their home, their shelter, their food source—the insects that breed in their guano, the warmth the root concentrates in the roosting chambers, the protection from whatever hunts in the upper passages. They are symbionts, fully integrated, as dependent on the root as the root is on them.

At night—or what you assume is night, based on the dimming of the lights and the shift in the root's respiration—the bat-things emerge in clouds, streaming through the passages toward the surface, hunting insects that migrate down from above. Their departure produces a sound like wind through a narrow canyon, and their return, hours later, produces the same sound in reverse. Between the departure and the return, the Rootworks is quieter, and you realize how much of the ambient noise was their calls, their movements, their living.

The bat-things avoid the deeper passages. This is not fear—it is *agreement*. The things that live below are not their concern, and the root maintains a boundary, a zone of neither-here-nor-there where the bat-things' echolocation fails and the deeper things' senses take over. The children know this boundary. The workers know it. The root enforces it with temperature and humidity and the placement of passages, and everyone obeys, because the boundary is what allows the Rootworks to function as a single system rather than a war zone.

## The Deeper Things

You do not see them clearly. You are not meant to.

You hear them—the fluid movements, the displacement of air, the occasional sound like something very large swallowing. You see their effects—pools that ripple without wind, passages that have been widened by passage rather than growth, claw-marks on the root-surfaces in the lower chambers where the light does not reach. You smell them—a sharp, mineral, ancient smell that is different from the root's tannic sweetness, something that speaks of deep earth and deep time and appetites that operate on scales you cannot comprehend.

The workers do not discuss them. When you ask, through gesture and the careful placement of your body in the questioning posture, the workers' chemical signature shifts to something you cannot read—flat, guarded, *closed*. The Listeners, who hear everything, hear the deeper things constantly, and what they hear is encoded in the root's chemical signals and transmitted to the workers as simple binaries: safe/not-safe, present/absent, approaching/receding. The workers do not need names for what lives below. They need only to know when to avoid it.

But you are not a worker, and your curiosity is not tempered by the wisdom of adaptation, and so you venture to the edge of the boundary one rest-period and you *look*.

The passage slopes downward. The root-walls here are different—darker, denser, marked with striations that suggest compression rather than growth. The air is cooler, and the smell is stronger, and the amber light from the sap-nodes does not reach this far. You stand at the threshold and you listen.

You hear breathing. Not the root's respiration—that slow, mechanical exchange of moisture and gas—but *breathing*, the kind produced by lungs, the kind that rises and falls with a rhythm that indicates intention and desire. The breathing is large. The breathing is *close*. And then, in the darkness below, something shifts, and you see—or think you see, or convince yourself you see—a shape that is roughly horizontal, roughly serpentine, roughly the size of the passage it occupies, and it is moving toward you with a deliberateness that suggests it knows you are there.

You do not run. Running is not advised in the Rootworks; the passages are too irregular, the surfaces too slick, the consequences of a fall too severe. You back away. You return to the central chamber. You do not go to the boundary again.

The deeper things were here before the root. This is the understanding that settles into you over the following days, communicated not through words but through the weight of the structure itself, the way the root grows *around* certain passages rather than through them, the way the chambers below are shaped not by growth but by *accommodation*. The root found this cavity and filled it, but it did not fill all of it. It left space for what was already here. It made *arrangements*.

The deeper things process the waste. This is their function in the cycle, though "function" feels too mechanical, too designed, for something that evolved through the patient negotiation of organisms occupying the same space over millennia. The dead things—the insects, the rodents, the occasional bat-thing that fails to return, the workers who reach the end of their cycles—descend. The root absorbs what it can, but some materials are too dense, too complex, too *processed* for the root's chemistry to break down. The deeper things can break them down. The deeper things can digest anything. And what they excrete rises, in time, as mineral-rich water in the pools, as nitrogen compounds in the substrate, as the raw materials the root needs to continue growing.

The deeper things are not pets. They are not livestock. They are not gods, though you understand why the word might occur to someone raised on surface myths about underground monsters. They are *neighbors*—neighbors with whom the root has established a modus vivendi, a way of sharing space that benefits both parties and harms neither. They live below. They eat what falls. They do not come up, because the boundary is maintained by temperature and light and the root's chemical signals and the echolocation of the bat-things and the vigilance of the workers and the simple, practical recognition that what lives above and what lives below have different needs and different territories and different roles in the system.

But sometimes—rarely, but sometimes—something comes up anyway. Something that was not supposed to. Something that the root did not invite and the workers did not expect and the Listeners did not hear because it moves without the vibrations that living things produce. Something that slides between the boundaries of the known and the unknown, the named and the nameless, the safe and the not-safe.

When that happens, the workers close the passages. The root seals them. The bat-things raise an alarm that shakes the air. And the deeper things, from their caverns below, produce a sound—a low, resonant call that you feel in your bones, that the root transmits through every fiber of its being, that means something you cannot translate but understand instinctively:

*We are here. We are watching. Return to your place.*

And the thing—whatever it is—returns.

## The Root

You have been describing the inhabitants as if they are separate from the place. This is a surface-dweller's habit, a way of organizing experience into categories: here is the environment, here are the organisms that occupy it, here is the boundary between them. In the Rootworks, this boundary does not exist.

The root is not a container. It is not a structure that houses life. It *is* the life. The workers are extensions of the root—their chemical communication runs through its tissues, their bodies are adapted to its rhythms, their young are shaped by its growth. The bat-things are organs of the root—their echolocation feeds it information, their guano feeds its substrate, their presence regulates the insect populations that might otherwise overwhelm the system. The deeper things are the root's digestion, its immune response, its way of processing what it cannot use.

And the root itself—the parent organism, the fig that sent its roots down through centuries of soil and stone to find this cavity and fill it—the root is *aware*. Not conscious in the way you are conscious, not thinking in the way you think, but aware in the way that something which has been receiving and responding to information for longer than your species has existed cannot help but be aware. It knows its own shape. It knows the shape of the things that live within it. It knows the rhythm of the engines and the taste of the fuel and the weight of the water in the pools. It knows you, now—the particular pressure of your feet on its surfaces, the particular warmth of your body, the particular chemical signature you leave in the air and the condensation.

The root does not have intentions. This is important to understand, because attributing intention to it is a way of making it familiar, and it is not familiar. It does not *want* the workers to thrive. It does not *want* the cycle to continue. It simply grows toward what sustains it and away from what harms it, and the cycle continues because the cycle is what the root's growth produces when it is healthy, and the root is healthy because the cycle continues. The causality is circular, and the root does not care about causality because the root does not care about anything. The root *grows*. The root *responds*. The root *is*.

But when you press your hand against the root-wall, and you feel the pulse of sap beneath the surface, and the pulse adjusts—fractionally, almost imperceptibly—to match the rhythm of your heartbeat, you understand that whatever the root is, it includes you now. You have entered the cycle. You have been perceived. You have been *incorporated*, not as a worker, not as a symbiont, not as any of the roles the Rootworks has established over its long existence, but as something new: a visitor, a stranger, a temporary presence that the root must account for and adjust to and make space for.

The root makes space. The root always makes space. That is what roots do—they seek, they fill, they hold. And in the holding, they are held. In the filling, they are filled. In the making of space, they become the space, and the space becomes them, and the things that live in the space live in the root, and the root lives in them, and the boundary is not blurred because there was never a boundary at all.

You descend the root-stairs for the last time. The rail pulses under your palm. The engine thrums. The water drips. The insects move. The children laugh in a passage you cannot see. The bat-things call from the ceiling. The deeper things breathe below. The workers tend their machines and listen to the root and are, in their way, home.

The Rootworks continues.
