# Who Lives Here

## The People (they have no collective name for themselves—why would they?)

You meet her at the base of the third Column you've passed, or perhaps the fourth; the counting blurs in the heat and the sameness. She is working a section of the Mat's surface with a stone blade, not cutting—harvesting. The motion is specific and practiced: press, twist, lift. Peel back a section of the outer dermis roughly the size of your palm. Beneath it, the wet layer, pale and yielding, and this she scrapes into a woven pouch that hangs from her shoulder on a cord of braided filament.

She does not look up for a long time.

When she does, the first thing you notice is not her face but her skin—not the color of it but the texture, the way it has become something other than what you'd call skin. She is old, or weathered, or both. The damp has done something to her over the years: softened her, you think at first, but that's wrong. Toughened her. The way rawhide toughens when it's wet and dried and wet again, cycling through the condensation and the sun until the surface goes slightly repellant, slightly slick, a membrane that the moisture beads on and runs off. Her forearms are scarred in patterns that might be decoration, might be record-keeping, might be the simple accumulation of a hundred small encounters with things that bite and sting and burn. Her feet are bare and they grip the Mat with a sureness that suggests the nerves run deeper than the soles.

She wears the Mat. Everyone here does. Not metaphorically—they wear it literally, garments cut and shaped from the outer layers, cured somehow, though you don't see a tanning process anywhere. The material is supple, warm, and it breathes in a way that suggests it hasn't quite died, hasn't quite stopped being the thing it was before it became a wrap for her shoulders, a tie for her hair. The color is the color of the surface—dull ochre, brown-green, the nothing-color of something that wants to be overlooked—and she is the same color, or has become so, and at a distance she would vanish against the living floor and you would pass her by.

Her eyes are the wrong color. Not the iris—the sclera. A faint yellowing, like old paper, and you realize this is not disease but adaptation, some pigment responding to the constant ambient light, the double-radiance of sun and Mat, the years of it. She squints without seeming to know she's squinting.

She says something. The language is not one you know, but you hear the rhythm of it—low, clipped, the vowels swallowed, the consonants soft, as if the words themselves have learned not to carry. Sound carries here. Sound draws things. She is speaking in a way that will not travel past the two of you, and this is the first thing the children learn, probably, before they learn to walk: *be quiet, be quiet, the Mat is listening, the things on the Mat are listening, keep your voice in your throat.*

---

There are not many of them. You understand this gradually, the way you understand most things here—not through evidence but through the absence of it. No paths between the shelters. No middens that suggest shared meals. No meeting-places, no clearings, no marks on the Columns that might serve as waypoints for a gathering. If they come together, they do it rarely, and they do it somewhere you haven't found, and they do it in a way that leaves no trace.

Or they don't come together at all. This is possible too. The old woman at the third Column—or fourth—works alone, and the shelter she returns to holds one sleeping-roll, one hearth-ring, one rack. The next shelter you find, two days' walk to the east, is the same. One. Always one.

But there are children. You see evidence: smaller footprints in the soft tissue near the pools, smaller tools, a tiny shoe made of Mat-material abandoned at the edge of a basin as if the child who wore it had simply outgrown it between one condensation and the next. So they reproduce. So there is some mechanism for finding each other, at least sometimes, at least enough. Maybe the Columns serve as signals—maybe the vibration of the Mat, which they feel through their feet, carries information you can't read. Maybe there is a season for it, a cycle, a blooming of the people the way the surface blooms in the afternoon, and they find each other by scent or by sound or by some chemical cue that the Mat itself facilitates, carrying their loneliness across the kilometers in a molecular whisper that says: *here, here, I am here, and I am still here.*

You will never know. The old woman does not explain herself, and you do not know how to ask.

---

What they eat: the Mat. What else? The wet inner layer that she scrapes with her blade—this is the staple, the staff, the thing that keeps the body running between one sunrise and the next. The taste, she shows you (not with words, with a gesture, an offering, a piece pressed into your hand): mineral, vegetal, faintly sweet, faintly spoiled, the taste of something alive that has not been killed so much as *sampled*. It sits in the stomach with a weight that is not unpleasant. It gives what it gives.

But not only the Mat. The pools yield things that swim—pale, soft, eyeless like everything that lives in the permanent waters, adapted to warmth and darkness and the chemical runoff of the surface above. She catches them with her hands, quick, a motion practiced ten thousand times, and guts them with the stone blade and lays them on the rack to dry in the midmorning heat. They shrink to almost nothing. She eats them like strips of leather, chewing for a long time.

And the insects. Yes—the insects. She harvests them in a way you would not have thought of: she waits for the Mat to shed. When the surface rolls up and the fresh layer emerges, the grazers are momentarily confused, momentarily unmoored, and in that window she sweeps them up by the handful, by the hundreds, and crushes them into a paste that she spreads on strips of Mat-material and dries into something like a wafer. Protein. Fat. The concentrated essence of ten thousand tiny lives, rendered neutral, rendered food.

She does not hunt the larger things. No one does. The larger things are not for her.

---

The child finds you on the seventh day, or the eighth. Time has become a function of condensation and evaporation, not hours.

She is perhaps eight, perhaps ten—children here age in ways that make the counting uncertain, their bodies compact, their faces old early. She has been watching you. You understand this because she knows things about you that she could not know without observation: the way you favor your left foot on the uneven surface, the way you flinch when the fliers pass overhead, the way you sleep with your back against the Column like something might come for you from the open Mat. She has been watching and she has decided, for reasons you will never understand, that you are not a threat.

She follows you for a day. Then two. She does not speak. She shows you things instead: which pools are safe to drink from (the ones where the Mat's surface is slightly raised around the rim, a natural lip that filters the condensation through an extra layer of tissue), which insects sting (the red ones, the metallic ones, the ones that move in pairs), which way to walk when the ground vibrates with the movement of the larger things (toward the nearest Column, press yourself against the bark, go still, go silent, wait).

She has a name. You know this because the old woman at the third Column said it once, or something like it, a sound that might have been a name or might have been a term of relation or might have been the word for *the one who follows the stranger*, if there is such a word. You cannot pronounce it. You think of her as the child, and this is inadequate, and you know it, and you do not know what else to call her.

She carries a blade like the old woman's blade—stone, chipped, sharp enough to cut the Mat, sharp enough to cut herself, which she has done, you see the scars on her fingers, thin white lines against the weathered brown. She carries a pouch like the old woman's pouch. She walks like the old woman walks—flat-footed, gripping, a motion that is not walking so much as *climbing a horizontal wall*, and you realize that this is what it is, this is what the Mat demands, you do not walk on it, you climb it, you hold on to it, because it is alive and it moves and if you do not pay attention it will shift beneath you and you will fall in a way that has nothing to do with height.

She is not afraid. This is the thing that strikes you most. The roar of the ecosystem surrounds her—the hum, the buzz, the crash of the fliers, the subsonic moan of the large things moving between the Columns—and she moves through it the way you move through air, the way a fish moves through water, without gratitude, without complaint, without any awareness that it could be otherwise. This is her world. This has always been her world. The Mat breathes beneath her and she breathes with it, her chest rising and falling in a rhythm that matches the slow swell of the surface, and you think: *she does not know she is separate from it.* Or: *she knows she is not.*

---

The man at the sixth Column is dying.

You understand this not from his body—he is thin, yes, and his skin has the gray undertone of someone running on reserves—but from his shelter. It is overgrown. The Mat has begun to reclaim it, the edges softening, the cordage rooting where it touches the surface, the hearth-ring narrowing as the surrounding tissue creeps inward. He has stopped maintaining it. He has stopped fighting the growth. He is letting the Mat take it back.

He sits outside, cross-legged, his hands flat on the surface, and he is feeling something. You see this in his face—the closed eyes, the stillness, the concentration that goes beyond concentration into something like communion or conversation. He is listening to the Mat. Not to the sound of it, the roar, the noise, but to something beneath the noise, something in the tissue itself, a signal or a pulse that you cannot feel because you have not spent a lifetime with your palms pressed to this living floor.

He does not open his eyes when you approach. He says something—a word, a phrase, a sentence—and the child responds, and there is an exchange that goes on for several minutes, voices low, the rhythm of it almost musical, and you realize that this is not conversation but *transmission*. He is telling her something. He is passing something to her. Knowledge, maybe. Or a history. Or the location of a pool you haven't found, a Column that produces a particular growth, a place where the metal comes from, the worked metal, the rare thing that passes from hand to hand across the continental reach of the Mat.

When he opens his eyes, they are the same yellowed color as the old woman's, but more so, the whites almost tan, and they do not focus on you or on the child but on something intermediate, something in the middle distance, the way a person looks when they are attending to an inner sense. He presses his palms harder against the Mat. He nods, once, as if in response to a question no one asked aloud.

The child touches his shoulder. A brief contact—a gesture you have not seen among these people, who seem to communicate through proximity and shared attention rather than touch. Then she turns and walks away, and you follow, and you do not look back, and by the time you reach the next Column you understand that when you return this way—if you return this way—the shelter will be gone, and the man will be gone, and the Mat will have closed over the place where he sat as if he had never been.

---

There are things you will never see.

The things that move beneath the Mat, for instance—she shows you the traces, the raised lines where something large has passed through the tissue, tunneling, and the surface has swelled and healed behind it but not quite flattened, leaving a scar that takes weeks to subside. The things that make these tunnels are not the same as the things that walk on the surface. They are deeper. They live in the body of the Mat itself, the kilometers of dense tissue, and they are to the surface ecology what the deep ocean is to the tide pools—a connected system, but so remote, so vast, so *other* in its pressures and its darkness and its timescales that the relationship is more theoretical than observed.

The things that come at night and take the careless—she does not speak of these directly, but she shows you a place where a shelter was, or should have been, and the Mat's surface is disturbed in a way that is not natural growth, not shedding, not the movement of rhizomes beneath. A violence happened here. A brief and decisive violence. And then the Mat grew over it, because the Mat grows over everything, because the Mat is the medium in which all things happen and the solvent in which all things dissolve.

The place the Columns reach toward—she looks up, sometimes, at the crowns, the branching tops sixty or a hundred meters above, and her face does something that might be longing or might be calculation or might be the simple recognition of distance. The Columns are continuous with the Mat. What happens in the canopy, in the high structure where the fronds and the filament-trails and the glowing moss-analogs cluster, is a different world. Different fauna. Different moisture. Different rules. She has never been there. No one she knows has ever been there. But she looks up, and you think: *she wants to go.* Or: *someone she loved went.* Or: *there is a story about what lives at the top, and the story is not a happy one, and she is remembering it.*

You will never know.

---

The ratio of their lives:

So much silence. So much waiting. The roar of the ecosystem goes on around them, ten thousand sounds collapsed into a single sustained shout, and within it they move like particles in a solution, small, quiet, paying attention. They do not waste motion. They do not waste sound. They harvest and they eat and they maintain their shelters and they watch the sky for the storms the Mat calls into being, and in between they sit with their hands on the surface and they listen, and you cannot tell if what they hear is information or prayer or simply the pleasure of contact, of touch, of the warmth that rises from below and says: *this thing you live on is alive, and it does not know your name, and it holds you anyway.*

They do not live long. You piece this together from the scarcity of the old, the prevalence of the young, the speed at which the Mat reclaims the shelters of the dead. Fifty years, perhaps. Sixty, for the lucky, for the careful, for the ones who learn every insect and every chemical trail and every vibration of the larger things approaching through the tissue. The roar takes them if they are inattentive. The damp takes them if they are weak. The Mat itself takes them, sometimes, you think—the old man pressing his palms to the surface, listening, and something in the listening that was also a leaving, a release, a decision to stop maintaining the shelter and let the growth close over.

But in the time they have, they know this place with a thoroughness that makes your understanding look like a sketch, a crude cartoon, a child's drawing of a cathedral. They know the Mat the way you know your own body—not through study but through inhabitation, through the accumulation of small sensations that add up to something that cannot be spoken, cannot be written, cannot be transmitted to anyone who has not stood barefoot on the warm living floor and felt it breathe.

The child knows which way the next Column lies without looking. The old woman knows the taste of rain before it falls. The dying man knows something he is trying to say, pressing his palms to the surface, and the words will not come, or the language for them does not exist, or the only language for them is the sensation itself, the heat and the vibration and the long slow pulse of the continental organism beneath, and he is saying: *this. this. do you feel this? this is what it is. this is all it ever was.*

---

And the others.

Not the people. The others. The fauna, the text called them, the inadequate word. The ten thousand sounds.

You see them now, or you begin to. Not as individuals—there are too many, the insects alone would take lifetimes to catalog—but as categories, as roles, as positions in the great circulating machinery of consumption and decay that the Mat sustains.

The grazers: they are the closest thing to companionship the people have, and even this is a stretch. The insects that coat the surface, that feed on the Mat's outer layers, that are kept in check by the Mat's periodic shedding—they are background, they are ambient, they are the white noise of the ecosystem. The people ignore them the way you ignore dust. They are part of the texture of living here. You sweep them from your shelter in the morning. You brush them from your food. You do not think about them. They are the Mat's problem, not yours, and the Mat has been managing them for eons without your input.

The fliers: you learn to read them, eventually, the way sailors read birds. When they cluster high above a Column, the larger things are not near. When they drop to the surface in feeding formations, the insect bloom is heavy, and the larger things may be coming, drawn by the concentration of prey. When they wheel and scatter without apparent cause, something has startled them from below, and you look to the Mat for the vibration, and you move toward the nearest Column, and you go still.

The crawlers: you avoid them. Everyone avoids them. The armored ones are harmless if you do not step on them, and the soft ones are fast, and the ones in between—the ones that move in lines, following chemical trails—you simply walk around, giving them a meter of clearance, because their trails sometimes lead to places you do not want to go, pools where the water is wrong, basins where the Mat's surface has thinned and the heat below is too intense, places where the larger things come to drink and to rest and to wait.

The larger things.

You see one, finally, on the ninth day or the tenth, and the seeing changes you.

It moves between two Columns a kilometer away, or more—distance is difficult here, the air shimmers, the heat distorts—and it is large, larger than you imagined, larger than the word *large* can hold. A shape. A mass. Something that walks on legs you cannot count because they are hidden by the body they support, and the body is covered in growths that might be symbionts or parasites or simply the Mat colonizing the surface of another organism the way it colonizes everything, and the head—if it has a head—is lifted above the crown-line of the Columns, and it moans, and the moan travels through the ground, and you feel it in your feet, in your shins, in your sternum, a vibration that is not sound so much as displacement, the Mat itself flexing around the weight of this thing passing through.

The child does not look up. She has pressed herself against the Column, her cheek flat against the bark, her eyes closed, her body absolutely still, and she is not afraid—you know this because you have seen her afraid, once, when a crawler got too close, and her fear was quick and electric and active, a reaching for the blade, a readiness—but this is something else. This is *protocol.* This is what you do when the large things pass. You become part of the Column. You become small and silent and inert, and you wait, and the large thing moves on, and it does not see you because you are not worth seeing, you are a nutrient source too small to register, a mote on the skin of a continent, and the large thing has larger concerns.

It passes. The moan fades. The ground stills. The child opens her eyes and steps away from the Column and resumes walking, and she does not speak of what just happened, and you do not ask, because this is not an event, this is not remarkable, this is simply the Mat being what it is, and what it is includes things that are too big to fight and too big to flee and the only option is to be invisible, to be beneath notice, to be the thing that the predator does not see because the predator is looking for something larger, something worth the effort, something that will sustain the enormous metabolism of a body that walks above the Columns and moans like the earth itself is grieving.

---

There are more. There are always more.

The pale swimmers in the deeper pools, eyeless and sensitive, that the people harvest with their quick hands. The things that live in the Columns themselves, in the fissures and clefts, the moss-analogs and the fern-fronds and the trailing filaments, a vertical ecology that the ground-dwellers never see. The things that come in the night, silent, purposeful, that take what they take and leave no trace. The things that tunnel beneath the surface, leaving raised scars that heal slowly. The things that bloom with the Mat in the afternoon, erupting from the tissue in a riot of color and scent, living and dying in the space of hours, so brief and so beautiful that the people have a word for them—you hear the child say it once, pointing, her face soft for the first time, and you think the word means *the ones who are only here for a moment, the ones who burn out, the ones who are so bright and so brief that you cannot hold them but you can remember them, and that is enough.*

And the Mat. Always the Mat. The living floor that is not a floor, the organism that is not an organism, the thing that breathes and shifts and sheds and blooms and holds the heat of the sun through the night and calls the rain from the sky and swallows the shelters of the dead and grows over everything, everything, everything, because growing is what it does, because growing is what it *is*, and the people and the insects and the fliers and the crawlers and the larger things and the pale swimmers and the brief bright bloomers are all just aspects of its growing, all just currents in the circulation of energy and matter that it has been running since before anyone was here to see it.

---

You leave eventually, or you try to. The child watches you go. She does not wave—waving is not something they do, you realize, gesture is minimal here, the body is for stillness, for pressing against surfaces, for listening, not for display. She watches. She stands on the Mat with her bare feet gripping the warm tissue and she watches you walk toward the edge of the plateau, toward wherever you came from, toward wherever the Mat is not, and she does not follow.

But something of the place follows you. The hum in your sternum, the vibration that you felt for days and thought was the roar of the ecosystem but now understand was something else, something deeper, the pulse of the Mat itself, the long slow heartbeat of the continental organism, and it is still there, in your chest, in your bones, a frequency you cannot unhear once you have heard it, a warmth you cannot unfeel once you have stood barefoot on the living floor and known—known in the way the child knows, in the way the old woman knows, in the way the dying man knew before he pressed his palms to the surface and let the growth close over—known that you are small, and the world is large, and it does not love you, and it does not hate you, and it holds you anyway, and this is what it is to live on the Mat, and this is what it has always been.
