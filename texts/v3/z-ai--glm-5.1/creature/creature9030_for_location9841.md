# The Inhabitants

They do not have a word for progress.

This is not because the concept is foreign to them—foreign implies it exists elsewhere and has simply not arrived here—but because the conceptual space for it never opened. The language has words for maintenance, for continuation, for the specific satisfaction of a task completed in the same manner it was completed before. It has words for the quality of a well-fitted stone, for the sound water makes at different volumes against different surfaces, for the precise texture of the channel-floor biofilm in its seasonal—no, not seasonal; its perpetual—state. The language is dense with distinction where distinction matters and absent where it doesn't. The word for *tomorrow* is the same as the word for *today*. This is not poverty of vocabulary. It is precision.

---

The bodies tell the story that the language doesn't need to.

They are small. Not stunted—stunting implies deprivation, and nothing here is deprived—but compressed, the way a hand compresses to fit a tight passage. Generations of adequate nutrition in adequate but unvarying quantities, generations of movement through shoulder-width corridors, generations of warmth that never breaks and never requires the body to burn for its own heat, have produced a people whose frames are dense and narrow. Their shoulders are slight. Their hands are small and endlessly dexterous, the fingers articulate, the nails kept short by the mineral work or by the simple habit of biting that no one taught them and no one needs to teach. Their skin is pale in the way of things that have never seen direct light—not the pallor of illness but the pallor of adaptation, a dermis that has never been asked to protect against radiation and has accordingly invested elsewhere. It is thin skin, almost translucent at the temples and the inner wrists, and it is always damp. Not sweating—the heat doesn't demand it—but kissed by the condensation that kisses every surface, so that they are as wet as the walls they live beside, as glistening as the stone.

Their eyes are the most remarked feature, if remarking occurred, which it largely doesn't. They are large—not in a way that seems disproportionate but in a way that suggests the face organized itself around them early and never found reason to reorganize. The irises are pale, washed-out, as if color were a luxury the environment didn't require and the body couldn't be bothered to sustain. The pupils are neither dilated nor contracted; the light has been the same amber-gray dimness for so many generations that the iris muscles have essentially stopped working, or have settled into a permanent middle state that serves the permanent middle light. They do not squint. They do not widen. The eyes receive what there is to receive and ask nothing more.

They do not see well. Not because the eyes are defective but because seeing well was never necessary. The passages are narrow enough that touch suffices. The work is repetitive enough that the hands know the shape of each task before the eyes could confirm it. The creatures they harvest do not flee in ways that require visual tracking. And so the visual cortex is present, functional, but unemphasized—a capacity maintained because maintaining is what the body does, not because it is used.

What they have instead—what has expanded to fill the space that vision might have occupied in a different environment—is a sense that does not have a clean counterpart in the vocabulary of the outer world. They feel the city's moisture. Not as a conscious calculation, not as a mapping of humidity gradients, but as a continuous proprioceptive extension: they know where the water is the way they know where their own limbs are. A change in the condensation rate on a distant wall registers somewhere below language, a shift in the background hum of their perception, and they turn toward it or away from it without deciding to turn. They know when a channel is running high before they reach it. They know when a settling-basin is silting—can feel the slight change in the air's water-content as the surface area decreases—without anyone reporting it. The city's water-architecture is not something they maintain from the outside. They are part of it. They are the component that walks.

This is not mystical. It is no more mystical than a body regulating its own temperature, than a hand pulling back from a flame before the conscious mind has registered the heat. The water has been so constant, so present, so much the medium of every experience, that the boundary between body and environment has softened at the edges. They do not think of themselves as separate from the wet. They are wet. The city is wet. The distinction is grammatical, not practical.

---

They do not count their dead.

This sounds like an affectation, or a grief too profound to enumerate, but it is neither. They do not count their dead for the same reason they do not count the water-drops falling from the ceiling: the quantity is stable, and counting stable quantities yields no useful information. The death rate is what it has always been. The birth rate matches it. The population holds.

When someone dies—and they do die, of the small failures that accumulate in any biological system, of the infections that find purchase in the always-damp skin, of the channel-accidents that occur when the footing is lost and the slow current proves stronger than the exhausted body—the body is brought to the lower pools. This is not ritual. Ritual implies that something symbolic is being enacted, that the action refers to something beyond itself. The body is brought to the lower pools because the lower pools are where organic matter is processed. The pale swimmers and the sediment-bacteria and the slow chemistry of decomposition will do what they do, and the minerals will return to the water, and the water will circulate, and the city will continue.

There is no mourning in the way the word is understood elsewhere. There is a gap—a specific person's habits no longer enacted, a particular pair of hands no longer working the channel-repair, a voice's particular timbre no longer joining the work-sounds—but the gap is not experienced as loss. It is experienced as a shift in the pattern, the way a channel's flow shifts when a stone is dislodged. The water finds a new path. The work is taken up by other hands. The sound of the city adjusts, as it always adjusts, by increments too small to hear.

This is not callousness. It is not emotional poverty. It is the natural psychology of a people for whom the individual has never been the unit of survival. The unit is the process. The unit is the circuit—the water's circuit, the work's circuit, the life-and-death circuit that flows through the city like blood through a body, each cell replaced over time while the body persists. They do not mourn their dead for the same reason a body does not mourn its shed skin: the skin was never the point. The continuing was the point. The continuing is still the point.

---

Children are born. They are born at the rate that maintains the population, which is the rate at which the old and the unfortunate die, which is a rate that has not changed in living memory or in the memory that precedes living memory, which is passed down not as history—the language has no word for history either—but as the simple fact of how things are.

The children are not taught, exactly. Teaching implies a transfer of information from a knowing mind to an unknowing mind, a directed process with a before and an after. The children learn the way the wall-clingers learn the wall: by being on it. They are present in the narrow passages from the moment they can move. They hear the water. They feel the moisture. They watch—no, they absorb—the work of maintenance, the rhythm of it, the way the hands find the stone and test it and replace it or repoint it or leave it be. No one sits a child down and explains the channel system. The child grows inside the channel system. The knowledge enters through the skin, through the ears, through the endless repetition that makes learning indistinguishable from being.

They do not play. This is the detail that would disturb a visitor most, if visitors came, which they don't. The children work alongside the adults as soon as they are physically able—sorting the harvested swimmers by size, carrying clay to the repair-sites, running small loads along the narrow paths. They are not forced to this. There is simply no alternative activity, no separate category of action called *play* that exists as an option. The work is what there is. The work is what everyone does. The children do it smaller, slower, with less skill, and then they do it larger, faster, with more skill, and the transition is as unremarkable as the gradual deepening of a channel.

They do not ask questions. Not because curiosity has been beaten out of them—there is no beating, no punishment, no discipline in any formal sense; there is only the work and the not-work and the quiet exhaustion that follows the work—but because the environment supplies no prompts for questions. Everything is as it was. Everything is as it will be. The child looks around and sees: the water, the walls, the creatures, the hands, the stone. This is what the adult sees. This is what the elder sees. There is nothing to ask.

They do not have elders. They have older people—bodies that have been in the circuit longer, hands that have repaired more stone, skins that have absorbed more moisture—but the concept of elder-as-authority, elder-as-wisdom-keeper, elder-as-guide, does not obtain. There is no wisdom to keep that is not already everywhere present in the work itself. There are no stories of how things used to be, because things used to be exactly as they are. There are no prophecies of how things will become, because things will become exactly as they are. The older people are not repositories of knowledge; they are simply repositories of the same knowledge, held longer, like a stone that has been in the channel for more seasons than a newer stone.

---

They are not unhappy.

This needs to be stated plainly, because the outer mind—the mind shaped by change, by trajectory, by the ache of becoming—will instinctively read this equilibrium as stagnation, this persistence as imprisonment, this sameness as a kind of death-in-life. But they do not experience it so. They do not experience imprisonment because there is nowhere they wish to go. They do not experience stagnation because the concept of flow-as-progress is not available to them; flow is simply flow, water is simply water, it moves and moves and arrives where it has always arrived, and this is not futility but the nature of water, the nature of the city, the nature of their lives.

They experience something that has no easy equivalent in the vocabulary of striving. A fullness, perhaps. A rightness. The sensation of being exactly fitted to exactly the space that awaits them. The work does not exhaust them; it occupies them. The work is the shape their action takes in the world, the way the water's action takes the shape of the channel. They do not resent the channel. They do not wish for a different channel. The channel is what carries them, and they are what maintains it, and the reciprocity is so complete, so long-established, so deeply inscribed in every moment of every day, that it has become invisible.

They do not wonder what else there is. The question does not arise. The walls are the walls. The water is the water. The creatures move around them and they move around the creatures and the sound continues and the warmth continues and the condensation falls and they reach up and touch the wet stone and find it sound, find it adequate, find it as it was and as it will be, and they turn back to the work.

They are not unhappy. They are not happy. They are something for which neither word works—a state of continuity so total that the distinction between feeling-good and feeling-bad has lost its edge, its reason, its reference points. There is the work and there is the not-work and there is the sleep that comes easily in the warm damp chambers, and in the sleep there are no dreams, or there are dreams of the same passages and the same water and the same hands on the same stone, and this is not nightmare, this is not bliss, this is simply the consciousness continuing along its channel, moving as it moves, arriving where it arrives, holding its shape the way the city holds its shape: not by will, not by force, but by the deep inertia of a system so perfectly balanced it has forgotten there was ever any other way to be.

---

And the creatures. The creatures that are not them but are also not not-them, in the way that everything in the basin is not not-everything-else.

The pale swimmers in the channels—larval, eternal, multiplying at exactly the rate of their consumption—they do not think of themselves. This is not a limitation. This is an optimization. The self is a concept that arises in environments of change, where an organism must track its own continuity against a shifting background. The background here does not shift. The swimmer does not need to know it is a swimmer. It swims. It feeds. It is fed upon. The channel carries it and then carries what remains of it and the cycle is so tight, so closed, so perfectly circular, that consciousness would be an extravagance, a cost with no benefit, a luxury the equilibrium cannot afford and does not require.

But there is something in them—or in the way they move, the way they brush against each other in the slow current, the way their soft bodies navigate the channel-stones without error or hesitation—that resembles contentment. Not contentment as a feeling. Contentment as a physical state. A body in its element. A process in its groove. They are so perfectly what they are that the distinction between being and doing dissolves, and what remains is simply the swimming, the feeding, the being-swum-and-fed, a living current within the larger current, a pulse in the water's endless circuit.

The wall-clingers—the thumbnail-shelled multitudes that cover every vertical surface in their hundreds per handspan, their legs working, working, working—they are the sound of the city made flesh. Or made chitin. Their mandibles grind and scrape and process and process, and the sound of their processing rises into the roar and becomes indistinguishable from the roar, and they are not disturbed by this because they have no concept of disturbance, no category for silence against which the sound could be measured and found excessive. The sound is. They are. The wall is. The moisture comes and they work it and the moisture comes again and they work it again, and the work is not labor, is not toil, is not effort—though it is all of those things in mechanical terms—but is simply their mode of existing, their way of being in the world, which is the same way as the water's way and the stone's way and the people's way: by continuing, by persisting, by holding the shape that the environment has given them and that they, in turn, give to the environment.

The larger creatures—the low, smooth passagers that move through the narrow corridors without eyes, without urgency, without any apparent purpose beyond the moving—they are the closest thing the city has to philosophers. Not because they think—they do not think, as thinking is understood elsewhere—but because they embody a mode of being that, in another context, might require philosophy to articulate. They navigate by touch and chemical sense and vibration, and their knowledge of the city is therefore not representational but practical, not a map but a movement, not a picture of the territory but the territory itself experienced through the body. They know the city the way the blood knows the veins: not by having learned it but by being it, by flowing through it, by having no option and no desire to flow otherwise.

They move around obstacles—you, if you stand in the passage—without acknowledgment, without irritation, without any change in their steady, pressure-driven progress. They are not ignoring you. They are not accommodating you. They are simply moving as the space allows, the way water moves as the channel allows, and if the channel narrows they flow narrower and if it widens they flow wider, and this is not flexibility in the adaptive sense—flexibility implies a preference, a shape the body would take if it could—but is simply the absence of rigidity, the absence of any shape that is not the shape the environment imposes.

They are content. If contentment can exist without consciousness—and it can, it does, it is perhaps the only form contentment can honestly take—then they are content. They are so perfectly fitted to their niche that the niche and the creature have become difficult to distinguish, and this—this dissolution of the boundary between self and environment, this loss of the edge that separates the swimmer from the stream—is the deepest truth of the Weeping City, the truth that underlies all the others.

---

There is no one here who wishes to leave.

This is not because leaving is forbidden—there is no authority to forbid it, no guard at the basin's rim, no punishment for departure—or because leaving is impossible—the rim can be climbed, the outer world exists, the air beyond the basin is drier and cooler and sharper. It is because the wish does not form. The circuit is closed. The water flows through the channels and the people flow through the work and the creatures flow through their niches and the sound flows through the air and the warmth flows through the stone, and at no point in any of these flows does an eddy appear, a vortex, a counter-current that might carry a mind to the rim and point it outward and suggest: *what else?*

There is no *what else*. The question requires a crack in the equilibrium, a deviation, a difference between now and then that might widen into a difference between here and elsewhere. But there is no difference. There is no then. There is no elsewhere. There is only the city, doing what it does, being what it is, holding its shape in the infant stone, weeping its perpetual tears, roaring its perpetual roar, and in the narrow passages, the small damp hands reaching up to test the stone and finding it sound, finding it adequate, finding it exactly as it was the last time they reached and exactly as it will be the next time, and the hands come down and the work continues and the sound continues and the water continues and nothing changes, and nothing changes, and nothing changes, and this is not despair—this is not even resignation—this is simply the way the world is when the world has finished becoming and has settled, at last, into the shape it was always going to hold.
