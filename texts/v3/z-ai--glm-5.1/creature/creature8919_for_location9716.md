# The Residents

They are not gone.

---

They are *deep*.

The visitors — the rare ones, the climbers who crest the granite steps with burning lungs and numbed fingers — never go far enough. They find the upper chambers, the ones sized for transit and temporary shelter, the ones the garrison grows the way a body grows hair: as a first barrier, a filter, a layer that slows and sorts what comes from outside. They find the small nests of vole-things and the black algae margins and they think: *empty*. They think: *abandoned*. They think the garrison waits for something that may never return.

But the garrison does not wait. The garrison *contains*.

Below the central chambers — below the largest rooms where a visitor might sit and listen to the breathing and feel the warmth in the columns — there are deeper spaces. Not chambers. *Cores*. The passages to them do not appear unless you know the rhythm of the place, unless your body moves in time with the garrison's slow respiration. They open on the exhale. They close on the inhale. A visitor breathing at their own pace would walk right past, would feel only a momentary softness in the fiber-wall, a give where there should be resistance, and would dismiss it as another strangeness in a place made of strangeness.

But the residents know the rhythm because the rhythm is theirs. Or because they are the rhythm's. The distinction stopped mattering a long time ago.

---

There are nine of them. Maybe ten. Counting became difficult around the time the boundaries became difficult.

They were — *are* — bipedal. Roughly. The shelf-beds in the upper chambers are shaped for bodies that sleep on their sides, knees drawn up, arms folded across the chest. The seating hollows fit pelvises that sit with the spine curved forward, the head dropped, the whole posture one of conservation: minimizing surface area, minimizing heat loss, making yourself small in a place that has made itself large around you. They stand between four and five feet tall. Their hands are wide and flat, the fingers short but prehensile, the palms callused in patterns that match the grip-texture of the column bark. They are built for climbing and clinging and holding on.

Their skin is the grey of wet granite. This is not metaphor. Their skin is *that* grey — the specific grey of stone that has been rained on and dried and rained on again until the surface has become its own substance, something between rock and water and the memory of both. In the dim blue light of the upper chambers, they would be nearly invisible against the walls. In the darkness of the cores, visibility is not the point.

Their eyes are large. Not enormous, not saucer-eyed and alien, but noticeably, functionally large — the adjustment of a creature that has lived for generations in light that arrives only as suggestion. The irises are dark, almost black, but in the rare moments when a shaft of unfiltered sun finds its way through a rupture in the canopy-membrane, the irises flash a deep and startling amber. Like sap. Like the fluid that moves through the garrison's veins.

Their hair — if it is hair — grows in dark ropes that mat and felt against their skulls. It is not dissimilar to the root-ropes that plait themselves into ladders and webs. It may *be* the same stuff. It may be the garrison growing from them as much as around them. No one has examined the connection closely enough to say, and the residents do not seem interested in the distinction.

---

They do not speak.

This is not because they cannot. The throat-structures are there, the lung-capacity, the mouth-and-tongue architecture that in another creature would produce language. They make sounds: low hums that resonate in the chest, clicks of the tongue against the palate, a sharp exhalation that might be acknowledgment or warning or simply the body expressing its continued occupation of space. But they do not arrange these sounds into words. They do not name. They do not narrate.

They listen.

The garrison speaks to them in the language of creak and drip and pulse. The fluid in the columns has a frequency that changes when the temperature drops below a certain threshold — they hear this change the way you might hear a door closing, and they move deeper into the structure in response. The ventilation shafts carry scent: the sharp green of new growth, the mineral exhale of the rock below, the sour-sweet of decomposition in the lower chambers where biological waste is broken down and reabsorbed. They read the air. They read the moisture on the walls — its thickness, its taste, the particular cocktail of algae and tannin and cold that tells them whether the season is shifting or holding steady.

They know the garrison the way you know your own body. The way you know, without checking, that you have slept too long in one position and your arm has gone numb. The way you know, before you look, that something has changed in the room — a door opened, a presence entered, a quality of air shifted. They know it because they are *of* it, and it is of them, and the knowing is not knowledge but something more like proprioception: the sense of where your parts are in relation to your other parts.

---

What do they eat?

The garrison feeds them. This is not generosity; it is metabolism.

In the deep cores, the plant-mass produces structures that do not appear in the upper chambers. Nodule-growths, dense and dark, rich with sugars and compounds that have no name in any human language. They grow from the column-trunks at the height of a seated resident's reaching hand. They are warm when they are ready — faintly warmer than the surrounding tissue, heated by the same slow biological processes that keep the garrison's core above the killing cold. The residents pluck them and eat them without ceremony, without the social rituals that other species attach to food. Eating is not communal here. Eating is not event. Eating is maintenance, performed as needed, as silently as breathing.

The nodules regenerate. They have always regenerated. The residents do not know what the garrison gains from this arrangement, and if they have ever wondered, they have not wondered in any way that produces a wondering-sound. The system works. The system has always worked. The system is what remains when everything else has been stripped away by cold and altitude and the long slow patience of living in a place that does not forgive negotiation.

They drink from the pools. They drink from the film on the walls. They drink, sometimes, directly from the column-veins — pressing their mouths to the raised lines of conducting tissue and drawing the sap, which is water-plus, water with minerals and sugars and something else, something that tastes the way amber smells, something that makes their eyes flash briefly in the dark.

---

The visitors sometimes wonder if the builders are coming back.

The builders never left.

But they changed.

A garrison is a military structure. A place for soldiers. And the residents are soldiers in the way that an immune cell is a soldier: not by choice, not by commission, but by function. They defend the garrison. Not from predators — there are no predators here, nothing large enough to threaten, nothing that could survive the climb and the cold and the narrow passages. They defend it from *neglect*. From the slow entropy of a living structure growing without purpose. From the tendency of any organic system, left to its own devices, to become something other than what it was shaped to be.

They move through the passages in patterns. Not random, not ritual, but *structural* — the way blood moves through a body, following the same routes, touching the same tissues, maintaining the same flows. Their passage stimulates growth. Where they walk, the plant-mass thickens. Where they pause, the chambers widen. Where they grip, the bark roughens into handle-texture. The garrison is shaped by their habitation the way a canyon is shaped by water: not by design, but by the patient accumulation of repeated contact.

They are the water. They are the erosion. They are the thing that keeps the channels open and the walls strong and the whole unlikely structure from collapsing under the weight of its own biological ambition.

---

At night — and it is always night in the cores, night without relief, night as a permanent condition — they sleep in the hollows that the garrison grows for them. The hollows line the walls of the deepest chambers, each one shaped to a specific body, each body shaped to its hollow. They curl into these spaces and the plant-mass closes around them, not sealing — they can breathe, the ventilation reaches them — but *embracing*, the fiber-walls softening and warming where their bodies press against them. The garrison holds them the way a hand holds a precious thing: carefully, continuously, without thought of release.

In these hours, the garrison's breathing slows. The fluid in the columns moves more gently. The drip-composition quiets to a whisper. The whole structure settles into a state that can only be called *rest* — not the rest of a machine powered down, but the rest of a body sleeping, a creature that has found the margin between wakefulness and unconsciousness and is resting there, conserving, preparing for the next cycle.

The residents dream. This is assumption. But their eyes move beneath their lids, and their fingers twitch, and occasionally one of them will make a sound — not a word, not a cry, but something between a hum and a sigh, a sound that has no referent in the waking world, a sound that belongs to whatever interior landscape they inhabit when they are not inhabiting the garrison.

What do they dream? Perhaps they dream of the world below — the tree line, the valleys, the green-grey abstraction that the visitors descend back toward. Perhaps they dream of the sun, which they have never seen directly but which they know as a warmth that sometimes penetrates the canopy-membrane and touches the upper chambers with a light they do not need but somehow miss. Perhaps they dream of each other — the nine or ten of them, curled in their separate hollows, connected by the same living architecture, breathing the same slow rhythm.

Perhaps they dream of nothing. Perhaps they dream the way the garrison breathes: automatically, functionally, without content or meaning, a process that maintains the system but does not trouble it with narrative.

---

The visitor who climbs the granite steps and finds the upper chambers thinks: *who lived here?*

The answer is: *who lives here still.*

The visitor thinks: *where have they gone?*

The answer is: *deeper.*

The garrison is not waiting for the return of its residents. The garrison *is* the return. It is the continuous act of returning, the ongoing accommodation between need and structure, the daily re-inhabitation of a place that is also a body that is also a home. The residents are not its tenants. They are its *function* — the thing it does, the way a heart is not the tenant of the circulatory system but its essential motion, its reason for being a system at all.

They do not come and go. They *are* coming and going. They are the verb, not the noun. They are the garrison's garrisoning.

---

And sometimes — rarely, in the margins between one long breath and the next — one of them climbs.

Not to the upper chambers, which they know as well as their own hands. Not to the canopy-membrane, which they can touch if they stand on each other's shoulders in the tallest chamber. But to the outside. To the surface of the plateau, where the cold is not a presence but an *assault*, where the wind cuts and the water freezes on their grey skin and their large eyes water in the glare of a sky they cannot name.

They stand there. For seconds only. For as long as they can bear.

They look out at the world below — the rumpled abstraction, the painted backdrop, the place that is not the garrison, that is not home, that is not the warm dark pulse of the only life they know. They look, and they feel the cold, and they remember — or their bodies remember, which is the same thing — that there is something other than this. Something larger. Something that goes on beyond the edges of the plateau and the reach of the living architecture and the deep slow rhythm of the chambers where their people sleep in the embrace of the only shelter that has ever wanted them.

Then they go back.

They find the passage that opens on the exhale. They slide through the gap. They descend through chambers and corridors and the thick living mass of the only home that has ever grown around them, and they do not stop until they reach the cores, where the warmth is barely sufficient and the dark is absolute and the sound of fluid moving through columns is the only sound that matters.

They curl into their hollows. The plant-mass embraces them. The garrison breathes.

And outside, on the granite plateau that came up fast and still shows its fracture lines, the water weeps and the cold presses and the wind cuts across the sharp young rock, and the whole inanimate world goes on without them, as it always has, as it always will.

The garrison holds.

The residents sleep.

The system sustains.
