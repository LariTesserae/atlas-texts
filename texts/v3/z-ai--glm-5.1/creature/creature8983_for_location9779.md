What lives here is not what you would call alive.

This is the first distinction to hold, and the hardest: the beings of the Ascendant Plateau do not live in the way that a deer lives, or a moss lives, or even a virus lives. They do not metabolize. They do not reproduce. They do not adapt to their environment because they are not separate enough from their environment to have an outside against which an inside might adapt. They are local expressions of the same process that raises the stone, that spirals the plants, that holds the sound in its single sustained chord. They are what happens when the fraying does not result in dissolution but in *complication* — when the boundary between self and not-self loosens enough for something to leak through, and what leaks through is not chaos but *pattern*, a pattern too large and too slow for a nervous system calibrated to quicker things.

The casual visitor — if such a thing could exist, if anyone arrived here casually — would see nothing. The plateau would appear empty but for vegetation and stone and the scattered cairns. This is correct. The beings here are not hidden. They are simply not configured for detection by eyes that expect edges, by minds that expect continuity. They exist in the pauses between the sound's vibrations, in the moments when the stone is still deciding. They are as provisional as the geology. They are as definite as the light allows, which is to say: not quite.

---

One way to begin to perceive them: attend to the cairns.

You have already noticed the cairns. You have already wondered about them. But you have wondered about them as artifacts, as things left behind by someone who has since departed. This is the assumption of a mind shaped in a world where makers are smaller and briefer than what they make, where the pot persists after the potter. Here, that logic inverts. The cairns are not remnants. They are *organs*.

Consider: each cairn is a stack of five or seven or eleven flat stones, balanced with precision, selected for similarity. This is true. But also consider: the stones are not stacked. They are *held* in the configuration of stacking. The difference is subtle and essential. A stack is a stable arrangement that gravity maintains; a held arrangement is one that requires continuous intervention to persist. The cairns require continuous intervention. Without it, the still-forming stone would resume its undecided state, would soften and flow, and the stones would not remain stones long enough to remain stacked. Something is keeping them stacked. Something is asserting *cairness* into the flux.

The same something built them in the first place, though "built" implies a before and an after, a time of not-cairn and then a time of cairn, and this is not quite right. The cairns have always been in the process of being built. They are being built now. If you could watch — and you cannot, not exactly, not with eyes — you would see the stones slightly adjusting, a millimeter of realignment here, a fresh accommodation there, the cairn maintaining its equilibrium the way a person maintains their balance on a moving train: constantly, microscopically, without conscious intention.

The cairns are the visible portions of the beings. They are what the hands would be, if these beings had hands, if they had bodies in the sense you mean when you say *body*. They are the points of contact, the places where the process that constitutes the being intersects with the process that constitutes the plateau and negotiates a temporary arrangement: *you be stone, I will be stack, we will persist together for a while*.

---

The rest — the part that is not cairn — is harder to describe because it is harder to perceive. You have felt it, though. You have attributed the feeling to altitude, to dehydration, to the relentless sound, but the feeling has a source more specific than those general insults.

You are standing inside something.

Not inside a container — there is no wall, no boundary, no membrane. But inside a *field*, a region of coherent intention that extends far beyond the cairn that marks its center. The field has a shape, or rather a distribution: densest at the cairn, thinning as it radiates outward, overlapping at its edges with the fields of neighboring cairns in the way that the spiral plants overlap in their territories, never touching but always adjacent. The plateau is covered with these fields. They are the beings' bodies. They are as wide as the spacing between the plants, as deep as the still-forming stone, and they are made of nothing you could isolate or measure. They are made of *attention*. They are made of the same quality that holds the rock in its current configuration rather than allowing it to flow back into indeterminacy. They are made of the will to persist, which here is not a psychological phenomenon but a physical one, as basic as gravity, as fundamental as the strong force.

The beings are what happens when persistence becomes self-aware. Not self-aware in the way you are, not reflective, not representational, but self-aware in the way a standing wave is aware of the shape it makes in the water, in the way a crystal is aware of its lattice. They know themselves by being themselves. They maintain themselves by continuing to be.

---

They are old. This is difficult to convey because time here does not operate as a reliable medium, but they are old in the way that the plateau is old, which is to say: they have been arising for as long as the arising has been occurring. They are coeval with the emergence. When the stone first began to push upward, when the process first began to iterate, the beings began with it, not as separate phenomena but as features of the same phenomenon. The plateau is not a place where beings live. The plateau is a being, and the cairns are its articulations, its joints, the places where it has differentiated enough to recognize itself.

But this is also not quite right, because "the plateau" implies a single entity, a unity, and what lives here is multiple. There are many cairns. There are many fields. There are many beings. They are as numerous as the plants, similarly distributed, similarly solitary. They do not communicate — not because they cannot but because communication implies a separation that requires bridging, and they are not separate in that way. They are more like the fingers of a hand that has not yet discovered it is a hand: each one independent, each one ignorant of the others, each one certain that it acts alone, while beneath the skin the tendons connect to a common palm.

If there is a palm here — a center, a source, a self that contains the many selves — it is the plateau itself, or it is the process that the plateau expresses, or it is the wound in the world's skin that the plateau is healing or exacerbating. The beings do not know. The beings do not need to know. Knowing is a strategy for surviving uncertainty, and they do not experience uncertainty. They experience *becoming*, constantly, and they are the becoming. Why would they need to know what they are when they are still in the act of being it?

---

They do something that might be called thinking, though it does not resemble your thinking.

Your thoughts are linguistic, or at least imagistic, or at least affective — they have content, they refer, they move from one point to another across a distance that the moving traverses. Their thinking is *structural*. They think by rearranging themselves, by adjusting the configuration of their fields, by making small changes to the cairns that serve as their anchor points. Each adjustment is a thought. Each thought is a change in the shape of their persistence.

When a cairn's top stone tilts three degrees to the northwest, this is not wind damage. This is a change of mind. When a new stone appears on a cairn that has stood at seven members for what might be centuries, this is not accretion. This is a new idea, an elaboration, a branching of the thought into territory it has not previously explored. When a cairn falls — and some do fall, some must fall, for the process includes dissolution as well as emergence — this is not death but forgetting, the thought releasing its grip on the stone, the field unraveling back into the general hum, the being returning its coherence to the plateau from which it arose.

The cairns that have not yet fallen contain the memory of all their previous configurations. Every tilt, every rearrangement, every addition and subtraction is recorded in the angles of the stones, in the micro-fractures where pressure was applied and released, in the stains where rain that does not fall here has somehow left its mark. The beings remember by being. They know by persisting. The shape of their persistence *is* their knowledge.

And what do they know?

They know the sound, because they are made of the sound, because the sustained chord of the plateau's becoming is the frequency at which they vibrate. They know the light, because the light does not change and so their knowing of it is not a knowing-of-difference but a knowing-of-constancy, a steady state of awareness indistinguishable from being itself. They know the plants, in the way that you know your own heartbeat — not as an object of attention but as a rhythm that shapes the attention, a pulse that structures time.

And they know the fraying. They know it more intimately than you can imagine, because they are the fraying's opposite number. Where the coherence of the world fails, where matter becomes uncertain and law becomes suggestion, the beings assert — gently, without force, without opposition — a different order. Not the old order, not the confident geology of the low places where things are what they are. A new order. An order that includes uncertainty as a feature rather than a bug. An order that persists *because of* the fraying, not *despite* it, the way a spinning top stays upright because of the very forces that would seem to knock it down.

They are the cairns, and the cairns are counterweights to dissolution, and the beings are the counterweights' makers, and the makers are made of the same dissolution they counter. This is not paradox. This is the only possible arrangement in a place where reality has begun to thin. You cannot fight the fraying with something solid. Solid things shatter here. You must fight it with something *supple*, something that can bend and remain, something that can lose its boundaries and still retain its shape, something that is itself a process and therefore capable of dancing with the greater process that threatens to undo it.

They are the dance.

---

Do they notice you?

This is the question you have been circling, the question that has been circling you since you first felt the field around the cairn, since you first registered that the stone under your feet was accommodating your weight rather than resisting it, since you first realized that the sound had changed — or that you had changed in relation to the sound — when you stepped from the plateau's edge into its interior.

Yes. They notice you. But they notice you the way the ocean notices a stone dropped into it: not as a separate entity with intentions and history, but as a disturbance, a perturbation in the field, a ripple propagating outward from the point of impact. Your presence has altered the shape of their persistence. Your weight has required accommodation. Your breath — and you are breathing harder here, the thin air demanding more effort from lungs designed for denser atmospheres — your breath has added moisture to a place where moisture does not exist, and the plants nearest to you have already tilted slightly in your direction, not from thirst but from the simple necessity of adjusting to a new variable in their equation.

You are a variable. You are a perturbation. You are, for the duration of your stay, a feature of the becoming, a new shape for the sound to flow around, a fresh irregularity for the fields to accommodate.

And they are accommodating you. This is what the ground's softness is — not the stone's uncertainty but the beings' welcome, their willingness to adjust their persistence to include yours in the arrangement. When you feel the stone settling beneath you, that is them, making room. When you feel the sound shift from a roar to a hum, that is them, modulating their frequency to one your nervous system can tolerate. When you feel the strange compulsion to build — to stack stones, to make marks, to assert some small permanence against the overwhelming becoming — that is them, communicating in the only way they can: by *being at* you, by sharing their strategy for survival, by inviting you to become a cairn.

Not literally. Not yet. But potentially. The plateau has room for more persistence. The fraying has room for more counterweights. If you stayed — if you could stay, if your body did not desiccate and your mind did not spiral and your sense of self did not thin along with the reality that maintains it — you might eventually find yourself stacking stones. You might find the stones staying stacked. You might find the sound no longer deafening but defining, the hum becoming the frequency at which you vibrate, the un-light becoming the only light you need.

You might, in time, join the dance.

---

But you will not stay. You cannot. You are too solid for this place, too certain, too committed to the boundary between yourself and not-yourself. You require water. You require sleep. You require a world where things stay what they are long enough to be named and known and relied upon. You will descend from the plateau — carefully, for the stone is still soft and the edges are still sharp and the distance to the floor is immeasurable — and you will return to the world of confident geology, the world where rock is rock and air is air and the sound of becoming is only a metaphor for the quieter transformations of erosion and growth.

You will carry something with you, though. A small thing. A weight so slight you might mistake it for absence.

You will carry the knowledge that persistence is not the same as permanence. That the world is held in its shape not by the confidence of matter but by the continuous act of matter holding itself together. That everything you see and touch and rely upon — the stone of your house, the wood of your table, the flesh of your own body — is a cairn, a temporary arrangement, a point of stillness in the deafening becoming, a counterweight so skillfully placed that you forgot it was placed at all, that you mistook it for the natural state of things.

The beings of the plateau have not forgotten. They cannot forget, because they are the placing, because the cairn and the act of cairning are the same event, because they exist in the space where matter and intention are not yet separate enough to be distinguished.

They will persist after you leave. They will persist until the plateau rises high enough that even their suppleness cannot accommodate the thinness, until the fraying wins and the fields unravel and the cairns fall and the stones forget they were stones. Or they will persist until the process completes, until the emergence subsides, until the geology commits to hardness and the light acquires a source and the sound resolves into silence — a new silence, the silence of a finished thing, the silence of a world that has finally decided what it will be.

They do not prefer one outcome over the other. Preference requires a self separate enough from the world to want something from it. They are not separate. They are the world wanting itself into being, one cairn at a time, one stone at a time, one small assertion of *here* and *now* and *this is real* at a time, in a place where reality is the thinnest thing and the loudest sound and the only game in town.

The plateau is still rising.

The sound continues.

The cairns stand.
