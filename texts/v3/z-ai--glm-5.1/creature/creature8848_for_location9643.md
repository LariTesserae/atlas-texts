# The Vast Weep

---

The ones who remain are not what you would expect.

Not because they are strange — although they are — but because the strangeness is so quiet, so thorough, so indistinguishable from the landscape that you could spend a lifetime here and never once realize you were not alone. The plateau has no use for the dramatic. The dramatic requires edges, contrasts, the sharp boundary between one thing and another. And edges, as has been established, are not a currency this place trades in.

But there are ones who remain.

---

Start with the simplest: the Deep-Dwellers are not animals.

This is important to understand, because the category of "animal" still implies a certain compactness, a certain self-containment, a body that begins and ends. The Deep-Dwellers have no such boundaries. They are masses of tissue that live within the mat, thirty meters down, fifty meters down, in the layers where the root-fiber has compressed into something approaching peat, approaching coal, approaching something for which no word exists because no other world has produced it. They are warm — of course they are warm; everything here is warm — and they are slow, and they are vast.

A Deep-Dweller might cover a hectare. Might cover ten. They are not distinct from the mat in composition; they are made of the same stuff, root and fiber and moisture, arranged in a different pattern. But the pattern is the thing. Somewhere in that compressed tangle of organic matter, the organization becomes something other than vegetable. The fibers interweave in structures that process, that respond, that *prefer*. A Deep-Dweller will grow toward certain minerals and away from others. It will pulse — slowly, over weeks — in a rhythm that corresponds to nothing external, some internal clock running on chemistry and patience.

They eat by absorption. The mat around them is also them, and they draw from it the way a sponge draws from water, except the sponge and the water are the same thing, and the drawing is also the giving, because what they excrete is precisely what the upper layers need. They are stomachs, these Deep-Dwellers. Digestive organs for the plateau. The mat feeds them its dead and its waste and its excess, and they return it processed, broken down, made available for the slow engine of growth. They are the reason the plateau does not choke. They are the reason the cycle can continue indefinitely.

Do they think? The question means less than you think it does. They do not think the way a fox thinks, or a whale, or a human. They do not think the way you think. But they respond to stimuli in ways that cannot be predicted by chemistry alone. They alter their growth patterns in the presence of certain frequencies. They avoid the shafts of indigestible material that puncture the mat from the old world, growing around them in thickened masses that are not quite the same as the growth around a natural obstruction. They are *aware*, in some fashion, of the shape of their own bodies, and they prefer certain shapes to others.

If you descended through the mat — if you could descend, which you cannot, not without cutting, and cutting here is a kind of violence that the tissue will remember and close over — you would feel them before you saw them. The warmth increases. The texture of the root-mass changes, becomes denser, more organized, almost muscular. And if you pressed your ear to the living wall, you would hear something that is not the drip of water, not the creak of growth, not the rustle of the Crawl. You would hear a low, continuous hum, felt more than heard, the sound of a process so large and so slow that it has become indistinguishable from silence unless you are close enough to be inside it.

The Deep-Dwellers are the plateau's gut. They have been digesting the world for longer than anything has been watching.

---

Above them, in the middle layers, the Thread-Mothers.

These you might see, if you knew how to look. They live in the spaces between the Stands, in the damp hollows where the Hanging descends most thickly. They are the size of a large dog, perhaps, but they do not look like any dog that has ever lived. They look, at first glance, like a particularly dense curtain of Hanging — a concentration of those thread-fine root-fibers, beaded with water, swaying in the faint air currents. This is by design. Or by nature. The distinction, as always, is unclear.

A Thread-Mother is a node. A point where the fiber-network concentrates, where the threads converge on a central mass that is slightly denser, slightly warmer, slightly more organized than the threads around it. The central mass is not a body in any traditional sense. It has no eyes, no mouth, no limbs. It is a thickening. A decision made by the roots to become something more than roots at this particular location.

But the threads that extend from it — the threads that connect it to the Hanging, to the Stands, to the Creep, to the mat itself — these are its senses and its hands and its voice. Through them, the Thread-Mother feels the vibration of every drop that falls within its reach. Through them, it tastes the mineral content of the water that slides down each fiber. Through them, it knows when a Lurk has passed beneath, when a section of canopy has grown heavy enough to release its load, when the Crawl have finished their work on a particular patch of debris and moved on.

A Thread-Mother does not move. It does not need to. Its threads extend for dozens of meters in every direction, and where they touch the mat, they are the mat. It experiences the world the way a spider experiences its web, except the web is the world, and the spider has no intention of catching anything because it has no need to eat anything that is not already coming to it.

What does it do? It tends. It adjusts the flow of water through the fibers, directing it toward pools that need replenishment, away from areas that are oversaturated. It encourages the growth of the Creep in places where the mat has thinned, and discourages it where it would shade a pool that the Swimmers need. It is a gardener, except the garden is itself, and the gardening is done through the slow, patient manipulation of moisture and nutrient and growth hormone, conducted through the fiber-network with the precision of a system that has had a hundred million years to refine its methods.

There are thousands of them. Perhaps more. Each one tends its own territory, and where territories overlap, the threads interweave and the Thread-Mothers share information in a chemical language too slow and too subtle for any brain to parse. They do not compete. Competition requires scarcity, and scarcity is a concept the plateau abandoned long ago, when it learned to become its own ground, its own water, its own food. They cooperate, instead, in the way that organs cooperate — not through agreement, but through shared purpose, through being part of the same body and knowing it in every fiber.

You will not see one unless it chooses to be seen. And it will not choose, because choice implies a preference for being perceived, and the Thread-Mothers have no such preference. They perceive, but they do not require perception in return. They are sufficient unto themselves, and the only acknowledgment they need is the continued health of the territory they tend.

But sometimes — rarely, once in a long while — a Thread-Mother will gather its threads more densely in one place, forming a shape. Not a face. Nothing so crude. But a shape that a human eye would recognize as intentional: a spiral, perhaps, or a curve, or a pattern that echoes the rectangular scar of those ancient foundations. It will hold this shape for a few hours, or a few days, and then let it dissolve back into the swaying curtain of fiber.

It is not communicating. There is no one to communicate with. It is remembering. The shape is a memory of something the threads learned long ago, from a people who sang to the roots and were answered. A memory of the sound a voice made when it asked the mat to hold a space open, and the mat, which had never been asked anything before, discovered that it could choose to comply.

The Thread-Mothers are the keepers of that memory. They wear it in their fibers the way old wood wears the grain of the years it grew in. They will carry it as long as the plateau stands, and when the plateau finally falls — if it ever does — the memory will dissolve into the lowlands with the rest, and become part of whatever grows there next.

---

And then there are the ones who live in the gaps between the seconds.

Not the Crawl — the Crawl are workers, processors, necessary and unremarkable in their industry. Something else. Something that moves through the plateau at a speed that makes the Thread-Mothers look hasty.

You will never see one. You will never hear one. You will never find evidence that one has passed, because evidence is a thing that remains, and what remains here is absorbed. But if you sit in one of those hollow spaces — those dry, preserved rooms where the roots refuse to grow — and if you sit there long enough, in the silence and the warmth and the patient dark, you might begin to notice something.

The silence is not complete.

You knew this. The dripping, the creaking, the hum of the Deep-Dwellers far below. But underneath all of that, beneath the perceptible sounds, there is something else. A presence. Not a sound, exactly, but a pressure, a feeling of being *regarded* by something that does not have eyes. It comes and goes. It fades in over the course of hours and fades out again just as slowly, and when it is present, the hollow feels different — less preserved, more *inhabited*. As if something has joined you in the room, something that takes up space without having volume, something that attends without having attention.

The Hollow-Walkers.

This is not their name. They have no name. They have no language, no communication, no interaction with the living world that can be measured or confirmed. But the hollows exist because of them, or because of something they do, or because of something they *are*. The roots refuse to grow into those spaces not because they were asked to hold the shape, but because something is already occupying it. Something that the roots can feel but cannot digest, cannot grow around, cannot incorporate. Something that is *there* in a way that matter is not.

The Hollow-Walkers move through the plateau like heat moves through metal — slowly, thoroughly, filling every space that can receive them. They are in the gaps in the metabolism, the spaces between processes, the instants when one chemical reaction has finished and the next has not yet begun. They exist in time rather than space, and their presence in a hollow is their presence in a moment that the living tissue has left vacant.

They do nothing. They affect nothing. They are, as far as can be determined, neutral — neither helpful nor harmful, neither part of the system nor opposed to it. They are simply present, the way a shadow is present, except they cast no shadow, and the light here is too diffuse for shadows anyway.

But they were not always here.

The hollows are shaped like rooms. Like shelters. Like spaces where people once lived and worked and sang. And the Hollow-Walkers occupy those spaces now, filling them with a presence that is not flesh and not root and not water. The people who built those rooms are gone — absorbed, digested, incorporated into the mat that has forgotten nothing except how to forget. But something of them persists in the Hollow-Walkers. Something that moved out of matter and into the gaps between moments, something that found a way to be present without being substantial.

Perhaps they are ghosts. The word is too simple, too loaded with assumptions about death and afterlife and the persistence of identity. But it is not entirely wrong. They are what remains when the body has been absorbed and the matter has been redistributed and the shape has been held in the memory of the roots. They are the part of the builders that the plateau could not digest. Not the mineral, not the protein, not the calcium of their bones — all of that was processed long ago, broken down by the Crawl and the Deep-Dwellers and fed back into the slow cycle of growth. But something else. Something that lived in the spaces between the cells, between the synapses, between the thoughts. Something immaterial. Something that could not be eaten because it was never made of eating.

The Hollow-Walkers are the breath of the builders, held in the preserved rooms, waiting to be exhaled.

They will wait as long as the rooms remain. They will wait as long as the roots refuse to grow. They will wait as long as the plateau remembers the shape of the question it was once asked, the question that taught it it could choose.

And if you sit in that hollow long enough, you will feel them not as a presence but as an absence — the absence of aloneness. The knowledge that you are sharing the space with something that was once a person, or several people, or a whole community of people who looked at the endless wet mat and the patient drip and the warm dark and decided not to fight it. Decided to learn it. Decided to speak to it in the only language it could understand — the language of slow growth, of patient tending, of boundaries given willingly.

They are still speaking. The Thread-Mothers hear them, in their fibers. The Deep-Dwellers feel them, in their slow hum. And the hollows hold them, in the only way that anything here holds anything: completely, patiently, without end.

---

So. Who lives here?

The Deep-Dwellers, digesting the world in the dark below. The Thread-Mothers, tending the world in the damp above. The Crawl, processing, the Swimmers, cycling, the Lurk, grazing in its invisible way. The Hollow-Walkers, remembering in the spaces between moments.

And the plateau itself — which is all of these things and none of them, which is one continuous body with a hundred million years of growth behind it and (perhaps) a hundred million ahead, which breathes and drinks and eats and remembers and tends itself and holds the shapes of those who once asked it for shelter.

It does not know you are here. But it knows that *something* is here, the way a body knows it has been touched, the way a wound knows it has been cleaned, the way a room knows it has been entered. It will close over your footprints. It will absorb your heat. It will process your cells and make them into root-fiber and moisture and the faint chemical trace of your passage.

And if you are very still, and very patient, and if you stay long enough to forget what it feels like to be separate from something — it might, in its slow, thorough way, begin to know you the way it knows itself. Not as a guest. Not as an intruder. But as another process in the endless, patient, dripping metabolism of the Vast Weep.

Another gap in the metabolism. Another hollow to hold. Another shape to remember.

It has room. It has time. It has nothing but room and time, and the gentle, ceaseless weeping that is not sorrow but simply the sound of water finding its way through living tissue, which is the sound of being alive, which is the only sound this place has ever needed to make.
