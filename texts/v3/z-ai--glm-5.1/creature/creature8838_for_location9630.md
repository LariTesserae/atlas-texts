# The Inhabitants

---

The Maintainers came first, though *first* is a word that means less here than it does elsewhere, where time moves in one direction and leaves evidence of its passage. The Maintainers may have come first or they may have always been here, which is to say: they may have been part of the initial specification, another system installed alongside the pipes and the engines and the endless metal bones of the place. They do not speak of origins. They may not speak at all.

There are not many of them. You could walk for days and not see one, and then, in a corridor where the light has taken on a particular sallow quality that makes the condensation on the walls glow like something bioluminescent, you might turn a corner and find one at work.

A Maintainer is roughly the size and shape of a person, if you are the kind of person who sees a coat on a hook and thinks *someone standing there* for just a moment. They are not humanoid in the way that suggests common ancestry or convergent evolution. They are humanoid in the way that suggests a template — that the makers of this place had a form in mind, and that form was *upright, ambulatory, capable of reaching*, and everything else was left to the materials at hand.

The materials at hand were metal and composite and the white fibrous growth from the warm junctions, and the Maintainers are built of all three. Their surfaces are not smooth — they are layered, like shingles, like scales, overlapping plates of something that might be steel and might be the same wrong-colored concrete from the older walls, held together by seams of the pale cartilage growth that seems to serve them as binding, as joint-tissue, as something between glue and gristle. Where the plates overlap, the white fibers have grown into the gaps and hardened, and the effect is something like a figure wrapped in ancient, laminated armor, if armor could also be scar tissue.

They do not have faces. The uppermost portion of a Maintainer — the part that tracks your movement when you enter its peripheral awareness — is a smooth, slightly convex surface, the same overlapping plate construction as the rest, but with a narrow horizontal aperture that might be an eye or might be a vent or might be both. Behind the aperture, something moves. Not an eye. A mechanism. A small, regular turning of parts that catches the light differently depending on the angle, and this, apparently, is sufficient for vision, or for whatever replaces vision in a thing that was made rather than born.

Their hands — and they do have hands, of a sort — are the most unsettling part, because they are the most obviously functional. Four digits and an opposing member, like a hand, but the digits are long and segmented and end in thin, flat tools that can splay into brushes or close into scrapers or rotate at the tip into something like a bore. They use these constantly. You watched one — the first one you saw clearly, in that sallow-lit corridor — running its digits along a seam in the wall where the white growth was thick, and the motion was so fluid, so precisely calibrated to the depth and spread of the fibers, that you understood: this is what it *does*. This is the whole of its purpose, or the whole of the purpose it knows. It maintains. It clears. It keeps the corridors passable and the seams from closing over and the systems from being choked by the only living thing in this place that grows without being told to.

When it noticed you — and it did notice, the head-unit turning with a mechanical deliberation that was somehow worse than if it had snapped toward you — it stopped. Not froze. Stopped the way a machine stops when it encounters a variable it was not programmed to expect. The digits retracted slightly. The aperture narrowed. You could hear something inside it — a shifting of parts, a recalculation.

Then it resumed work. It turned back to the wall. It scraped. It moved on.

You were not its concern. You were not *not* its concern either. You were simply outside the scope of what it had been made to address, and so it addressed you the way it addressed the air or the condensation — as a condition of the environment, neither obstacle nor object, simply *there*.

This is the closest thing to mercy the manufactory offers.

---

The others — the things that move in the pipes, the things that drink from leaks, the things you hear but do not see — are not Maintainers. They are not maintained. They are not part of any system that was designed. They are what happens when a place like this runs long enough without oversight: life emerges, or something like life, or something that has always been possible in the deep wet dark but has never before found conditions hospitable enough to become actual.

You have started calling them *the locals*, which is a joke you tell yourself in the half-lit hours when the sound of the engines has become so constant that silence would feel like an emergency. The locals are not a single species. They are not even a single *kind* of thing. They are the aggregate of whatever can survive here, whatever has found a niche in the endless manufactured geometry and filled it with something that breathes and moves and wants.

The dog-thing from the flooded room — you have seen others like it. They are the most common visible fauna, though *common* is relative. You might encounter three in a day of walking, or none for a week. They move low to the ground and they move with purpose, though you have never been able to determine what that purpose might be. They do not hunt. They do not forage in any way you recognize. They go from place to place within the manufactory, following routes that seem fixed but not obvious, pausing at junctions and at specific points along corridors, and sometimes they press their not-quite-heads against the walls or the floor or the pipes, and they stay that way for long minutes, and you have convinced yourself that they are *listening*, though to what you cannot say.

Their bodies are dark — not black but a deep, wet brown, like soil after rain, like the color of something that has never been touched by light long enough to develop a relationship with it. They are smooth in the way that the Maintainers are not, smooth the way something becomes when it has passed through enough tight spaces to wear away any protrusion that cannot negotiate a squeeze. And they *do* squeeze. You have seen one enter a pipe through a gap no wider than your forearm, its body compressing with a fluidity that made your skin crawl, not because it was unnatural but because it was *too natural*, the kind of thing an octopus does without thinking, the kind of adaptation that reminds you that *rigid* is not the default state of living things.

They do not seem to mind you. They do not seem to notice you, most of the time. But once, one of them was pressing its head against a section of wall where a pipe had burst and been sealed again, and it lifted its head as you passed, and you saw that it had eyes — or eye-spots, or light-sensing organs, something — on the underside of its not-quite-head, where they would press against whatever surface it was listening to. They were small and pale and slightly luminous, like the condensation in sallow light, and they were looking at you, or at least they were open toward you, and in that moment you understood that these creatures perceived the manufactory from the inside — that they felt it the way you feel a room with your eyes closed, by pressure and proximity and the subtle shifts of air and vibration, and that to them you were a warm spot moving through a cold system, a brief anomaly in the body of the place they called home.

You did not feel afraid. You felt *seen*, which is its own kind of discomfort.

---

In the higher pipes — the ones that run along the ceilings in thick bundles and sweat heat and condensation — there are smaller things. You hear them more than you see them: a skittering, a rapid clicking of small hard bodies against metal. Sometimes, if you stand very still and look up at the right moment, you can see them — not clearly, but as a suggestion of movement, a darkening that flows along the pipe's curve and disappears into a junction box or a gap in the housing. They are fast. They are numerous. They are the reason you do not sleep directly under the overhead runs, though you cannot say exactly what harm they might do, because they have never come close enough for you to find out.

You think they might be maintaining the pipes from the inside. You think this because sometimes, when a pipe has been groaning — a long, low complaint of expanding metal that goes on for hours — the skittering will converge on that location, and after a time the groaning will stop. This could be coincidence. This could be the pipes simply finishing their adjustment. But you have noticed the correlation enough times that the hypothesis has hardened into something like belief, and you have started to think of the skittering things as a parallel system to the Maintainers — smaller, faster, working the inside while the larger figures work the outside, keeping the circulatory system of the manufactory clear and functional and running.

Or perhaps they are eating something that grows in the pipes. Perhaps they are the reason the fluid that flows through the arterial bundles has not yet slowed or soured or stopped. Perhaps they are the manufactory's immune response, small and numerous and hungry for whatever might clog the works.

You do not know. You watch. You listen. You try not to be something that needs to be cleared.

---

There is something else. You have not seen it. You are not certain it can be seen, or that *seeing* is the correct relationship to have with it. But in the deepest sections — the ones where the infiltration has begun, where the water rises from below and the air tastes of metal and something older than metal, something geological and patient — you have felt it. A presence. Not a mind — nothing so organized, nothing so comprehensible. A *gravity*. A weight in the air that does not come from pressure or temperature or any physical quality you can name. A sense of vast attention, or if not attention then *potential for attention*, the way a sleeping body has the potential to wake.

The Maintainers do not go to the deepest sections. This is how you know. You have tracked their scrape-marks along corridors, followed the cleared paths through the white growth, and in every case the maintenance stops at a certain depth. Below that point, the fibrous growth spreads unchecked, thick and pale and branching, covering the walls and the floor and the ceiling until the corridor becomes a throat lined with cartilage, and the only way forward is to push through it, and the only sound is the wet compression of the fibers under your hands and the distant, muffled thrum of the engines far above.

You went down once. Not far. Far enough.

The growth was thick enough that it muffled the sound of the manufactory — the engines, the dripping, the electrical whine — and in the silence that remained you could hear something else. Not a sound. A *rhythm*. The slow, measured pulse of something very large moving very slowly, or the pressure wave of something breathing on a timescale that made your own breath seem frantic and brief. It came from below. It came from the walls. It came from the growth itself, which was not still but moving, expanding and contracting in a cadence too slow to see but undeniable when you pressed your palm against it and felt the fibers push back against your skin like a heartbeat.

You left. You did not run — running in the manufactory is how you attract attention, and you have learned by now that attention is not something you want from this place. But you moved quickly, and you did not go back.

Later, lying in a junction where the light was steady enough to sleep by, you tried to name what you had felt. The best you could do was: the manufactory is not empty. Not in the way that a building is empty when its workers have gone home. Not in the way that a body is empty when the person has left it. The manufactory is occupied — by its systems, by its processes, by the Maintainers and the locals and the skittering things in the pipes — but it is also occupied by something else, something that is not a resident but a *presence*, something that pervades the place the way a thought pervades a mind, the way a purpose pervades an action.

You think: the manufactory is building something. Not in a particular location, not on an assembly line, not according to a blueprint that could be found and read. The manufactory is building something the way a body builds itself — cell by cell, process by process, without conscious direction, without a plan, simply following the instructions encoded in its original design toward an end that was determined before it began.

And whatever that end is, it is not finished. It is not close to finished. The engines are still turning. The water is still circulating. The growth is still spreading. The Maintainers are still maintaining, keeping the corridors clear for — what? For whom? For the final connection that will complete the circuit and start the final process and bring the whole vast contraption to the point it has been working toward since before anyone now living was born?

Or for you? For the warm body that walks its corridors and drinks its water and sleeps in its junctions and is, itself, a kind of raw material, a kind of fuel, a kind of missing piece that the manufactory has been waiting for without knowing what it looked like until it arrived?

You do not know. You stand in the half-light. You choose a direction. The corridor accepts you.

And somewhere below, in the deep where the growth is thick and the rhythm is slow and the presence is vast and patient and utterly indifferent to your understanding of it, something shifts. Not toward you. Not away from you. Simply *shifts*, the way a sleeping body shifts in its sleep, adjusting itself toward a more comfortable position, preparing to continue the long work of whatever it is doing in the dark.

The work continues. It has always continued. It will continue after you have gone, or after you have stayed too long and become part of it, or after you have found the final corridor and the final door and whatever lies beyond it that the manufactory has been building toward all this time.

The water drips. The engines turn. The Maintainers scrape and clear. The locals press their heads against the walls and listen. The skittering things flow through the pipes like corpuscles through veins. And you walk, because walking is what you do here, because stopping is what you do not do, because the manufactory has no chairs and no beds and no places designed for rest — only corridors and junctions and rooms full of machinery that does not know you are there, and the endless descending geometry of a place that was built to build, and is building still, and will not stop until the building is done.
