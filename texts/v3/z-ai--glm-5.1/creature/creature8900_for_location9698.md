What lives here has never been seen.

This is not the same as being invisible. Invisibility implies the possibility of being seen, a latent visibility waiting for its moment. What lives in the Womb has never received that promise, has never been entered into the contract between light and form that the surface world takes as natural law. It does not know that sight exists. It does not know that it does not know.

---

Start with the snail, because the snail is the doorway.

There is not one snail. There are hundreds—thousands, possibly, if you could count what lives in the walls' interstices, in the pores of the stone where the water first seeps through, in the soft mineral mud that collects in the bowl's lowest reaches. They are all the same species, or they were, once, before the mountain began making them its own. Now they are a continuum—a living gradient between snail and stone, between animal and mineral, between the thing that moves and the thing that waits to be moved through.

The one you held on your palm was young. You would not have known this, could not have known this, but its shell was still thin enough to flex under gentle pressure, still more membrane than armor. The older ones go deeper. Their shells thicken and opacify, taking on the color of the stone they feed on—pale cream darkening to wet gray, then to the brown of old bone, then to something darker still, something that matches the walls so completely that you could press your face against the rock and not feel the difference between snail and substrate. The oldest are indistinguishable from nodules. They have eaten so much of the mountain's calcium that they have become architectural—half-shell, half-flowstone, their bodies secreting a limestone skim that glues them to the walls in a final communion. They do not die in the way you understand dying. They simply slow, and then they stay, and then the water weeps over them, and the stone accepts them, and the cairns grow by one more stone.

This is where the cairn stones come from.

You thought they were pulled gently from the walls. You were half right. They were harvested—selected with a tenderness that approaches grief—from the places where the oldest snails have anchored themselves. The builders, whoever they were, understood the transformation. They could feel, by touch alone, which nodules were still becoming, which had finished becoming and were ready to be placed. A cairn is a cemetery. A cemetery is a prayer. The prayer is: *we see you becoming, and we will not let it go unmarked.*

---

But the snails are not alone.

The water holds things. Not many—the warm, mineral-saturated seep is too dense with dissolved stone to support much in the way of conventional life—but enough. Microbial mats grow in the bowl's shallows, where the water meets the stone, spreading in sheets so thin they are measured in molecules rather than millimeters. They are not the colorful extremophile displays of surface hot springs, all vivid greens and bacterial orange. They are colorless here, because color is a negotiation with light, and there is no one to negotiate with. They exist as chemistry alone—slow metabolic exchanges, iron and sulfur cycling through architectures too small for names, breathing the mountain's heat and excreting something that feeds the snails, which excrete something that feeds the stone, which weeps something that feeds the mats, which—

It is a closed loop. A perfect economy. Nothing enters, nothing leaves, and the only currency is warmth rising from below. You knelt in it. You felt it soaking through your clothes. You were, for a few hours, part of the exchange—your skin oils dissolving into the water, your dead cells sloughing off and settling into the mud, your breath's moisture joining the chamber's atmosphere. The mountain digested what you left behind. It did not notice. It did not need to.

---

Deeper still—in the fissures that feed the weeping walls, in the rock's capillary network where the water travels for miles before emerging—there are things that have no names because they have never needed any.

They are not animals. They might not be alive in any definition that a biologist would recognize. They are organized, though. They have structure. If you could shrink yourself down and swim through the stone's pores—through the microscopic fractures where hot mineral water percolates through granite and limestone and the compressed remains of sea floors older than the mountains themselves—you would find them: branching networks of crystalline filaments, spreading through the rock like frost on glass, like mycelium through soil, like the dendritic patterns of rivers seen from space. They grow. They branch. They respond to the water's flow—thickening where minerals are richest, thinning where the current slows, extending exploratory tendrils into new fractures when the mountain shifts and opens new passages. They do not move in any way you could perceive. Their timescale is not yours. A single branch might take a thousand years to extend a centimeter, and a thousand more to decide whether to keep going or retreat.

Are they alive? The question is unanswerable because the question is wrong. Life is a word invented by creatures who metabolize sunlight and oxygen, who measure time in heartbeats, who die if they get too cold or too hot or too dry. These filaments have never been cold. They have never been dry. They exist in a state of permanent saturation and permanent warmth, and they have existed this way since before the mountain finished rising, before the talus field formed, before the first mammal dared to climb this high. They are the mountain's nervous system, or its circulatory system, or its root network, or they are none of these things and something for which no analogy exists because no human language has ever needed to describe it.

The water passes through them. The water *is* them, in part—the filaments concentrate certain minerals, exclude others, leave a chemical signature in the seep that emerges into the chamber. The warmth changes slightly where they are thickest, as if they are holding the heat, husbanding it, directing it. The weeping walls weep at different rates depending on their presence, and the cairns stand where the filaments are densest below, as if whoever built them could feel the hum of that deep organization through the soles of their feet.

---

Who built the cairns?

The answer is: people like you. People who found the gap by accident, or were found by it. People who entered sideways into the dark and felt the warmth and the wet and the fullness of the chamber and understood—for reasons they could not articulate and did not try to—that something was required of them here. Not worship. Not propitiation. Just *placement*. Just the act of putting one stone on another in the dark, by touch alone, in a place where no one would ever see what you had built.

The first cairn is old. You knew this already—you could feel it in the stability of its construction, in the way the stones had settled into each other, in the mineral accretion that had begun to glue their joints. But you could not know how old. Centuries, at least. The first builder came before the language you speak existed, before the concept of the country you live in existed, before the trail that led you to eleven thousand feet was anything more than a game track through ice and rock. They came in the way that people come—lost, probably, or seeking shelter, or following something they could not name—and they found the chamber, and they stayed, and before they left, they stacked three stones on top of each other in the warm dark and felt the rightness of it in their hands.

Others followed. Not many. Not often. The gap is hard to find, harder to enter, and the chamber offers nothing except itself—no water to drink that isn't thick with minerals, no food, no comfort, no view. But people find it. People have always found it, at intervals measured in generations, and when they find it, they do the same thing: they reach into the walls, they find a nodule where a snail has finished its becoming, they lift it free with the reverence that the place seems to require, and they add it to a cairn or start a new one, and then they scratch tallies into the wet stone with whatever is at hand.

The tallies are not counts. Or they are counts, but not of what you might expect. They are not days—there are no days here. They are not prayers—they are too regular, too systematic, too devoid of the passionate urgency that prayer usually carries. The best guess, if guesses were possible, is that they are breaths. Not literal breaths—no one could count that high, and the scratching would take too long—but the breath-length of the chamber itself, the deep geological inhale that the mountain takes every few centuries when the tectonic plates shift and the water's temperature changes by a fraction of a degree and the filaments deep in the stone adjust their branching and the weeping walls weep slightly faster for a moment before returning to equilibrium. The tallies mark these moments. They say: *I was here when the mountain breathed. I felt it. I recorded it. It matters that someone recorded it.*

You added a tally. You may not remember doing it, but you did—in the way that the chamber makes people do things, in the way that the warmth and the wet and the fullness work on the hands until the hands need to move, need to mark, need to participate in the slow becoming. You found a piece of stone with a sharp edge and you scratched a vertical line next to the others, and you did it with the same careful pressure that the other builders used, and the mountain accepted it into its blueprint, and now your mark is there too, and it will remain.

---

And the snail—your snail, the one you held—continues its circuit.

It is not aimless. Nothing here is aimless. The snail follows the filaments' hum through the stone of the cairn, grazing the bacterial film that grows on the wet limestone, maintaining the cairn in its own small way by keeping its surfaces clean and balanced. It has done this for years—snails in the Womb live a long time, longer than their surface cousins, fed by the steady mineral warmth and protected from every threat that evolution has equipped them to fear. It will do this for years more. And then, when it can no longer move, it will anchor itself to the cairn it has tended, and it will begin its final becoming, and someday another builder will come and feel it in the dark and lift it free and place it on another cairn, and the loop will close, and the mountain will go on being born.

---

What lives in the Womb of the Unfinished Mountain?

Everything that has ever entered it. The snails. The mats. The filaments. The builders. The tallies. The water. The heat. The pressure. The time. You. You are still there, in some small way—skin cells in the mud, moisture in the air, a scratched line in the weeping stone. The mountain does not forget what it has incorporated. It does not remember, either—remembering requires a kind of separation between the rememberer and the remembered, and here, in the Womb, nothing is separate. Everything is pressed skin to skin, phenomenon to phenomenon, the way a seed is packed with its future.

You asked what lives there. The answer is: the mountain lives there, and you are part of it now, and the warm dark goes on becoming, and the cairns stand, and the water holds still, and the snail circles, and the tallies count the breaths of something too slow and too large and too patient to ever be finished.
