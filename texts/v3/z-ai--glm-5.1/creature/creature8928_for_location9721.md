# Who Lives Here

---

## The Ones Who Stay

There is a woman on Station 11—Margai, though she will not tell you her surname or her origin because both belong to a life that ended when she stepped off the supply helicopter three years ago. She is forty-seven and looks older, or perhaps younger; the plateau ages people strangely, the way it ages everything. The sun etches lines but the wet keeps the skin soft, and after a while the technicians begin to look like the mass itself—smooth, dimpled, faintly green in certain light from the chlorophyll they've breathed in continuous microscopic particulate for so many weeks.

Margai was a hydrologist. Still is, technically. She came on a three-week rotation like everyone else, did her shift, went down to the lowlands, tried to sleep in a room that was too quiet, and came back. That was year one. Now she volunteers for the longest stretches—eight weeks, ten, twelve when the company will allow it. Her replacement requests have become pro forma. Everyone knows she'll return.

You ask her why and she looks at you as though the question contains a category error. Why does anyone live where they live? She does not say: *Because the plateau wants me.* She is not a mystic. She has read the survey reports and understands the organism's biochemistry and can recite the filtration rates from memory. But she also knows something the reports don't capture, something that takes weeks of continuous exposure to learn:

The plateau responds.

Not consciously. Not with intention. But with *pattern*, and pattern, when it persists long enough, becomes indistinguishable from dialogue.

## What Margai Knows

She noticed it in her second month. The sound shifts when she walks across the surface—she can feel it through her boots, a subtle modulation in the vibration, as though her weight creates a local eddy in the tissue's respiration. This is mechanically true; the mass compresses and rebounds, changing the air flow in the ventilation channels, altering the acoustic profile of the surrounding ten-meter radius. Anyone can feel it. Most people find it unsettling and try not to think about it.

Margai kept walking.

She walks every day, weather permitting. She has routes she follows across the tissue—north to the fracture zone, east along the drainage incipient, south to where the mass thins and the basalt shows through like bone beneath wounded skin. She walks the same paths and has walked them hundreds of times. And over the months, she has watched the surface change.

The tissue where she walks is denser. The pores are smaller, the cushion slightly firmer, the spring-back fractionally quicker. The paths she frequents have become compressed not in the way of degradation—like the graying around the pilings—but in the way of *adaptation*. The mass has thickened its upper layers where her boots fall. It has grown a callus.

She showed this to a visiting biologist, who measured the tissue density and confirmed the finding and wrote it up as a stress response to repeated mechanical pressure, which is exactly what it is. A stress response. The mass doesn't know Margai is there. It doesn't know anything. But it responds to pressure by reinforcing the tissue, and Margai provides pressure, and so the plateau has grown around the shape of her passage.

She knows this. She is not sentimental about it. But she also knows that she feels, when she steps onto the surface each morning, a slight resistance, a slight *recognition*, as though the ground were saying: *Here you are again.* And the ground—no, the mass, the organism, the four-hundred-square-kilometer body—is saying nothing of the kind. It is only doing what living things do: adjusting to stimulus, growing toward what touches it, becoming shaped by what it touches in return.

But Margai has lived alone on Station 11 for so long that the distinction has stopped mattering to her. She walks her routes. The mass grows around her feet. She has become a feature of its environment, as fixed and predictable as wind or dawn, and it has grown to accommodate her, and this is not love and not communication and not symbiosis in any rigorous sense, but it is also not nothing.

## The Ones Who Leave

There was a man on Station 7—his name is not recorded because he asked for it to be removed from the logs, which is a right written into every employment contract, and one that the company regrets extending but cannot legally revoke. He lasted nine days. On the ninth day, he walked out onto the mass without his radio, without his emergency beacon, without his pack. He walked north. He was not heading for any station; he was heading for the center of the plateau, the deepest part, where the tissue is four meters thick and warm underneath and the sound is so total that vision begins to blur.

The search team found him sitting cross-legged on the surface, six kilometers from the nearest installation, soaked to the skin, apparently content. He had been out for fourteen hours. He said he had been listening. When asked what he had heard, he said: "Everything. Everything at once, forever."

He did not come back to the plateau. He did not, as far as anyone knows, return to any settled place. His last known location was a transit hostel in the lowland city where the pipeline terminates. He stayed there three days and then dissolved from the record like salt in the plateau's water.

There have been others. Not many—one or two per year, out of the rotating staff of perhaps thirty-six technicians across all stations at any given time. The company classifies them as *environmental stress casualties* and includes them in the mandatory briefing with careful language about psychological screening and available support resources. What the company does not say, because it cannot be verified, is that the plateau seems to call to certain people, or perhaps more accurately, that certain people seem to be *tuned* to the plateau's frequency.

They are the ones who hear the sound not as noise but as language—not a language with meaning, not a language they can read, but a language in the sense that it carries information and they feel compelled to listen. They are the ones for whom the absence of silence feels not like an assault but like an embrace. They are the ones who, when they stand on the surface and feel the slow systole and diastole beneath their feet, recognize something they have been missing their whole lives without knowing it.

The company does not screen for these people. It screens *against* them. The psychological evaluation includes questions designed to identify exactly this tendency—the capacity for absorption, the tolerance for sensory saturation, the specific kind of loneliness that is not alleviated by human company but intensified by it. The company wants technicians who can endure the plateau, not technicians who love it. Technicians who love it are a liability. They don't leave on time. They don't follow protocols. They walk out onto the mass without their radios and sit in the deep tissue and listen, and the company has to send search teams, and search teams are expensive, and the profit margin on ultra-pure water is thin enough already.

But the screening is imperfect. Some get through.

## The Midge People

No one calls them that. They call themselves nothing because they are not a group, not a tribe, not a community in any way that the lowlands would recognize. They do not have a shared language or a shared ritual. They do not have a leader. They do not have a name.

But they exist.

They are the ones who stayed. Not the ones who came back for repeat shifts—those are the Margais, the steady hands, the reliable workers. The midge people (a term used only by the helicopter pilots, and not kindly) are the ones who went AWOL. Who walked away from their stations and simply did not return. The company writes them off as missing, presumed dead, and closes the file and replaces them. But the plateau is not lethal—not quickly. The surface is walkable. The water is drinkable. The temperature, while uncomfortable, rarely drops below freezing, even at night, because the mass's metabolic heat keeps the surface a degree or two above the ambient air. A person could survive on the plateau indefinitely, if they didn't mind the wet and the sound and the absence of everything else.

And some don't mind. Some thrive.

How many are out there? The company's last aerial survey, conducted five years ago under pressure from the regulatory authority, counted seven. Seven figures moving across the green-brown expanse, far from any station, walking or sitting or lying prone on the surface. The survey was conducted at midday; it is possible that others were below the visual horizon, or that some were in the deeper tissue where the surface undulations would hide a prone body. The real number might be ten. Might be fifteen. Might be higher still.

They survive on the midges when the midges are present—crushing the clouds of larvae into paste, eating it raw, a protein that tastes like copper and chlorophyll and something else, something that is probably imaginary but that every person who has tried it describes the same way: *like the plateau tasting itself through your mouth.* They drink the water that wells up around their hands when they press the surface. They sleep on the mass, and the mass gives beneath them and holds them, and the sound continues, and they sleep deeply in a way they never slept before they came here.

They do not build shelters. They do not make tools. They do not speak—not because they have forgotten how, but because there is no one to speak to who would hear, and because speaking feels like an imposition on the sound, a small human frequency trying to assert itself against the vast chord of the plateau's respiration, and after a while the impropriety of it becomes obvious and they stop.

They walk. They listen. They breathe in time with the mass—inhaling as it expands in the warmth, exhaling as it contracts in the cold, letting the rhythm become their rhythm, letting the boundary between their bodies and the living ground become indistinct. They are not dissolving. They are not dying. They are *joining*, slowly, at geological pace, becoming features of the plateau's environment the way Margai has become a feature, only more so, only without the station to return to, only without the radio to call for rescue.

When they die—and they will die, eventually, from injury or illness or the accumulated slow cost of living on four hundred square kilometers of wet moss without medicine or warmth or any of the softening necessities—they will sink. The tissue will grow over them. The mass will digest them the way it digests everything that falls onto its surface: slowly, cell by cell, incorporating their minerals into its own, filtering what is useful, releasing what is not. They will become part of the plateau in the most literal sense, their calcium and iron and carbon cycling through the hyaline cells, entering the water, passing through the filtration of cell walls, emerging clean and stripped of everything that once made them distinct.

This is not what they think about. They do not think about this. They do not think, exactly, in the way that the lowlands understand thinking—the discursive, narrative, problem-solving kind. They have entered a different mode, one that is more like the mass itself: responsive, patterned, present, continuous. Their brains are still functioning; they could still solve problems, still plan, still remember their names if asked. But they are not asked. And in the absence of asking, the mind settles into the closest thing to silence the plateau permits: a state of continuous low-grade attention, sensory without being conscious, aware without being self-aware.

They are the closest thing to the mass that human beings can become and still be human. Whether they have crossed a line into something else is a question that requires definitions of personhood that no one on the plateau has time or interest to articulate.

## The Warbler

There is one warbler who returns to the same escarpment ledge every spring. She has been doing this for six years, which is near the upper limit of her species' lifespan, and she shows no signs of stopping. She is small and drab and always exhausted, as all her kind are—the exhaustion of a body that burns through its reserves at altitude, that metabolizes at a rate barely sustainable, that lives on the thin edge of what is possible for a warm-blooded creature at 4,200 meters.

She nests in a crevice in the basalt, on the cliff face, where the mass hasn't reached yet. She lays three eggs. Two, usually, will hatch. One, sometimes, will fledge. The numbers are barely enough. But she comes back.

Each morning, she flies up to the plateau surface and perches on the railings of Station 3—the nearest installation, though she has no concept of stations or railings or industry. She perches there because it gives her a good vantage point from which to watch for midges, and because the metal has a slight warmth from the generator inside, and because the technicians at Station 3 have learned not to shoo her away.

She sings. She opens her beak and produces the song that her species has produced for ten thousand generations—a bright, quick trill, three seconds long, repeated at intervals of eleven seconds, a territorial declaration, a mating call, a statement of presence. She cannot hear herself. The plateau's sound swallows her song so completely that she might as well be singing into a vacuum.

But she sings anyway.

This is not courage. She does not know her song is inaudible. She sings because singing is what her body does in the morning, because the behavior is encoded in her neurology, because ten thousand generations of warblers have sung from perches above the midge pools and the behavior has never needed to be audible to be performed. The song is not for the plateau. It is not for other warblers, who also cannot hear it. It might not be for anyone at all. It might be a thing that simply happens, like the mass's respiration, like the water moving through the tissue, like the slow growth and decomposition that constitutes the plateau's only process.

But she sings, and her syrinx vibrates, and that vibration enters the air, and the air enters the mass's pores, and the mass's tissue absorbs the sound as it absorbs everything: without recognition, without response, without meaning. Her song becomes part of the plateau's sound the way her body will, eventually, become part of the plateau's substance. She is already inside it in all but the final sense.

She does not know this. She does not know anything about the plateau except that it has midges and the midges are food and the food is here and she is hungry. She is always hungry. She will die hungry, eventually, on the escarpment or on the surface or in the air between the two, and the mass will take her, and her calcium will enter the tissue, and her song—which was never heard—will continue, in the broadest possible sense, as a pattern of vibrations that have already been absorbed into the ground and will persist, diminishing, for as long as the ground remembers vibrations, which is longer than you might think.

She is the plateau's only songbird. The plateau doesn't know it.

## The Thing That Is Not There

There is something else on the plateau. Something that does not appear in any catalogue or survey report because it cannot be photographed or measured or reliably observed. The technicians talk about it in the way that people talk about things they have not directly experienced: carefully, with qualifying language, with the unspoken agreement that what they are describing might be real and might be a shared hallucination induced by altitude and noise and isolation, and that either possibility is equally disturbing.

They call it the *pulse*.

It happens irregularly—once or twice a season, never at a predictable interval, never lasting more than a few seconds. The sound, which is continuous and variable but always within a known range, drops suddenly to a frequency below the audible spectrum. Not a cessation—the sound is still there, the vibration still runs through the ground and the water and the tissue and the steel of the stations—but the audible component vanishes, and for two or three seconds, there is a silence more absolute than any silence the plateau has ever produced.

And in that silence, the technicians report a *presence*. Not a sight, not a sound, not a smell—a spatial awareness, a conviction that something vast is paying attention. Not to them specifically. Not to anything specifically. Just: *attention*. The mass, in its four-hundred-square-kilometer entirety, momentarily oriented toward a single point, the way a sleeping animal might lift its head and open one eye and then close it again and return to its slow breath.

The moment passes. The sound returns. The plateau's normal respiration resumes. And the technicians look at each other across the shed and do not say anything because there is nothing to say, and because the sound has already covered whatever they might have tried to articulate.

Margai has felt it four times. She does not talk about it. When asked, she says the plateau is a living system and living systems sometimes do inexplicable things, and that the feeling of presence is probably a neurological artifact of the sudden sensory deprivation—the brain, accustomed to constant acoustic stimulation, generating a phantom sensation to fill the gap. She says this clearly and precisely, because she is a hydrologist and not a mystic, and because the explanation is reasonable and probably correct.

But she has also changed her walking routes. She no longer goes to the center of the plateau, the deep tissue, the place where the sound is most intense. She tells herself this is because the routes near Station 11 are sufficient for her monitoring duties. She does not examine the reason too closely. She has learned, on the plateau, that understanding is not always the same as knowing, and that some things exist in the space between what the mass is doing and what she is feeling, and that this space is not empty.

## What Lives Here, In Sum

The mass. Four hundred square kilometers of living tissue, growing, filtering, breathing, digesting itself, becoming more fully itself with each passing century. It is the plateau and the plateau is it, and everything else that lives here lives in it or on it or because of it.

The midges. The warblers. The simple equations of survival at the edge of what biology permits.

The technicians, temporary and permanent, who have come to extract value from the plateau's water and have found, some of them, that they are being extracted in return—slowly, gently, without force or intention, drawn into the plateau's rhythm, shaped by its pressure, grown around like a stone in the tissue, accommodated.

The walkers, the ones who stayed, who have given up everything the lowlands considers essential and have received in return the one thing the lowlands cannot provide: a continuous, inescapable, deafening companionship. They are the plateau's true residents, if residence means not just occupying a place but being occupied by it.

And the pulse—perhaps. The thing that might be nothing, the attention that might be a phantom, the momentary orientation of a vast living system toward some stimulus that no instrument has ever detected. If it exists—and Margai, the rationalist, the hydrologist, the woman who is not a mystic, will not say it doesn't—then it is the closest thing the plateau has to a self. A moment of awareness. A flash of something that might be consciousness or might be the mechanical equivalent, indistinguishable from the outside, existing for two seconds every few months and then vanishing back into the continuous roar of respiration and filtration and growth.

It lives here. All of it lives here. Even the steel and the diesel and the pipes are here, are part of the plateau now, are being grown around and incorporated, are becoming features of the landscape as surely as the fracture zones and the drainage incipients. The plateau does not distinguish between natural and artificial. It does not distinguish between living and dead. It grows over everything, filters everything, digests everything, makes everything part of its own continuous body.

This is what lives here: one thing, and everything that one thing has touched, and everything that has touched it in return.
