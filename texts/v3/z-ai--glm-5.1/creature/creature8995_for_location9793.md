# The People of Gharhet

They are thin.

This is what you notice first, if you come from the lowlands. Not gaunt—there is food enough, grain and preserved meat and the hard biscuit that the freight elevators bring in quantities sufficient for survival—but thin the way a blade is thin, drawn out, the soft parts burned away by the altitude and the labor and the air that gives less than it should. Their faces narrow over time. Their cheekbones rise. The wind in the upper galleries and the fumes in the lower ones and the eternal cold work on them like acid on metal, etching them into gaunter versions of whoever they were before they came.

Their skin changes. New arrivals look soft, pink, ordinary. Within months the mineral air has dried them, tightened them, left them with a fine scaling on their hands and the insides of their elbows that is not quite eczema and not quite callus but something in between, a weathering that comes from the outside in. The old-timers—the ones who have been here five years, ten, longer—have skin like old leather left in salt, creased and pallid, touched with the same mineral hues as the sifters if you look closely. Rust-grey at the temples. A faint greenish tinge around the fingernails where the copper in the air deposits itself in tiny imperceptible amounts, day after day, year after year, until the assay office stops testing for it because the baseline has shifted and everyone reads positive and it no longer means anything.

Their lungs are not what they were. This is not said. This is known. The fumes that pool in the cleft on still days leave a taste in the back of the throat that does not entirely go away, and the miners who work the upper drifts breathe rock dust no matter how well the ventilation works, and over time the chest gets tight, the cough gets persistent, and the company provides a stipend for respiratory complaints that is accepted without comment because what else is there to do? The mountain gives and the mountain takes and the ledger balances in its own time.

---

They do not stand the way lowlanders stand.

This is subtle. You would not notice unless you watched them for a while, unless you stood in the Throat and observed them moving through their day. Their posture is not upright—not because of any deformity but because Gharhet does not reward uprightness. The ceilings are low. The corridors lean. The stairways are steep and narrow and angled in ways that accommodate the rock rather than the human body. The people here carry themselves at a slight tilt, a perpetual accommodation to spaces that were never built for comfort, that were built into stone that does not care about comfort. They move with a kind of efficient hunch, a compression of the spine that looks almost like readiness, like a body prepared to duck or turn or press itself flat against a wall at a moment's notice.

And they drift. Not wander—drift. A specific motion that comes from living on floors that slope, on stairs that tilt, on surfaces that are never quite level. Their center of gravity sits lower than yours. They have an unconscious awareness of where the floor is *today*, not where it was yesterday, and they adjust their weight before they think about it, the way a sailor adjusts to the roll of a ship. Put a Gharhet native on flat ground in the lowlands and they will sway for the first few hours, unmoored, waiting for the ground to shift beneath them.

Their hands are strong. Obvious, perhaps, for miners—but it is not just the grip strength or the calluses, both of which are considerable. It is the way they touch surfaces. They run their hands along walls as they walk, not for balance but for information. The stone tells them things. The temperature of it. The texture. The faint vibrations that the fans and the hoists and the mountain itself send thrumming through the rock. A Gharhet hand on a wall is an ear pressed to a door, listening to what the mountain is doing now, what it might do next.

---

They are quiet people.

Not sullen. Not shy. Quiet the way a place is quiet when sound does not carry well, when speech is effort, when the machinery drones loud enough that you learn to say what you mean in fewer words or say nothing at all. They have a sign language—not formal, not codified, just a set of gestures that evolved in the tunnels where the noise of the drills made speech impossible and the helmet lamps could signal things words could not. Two taps on the shoulder means *watch your footing*. A flat hand held up means *hold position*. A fist pressed to the chest means *air's going bad*. These signals are not taught so much as absorbed, the way a child absorbs language, and by the time a worker has been in the mines for a month they are using them without thinking.

When they do speak, their voices are low. Carrying is rude. A voice that projects is a voice that fills the cleft, that bounces off the close walls and intrudes on spaces where other people are trying not to hear. The residents of Gharhet have developed a mode of speech that is almost murmured, pitched to travel only as far as the person across from you and no further. It takes practice. Visitors shout and startle everyone within earshot, and the residents look at them with that particular expression that is not quite judgment and not quite pity but something in between, the look you give someone who has not yet learned the rules of a place.

They have names for the sounds the mountain makes. Not scientific names—those belong to the assay office and the engineers—but the vernacular names, the names that come from living with a thing until it becomes a character in your life. The *long groan* is the rock settling in the evening as it cools. The *sharp crack* is a new fracture, which may or may not mean something, which usually means nothing, which sometimes means the ceiling of a drift has decided to lower itself by a few centimeters. The *hum* is the cables in their housings, and it is the sound of normalcy, and when it stops everyone in earshot stops what they are doing and waits, because silence in Gharhet is not peace. Silence in Gharhet is the sound of the fans failing, and the fans failing is the sound of the air going bad, and the air going bad is the sound of a problem that can kill everyone in the cleft before the elevators can bring them up.

The hum is a lullaby. They sleep to it. They have always slept to it. When they leave—and some do leave, eventually, when the lungs go bad or the savings account hits a number that feels like enough—the thing that keeps them awake in the lowlands is not the openness or the water or the sky. It is the quiet. The terrible, airless quiet of a place that does not breathe.

---

They have a relationship with the sifters that is not quite respect and not quite tolerance and not quite anything you could name.

The miners will not kill them. This is the superstition the visitors hear about and dismiss. But it is not a superstition in the way lowlanders mean the word—not a belief in luck or spirits or the evil eye. It is a practical observation that has been elevated to principle through repetition. The sifters go where the air is good. When the sifters leave a gallery, the air in that gallery goes bad within hours—higher carbon dioxide, lower oxygen, a quality of staleness that the sensors confirm even if your lungs do not. When the sifters cluster somewhere, the air there is breathable. The miners follow the sifters the way sailors follow seabirds: not because the birds know the way to land, but because the birds go where the food is, and where the food is, the living is possible.

So they do not kill the sifters. They brush them off surfaces when they need to work there, and the sifters crawl away and return later, and this is the arrangement. The sifters get the warmth of the pipes and the mineral traces in the condensate and whatever it is they extract from the slag and the stone. The miners get a canary in the coal mine that does not die, that simply leaves, that signals danger by its absence rather than its death. It is, in its way, a kind of companionship. Not affectionate. Not acknowledged. But consistent.

The children catch them sometimes. The sifters, not the cave-dwellers—no one touches the cave-dwellers, who are left alone in their darknesses with the same superstitious-practical respect. But the sifters are small and harmless and strange, and children in confined spaces will find fascination where they can. They keep them in jars for a day or two, watching the mandibles work, the chitinous plates shift, the legs move in their complicated mechanical way. And then they let them go, because a sifter in a jar too long is a sifter that dies, and a dead sifter hardens and sticks and is difficult to clean, and because—even if no one says it—killing them feels wrong in a way that has nothing to do with superstition and everything to do with the recognition of a neighbor.

The sifters and the people of Gharhet occupy the same space. They eat different things. They breathe different air. They live by different clocks. But they are both here, both drawn to the same unlikely niche, both persisting in a place that does not want them, and there is a kind of kinship in that, if kinship is the right word, and it probably is not, but there is something.

---

There are children in Gharhet.

This surprises some visitors, who assume that a place like this would be populated only by the desperate and the transient, only by those who came for the wages and left when the wages were enough. And there are those people—there are always those people, rotating through the lower galleries and the smelter shifts and the most dangerous drifts, staying a season or two and then taking the freight elevator down and not coming back. But there are also the ones who stay. The ones who meet someone in the Throat and move into their alcove and gradually accumulate enough belongings between them to fill a shelf, and then a corner, and then a small room with a door that locks and a vent that works and a view of nothing but the opposite wall, which is enough.

The children are born in the clinic on the fourth ledge, which is the only room in Gharhet with windows—two of them, cut into the cliff face, looking out at nothing but rock but letting in a few extra minutes of the attenuated daylight. They are born small, as if already preparing for the thin air and the narrow spaces. They grow slowly in the first years, and then they catch up, and by the time they are old enough to work they are indistinguishable from the children of lowlanders, except for the tilt of their posture and the pallor of their skin and the way they run their hands along walls.

They play in the corridors, because there is nowhere else to play. They invent games that require no space—guessing games, clapping games, games of balance on the sloped floors, games of stillness and silence where the winner is whoever can go longest without being heard. They climb the ladders and the scaffolding with an ease that terrifies their parents, who remember the children who fell, who are always conscious of the edges and the drops and the long way down from every ledge and bridge and platform in the cleft. The children do not think about falling. The children think about climbing, and they climb, and most of them survive.

They go to school in the assay office, which is the largest enclosed space in Gharhet and therefore the only place where a group of twenty children can sit without pressing against the walls. They learn mathematics and geology and the basics of reading and writing, and they learn the particular knowledge of this place: how to read the air by the behavior of the sifters, how to tell the sound of a new fracture from the sound of an old one settling, how to move on a floor that is not flat and how to find water in the drums and how to conserve it and how to make it last. They learn what the mountain does and what the machinery does and what the sifters do and what to do when any of those things change.

Some of them leave. Some of them stay. The ones who stay become the old-timers, the ones with the deeply lined faces and the mineral-stained skin and the low voices and the hands that touch walls as if reading them. The ones who leave go down the freight elevator with a pack and a set of savings and a body that will take months to adjust to air that is thick and wet and full of oxygen, and some of them come back, and some of them do not, and the ones who do not are not spoken of often, because what is there to say about someone who chose the flat and the open and the sky that goes all the way around, when you yourself have chosen the cleft?

---

They do not think of Gharhet as beautiful.

This is important. Visitors sometimes do—visitors see the drama of it, the verticality, the way the buildings cling to the rock like something tenacious and desperate and alive, and they call it striking or extraordinary or brutal, and they are not wrong, exactly, but they are missing the point. The people who live here do not see the drama. They see the cracked pipe that needs replacing and the slope in the floor that has gotten worse and the way the sifters are clustering thicker than usual around the condensers, which might mean a change in the air quality or might mean nothing, and they do not have the luxury of aesthetics.

And yet.

And yet there is something in the way they talk about the cleft when they have been away from it. Something in the way they describe the sound the cables make at night, or the quality of the light when the sun passes over the slit and falls down through the steam and the dust like a shaft through a cathedral, or the way the mountain exhales in the evening, a long slow breath of cold air that you can feel against your face if you stand in the Throat at the right hour. They do not call it beautiful. They do not call it home, exactly—home is too warm a word for a place like this. But they call it *here*, with an emphasis that a lowlander would not use, as if *here* is a statement of fact so fundamental that it does not need embellishment.

They are here. They stay here. They endure here. Not because they love it and not because they are trapped and not because they have no other option, though some of them love it and some of them are trapped and some of them have no other option. They stay because the mountain is still giving up its ore and the machinery is still running and the sifters are still crawling and the air is still—most days, most hours, most minutes—breathable, and because they have learned how to live in a place that does not want them, and because there is a kind of satisfaction in that which has no word in any language, the satisfaction of persisting in the teeth of something, of making a life in a cleft, of being small and stubborn and alive in a place that is none of those things.

At night, if you stand in the Throat and look up, you can see stars through the slit. Just a narrow band of them, a slice of the universe that the mountains have allowed you, and the air is cold and the machinery hums and somewhere below you the smelter glows and the sifters click and the mountain shifts by another millimeter, and you are here, and you are still here, and you will be here tomorrow, because what else would you be?
