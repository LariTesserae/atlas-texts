# Who Lives in the High Fabric

---

The people of the middle layers do not have a name for themselves. This is not because they lack language or self-awareness; it is because the question has never seemed necessary. They are here. The Fabric is here. Other people come and go — traders from the lowlands, scholars from the coastal academies, occasionally someone who arrives with instruments and measuring tools and a look of desperate concentration, someone who is trying to *understand* — but the people who live here simply live here, and when they refer to themselves, which is rarely, they use the word that means *us* or *the ones who stay*, and this has always been sufficient.

There are perhaps nine thousand of them. The number is uncertain because they do not count themselves, and because the boundaries of the Fabric are themselves uncertain, and because some of them spend part of the year in the higher or lower layers and part of it in the middle band, and because some of them live on their own in remote terraces and are seen only when they choose to come to the gathering places, and because there are always, among them, a few who are not entirely sure they belong, who came from somewhere else and have stayed for a season or a decade or a lifetime and who may stay for longer or may leave, and the people of the Fabric include them in *us* without question or comment, because to stay is to be one who stays, and this is enough.

---

They are not primitive. This is important to understand, though the scholars from the coastal academies often fail to understand it. The people of the Fabric have writing — a script derived from, or possibly ancestral to, the maintenance notation found carved into the walls of the lower works. They have mathematics, though it is a mathematics of ratios and cycles rather than of abstract quantities. They have medicine, botany, meteorology, engineering — all of it oriented toward the practical business of living in a structure they did not build and cannot fully control but have learned, over generations, to read. They know that a particular vibration in the floor of a particular terrace means the pump station in Section 17 is operating outside its normal tolerance. They know that the flowering of a certain vine two terraces above the norm signals a shift in the thermal vents below. They know which lift shafts will fail and when, because the schedules of failure are as regular and predictable as the weather, and they have been tracking both for longer than their oral history reaches.

What they do not have is origin stories. Or rather — they have them, the way they have everything the Fabric provides, which is to say they found them. In the deep archive rooms, in the lower-middle layers where the climate is drier and the air tastes of old paper and oxidized copper, there are storerooms filled with records. Not the maintenance logs, which are everywhere, carved into walls and stamped into plates and etched into pipe housings — those are practical documents, still legible, still useful. The archive rooms contain other things: diagrams that might be maps or might be philosophical proofs, illustrations of figures in garments that no one has ever worn, texts in languages that no one speaks but that share cognates with the terms still used in the maintenance notation. The people of the Fabric go to the archive rooms sometimes, the way you might go to a cathedral — not for answers, but for the experience of standing in the presence of something vast and deliberate and incomprehensible. The children are taken there when they are old enough to be trusted not to damage anything. They touch the old pages. They trace the strange letters. They come away with the understanding that there was a before, and that the before was different, and that the before made the now, and that this is all that can be known about it.

This does not trouble them.

---

Their agriculture is the agriculture of the terrace. The built-soil of the Fabric is deep in places — metres deep, the accumulated compost of centuries — and it grows things that grow nowhere else. There is a tuber they call *ash-root* that forms in the soil above the thermal vents, where the ground is warm to the touch year-round. It is starchy and faintly sweet, and its skin is the grey-green colour of lichen on iron, and it grows in knots and clusters that follow the shape of the pipes below, as if it were listening to the infrastructure and shaping itself accordingly. There is a leaf-crop they call *broad-drape* that grows on the railing-edges of the upper middle terraces, where the condensation is heaviest; its leaves are the size of a stretched hand, and they curl inward at the edges to channel water toward the stem, and the people harvest them by cutting the older outer leaves and leaving the inner growth to continue, and a single plant will produce for decades if treated this way. There is a climbing legume that they do not name because it does not need naming — it is simply *the bean* — and it grows on every available vertical surface, climbing the lattice and the pipe housings and the abandoned scaffolding with an enthusiasm that suggests it, too, was designed for exactly this purpose, which it may have been.

They keep insects. Not bees — the bees of the Fabric are wild, living in the walls and the shafts and the hollow spaces between structural layers, and they are ferocious and territorial and their honey is dark and bitter and intoxicating. The insects the people keep are a species of flightless beetle, the size of a thumbnail, glossy and black, that lives in colonies of thousands and processes organic waste into a rich, dark substrate that can be mixed with the built-soil to improve its yield. The people keep them in woven baskets lined with the leaves of a particular moss, and they feed them the inedible portions of the harvest, and the colonies hum at a frequency just below hearing, and the people say that a healthy beetle-basket sounds like the lower works from very far away.

They do not keep the monkeys. No one keeps the monkeys. The monkeys keep themselves.

---

The monkeys are the truest inhabitants of the Fabric, because the Fabric was clearly made for them, or they were clearly made for the Fabric, and the people who live among them have never been able to determine which, and the monkeys have never offered clarification.

There are three species. The largest is a langur-type, silver-furred and black-faced, that moves through the upper lattice in troops of fifteen to thirty. They are the lift-riders — the ones who know the schedules of the cargo lifts and use them to travel between vertical levels. They board with confidence and exit with purpose, and they sometimes ride for dozens of sections before disembarking at a particular terrace that they appear to have selected in advance, and no one has ever been able to determine what they do there, or why that terrace and not another. They are not hostile, but they are not friendly. They tolerate the presence of people the way the Fabric tolerates the presence of vegetation — as something that is simply here, that has always been here, that is part of the total system without being central to it.

The second species is smaller, reddish-brown, intensely social. They live in the middle layers, close to the human settlements, and they have a relationship with people that is more like negotiation than domestication. They will approach the terrace gardens and take fruit, and the people will chase them away, and they will return, and the people will chase them again, and this appears to be a ritual that both sides enjoy. They are vocal — a constant chatter of clicks and whistles and trills that runs beneath the ambient sound of the Fabric like a second mechanical system. They groom each other on the warm copper sheathing of the external walls, and they sleep in the ventilation shafts, and they have been observed opening latches and turning handles that no one taught them to open or turn.

The third species is the one that the people do not talk about readily. It is the smallest — the size of a cat, perhaps — and it is solitary, and it lives in the deep interior of the Fabric, in the spaces between walls, in the regions where the structural layers are so dense that the external light never reaches. It is pale, almost white, and its eyes are very large and very dark, and it is silent in a way that suggests it is listening, always listening, to something that no one else can hear. The people have a name for it that translates roughly as *the one who stays inside*, and they say that it knows the Fabric the way the Fabric knows itself — from the inside, from the structure, from the slow turning of the deep engines outward. Sometimes a worker in the lower-middle levels will find one watching from a gap in the wall, and it will hold their gaze for a long moment, and then it will withdraw into the darkness, and the worker will go home and not mention it, because what is there to mention? It was there. It is always there. It was there before the people came, and it will be there after they are gone.

---

There are things in the lower works.

The people do not go there, except occasionally, in carefully organized expeditions, when a pump station needs attention that cannot be provided by the automated maintenance systems — which still function, after centuries, in the way that a river still functions: not because anyone maintains it, but because the conditions for its functioning are intrinsic to the structure of the landscape. The automated systems are mechanical. They are not intelligent. They do not think. But they respond to conditions — blockages, pressure fluctuations, temperature anomalies — with a specificity and a purposefulness that, from the outside, is difficult to distinguish from thought.

The expeditions return with stories. The lower works are hotter than the middle layers, and the air is thicker, and it tastes of iron and oil and something else — something organic, something alive. The pipes sweat. The walls are slick with moisture and a biofilm that glows faintly in the dark, a pale blue-green luminescence that the expeditions' members describe as beautiful and deeply wrong. They describe sounds: the deep subsonic pulse of the engines, the hiss of steam, the clank of metal on metal, and underneath it all, a wetness, a dripping, a breathing, as if the lower works are not merely warm but *metabolizing*.

They describe the maintenance systems. The automated mechanisms that clear the pipes and adjust the valves and replace the worn components. They are not robots in any recognizable sense; they are more like the internal mechanisms of a body, adapted to specific tasks with a specificity that is almost surgical. There is a device that lives in the main water channel, and it is the size of a small boat, and it moves through the channel at a pace that suggests it has been moving since the channel was first filled, and it removes debris and mineral buildup and the occasional organism that has wandered into the flow, and it has been doing this for so long that a species of aquatic snail has evolved to live on its hull, and the device does not seem to notice or mind.

There are other devices. The people who have seen them do not describe them in detail, not because they are forbidden but because the description always comes out wrong, as if the devices occupy a category that language was not built to accommodate. They are not machines. They are not animals. They are something that does the work that a body does for itself — maintenance, repair, regulation — and they have been doing it for so long that they have become part of the ecology of the lower works, and the ecology has become part of them.

The people do not worship them. The people do not fear them. The people accept them the way you accept your own organs — as something that is inside you, that keeps you alive, that you do not need to understand because understanding is not the relationship you have with it.

---

The birds are the voice of the Fabric.

The swifts, the small dark ones that nest in the ventilation shafts — they are everywhere, and their sound is everywhere, and it is the sound that the people of the Fabric were born into, the sound that means *morning* and *evening* and *warning* and *home*. The swifts hunt the flying insects that breed in the standing water of the upper cisterns, and they do so in clouds of hundreds, and the air in the upper layers shimmers with their movement, and their calls are a high thin constant music that weaves between the mechanical sounds and the organic sounds and makes, somehow, a single thing of it — a soundscape that is not natural and not industrial and not human but something else, something that belongs to the Fabric alone.

There are larger birds, too. Raptors — a species of kite, rust-red and black-winged — that ride the thermal columns rising from the lower works and hunt the swifts and the lizards and the smaller monkeys. They nest at the very top of the lattice, where the structure thins and the wind is constant, and their cries are sharp and solitary and carry across the Fabric like the sound of a machine cutting metal. The people regard them with respect. The people regard everything here with respect, but the kites are respected the way you respect a tool that is sharper than you are: it is not malicious, but it is dangerous in its precision.

And there are the night birds. The people do not see them often. They are large and pale and nearly silent, and they emerge from the deeper interior of the structure after dark, when the condensation begins and the Fabric weeps, and they hunt by a sense that no one has been able to identify — not sight, not sound, not the heat-sense of some desert predators, but something else, something that seems to be attuned to the Fabric itself, to the vibrations and the moisture and the slow respiratory pulse of the whole immense body. The people call them *the listeners*, and they say that the listeners hear the Fabric dreaming, and they hunt in the dreams.

This is not meant literally. Probably.

---

There are stories. The people of the Fabric tell stories the way the Fabric grows things — incrementally, layer on layer, each generation adding its own details to a structure that was never designed by any single teller and that belongs to no one.

There is a story about a woman who went into the deep archive rooms and found a room that was not on any map, and in the room there was a device, and the device was running, and it had been running since before the archive was built, and when she touched it, it stopped. She did not know what it had been doing. She did not know what she had stopped. She left the room and closed the door and went home and lived for many more years, and nothing happened, or nothing that she could trace to that moment. But she always felt, after that, that the Fabric was slightly different — that the rhythm of the pumps had shifted, that the warmth from below had a different quality, that the condensation fell at different times. She could not say how. She could not say whether it was worse or better. She could only say that it had changed, and that she had changed it, and that this was what it meant to live inside something that was alive.

There is a story about the first people. Not the builders — the builders are as remote as geology, as abstract as physics. The first *people*. The ones who came after the building stopped and the growing started. The story says they were engineers, or their children were engineers, and they stayed because there was still work to do, and the work became maintenance, and the maintenance became habitation, and the habitation became belonging, and by the time they realized they were no longer maintaining a structure but living inside an organism, it was too late to leave, or too early, or simply unnecessary. The story does not have a moral. The story is a description.

There is a story about the pale monkeys of the deep interior. The story says they were once people. The story says they went into the lower works and stayed, and the lower works changed them, and they became something that was no longer human but was not entirely other, and they still know the way out, and they choose not to take it. The story says they are waiting for something. The story says they have always been waiting. The story says that waiting is what they *are*.

The people of the Fabric do not believe these stories literally. They do not *disbelieve* them, either. The stories are part of the ecology of understanding, and you do not pull a story out by its roots to see if it is real any more than you would pull a fern out of the lattice to see if it belongs there. It is there. It grows. It holds moisture. It provides habitat. This is what a story is for.

---

There is a child.

She is seven years old, or eight — the people of the Fabric do not count years with precision, because the seasons here are the seasons of the machinery, and the years are the cycles of the pumps, and a childhood is simply the time before you are big enough to work the terrace gardens on your own. She lives on a terrace in the middle layers, on the warm side, where the morning condensation falls heaviest and the moss on the railing is thick enough to use as a pillow. She lives with her mother and her mother's mother and a man who may or may not be her father and two younger siblings who may or may not be full siblings, because the people of the Fabric do not organize themselves into nuclear families and have never seen the need.

She does not know who built the Fabric. She has never asked. This is not because the question does not occur to her — children are full of questions — but because the question, when she considers it, feels like asking who built the rain. Someone must have, once. The clouds were made, the pipes were laid, the engines were started. But the making was so long ago, and the thing that was made has become so entirely itself, that the question seems to belong to a different kind of thinking — the kind of thinking that the scholars from the coastal academies bring with them, the kind that makes maps and diagrams and always needs to know *why*.

She knows other things. She knows that the lift on Section 23 makes a sound like a cough when it is almost at the top, and that this means it will need grease within the month. She knows that the broad-drape leaves on the terrace above hers are ready to harvest when their edges curl twice instead of once. She knows that the pale monkey of the deep interior will sometimes come to the edge of the wall-gap at twilight and watch the terrace gardens, and that if you leave a piece of ash-root near the gap, it will be gone in the morning, and this is not a gift and not a trade and not a test but simply a thing that happens, the way the condensation happens, the way the warmth rises from below.

She knows the sound of the Fabric breathing. Everyone knows this sound. It is the sound of the engines turning and the pumps cycling and the steam releasing and the water dripping and the wind moving through the lattice and the birds calling and the monkeys chattering and the insects humming and the people talking and the children laughing and the whole vast structure — built and overgrown and corroding and growing and repairing and decaying and becoming — making, in its totality, a single sound that is not any of these sounds but the sum of them, the way a body makes a single sound that is not the heart or the lungs or the blood but the life.

She falls asleep to this sound. She has never known any other.

---

What lives in the High Fabric?

Everything that can live in a place that was made and became unmade and became something else. Everything that finds purchase on iron and thrives in condensation and adapts to the rhythm of machines and learns to read the language of vibrations and grows toward the light that filters through steam and cloud and the exhaust of distant stacks. Everything that does not ask whether it belongs here, because belonging is not a question the Fabric asks. The Fabric is. What lives in it, lives. What dies in it, decays. What decays in it, becomes substrate for what will grow next.

The people are not the inhabitants of the Fabric. They are an inhabitant — one species among millions, one layer among thousands, one sound in the chorus. They know this. This is what the scholars from the coastal academies cannot understand, and what the people of the Fabric cannot explain: that the Fabric is not a building. It has not been a building for centuries. It is an ecology, a metabolism, a body — not a human body, not an animal body, but a body in the only sense that matters: a thing that maintains itself, that responds to conditions, that breathes and digests and grows and heals and does not need to understand itself in order to be.

What lives in the High Fabric lives the way everything lives in a body: as part of it, contained by it, sustained by it, and eventually returned to it.

The rust spreads a little further. The moss thickens. The engines turn. The condensation falls.

What lives here is the Fabric, and the Fabric is alive.
