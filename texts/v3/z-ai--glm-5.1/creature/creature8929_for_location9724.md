# The Inhabitants

---

The things that live here do not live here.

This is the first contradiction, and it is not a contradiction at all—only a failure of language prepared for older worlds. The plateau's inhabitants do not inhabit the way a creature inhabits a burrow or a bird inhabits a branch. They are not *in* the landscape the way a stone is in a river. They are *of* it. They emerged from the same processes, attend the same cycling, and will—perhaps—dissolve back into the same industrial substrate when whatever passes for their lifecycle concludes. They do not occupy space on the plateau. They are space the plateau has temporarily allocated to mobility.

This is why they shift. This is why they flicker at the margins of observation. They are not changing from one form to another. They are *expressions of the same process* that has not yet committed to a single output format. The creature drinking at the hot pool and its changing reflection are not animal and image. They are two nodes of the same procedural event, and your eyes—which expect consistency, which demand that a thing be one thing—are insufficient to the task of watching something be several things in the same moment.

---

## The Sitters

You encounter them first in the quieter zones, where the canopy thickens and the industrial processes run deeper and cooler. They sit. That is what they do. That is nearly all they do.

The Sitters are roughly the size and proportion of large dogs, but the comparison fails immediately because they have no fixed proportion. They are *sitting shapes*—forms that have solved for the problem of remaining still on a surface. Four points of contact, usually. A low center of mass. A body that seems to have been extruded downward from a central mass, pooling against the warm stone like a thick liquid that found its level and stopped. Their surfaces are the color of the immediate environment: not camouflage, not in the strategic sense, but *material continuity*. They are made of what they sit on. The ones on lichen-covered rock are lichen-colored and slightly fuzzy. The ones on bare stone are grey and smooth. The ones in the leaf litter seem to be leaf litter arranged in a sitting configuration, and perhaps they are.

They do not eat. They do not sleep. They do not communicate in any way you can detect. They sit, and in sitting, they *attend*. You realize this slowly, over days of observation: the Sitters are positioned at the junction points of the plateau's industrial systems. Where a mineral pipe surfaces. Where a thermal vent cycles. Where a fault is propagating. They sit at these locations the way a gauge sits on a machine—not performing the work, but *present at the work*, registering something by their presence.

When a thermal vent changes rhythm—and you learn to feel this, the subtle shift in the pulse of the rock—the nearest Sitter will change too. Its surface ripples. Its sitting shape redistributes, slightly lower, slightly wider, as though it has received information and is processing it through posture. Then it settles. And the vent, often, will adjust. Not always. But often enough to suggest that the Sitter is not merely attending the process. The Sitter is *part of the control system*.

Are they alive? The question is a category error here. They are *functional*. They are features of a self-regulating landscape that has grown its own monitoring instruments. The plateau does not have a brain—brains are solutions for organisms that need to be small and mobile—but it has Sitters. Distributed. Passive. Patient. Receiving and damping and feeding back, one sitting shape at a time, across the vast dim expanse of its surface.

You walk past a row of them along a fissure edge—seven, eight, nine sitting shapes, evenly spaced, each one slightly different in configuration, each one facing the fissure, each one absolutely still—and you feel as though you are walking past the control panel of something enormous and largely invisible. You are. You are walking past the control panel of the ground beneath your feet.

## The Columns

They move through the vegetation in lines.

The Columns are what you first mistook for the insect-analogues, the supply-line processions carrying material between collection points. But the Columns are not insects. The Columns are not carrying anything. The Columns *are* the material.

A Column is a procession of small, roughly cylindrical bodies—each one perhaps the size of a thumb, each one moving on a variable number of leg-like projections that seem to form and dissolve as needed—flowing through the undergrowth in a continuous stream. Hundreds of them. Thousands. The line extends forward and backward beyond visibility, a river of tiny bodies moving with the synchronization of a single organism, because that is possibly what they are. Not a colony. Not a swarm. A *linear distributed entity* that happens to be expressed in discrete segments.

When a Column encounters an obstacle—a fallen pseudo-tree, a hot pool, your boot—it splits. The procession bifurcates, flows around the impediment on both sides, and merges again on the far side with a fluidity that suggests the segments are not individuals but *units of flow*, the way water molecules are units of a stream. You could scoop up a handful of the segments and move them elsewhere and the Column would adjust. It might even send a tributary toward wherever you placed them, incorporating the displaced segments back into the flow. The Column is a pattern, not a collection of things. The pattern *wants* to exist. The pattern will reconstitute itself around interruptions the way a wound heals.

They move between the industrial features of the plateau. They flow from fissure to pool, from pool to vent, from vent to the root-mats of the processing vegetation. They are not transporting material; they are *exchanging* it. Each segment absorbs something at one location—heat, moisture, mineral compounds, biological product—and releases it at the next. The Column is a circulatory system. The plateau has grown its own blood, and the blood walks.

At night—or the dimmer approximation of night—the Columns glow. Faintly. A low bioluminescence that tracks along their paths like flight guidance lights, marking the routes of exchange in the darkness. You lie in your bivouac and watch the lines of light move through the vegetation—converging, diverging, merging, splitting—and you understand that you are watching the plateau's metabolism visible for the first time. The industry below ground has its mechanical processes: the gears, the vents, the mineral pipes. The industry above ground has the Columns. Same system. Different organs.

Do the segments have internal experience? Do they know they are parts of something larger? You pick one up—gently—and it continues walking in the air, legs cycling, body warm and slightly yielding, like holding a single word of a sentence and feeling it try to make meaning on its own. You put it down. It reorients. It finds the Column. It flows. It was never not flowing.

## The Mimes

These are the ones that shift.

You see them first as movements at the edge of peripheral vision—quick, darting, not quite the motion of any animal you know. When you turn, you see nothing. When you turn back, you see something that was not there before: a shape in the vegetation, approximately the size and configuration of whatever you were just looking at. A plant that looks like the plant beside it, but slightly *off*. A rock that has the color of adjacent rock but the wrong texture. A shadow that has no source but persists anyway.

The Mimes are the plateau's most indeterminate inhabitants, and they are the most direct evidence that reality here has not yet hardened into consistency. A Mime is not a shape-shifter in the dramatic sense. It does not transform from one creature to another with fluid cinematic grace. A Mime *flickers* between possible forms the way a damaged video file flickers between frames—and for the same reason. The data is not yet committed. The resolution has not yet been selected. The Mime exists in a state of persistent optionality.

You watch one for an hour from behind a moss-covered boulder. It is in a small clearing near a hot pool, and it is doing something that might be feeding, or might be bathing, or might be performing some third function for which no verb exists. As you watch, its form cycles:

A quadruped, low-slung, drinking. A biped, taller, reaching into the low canopy. Something with too many limbs, splayed against the warm rock, absorbing heat. A thing like a large insect, clicking and mandibled, processing vegetation. And then back to the quadruped, and then something you cannot name—a configuration of limbs and orifices and surfaces that seems to exist for a half-second before the Mime moves on, leaving the shape behind like a shed skin of possibility.

It does not seem distressed. It does not seem aware of its own shifting. The Mime moves through its forms the way a thought moves through associations—not deciding, just *proceeding*, following whatever logic of adjacency the plateau's processes dictate at that moment. It is at the pool because the pool is where things gather. It is shifting because the pool is a junction point, and at junction points, the plateau's reality is more fluid, and the Mime—whoever it is, whatever it is—is taking advantage of the looseness.

Or perhaps *being taken advantage of* by the looseness. Perhaps the Mime does not choose its forms. Perhaps the forms choose the Mime—the plateau cycling through biological possibilities the way it cycles heat and water, trying configurations, discarding them, moving on, the Mime merely the local substrate where this experimentation becomes visible.

There are many Mimes. They cluster at the industrial features—fissures, vents, pools—where the reality is thinnest. They avoid the quieter zones, where the Sitters hold things stable. They are, you realize, the *research and development division* of the plateau's ecology. The plateau does not have DNA, does not have evolution in the Darwinian sense. It has Mimes. It has spaces where the forms are loose, and it has creatures that move through those loose spaces trying on biological solutions, and the ones that work—whatever "work" means in a system without fixed goals—those configurations propagate. The ones that don't dissolve. The Mime moves on.

You wonder how many current plateau species began as Mime configurations. You wonder how many *will*. You wonder, watching a Mime cycle through seven forms in ten minutes, whether there is a form it hasn't tried yet—a form that will *stick*, that will resolve, that will become the first stable species of a new ecological order. Or whether the Mime itself is the stable form: a creature whose function is to be unstable, whose ecological niche is *indeterminacy itself*, and whose persistence is proof that the plateau needs its loose ends, needs its unresolved spaces, needs things that do not commit in order to keep the system's options open.

The Mime settles—for a moment—into something almost canid. It looks at you. Its eyes, in this configuration, are forward-facing, amber, approximately the eyes of a predator assessing threat. Then it shifts again. The eyes change position, number, configuration. The assessment, if it was an assessment, continues in a form you can no longer read.

## The Throat

You find it at the center of the deepest fissure system, where the industrial processes are densest and the heat is nearly unbearable and the vegetation has given up entirely and the rock is being processed as fast as it can extrude.

The Throat is not an animal.

The Throat is not a plant.

The Throat is not a geological feature.

The Throat is a *process that has become localized*.

It occupies a chamber perhaps thirty meters across—a bubble in the fresh rock where the mineral pipes converge and the thermal vents empty and the gears are largest and the cycling is most intense. The chamber is dark, and hot, and wet, and it *pulses*. The pulse is the plateau's heartbeat, concentrated and made audible: a slow, massive rhythm that you feel in your chest cavity, in your long bones, in the fluid of your inner ear. The Throat is where the engine is loudest. The Throat is where the becoming is most *becoming*.

You cannot see the Throat's edges. It may not have edges. What you can see, in the dim bioluminescent glow of the Column segments that flow through its chamber in thick luminous streams, is a surface—interior, curved, glistening—that moves. Not the movement of flesh, not the peristalsis of a digestive tract, but the movement of *manufacture*. The surface is *producing*. It extrudes. It generates rock and mineral and heat and moisture and the chemical precursors of biological material, and it extrudes them in patterns—layered, structured, dimensional patterns that look like nothing so much as the cross-section of a 3D printer's output, if the printer were the size of a cathedral and were printing a world.

The Columns flow into the Throat's chamber and distribute what they carry—minerals from distant fissures, biological compounds from the processing vegetation, heat from the pools. They deposit these materials on the Throat's surface, and the surface *incorporates* them, and the pulse continues, and the Throat produces whatever it produces, and the products flow outward through the mineral pipes and the fissure systems and the root-channels of the vegetation, back to the surface, back to the ecology, back to the Sitters and the Mimes and the desperate growing things and the flickering fauna and the warm dim air.

The Throat is the plateau's heart. The Throat is the plateau's factory floor. The Throat is the place where the self-assembly is assembled, where the engine generates the engine, where the process processes itself.

You stand at the edge of the chamber—close enough to feel the spray of the Throat's exhalations, warm and mineral and tasting of iron and copper and something sweeter, something organic, something *new*—and you watch it work. And you realize that the Throat is not *making* the plateau. The Throat is *being* the plateau, in concentrated form. The distinction between the maker and the made does not exist here. The Throat is the ground you stand on, expressed at the point of production. It is the rock and the vegetation and the animals and the weather and the ambiguous light and the shifting reality, all of it emanating from this single point, cycling outward, processing, and eventually cycling back.

And it is *new*. The Throat is new. The plateau is new. Everything here is new, not in the sense of recently created, but in the sense of *still being created*. The Throat has not finished. It will not finish. The plateau is not a product. It is a production. The Throat does not *make* a world. It *is* the making, ongoing, continuous, without endpoint, without goal, without any external standard of completion against which it could be measured and found wanting or sufficient.

The Throat pulses. The engine runs. The world continues to become.

## The Others

There are things on the plateau that are not part of the plateau.

You sense them before you see them—or before you *almost* see them, which is the only way to see things that may not entirely be here. They are at the margins. They are at the edges where the reality is unstable, where the vegetation grows in impossible configurations and the stone shows its indecision. They are in the places where the plateau's self-assembly is *leaking*—where the processes that hold the landscape together are insufficient to prevent something else from leaking in.

The Others are not from elsewhere in any spatial sense. They are not invaders from below the escarpment, not arrivals from some distant geography. They are *from elsewhere in the possibility space*. They are what exists in the gaps when a world has not yet fully decided what it is. They are the things that *could be* here, that *almost are* here, that occupy the same conceptual territory as the plateau but have not been selected for, have not been processed, have not been incorporated into the engine's output.

You see one at the edge of a rain column, standing where the water falls and does not fall, in the boundary between wet and dry. It is a shape. It is only a shape—a silhouette with no interior, a hole in the dim light that is approximately the size and configuration of a person. It stands there. It does not move. It does not acknowledge you. It is *present* in the way that a reflection is present: derivative, contingent, dependent on the observer and the conditions of observation.

The Others do not interact with the plateau's ecology. The Columns flow around them. The Mimes avoid the places where they gather. The Sitters do not sit near them. The plateau's industrial processes do not process them. They are *outside the system*, not as opponents or alternatives, but as *unrendered data*—the background noise of a reality that is still compiling itself.

You walk toward one and it recedes. Not moving away—*distancing*, increasing the gap between your position and its position without traversing the intervening space. You stop. It stops. You raise your hand. Something that might be a hand rises in the silhouette. You lower your hand. The silhouette's hand—if it is a hand—lowers. You are being mirrored, or you are being *read*, or you are encountering the plateau's attempt to understand what you are by generating a configuration that matches your form.

The Others are mirrors. The Others are echoes. The Others are the plateau's *drafts*—its attempts to produce inhabitants that make sense within its system, running on templates provided by whatever has wandered in from outside. You are the template. You are the input that the engine is processing, and the Others are the output, the half-formed attempts at integrating your form into the plateau's logic.

This is why they are at the edges. This is why they are in the unstable zones. They are the plateau experimenting with the concept of *you*, and the experiment is not yet complete, and the results are provisional, and they stand in the margins like notes in a margin—present, relevant, but not yet incorporated into the main text.

You wonder what will happen when the plateau finishes processing you. You wonder if the Others will resolve into something coherent, something stable, something that walks and speaks and claims to remember being you. You wonder if they will dissolve, deemed incompatible with the system's requirements, recycled back into the Throat's endless production. You wonder if they will simply *remain*—perpetual drafts, permanent sketches, your shape echoing at the edges of a world that is still deciding whether it has room for the things it is trying to imagine.

The Others stand in the margins. The rain falls between them. The plateau continues its work.

## What Lives Here

What lives here is the question.

Not *who*. Not a list of species, a taxonomy of forms, a field guide to the fauna of an impossible plateau. What lives here is the *question itself*—the ongoing, unresolved, industrially-processed question of *what lives here*. The plateau is not an ecosystem. An ecosystem has settled. An ecosystem has committed to its inhabitants, has woven them into a stable web of relationships that can be mapped and studied and understood. The plateau has not settled. The plateau is the *process of settling*, and it may never finish, and the inhabitants—the Sitters, the Columns, the Mimes, the Throat, the Others, and all the forms that have not yet been named because they have not yet held still long enough to be named—are *the settling itself*.

You are an inhabitant too, while you are here. Your presence is being processed. Your form is being mirrored. Your biological patterns—your breathing, your warmth, the moisture you exhale, the pressure of your feet—are being incorporated into the plateau's cycling. You are feeding the engine. You are being fed *by* the engine—the warm air you breathe is the Throat's exhalation, the water you collect from condensation is the plateau's breath, the food you find growing in the desperate vegetation is the product of the same processes that produce the rock and the shifting fauna and the industrial features below.

You are not separate from what lives here. This is the final lesson the plateau teaches, the one that takes the longest to learn because it requires unlearning the boundary between observer and observed that has served you well in every other landscape you have entered. Here, observation is participation. Here, presence is processing. Here, to be *in* the plateau is to be *of* the plateau, temporarily, provisionally, for as long as the engine cycles and the Throat pulses and the reality holds—almost—at the edge of becoming.

The Sitters sit. The Columns flow. The Mimes shift. The Throat produces. The Others echo. The plateau continues.

And you—you stand in the warm dim, and the moisture condenses on your skin, and the rock pulses beneath your feet, and you understand that what lives here is *this*: the ongoing, the unresolved, the nearly-coherent, the almost-stable, the perpetually-arriving. What lives here is the process. What lives here is the engine. What lives here is the *becoming*.

And the becoming does not end.

And the becoming does not need to end.

And the becoming *is* what lives here.
