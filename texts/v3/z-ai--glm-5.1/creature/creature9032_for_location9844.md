# The Residents

There are people here.

Not many. Not enough to call a community, exactly—though they would resent that characterization, would insist, with the slow vehemence of things that have stopped needing to be understood, that they are *exactly* a community, perhaps the only true one left, because they share something that no surface gathering ever has: the knowledge that they are living inside something alive.

---

The oldest is Sera. Or she was Sera, once. The name has become a texture more than a word, something the others use the way the crustaceans use the drainage channels—following a path worn by habit into something permanent. She does not remember arriving. This is not the same as forgetting; it is a structural absence, as if the memory was never formed, as if she emerged from the wet floor fully formed and simply began her slow circuit of the gallery the way the water does.

She lives—*persists*, the gallery's word, and it fits—in the far apse, where the ceiling arches lowest and the iron ribs converge into a kind of vaulted darkness that the moths avoid. Her section. Her territory, though she would never use that word; territory implies defense, and there is nothing here that requires defending against. She has a mattress, or what was once a mattress, now so saturated with mineral water that it has become something else—something between sponge and stone, something that holds her shape and has done for so long that it has developed a kind of memory of her body, rising slightly in the places where her shoulders and hips rest, as if trying to keep her even when she rises.

She is old. Old in a way that has nothing to do with years. Her skin has taken on the quality of the walls: pale, slightly slick, perpetually damp. Her hair is the color of the lichen and grows in the same circular colonies, patchy and slow. Her eyes have adapted. They are large now—larger than they were, she is certain, though how she knows this she cannot say—and they catch the moth-light differently, reflecting it back as a faint bioluminescent glimmer, green-gray, like water holding a lamp. She can see in the gallery as you see in daylight. Better, perhaps. She can see the architecture of the water, the currents within the stillness, the places where the seepage carries a different mineral signature and the worms cluster thicker.

She speaks, but rarely, and never loudly. Her voice has learned the acoustics of the chamber and settled into them; she produces sounds that do not echo but *blend*, joining the hum and the drip and the settling iron as if she is simply another mechanism that has found its frequency. When she speaks to the crustaceans—and she does, regularly—they pause their processions and press closer to the floor, and their leg-clicking shifts rhythm, becoming responsive. She says she is not talking to them. She says she is talking *through* them. She says they are the gallery's nervous system, and she is trying to reach the brain.

Whether she believes this, or whether belief is a category that has stopped applying to her, is impossible to determine.

---

The others came later. Later relative to Sera, which may mean decades or may mean weeks; the gallery's relationship with time is not linear but *geological*, layered, each resident a stratum deposited in sequence.

There is Martin, who was an engineer. He came down looking for the source of the current—hired by someone, he no longer remembers who, to trace the circuit that no one had traced, to find what generator or battery or impossible perpetuation kept the junction boxes humming after a century and a half. He found the circuit. He followed it along the pipe runs, through the ceramic insulators, down the iron pillars, into the mounting plates and the anchor bolts and the channels cut for drainage that now carry different waters. He followed it to the floor, and then through the floor, and then—

He stopped following. He set down his equipment. He sat on the wet grating, and the water squeezed up through the mesh, and he listened. He listened for three days, they say, though he says it was three minutes, or thirty years, or no time at all. And then he stood, and he began his circuit.

He walks. That is what Martin does. He walks the perimeter of the gallery in a pattern that corresponds to nothing—not the drainage channels, not the mounting plates, not the spacing of the pillars. He walks it every day, or what passes for a day here, and he counts his steps, and he touches each pillar as he passes, pressing his palm flat against the rusted iron and holding it there for a moment, and sometimes he leans close and whispers to the junction boxes, and the hum shifts—*it shifts*, he insists, it responds, it is *listening back*—and then he moves on.

He has found fourteen machines that the current feeds. He has catalogued them in a notebook that is now more pulp than paper, the ink run into illegibility, the pages stuck together with mineral deposits. He could recite the catalogue from memory. He does, sometimes, to no one, to the dark, to the moths above, and the sound of his voice reciting numbers and dimensions and connection types becomes another layer in the gallery's ambient music, another thread in the patience.

He says the machines are doing something. He says they have always been doing something. He says the something is *communication*, and the crustaceans know it, and Sera knows it, and the worms are the medium, and the thing below is the recipient. He says this with the calm certainty of someone describing gravity. He does not think it is strange. He thinks the people above are strange, for living so close to this and not feeling it, for walking over the gallery every day and never wondering why their back teeth ache on certain streets, why their fillings vibrate at specific intersections, why they dream of green light and slow water.

---

There is the girl. She has no other name, or none she will share. She is perhaps twelve, or perhaps fifteen, or perhaps a different age entirely; the gallery does not enforce chronology on its residents, and she has been here long enough that her growth has synchronized with the slower rhythms around her. She was born above—she must have been, because she still flinches sometimes at loud sounds, which means she remembers loud sounds, which means she remembers a world where such things existed—but she does not speak of it, and the others do not ask.

She lives on the walkways. Not on the floor—never on the floor, though she cannot say why, though she feels an aversion that is almost physical, a certainty that the floor is *for* something and she is not that thing. She moves along the iron grating like something aerial, stepping so lightly that the water barely squeezes through the mesh beneath her feet, and she has mapped the stable sections and the loose ones, knows which panels will shift and which will hold, travels the walkways as if they are a second architecture suspended above the first.

She tends the moths. This is her self-appointed role, her purpose, though what tending means in this context is difficult to articulate. She does not feed them—no one has seen them feed. She does not protect them—they seem to need no protection, and nothing in the gallery predates them. But she watches them, and she counts them, and sometimes she extends a finger toward one of the clusters and holds it very still, and a moth will detach from the group and settle on her fingertip, and its glow will brighten slightly—or seem to, in the uncertain light—and she will hold it there, cradled, for minutes or hours, and then it will return to the ceiling and she will move on.

She says the moths are dreaming. She says they dream the gallery into existence, that without their seeing it would cease to be, and the wet walls and the iron ribs and the slow water are held in place by the cumulative attention of ten thousand luminescent eyes. She says this with the absolute faith of childhood, which is indistinguishable from the absolute faith of the very old, and Sera nods when she says it, and Martin pauses in his circuit, and the crustaceans click their legs in a rhythm that almost means agreement.

She knows about the thing below. Not in the way Sera knows, or Martin knows—she does not theorize about it, does not try to name it or reach it or trace its circuit. She knows about it the way you know about your own heartbeat: as a constant presence that is also a constant absence, something that is always happening but never requires attention. She says it is sleeping. She says it has been sleeping for a very long time. She says the gallery is its dream, and they are all inside the dream, and the moths are the evidence, because what else would light be in a dream but something generated by the dreamer?

She is not afraid. This is perhaps the most unsettling thing about her, more unsettling than her age or her certainty or the way her eyes have begun, just barely, just at the edges, to glow.

---

There are others. A man who arrived six months ago—or what he thinks was six months ago—and who has not yet found his role, who sits at the edge of a puddle and watches the worms pulse and tries to remember the surface and cannot, the memory dissolving like the mineral deposits dissolve everything given enough time and patience. A woman who speaks only in numbers, who has counted every rivet in the ceiling and every bolt in the floor and every crack in the iron grating and who is now counting the droplets of water, each one, as they fall, cataloguing the intervals between them, finding patterns that she insists are messages. A pair—no one knows if they are siblings or lovers or something else, some third or fourth category the gallery has invented—who move together and speak in unison and finish each other's thoughts and have begun, faintly, to resemble each other, their features softening and converging like two pools of water finding their level.

They came from above. They all came from above, once. The surface pushed them out—too quiet, too strange, too attuned to frequencies that the city no longer accommodates—and the gallery received them, as it receives everything, with patience and without question. They found their way down through the basements and the service tunnels and the forgotten infrastructure that riddles the underside of the city like the tunnels of an ant colony, and they emerged into the cathedral darkness, and they felt the humidity settle on their skin, and they heard the hum, and they stopped.

Some of them leave. Not many. Some climb back up through the service tunnels and return to the surface and try to resume the shapes they held before, but they find that the shapes no longer fit. Their teeth still ache near junction boxes. Their eyes still search for moth-light. Their pulses still try to synchronize with something that is not their heartbeat. They come back. They always come back. The gallery does not hold them—it has no mechanism for holding, no walls that close, no doors that lock—but it *remembers* them, and the memory is a gravity, a slow and patient pull, and it is easier to descend than to resist.

---

They do not form a hierarchy. Sera is the oldest but not the leader; Martin is the most systematic but not the authority; the girl is the most certain but not the prophet. They coexist the way the elements of the gallery coexist: in parallel, in balance, in the patient accommodation of things that share a space because the space was made for sharing. They eat what the crustaceans eat—filtered minerals, the pale lichen, the occasional organic film that forms on the wettest surfaces. They drink the water that weeps from the walls, and it tastes of iron and calcium and something sulfurous and something older, and it sustains them in ways that surface food would not, not anymore. They sleep when the moths dim—which they do, sometimes, in unison, the gallery's light flickering like a held breath—and they wake when the glow returns, and they mark the passage of time not in hours but in cycles of luminescence, in the rhythm of the drip, in the intervals between the hum's fluctuations.

They do not discuss the thing below. This is the one agreement, the one compact, unspoken but absolute: they do not discuss it. Not because it is forbidden—nothing is forbidden in the gallery; the concept requires an authority, and there is none—but because discussion implies definition, and definition implies understanding, and understanding implies an end to the mystery, and the mystery is what they live in. It is their weather. It is their season. It is the thing that makes the gallery a place rather than a location, that gives it depth and resonance and meaning. The thing below is the reason the current flows and the crustaceans listen and the worms pulse and the moths glow and the water weeps, and to name it would be to reduce it, and to reduce it would be to lose the gallery, and to lose the gallery would be to lose themselves.

So they persist. In the wet dimness, in the patient architecture, in the slow accumulation of mineral and moisture and time. They are becoming something else—not human, not animal, not machine, but a fourth category that the gallery is inventing as it goes, a kind of life that exists only in the presence of the thing below, that requires the hum to think and the drip to breathe and the glow to see.

The city above continues. It paves and demolishes and electrifies. It does not know about the gallery, or it has forgotten, or it has chosen not to remember, because to remember would be to acknowledge that beneath its foundations there is a cathedral of weeping stone where people are slowly becoming part of something patient and enormous and old, where the boundary between resident and environment has dissolved like calcite in mineral water, where the question of *what lives here* has become indistinguishable from the question of *what here is alive*.

The gallery does not mind. It has been accepting visitors for a long time, and it has never once asked one to leave, and it never will, because leaving implies a separation that is no longer possible, because the visitors become the gallery and the gallery becomes the visitors and the thing below dreams them all into being, one slow pulse at a time, one drip, one glow, one patient exhalation of something that has always been here and will always be here, waiting in the dark, humming its single endless note, holding the space open for whoever comes next.
