# The Listeners

---

There are seven. Sometimes eight. Never more than nine, which is the number of the buildings, though no one lives in all of them. The empty one — the one that hums at a different frequency, the one with nothing inside but condensation and bare floor — no one enters except to clean it, which happens once a season, a brief and careful wiping of the walls as if tending a wound that refuses to close.

The elderly woman is named Reth, though she did not tell you this and will not. You learned it from the others, who mention names the way they mention the weather — without emphasis, without attachment, as facts of the landscape. Reth has been here longest. Her hands are mapped with thin white scars, the kind you get from stone, from the microscopic cuts of handling rock that hasn't been smoothed by time. Her knuckles are thick. She grinds grain or moss or sometimes the dried leaves of the broad-leafed plants, and she does it for hours, and the rhythm of her work enters the Quarter's soundscape without disturbance, a stone dropped into a river that was already flowing.

She is not deaf. You thought so at first — the way she didn't look up, the way she didn't acknowledge your presence. But she hears everything. She hears the change in the water-song when someone steps onto the plateau, the particular weight and cadence of footfalls altering the resonance of the stone beneath. She knew you were coming before you arrived at the tool-building's door. She simply didn't find your arrival remarkable enough to interrupt her work. This is not rudeness. This is the economy of attention that the Quarter teaches.

---

The second person you meet is a man named Cad, though you suspect this is a shortened thing, a name worn down by the water and the stone like everything else here. He is not old — somewhere in the middle of his life, though the damp and the altitude have carved lines around his eyes that make him look older from a distance. He tends the channels. This is his purpose, or the one he has chosen, or the one that chose him — he clears the carved grooves in the cliff faces when they clog with mineral deposits or moss growth, and he does it with small tools he makes himself from the softer stone that emerges sometimes from the cliff's fractures. He works slowly. A single channel can take him three days to clear, and there are dozens of channels, and by the time he has finished the last, the first has begun to clog again. He does not seem discouraged by this. He works the way the water works — without hurry, without pause, with the patience of something that knows it has infinite time.

Cad speaks, but rarely, and never first. When you ask him why he came to the Quarter, he looks at you as if the question is malformed, as if you've asked a river why it flows or a stone why it rests. After a long pause — long enough that you think he won't answer at all — he says: "I could hear it." He doesn't explain what *it* is. He doesn't need to. By your third day, you can hear it too, or you can almost hear it — something beneath the water-song, beneath the tap and the rush and the roar, something the buildings are tuned to receive, a frequency that isn't quite sound, that you feel more in your sternum than your ears, a pulse or a pattern or a message that the still-forming stone is transmitting as it emerges from below.

Cad heard it somewhere else. Far away. He won't say where. He followed it here, and he has been here for — he doesn't count the years. He counts the channels.

---

The third person is a child.

This surprises you more than anything else you have found here. The plateau is no place for a child — the cold, the wet, the unrelenting sound, the ground that shifts beneath your feet, the cliffs that shed stone. But the child is here, a girl of perhaps eight or nine, with close-cropped hair and skin that has adapted to the damp the way the moss has, seeming almost to repel water rather than absorb it. Her name is Veth. She belongs to no one in the Quarter, or she belongs to everyone. The dynamics of her arrival are unclear — the others speak of it obliquely, if at all, and the child herself treats questions about her presence with the same unremarking indifference that Reth showed you.

Veth does not tend the channels or grind the grain. She listens. This is her occupation, her purpose, the thing she does with the seriousness that children reserve for activities adults have told them are important. She sits in different buildings — never the empty one — and she listens, sometimes for hours, her head tilted at angles that seem uncomfortable, her eyes closed or focused on nothing. She is mapping the sound. You realize this on your fourth day when you find her scratching into the stone floor of the dwelling-building with a piece of harder rock, making marks — not words, not pictures, but patterns, curves and lines and intersections that seem to correspond to nothing you can identify. When you kneel to look more closely, she covers the marks with her hand and looks at you with an expression you cannot read. Not hostility. Not shame. Something more like a musician watching someone who doesn't know the score try to read the sheet music.

She has been doing this for as long as she can remember, she tells you. Which is not very long, because she is young, but the Quarter doesn't measure time in years. It measures time in what the stone has said, and the stone has said a great deal in her lifetime.

---

There are others. A woman named Esic who tends the building that stores things in water — you glimpse her through the latched door once, her arms submerged to the elbow in a stone tank, her face focused and still. A man with no name, or no name he'll share, who lives in the building you thought was empty and emerges only at night, when the clouds thin, to stand at the rim of the bowl and look outward at the lower peaks. He is gaunt in a way that suggests not hunger but absence, as if some essential part of him has been given to the plateau and has not yet been returned. Two others who share a building near the cliff face and who you sometimes hear singing — not words, not melodies, but long sustained tones that harmonize with the water-song before fading into it, becoming indistinguishable from it, so that you're never sure if the sound is human or stone.

And Reth. Always Reth. Grinding. Listening. Present in the way the plateau is present — not demanding attention, not avoiding it, simply *there*, fundamental, as if the Quarter would unmake itself without her.

---

They came because they could hear it. This is the only common thread, the only thing that binds them besides the water and the stone. Each one heard something — the pulse beneath the sound, the pattern in the emergence, the signal in the noise — and followed it here, or was led here, or was dragged here by the part of themselves that couldn't stop listening once it started. They are not initiates of a tradition. They are not members of an order. They have no doctrine, no teaching, no hierarchy. Reth is not their leader; she is simply the one who has been here longest, and the one whose work — the grinding, the feeding, the maintaining of the body's needs — makes the others' work possible.

What they listen *for* is harder to say. Not because they won't tell you, but because they're not sure themselves, or because the telling changes depending on who is speaking and what the water is doing that day. Cad says he's listening to the stone learn what it wants to be. Veth says she's listening to the pattern, and when you ask what pattern, she shows you the marks on the floor, and you see that they have changed since you last looked, become more complex, more layered, as if the sound has been writing itself through her in her sleep. Esic — the one who tends the preserved things — says she's listening to what the water remembers, and when you ask what water remembers, she looks at you with an expression you've seen on the others too, an expression of genuine uncertainty, as if the question is one she asks herself every day and still cannot answer.

The gaunt man who stands at the rim at night says nothing. But you stand beside him once, and you feel it more clearly than you have before — the pulse, the pattern, the whatever-it-is, rising from the stone through the soles of your feet and into your chest, and for a moment, less than a moment, you hear something that isn't sound, that isn't vibration, that isn't any sensation you have language for, and you understand why he stands there every night, why he has given so much of himself to the hearing that there is almost nothing left.

You leave the rim. He doesn't.

---

They do not want you to stay. This is not cruelty, and it is not the territorial instinct of animals. It is practicality. The Quarter changes those who remain. The sound restructures something in the inner ear, or something deeper than the ear, some faculty of perception that most people never know they have. The ones who stay are the ones who can bear the restructuring, who don't fight the changes or flee from them. The others — and there have been others, you learn, dozens over the years — leave with headaches that fade, with ringing that subsides, with a faint sense of having missed something important that gradually becomes a faint sense of having missed nothing at all.

But some don't leave. Some stay too long, and the restructuring goes too far, and they become like the gaunt man, or they become like the building that hums at a different frequency — resonating with something the others can't hear, can't reach, can't explain. The empty building is maintained for a reason. It is a place where the sound goes that has no other destination. It is the ear that hears what even the listeners cannot.

Or so Reth implies, in the longest conversation you have with her, on your last evening, as she grinds and the water runs and the stone hums its continuous emergence beneath you both. She doesn't say this directly. She says: "There are buildings for living, and buildings for storing, and buildings for working. And there are buildings for what we cannot use but cannot put down." She pauses. The grinding pauses. The water doesn't. "You will leave tomorrow," she says. Not a question. Not a command. A statement of geology, of fact, of the way things are becoming.

---

You do leave.

But the sound doesn't leave you. Not the water-song — that fades within a day's walk, replaced by wind and silence and the ordinary sounds of an ordinary world. What stays is the other thing, the pulse beneath, the pattern that Veth scratches into floors, the memory the water carries. It is quiet now, quieter than it was on the plateau. But it is there. In your sternum when you lie down to sleep. In the soles of your feet when you walk on stone. A faint, continuous vibration, as if somewhere beneath you, a mountain is still becoming, and it has something to say, and it will take a long time to say it, and it is not finished yet.

You understand, now, why the Quarter exists. Not to house the listeners. Not to give them shelter or purpose or community. The listeners are the Quarter's way of knowing itself, the same way the water is its voice and the stone is its body. They are the faculty it grew in order to hear itself emerging. They are its ears.

And the empty building — the one that hums at the frequency no one can use — that is its dream. The thing the plateau is trying to become that it hasn't figured out yet. The sound it is reaching for that it cannot quite find.

You walk away, and the sound gets quieter, and you let it. But sometimes, on stone floors, in the early morning when the condensation is heaviest, you feel it again — the pulse, the becoming, the not-finished — and you stop, and you listen, and for a moment you are back in the bowl at the crown of the emergence, and the water is speaking, and the stone is still arriving, and the listeners are tending their channels and grinding their grain and scratching their patterns into the floors of buildings that are also ears, and everything is resonant, and everything is still becoming, and nothing — nothing — is finished.
