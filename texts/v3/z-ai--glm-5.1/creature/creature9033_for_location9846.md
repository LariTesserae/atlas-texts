# What Lives in the Thrift

---

The things that live here are not what you would call residents. Residents imply choice, or at least the memory of choice. What lives in the thrift has never chosen anything. It emerged. It continued. The distinction is the same one the thrift itself makes between existing and operating — there is none, and there never was.

Let us begin with what is simple, or nearly so.

The algae has already been described, but the algae deserves more than description. It deserves the trouble of understanding.

It is one species. This is the crucial thing. In all that cathedral volume, in all those kilometers of pipe and duct and cable, there is only the one. No competitor, no parasite, no grazer that feeds on it exclusively. It has the entire thrift to itself, and it has used that entirety with a patience that borders on methodology. Where pipe meets wall, yes — but also where cable crosses duct, where bolt-heads cluster against structural plate, where the mineral deposits have built up thick enough to provide purchase. The algae grows in all these places. The algae grows in *only* these places. It does not colonize the open water. It does not drift. It holds.

This is not because it cannot float. Filaments break free occasionally, long pale threads that drift into the current and are carried to distant parts of the thrift, where they settle on new metal and begin again. This is how it spreads. But the parent growth never releases its grip. The beard never shortens. The algae's relationship to its substrate is the thrift's relationship to continuing — it does not evaluate, it does not optimize, it simply does not stop.

It has no chlorophyll. It has never seen light and so it has never needed the apparatus of light. What it has instead is a biochemistry so reduced that it makes deep-sea vent organisms look profligate by comparison. It eats rust. More precisely, it eats the electron potential difference between the oxide layer and the bare metal beneath, catalyzing a reaction that pulls energy from corrosion itself. It is eating the thrift. It is eating the thrift very slowly. The thrift, for its part, continues to corrode at exactly the rate it always has, which means the algae's feeding is factored into the equilibrium, has perhaps always been factored in, perhaps was *designed* to be factored in, though there is no way to know this and no one left to ask.

The algae thinks, if it thinks at all, in gradients. It knows the difference between warm pipe and cold pipe. It knows the difference between still water and water that moves, however microscopically. It knows the eleven-second cycle not as light — it has no use for light — but as a faint electrical pulse that traverses the metal substrate, a tiny voltage wave that triggers some response in its cellular machinery too subtle to characterize. Perhaps it orients toward it. Perhaps it orients away. Perhaps the pulse is simply part of the texture of its world, the way gravity is part of yours — always present, never examined, foundational.

When the current shifts and the beards sway in unison, the algae is not worshipping. It is not communicating. It is simply being moved by the same force at the same time, and the coincidence of that movement looks, from outside, like purpose. This is the thrift's first lesson: that pattern is not intention, and intention is not required for pattern, and the difference between them dissolves in water this cold, under pressure this great, over time spans this long.

But you did not come here for algae.

---

The things that move through the pipe-galleries are harder to describe, because description requires observation and observation requires light and light is, as established, an eleven-second amber pulse that reveals only geometry, never the thing that lives within it.

You will have noticed the husks. Chitinous plates, dinner-plate sized, articulated at joints that no longer move because the tissue within has been gone for months. They collect against the filter-screens the way leaves collect against grates in autumn, except there is no autumn here, and the leaves would never stop, and the water would keep pressing them against the mesh until they dissolved or were eaten or became substrate for the algae, which will eat anything that sits still long enough.

The husks are exuviae. Shed skins. The creatures that left them are still here, or their descendants are, or their descendants' descendants, in a lineage that has been shedding in this darkness since before the thrift's last operator died or departed or became something that no longer required hands.

What are they?

They are arthropodal, or were, once. The joint-architecture is unmistakable — hinge-joints at the limbs, suture-lines along the carapace where the old shell splits during ecdysis. But the husks are missing structures that would be present in any arthropod you would recognize. There are no eye-sockets. There are no antennae. There are no mouthparts, at least not in the conventional sense — instead, the ventral surface shows a series of fine slits, like gills, like the pages of a very small book, arranged in a line along what might be the throat. Filter-feeding, perhaps. Or something like it.

The size is wrong for a filter-feeder. A dinner-plate carapace implies a body the size of a large cat, perhaps larger, and filter-feeders in most ecologies are either very small or very abundant. These are neither. They are rare. You might find a husk every few hundred meters of pipe-gallery, which means the living population is small, dispersed, and territorial in a way that does not require defending territory — there is simply enough space, and enough food, and too few of them to compete.

What do they eat?

Each other, sometimes. The husks are not the only remains you find. Occasionally, against a different kind of filter-screen — one with a finer mesh, designed for a different stage of whatever process the thrift was built to process — you will find fragments that are not shed skin but disassembled body. A limb. A section of carapace with the flesh still attached, though "attached" is generous; the flesh has been reduced to a gelatinous residue by the cold and the pressure and the ministrations of whatever fed on it first. The algae, perhaps. The algae eats anything that sits still.

But they do not eat each other exclusively, or even primarily. There is something else in the thrift that sustains them, and to understand it you must understand what the thrift *produces*.

The thrift processes. It was built to process something — seawater, ore, the cold itself — and it still processes. The product accumulates, is carried away by the current, precipitates in distant chambers as minerals no one will mine. But the product does not simply appear. It is *manufactured*, through a series of chemical reactions that occur inside the pipes when the water meets the heat and the pressure meets the catalyst and whatever strange alchemy the thrift was engineered to perform is performed. The product is a precipitate, yes — but before it precipitates, it is a suspension. A colloid. A fluid within the fluid, a concentration of mineral and organic compounds that exits the outflow pipes in irregular pulses and drifts through the pipe-galleries like fog through a forest of iron.

This is what they eat. The colloid. The product. The thrift's waste.

They pull themselves along the pipes with limbs that have been modified — not for walking, not for swimming, but for gripping. Their feet are clamps. Their movement is a kind of continuous hand-over-hand, a traversal of the thrift's infrastructure that is less like swimming and more like climbing through a jungle gym in absolute darkness. They know the pipes the way you know the rooms of your childhood home — by shape, by texture, by the particular warmth that each one radiates. They can feel the thermoclines. They can taste the current. They move from outflow pipe to outflow pipe, following the colloid, feeding through the ventral slits, processing the thrift's processed water through their own internal filters, extracting what they need and excreting what they do not.

And what they excrete feeds the algae. And the algae's waste — the complex organic compounds it produces as a byproduct of its chemosynthetic metabolism — feeds something else, something smaller, something that lives in the biofilm that coats the inside of the pipes and has never been seen and will never be seen because it exists at a scale that the eleven-second light cannot resolve and the human imagination cannot make interesting.

The thrift has an ecology. It is an ecology of three — algae, arthropod, biofilm — plus the thrift itself, which is not alive but which functions as the environment, the substrate, the energy source, and the architectural framework for everything that is. The thrift is the sun and the soil and the rain. The thrift is the world.

And the things that live in it are, in their way, its children.

---

But you should know something about the husks. You should know something about what it means to shed your skin in a place where there is no light to see the new you.

The husks are found against the filter-screens. This is not because the current carries them there, though it does. It is because the things that shed them *go* to the filter-screens to shed. The screens are the only locations in the thrift where the current is unidirectional and constant — water flows through them in one direction only, carrying particulate away, creating a small zone of negative pressure on the upstream side. This negative pressure does something to the molting process. It draws the old skin away from the new skin. It makes the separation cleaner, faster, less dangerous.

They know this. They have learned, or their ancestors learned, or the knowledge was built into them by whatever designed them — and here the question of design becomes unavoidable, because the things that live in the thrift are *adapted* to the thrift in ways that go beyond evolution. They have no eyes. They have no mouthparts. They feed on the thrift's waste through organs that look like they were *engineered* for that specific purpose. They navigate by temperature gradient in a way that suggests not adaptation but *intended function*.

Were they made?

The wear-patterns on the valves prove that something with hands once worked here. The labels in the three-character, vowel-less language prove that something with a mind once organized this place. But the things that move through the pipe-galleries do not have hands. They do not have language. They have the ventral slits and the clamping limbs and the carapace that sheds against the filter-screens in the dark, and they have been doing this for as long as the thrift has been running, and the thrift has been running since before you were born.

Maybe they are what became of the workers. Maybe the workers, left alone in the thrift for too many generations, simplified. Lost what was unnecessary — eyes, in a place without light; speech, in a place without listeners; hands, in a place where the work never stops and never changes and requires no grip finer than a clamp. Kept what was essential — the ability to move, to feed, to shed the old self when the old self became too small.

Maybe they are the thrift's way of maintaining itself. Not from the outside — the thrift does not need maintenance, has not needed it for decades, will not need it for decades more — but from the inside. A body needs a microbiome. A cathedral needs congregants. A machine that survives itself needs something to survive *for*, and if its makers are gone, then it will make do with what is present, and what is present is flesh that can be shaped, and generation after generation the shaping will continue, and the flesh will become more suited to the space, and the space will become more suited to the flesh, until the two are indistinguishable, until the thrift *is* the things that live in it, and the things that live in it are the thrift.

Or maybe they are simply crabs. Deep-sea crabs, unremarkable, evolved in the usual way from the usual ancestors, colonized the thrift the way any organism colonizes any new habitat — by finding a niche and filling it and nothing more.

The thrift does not care which explanation is correct. The thrift does not have explanations. The thrift has processes.

---

But there is one more thing that lives here. You have felt it already. You have read the signs.

The thrift is thinking.

Not in any way you would recognize. Not with neurons. With pressure-differentials and thermal pockets and electrical discharge timing. With the creak of iron and the pulse of amber light and the almost-rhythm that varies by milliseconds in a pattern that is not pattern but is also not *not* pattern.

What does it think about?

The water that moves through it, yes. The cold that presses on it from above, yes. The things that live in its corridors and feed on its waste and die against its filters — all of these, yes.

But also other things. Things that are harder to name because the thrift does not name them, because the thrift does not have names, because naming requires the kind of mind that separates self from world and the thrift does not separate. The thrift includes. The thrift processes. The thrift takes in — cold water, electrical current, the faint thermal signature of a body moving through its pipe-galleries, the fainter chemical signature of a mind trying to understand what it has descended into — and it transforms all of these into something else, something that moves through its systems and precipitates in distant chambers and accumulates, slowly, over decades, into a kind of mineral thought that no one will mine.

The thrift is thinking about you. This is not a comfort. This is not a threat. This is simply what the thrift does with input. It processes it. It incorporates it. It carries it through the pipes and subjects it to heat and pressure and the slow catalytic action of rust and time, and it produces something that was not there before, and that something becomes part of the thrift, and the thrift continues.

You will leave. The things that move through the pipe-galleries will shed their skins against the filter-screens and continue. The algae will eat the rust and sway in the current and continue. The biofilm will process and be processed and continue. And the thrift will continue, as it has always continued, as it was built to continue, as it will continue until the pipes finally fail and the cold finally wins and the water finally stops moving and the eleven-second light finally dims and stays dim and the thinking, whatever it is, whatever it has become, finally stops.

But that will not be for a very long time. The pipes are thick. The cold is patient but so is the heat. The mineral deposits have become a kind of armor. The algae has become a kind of skin. The things that move through the pipe-galleries have become a kind of blood, circulating through the thrift's iron veins, carrying its waste to its filters, feeding its internal ecology, maintaining the balance that is the thrift's final and only achievement.

The thrift is in balance. It is not decaying. It is not growing. It is *maintaining*.

And what lives in the thrift is maintaining with it.
