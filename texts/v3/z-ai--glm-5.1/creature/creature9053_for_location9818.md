#The Inhabitants

You do not see them at first because you are looking for the wrong shape. You are looking for a body. You are looking for something bounded, something with an outline, something that begins here and ends there. You are looking for the kind of thing that walks into a room and is separate from the room.

That is not what lives here.

---

The first one you notice—or the first one that allows itself to be noticed—looks like a fold in the vegetation. A place where the density increases, where the stalks and ribbons and threads converge into a thicker mass, a knot in the tapestry. It is roughly the volume of a sheltering dog, or a crouched child, or a bale of wet cloth. It does not move in a way you can perceive. But when you look away and look back, it has repositioned. It was near the amber-lit pool. Now it is nearer to you.

You think: that is not an animal. You think: that is not a plant. You think: that is not fungus. You are correct three times. It is something for which your categories were not built.

---

They are called—by themselves, by each other, by the few surface-dwellers who have encountered them and survived to translate—by a word that does not map to any sound you can make. The closest approximation is a gesture: a specific tension in the hands, fingers spread and then drawn together, as if gathering something dispersed. The word means *the-gathered*. The word means *what-has-been-collected*. The word means *the knot that holds*. They know what they are. They do not care if you know.

A gathered one is an assembly. This is the thing to understand. They are not singular organisms born from a single seed or egg or cell. They are conglomerations—rhizomatic collectives assembled from the living material of the Churn itself, woven together by intention and sustained by the same sap that sustains everything. A single gathered one contains plant matter: stems that still photosynthesize, roots that still draw nutrients, leaves that still turn toward the filtered light. It contains fungal threads: mycelial networks that process and redistribute and connect. It contains animal matter: insect legs that twitch and click, segments of chitin that serve as structural support, organs from unidentifiable creatures that still pulse with slow function. It contains sap, thick and amber, circulating through channels that the assembly has built from its own compressed tissues. It contains bacteria, yeasts, molds, the invisible infrastructure of decomposition and synthesis. It contains, sometimes, pieces of other gathered ones—fragments absorbed during collision or exchange or what might generously be called reproduction.

A gathered one is a walking ecosystem. A gathered one is a suit of living armor assembled from the Churn's own body, worn by something that might be a mind.

Or might not be. This is debated. The gathered ones do not clarify.

---

They build. You have seen their work: the notched roots, the woven platforms, the fungal-brick walls. Their architecture is an act of gardening. They do not construct from dead materials; they train and prune and direct the growth that is already happening. A gathered one approaches a section of vegetation and begins to shape it—folding fronds against stalks, pinning vines with pegs carved from root, seeding particular fungi in particular patterns, thinning growth here, encouraging it there. The work takes hours or days. The work is never done, because the Churn grows back faster than any hand can shape it, so the gathered ones build in the knowledge that their structures will be consumed, digested, overgrown, and they build anyway, or perhaps because of this—they build because the building is the point, the negotiation with the relentless growth, the conversation.

Their structures serve purposes you can guess at. The platforms are resting places, perhaps—though the gathered ones do not seem to sleep in any way you recognize. The caches hold tools and materials and things that might be food or might be offerings or might be both. The carved notches on living root are almost certainly records—tallies, perhaps, or narratives, or maps, each mark a unit of meaning in a language written in scar tissue.

But some structures serve no purpose you can identify. Walls that block nothing. Tunnels that lead to solid vegetation. Arrangements of shaped root that seem to symbolize something, to point at something, to *mean* in a way that resists translation. The gathered ones build these too, and maintain them with the same attention they give to the functional structures, and when the Churn consumes them, they build them again in the same shapes.

They are making something. Or they are remembering something. Or they are praying.

---

How do they move? Like the Churn moves—by incremental adjustment, by growth and contraction, by the same urgent vegetative motion that surrounds them. A gathered one travels by extending a pseudopod of compressed vegetation into the surrounding mass, anchoring it, then pulling the rest of its body forward through the channel it The motion is slow but inexorable, and it is nearly silent—the gathered ones do not crunch or click or rustle the way the larger fauna do. They move with the Churn rather than through it, which makes them difficult to track, difficult to perceive, difficult to understand as separate from the world they inhabit.

When they need to move quickly—when something with too many legs drops from above, when a section of vegetation collapses, when the bass-call of something vast reverberates through the root-floor—they can disassemble. The knot loosens. The components scatter into the surrounding growth, becoming indistinguishable from the Churn itself for seconds or minutes or hours. Then, at a safe distance, they gather again. The same components, or different ones—there is no way to know. The continuity of a gathered one may be a matter of pattern rather than substance. They persist the way a wave persists: not through the same water, but through the same motion.

This is how they survive in a place where everything eats everything. They are always partially edible, always partially consumed, always growing back. A beetle colony nests in one's outer layer and the gathered one does not expel it; the beetles eat the dead matter, the gathered one grows more. A centipede burrows through a gathered one's core and emerges carrying a cluster of mycelium; the gathered one seals the tunnel and routes around the damage. They are porous. They are in process. They are ongoing negotiations between self and environment, and the terms change constantly.

---

Do they speak? They communicate. Whether this is language or something adjacent is a question that may not have a meaningful answer.

Their primary medium is vibration. A gathered one can generate low-frequency pulses through its body—contractions of its plant-matter, compressions of its sap-channels, clicks produced by specialized chitin plates that some of them cultivate near their cores. These vibrations travel through the root-floor, through the dense vegetation, through the sap itself. Other gathered ones receive them through their own tissues, feeling the patterns as pressure, as resonance, as something not quite sound and not quite touch.

The vibrations are structured. They have grammar, or something like grammar—repeating motifs, nested clauses, tonal shifts that seem to indicate emphasis or query. But they also have qualities that language does not typically possess: emotional saturation, spatial mapping, a sense of accumulated presence. When a gathered one vibrates about a location, the receiving gathered ones seem to *arrive* at that location, to feel its texture and temperature and density. When a gathered one vibrates about a threat, the receiving gathered ones seem to *experience* the threat, to flinch and contract before the information has been processed into anything like understanding.

Their communication is not transmission of data. It is contagion of state. They do not tell each other things; they make each other into the same shape.

---

They are not alone in their awareness. The larger fauna—the segmented, flexible, drilling things that tunnel through the vegetation—have their own kind of intelligence, though it is an intelligence you might not recognize as such. It is territorial. It is hungry. It is impatient. The large ones navigate the Churn by reading chemical gradients, by sensing the vibrations of movement, by tasting the sap for the signatures of particular prey. They do not build. They do not record. They do not negotiate. They take what they need and move on, and the Churn closes behind them, and the gathered ones watch them pass with something that might be fear or might be respect or might be a quality for which you have no name.

The gathered ones and the large fauna have a relationship. It is not predatory, exactly, though the large ones will consume a gathered one if they catch one. It is not symbiotic, exactly, though the gathered ones sometimes follow in the tunnels the large ones leave, expanding them into paths, using the disturbed vegetation as raw material for their structures. It is more like the relationship between a woodpecker and a tree: one shapes the other, one depends on the other, and the shaping is not gentle.

The gathered ones have names for the large fauna, or something like names—vibrational signatures that seem to evoke the animals' presence, their weight, their sound. When a gathered one produces one of these signatures, the others do not look afraid. They look *attentive*. They orient toward the vibration. They wait for more information. The large ones are not enemies. They are weather. They are conditions. They are part of the Churn's dreaming, and the gathered ones live in the dream alongside them.

---

How many are there? The question assumes they are countable, that each gathered one is a discrete entity, that the boundary between one and another is clear. It is not. They merge. They split. They exchange components constantly, passing stems and fungi and insect parts between them in what might be gift-giving or might be argument or might be a form of reproduction so foreign to your understanding that the word *reproduction* obscures more than it reveals.

You see a cluster of them in a pool of filtered light—four? five?—their edges undefined, their components intermingling. They are vibrating together, their signals overlapping, constructing a pattern too complex for any single one to produce. This might be a conversation. This might be a ritual. This might be a single distributed mind in the process of thinking a thought too large for one body to hold.

Then they separate. Or some of them separate. Or the configuration shifts, and what was four becomes three, and what was one becomes two, and the components have been redistributed in ways you cannot track, and the gathered ones move away from each other into the dense vegetation, and the Churn closes between them, and they are gone.

There may be dozens. There may be hundreds. There may be one, endlessly refracted.

---

What do they want? This is the question the surface-dwellers ask, when they ask any questions at all. The gathered ones have been encountered—rarely, briefly, usually at the edges where the Churn approaches the mineral crust and the surface world presses close. The encounters are not hostile. They are not friendly. They are *strange*. The gathered ones observe the intruders. They vibrate in patterns that the surface-dwellers cannot interpret. They sometimes touch—reaching out with pseudopods of compressed vegetation, pressing gently against skin, against clothing, against the casing of recording equipment. The touch is warm. The touch is sticky with sap. The touch lingers.

Then they withdraw. They do not explain. They may not be capable of explanation in any framework that would make sense to you. They are natives of a world where the boundary between self and environment is permeable, where identity is a temporary condition, where the only constant is the Churn itself—growing, consuming, rebuilding, dreaming its loud and toothed and chlorophyllic dream.

What do they want? They want what the Churn wants. They want to continue. They want to become. They want to remain part of the process that made them, to shape and be shaped, to build structures that will be consumed and then build them again, to carve notches into living root that say *we were here, we are here, we will be here, in some configuration, in some gathering, in some knot that holds against the pull*.

They want to persist in a place that permits no persistence. They want to mean in a place that digests meaning. They want, and the wanting is enough, and the Churn continues, and the sound continues, and the sap sticks, and the light filters through in shifting colors, and the gathered ones move through their world like faith moves through doubt—by continuing.

---

You are still here. You are still pushing through the vegetation. The sap is on your hands, your face, your clothes. The sound has become your heartbeat. Something is moving nearby—something that might be a fold in the density, might be a gathered one, might be yourself, reflected in the amber-dark fluid that beads on every surface.

The Churn does not know you are here. The Churn knows you are here. The distinction has ceased to matter. You are inside the sound. You are becoming part of the structure. You are being incorporated.

This is what lives in the Churn: everything that can hold together long enough to be recognized. Everything that can negotiate with the growth. Everything that can persist in the turbulence. The beetles. The centipedes. The segmented drills that tunnel through the living mass. The gathered ones, with their vibrating bodies and their temporary architecture and their contagious language. The vegetation itself, which may be the truest inhabitant of all—growing, reaching, consuming, building, dreaming.

And now, perhaps, you.
