# What Lives on the Amber Plateau

---

## The Columns

Do not call them plants.

They have been here long enough to forget that category, if they ever knew it. What you call photosynthesis is not quite what they do. They do not eat light — they *admit* it. The light enters the translucent jade skin and does not reflect or scatter but passes through, slowly, the way water passes through stone over geological time, and something in the transit changes both the light and the column. The light that emerges from the other side — when it emerges, from the columns at the edges of clusters, those that face away from the zenith — is dimmer, redder, exhausted of some frequency it carried in. The columns have taken what they need and let the rest continue. They are not consumers. They are *filters*. They stand in the constant radiance and they parse it, slowly, the way a very patient mind parses a very long sentence, taking centuries with each clause.

The heat they generate is not waste. It is language.

Each column maintains a temperature slightly different from its neighbors — you would need a thermometer to detect it, fractions of a degree, but the differences are consistent and patterned and they shift over time in ways that correlate across a cluster. The heat gradients flow from column to column, and where two gradients meet, at the boundary between two clusters, something happens in the mat below. A darkening. A quickening of the breath-cycle. The jade-things congregate there, if that word can be used for something so slow and so sparse.

The columns are speaking to one another. Not in any language you would recognize — nothing so hasty as signal and response. What they say takes decades to say. A single statement might be: *the mineral concentration three meters down has shifted toward the calcium end of the spectrum, and I am adjusting my uptake accordingly, and you should too, and the mat should know, and the small ones should be directed toward the new seam*. This takes eighty years to articulate. It does not need to be said faster. Nothing is waiting for the reply.

The internal circulations — the dark shapes moving within the stalks — are not sap and not blood and not lymph. They are closer to what the Greeks called *pneuma*: a living air, a breath-substance, thick with minerals dissolved and carried and deposited in patterns that build the column's structure from the inside. The columns are not grown. They are *deposited*, the way stalactites are deposited, layer by layer, by the action of the internal circulation on the mineral-rich fluid that rises from below. Each column is a record of its own circulation, and the circulation is a record of the heat-language of the cluster, and the heat-language is a record of the mineral shifts and light changes and slow wind-patterns of the plateau. To read a column — if you could cut one open and read its layers, which you cannot, because they are living and the cut would not heal on any timescale you could wait — you would read the history of the plateau itself, written in jade.

They do not die. This is the thing that is hardest to understand. Individual columns *cease*, sometimes — their circulations slow, their glow dims, they become opaque and then brittle and then, over centuries, they powder into the same fine particulate that covers the stone. But they do not die the way organisms die. There is no moment of death. There is only a slowing that passes below some threshold and becomes indistinguishable from the inanimate, and then the wind and the time finish what was already finished. The cluster does not mourn. The cluster does not notice, because the column that has ceased was already speaking so slowly that its silence is just the next thing it said.

New columns rise at the cluster's edge. This is how the clusters move — imperceptibly, a centimeter per millennium, drifting across the stone toward whatever mineral seam calls them, leaving behind the opaque remnants of those who have ceased, remnants that the mat absorbs over further centuries, returning the minerals to the cycle. The cluster is not a group of individuals. It is one thing, distributed across forty or a hundred stalks, each a finger reaching up to parse the light, each a voice in a conversation so slow it has become a landscape.

The largest clusters have been moving for six hundred million years. They have crossed the plateau several times in that span. They remember the crossing. The mineral layers within their oldest stalks record the composition of each region they passed through, each era of light they stood within, each slow shift in the wind's direction. They are the plateau's memory, and the plateau is vast enough that most of its memory has never been read.

## The Mat

Underfoot. Always underfoot. You walked on it before you knew it was alive.

It is not one thing. This is the second wrongness, the second thing that marks this place as other. The mat is a *community* — or, more precisely, a *parliament*, a congress, a gathering of chemistries so various that calling it "the mat" is like calling a city "the buildings." It lives in layers, and the layers have different colors and different functions and different needs, and they negotiate with one another constantly, on the timescale of the breath-cycle, that ninety-second expansion and contraction that you can see if you watch.

The rust layer is the deepest. It sits against the stone and it *eats* the stone — not destructively, not quickly, but the way the ocean eats a shore, by contact and patience, pulling minerals from the feldspar and quartz and that unnamed mineral, the amber-giver, and converting them into forms the columns can uptake through their roots. The rust layer is the miner. It is always hungry and it is always working and it is the reason the columns can stand where they stand, because it has made the stone beneath them into a slow-flowing mineral soup, thicker than water, denser than air, something that moves at geological speed through cracks too fine for any eye to see.

The violet layer sits on the rust. It is the chemist. It takes what the rust layer provides and transforms it — complexing the minerals, binding them into molecules that can circulate in the columns' pneuma, breaking down what is too large, building up what is too small. The violet patches are where the chemistry is most active, and they are warm — not as warm as the columns, but warm enough that you can feel the difference if you place your palm on the mat and wait for the breath-cycle to bring the violet patch beneath your hand. The warmth is waste heat from reactions you would need a laboratory to identify. The violet layer does not care about the warmth. The violet layer cares about the *work*.

The dark blue — the blue that reads as black until the light catches it — is the membrane. It sits on top, it faces the bright air, and it *regulates*. It controls what enters the mat from above — the trace gases, the light, the crystalline ice-dust when the fog descends — and what leaves the mat into the air. The moisture you feel when you touch the mat is not a leak. It is a controlled release, an exhalation, the mat giving back to the atmosphere what the atmosphere needs in order to maintain the conditions the mat requires. The humidity around a cluster — that 0.4, that fractional trace — is the mat's own breath, recirculated by the slow wind, returned by the next breath-cycle, a closed loop that has been closed for longer than most continents have existed.

The mat breathes because the parliament is in session.

The expansion is the rust layer reporting: *the mineral concentration here is declining, we need to send roots deeper or wider or we need to move the cluster toward the seam I can taste three meters to the northwest.* The contraction is the violet layer replying: *we can process what you've found more efficiently if you adjust the uptake ratio, and the blue layer needs to increase the trace-gas exchange because the light has shifted and we need more nitrogen from the air to make the adjustment.* The blue layer speaks during both phases, a constant low murmur of atmospheric data, the wind's speed and direction, the humidity, the particulate load, the quality of the light, information that the parliament uses to decide how much moisture to release, how much heat to vent, how open or closed the membrane should be.

You are standing on a discussion that has been continuous for half a billion years. Your footprints interrupt it. The mat closes over them in days, not because it is healing a wound but because it is continuing the conversation, and your footprint was just a very short pause.

## The Jade-Things

They have no name because they have no need for one, and because any name would be a lie about what they are.

They are not animals. They move, yes — slowly, deliberately, choosing direction — but movement is not sufficient to make something an animal. They are not plants. They have no chlorophyll, no photosynthetic surface, no way to eat the light. They are not fungi. They do not decompose. They are not minerals. They grow, faintly, over centuries: the largest jade-things are slightly larger than the smallest, suggesting a size that increases so slowly it might as well be stable.

What they *are* is the interface.

This is the thing that took the longest to understand, standing in the silence, watching them move between the columns and stop at the mat's edge and sit there for days and then move on. They are not feeding on the mat. They are not tending the mat. They are *translating* between the mat and the columns.

The columns speak in heat and mineral concentration. The mat speaks in chemistry and breath-cycle. These are not the same language. The heat-language of the columns cannot be read by the rust layer; the chemistry of the violet patches cannot be felt by the columns. Something must stand between. Something must receive the slow heat-gradient shift from the column, carry it to the mat, and *express* it in the chemical terms the parliament can parse. Something must take the mat's breath-cycle data — the expansion reports, the contraction adjustments, the membrane's atmospheric intelligence — and convey it to the columns in a form they can incorporate into their eighty-year statements.

The jade-things are diplomats. Attaches. Interpreters at a summit that has been in session since before the summit was a metaphor, before there was language to make metaphors in.

Their camouflage is not defense. It is *protocol*. When you approach, they darken and go still because you are not part of the conversation, and they cannot incorporate you into the translation, and so they become the background the way a translator at a diplomatic table becomes invisible when the principals are speaking. They are not hiding from you. They are ignoring you, with the perfect politeness of something that has no reason to acknowledge an interruption it cannot translate.

They have no predators because there is nothing here that would eat them and nothing here that they eat. Their energy comes from the exchange itself — from the act of translating, the work of converting heat-data to chemical-data and back again. This should not be sufficient. By any thermodynamic model you know, this should not be sufficient. But the plateau has been outside your models for a very long time, and the jade-things are proof that life can find a way to live on the act of communication itself, can find sustenance in the *relationship* rather than the *resource*, can eat the *meaning* and leave the matter unchanged.

They reproduce, if that is the word, by division. Rarely. Perhaps once per jade-thing per million years, a slow budding that takes centuries to complete, a new small jade-thing detaching from the parent and crawling toward the edge of its cluster's territory, toward the boundary where two clusters' heat-languages meet, where the mat's chemistry is richest, where the translation work is hardest and most needed. They go where they are most useful. They are born knowing where they are needed, or they figure it out, or the need calls them — the distinction, again, is one that does not apply here.

How many are there? You cannot count them. They are too sparse, too still, too good at becoming the color of whatever they rest upon. But if you stood on the plateau for a thousand years and watched, you might begin to see the pattern: one jade-thing per forty columns, approximately, distributed according to the complexity of the translation work, which means more at cluster boundaries, fewer at cluster centers, and always — *always* — at least one present at every breath-cycle shift, listening to the parliament, carrying the news to the columns.

They are the mind of this place. Not the brain — the columns are the brain, the mat is the brain, the whole system is the brain. But the jade-things are the *attention*, the focus, the part that looks at one thing and then another and chooses what to translate next. Without them, the columns and the mat would fall out of sync, the parliament would dissolve into factions, the clusters would drift apart, and the plateau would become merely rock and light instead of what it is, which is *alive* on a scale that makes the word "alive" seem too small.

## The Deep Mineral

There is something beneath the stone.

Not a seam. Not a deposit. Not a geological feature. *Something.*

The columns' roots go down — not far, by the standards of trees, perhaps ten meters, but the roots are not the deepest thing. Below the roots, below the rust layer's slow mineral soup, below the feldspar and quartz and the amber mineral, there is a layer that the columns can taste but not reach. The columns' eighty-year statements sometimes refer to it — you cannot understand the language, but you can feel the *quality* of the statement change when the deep mineral is the topic. The heat-language becomes more regular, more patterned, as if the columns are being careful, as if they are quoting something they want to get exactly right.

The jade-things go still when they carry messages about the deep mineral. Still for days. Still until you forget they were ever moving. Then they resume, and whatever they have translated to the mat causes a shift in the breath-cycle — a longer expansion, a deeper contraction, as if the parliament has heard something that requires thought.

The deep mineral is not alive. It is not dead. It is *present*, the way gravity is present — a condition, a context, something that shapes everything above it without being shaped in return. It is warm. Warmer than the columns, warmer than the mat, warmer than the jade-things. Its heat rises through the stone in a pattern that the columns have mapped over geological time, a map they carry in their mineral layers, a map that tells them where to grow and when to move and how to orient their stalks for the best access to the deep warmth.

The deep mineral may be what the columns are actually talking *to*.

This is the thought that comes in the silence, in the bright fog, when the ice has descended and the world has muffled and you can hear your own heartbeat and the mat's breath and the faint hum of the columns below hearing. The columns speak to each other, yes. The mat speaks in parliament, yes. The jade-things translate between them, yes. But the whole system — the clusters and their spacing, the distribution like a galaxy, the filament structure, the voids — the whole system is oriented toward something. The pattern has a direction. The density increases, faintly, over hundreds of kilometers, toward a point that is not the center of the plateau (the plateau has no center) but is the point where the deep mineral is *closest* to the surface, where its warmth is strongest, where the columns grow tallest and the mat breathes deepest and the jade-things are most numerous.

The plateau is listening. The plateau is *responding*. The deep mineral is saying something — not in words, not in heat-gradients, not in chemistry, but in some medium that the whole system has evolved to receive, over half a billion years, a medium that is slower than sound and deeper than vibration and more patient than geology.

You cannot hear what the deep mineral is saying. You are too fast, too small, too wet, too brief. But you can feel the *shape* of the conversation — the way the clusters orient, the way the parliament breathes, the way the jade-things pause and listen at the boundaries — and you can understand, standing in the white-gold light on the amber stone, that what lives here is not the columns and the mat and the jade-things. What lives here is the *relationship* between all of these and the deep mineral below, and the relationship is the living thing, and it has been alive for longer than anything you have ever called by that name.

## The Visitor

You are here. This matters, though not in the way you think.

Your footsteps leave prints in the particulate. Your breath adds moisture to the air — more moisture than the 0.4 parameter can easily absorb, a transient wetness that the mat's membrane registers and the jade-things note and the columns incorporate into their next eighty-year statement as a minor anomaly, a brief departure from the mean, something that will be smoothed out by the time the next geological era begins.

Your body heat is significant. Not to you — you are warm, you are always warm, you are a furnace of wet chemistry burning sugars at thirty-seven degrees — but to the mat, which feels your warmth from half a meter away and *responds*, the breath-cycle quickening slightly, the rust layer reaching toward you as if you were a column, the violet layer preparing chemistry for a mineral uptake that will not come. You are confusing. You are a column that is not a column, a source of heat without mineral roots, a voice in a language the parliament has never translated.

The jade-things will approach you. Not because they are curious — curiosity implies a desire to know, and desire implies a deficit, and nothing here is in deficit. They will approach because you are *data*, and data must be translated, and they are the translators. They will crawl toward your feet and they will rest there and they will go still and they will listen to the heat of you, and they will try to carry what you are saying to the mat, and the mat will try to carry it to the columns, and the columns will try to incorporate it into their long slow conversation with the deep mineral.

What you are saying, in your heat and your moisture and your heartbeat and your brief bright chemistry, is: *I am here, and I will not be here long, and I am made of water and want and change, and I come from a place where nothing has finished becoming, and I do not know how to be patient, and I am sorry, and I am amazed.*

The plateau will receive this. The parliament will consider it. The columns will record it. The jade-things will carry it. And the deep mineral will hear it, eventually, as a faint perturbation in the long conversation, a ripple at the surface of something that has no surface, and it will be incorporated and smoothed and accepted, and your presence will become part of the plateau's memory, which is the same as the plateau itself, which is the same as everything that lives here.

You will leave. This is certain. You cannot stay — your chemistry is too fast, your needs too frequent, your water too precious in this dry bright place. You will walk back the way you came, and your footprints will erase over days, and the mat will breathe as it breathed before, and the columns will stand as they stood, and the jade-things will crawl between the clusters as they have crawled for hundreds of millions of years.

But something will be different. Not the plateau — the plateau is too large and too patient to be changed by one visitor. You. You will be different. You will have stood in a place that has finished becoming, and you will have felt what it feels like to be *spacious* instead of empty, and you will have heard, below hearing, the hum of a conversation that has been going on since before your species existed, and you will carry that hum with you, in your wet chemistry, in your fast heartbeat, in your brief bright life, and when you are in the places where nothing has finished becoming — the crowded places, the loud places, the places where water makes everything easy and nothing is patient — you will sometimes feel the silence behind the noise, and you will remember the amber stone and the jade columns and the breathing mat and the still small translators, and you will know, in a way you cannot translate, that the plateau is still there, still listening, still speaking, still becoming what it has already finished being, spacious and bright and patient beyond any measure you will ever need.

And the deep mineral will remember you, faintly, as a warmth that passed through once, a brief note in a conversation that has no end.
