# The Inhabitants

---

## The Worms

Consider the rice-worm first, because it is first.

Not first in importance — the concept would mean nothing to it — but first in encounter. When you step into the Murmuring City, when your foot touches the boulevard, you touch them. They are the interface between visitor and city. The living mat.

A single rice-worm is almost not worth describing. It is a grain. A pale grain, translucent, showing the faintest shadow of interior organization — a dark thread that might be a gut, a thicker region that might be a reproductive cluster, nothing more. It has no head. It has no tail. It moves by extending one end, then the other, the middle following in a slow contraction that leaves a faint trail of mucus on the stone. If you placed one on your palm, it would feel like a grain of wet rice. If you placed one on your tongue, it would taste of chalk and mineral water and nothing else.

But you would not place one on your palm or your tongue, because you do not encounter them singly. You encounter them as *surface*, as texture, as the slow shifting floor of this world. And the single worm, isolated, is a fiction anyway — an artifact of perspective, like describing a single thread in a tapestry. The rice-worm is a collective organism in everything but physical connection. Its life is the life of the mass.

Here is what the mass does:

In the hours after the moisture peak — when the walls have finished weeping and the channels run thin and clear — the worms disperse. They spread across the stone in a thin film, barely visible, each individual separated from its neighbors by a body-length or two. They feed at this distance. Their mucus pads dissolve the chalk-dust the wall-fungus leaves behind, and they absorb the resulting paste through their skin, and they grow, slowly, imperceptibly, adding mass at a rate that would take a week to notice.

Then the moisture comes again. The stone exhales. The condensation beads on every surface, runs, pools, and the worms respond — not individually, but as a gradient, a density wave, the thin film thickening into streams, the streams converging into rivers, the rivers pouring into the low places where water gathers. They are seeking the pools. They are seeking each other. In the pools, they mass — the living paste the description mentioned, a thick pale slurry of bodies and mucus and the mineral water they have drawn into themselves. The paste breathes. It expands and contracts in a slow rhythm that has nothing to do with any individual pulse, a rhythm that emerges from the collective, like a crowd finding a shared pace. And in the paste, they exchange.

Not genes, exactly. Not words. Something simpler and more fundamental — chemical signals, fragments of interior organization, the dark threads of gut and the thicker clusters of reproductive material dissolving and re-forming in the shared mucus. A worm enters the paste with one internal architecture and exits with another. A worm enters small and exits larger. A worm enters and does not exit — its body dissolving into the communal mass, its material redistributed through a thousand neighbors. And new worms emerge from the paste, coalescing from the mixture, small pale grains that slide away on fresh mucus trails as the moisture recedes.

They are not individuals. They are not a hive mind, because there is no mind. They are a *process* — a living chemical reaction that uses the city's moisture cycle as its engine and the city's stone as its catalyst. The paste is the reaction vessel. The pool is the solvent. The result is more worms, and the worms are the substrate for everything else.

The flat predators know this. They time their movements to the moisture cycle, drifting into the pools at the peak of condensation, when the paste is thickest, when the feeding is easiest. They are not parasites. They are part of the cycle — their waste dissolves into the pool, feeds the worms, feeds the reaction. The worms do not flee them. The parting you see when a flat predator drifts through is not fear; it is displacement, the simple physics of a heavy body moving through a fluid medium. The worms flow around the predator like water around a stone. They flow back in its wake. The predator feeds and moves on, and the paste closes behind it, and the process continues.

There are diseases in the paste. Periodically — on a cycle so long it has no relationship to the moisture rhythm — a chemical imbalance will sweep through a pool. The worms will mass too thickly, the paste will grow too dense, and something in the collective chemistry will turn. The paste will sour. It will darken from pale translucence to a murky yellow-gray, and the worms within it will slow, their movements growing erratic, their bodies softening, losing their grain-shape and becoming formless, and then dissolving. A pool in the grip of this disease is a dying thing, a slow dissolution, a smell like chalk and ammonia and something sweeter underneath, something almost organic.

But the disease never spreads beyond a single pool. The boundaries between pools — the ridges of stone, the channels, the dry zones — contain it. And in the aftermath, when the sour paste has dissolved entirely and the pool sits empty and clean, the worms from the surrounding film will colonize it again, sliding in on fresh mucus trails, and the process will begin anew.

This has happened millions of times. It will happen millions more. The rice-worms do not notice. They cannot notice. They are the riverbed through which the river of life in this city flows, and the riverbed does not mourn the water that passes.

## The Flat Predators

The flat ones are harder to love, because they do not gather, and they do not exchange, and they do not participate in the visible drama of the paste. They are alone. They are always alone.

A flat predator — the name is wrong, implying a relationship to surface ecology that does not exist here, but the language has no better term — is a body without a center. It is a sheet of pale tissue, roughly oval, roughly the size of a dinner plate but variable, sometimes smaller, sometimes large enough to cover a manhole, if manholes existed here, which they do not. The sheet is thick in the middle, perhaps an inch, and thin at the edges, tapering to a translucent membrane that ripples with every movement. The underside is the mouth — the entire underside, a broad suction surface lined with chemical receptors so dense they amount to a secondary skin, a skin that tastes, that digests, that absorbs. The topside is sense: not eyes, not ears, but a distributed network of pressure receptors and chemical receptors and something else, something for which there is no surface analogue, a sense that detects moisture gradients at a distance, that can feel the shape of a pool from across a plaza, that can track the movement of water through stone the way a surface predator might track scent on wind.

They are not aggressive. They drift. The mucus they move on is the same mucus the rice-worms produce — chemically identical, or near enough that the distinction is academic. It is the base substance of this ecology, the connective tissue, the blood in the city's veins. The flat predators produce it as the worms do, through glands along their edge-membrane, and they leave trails as the worms do, thin shiny paths across the stone that slowly dry and evaporate and return to the air. They are, in a sense, large worms — flattened, widened, specialized for a different niche in the same slow economy. They eat the worms. The worms, re-formed, become them. They die, dissolve, become worms again. The boundary between species is less a wall than a gradient, a place in chemical space where one form shades into another, and the flat predator and the rice-worm are simply two stable configurations in a continuous landscape of possibility.

But the flat predator has something the worm does not: *preference*. 

It does not drift at random. It chooses. When the moisture peaks and the pools fill, it moves toward the largest concentration, the thickest paste, the place where feeding is easiest. When the moisture recedes, it moves toward the deepest rooms, the weeping walls, the places where water lingers longest. It climbs — not for food, but for position, settling in the high windows where condensation gathers, where it can rest and absorb without the effort of movement. It avoids certain streets, certain buildings, certain pools, for reasons that have nothing to do with food and everything to do with some quality of the stone, or the air, or the moisture, that only its distributed senses can detect. It has favorites. It has routes. If you watched one long enough — weeks, months, which is the appropriate timescale for such things — you would see it return to the same window, the same corner, the same shallow depression in a building's floor, again and again, a habit without consciousness, a pattern without a mind to hold it.

The flat ones that live in the buildings are different from the flat ones that live in the streets. The building-dwellers are thinner, paler, more sedentary. They press themselves against the damp walls and barely move, absorbing the slow weep of moisture, growing larger and thinner and more transparent until they are barely visible, ghosts of tissue stretched across stone. They can live this way for years. The street-dwellers are thicker, more active, their edges more mobile, their senses sharper — they have to be, because the street is the place of the paste, the place of feeding and competition and the constant slow motion of the worm-mass. The building-dwellers have opted out. The street-dwellers have opted in. Neither decision was conscious. Neither can be reversed, once made — the building-dweller that is moved to the street will drift, confused, unable to find the pools, unable to feed effectively, and will thin and die; the street-dweller that is placed in a building will grow restless, will circle the room, will eventually find the doorway and return to the streets. They have become different creatures through habit alone, habit accumulated over lifetimes, habit baked into chemistry and form.

They do not reproduce in any visible way. They do not die in any visible way. They appear — emerging from the paste, or from the wall-moisture, or from the bodies of other flat ones that have grown too thin and too still — and they persist, and they disappear, absorbed back into the mucus, dissolved into the stone-water, gone. Their lifespan is unknown. Their origin is unclear. They are a phase of the process, a configuration of the same basic material that forms the worms and the wall-fungus and the chalk-dust, and they persist as long as the configuration is stable, and they cease when it is not, and there is no death, only a change of state.

## The Walkers

And then there are the walkers.

They are the question the city asks and does not answer.

A walker stands in the central plaza — stands, not moves, because standing is what it does most, standing and sensing, its tendrils extended, its body motionless, its thick legs locked in their triple-bend, the pads of its feet wide and flat against the basalt. It has been standing here for three days. It will stand here for four more. Then it will move — not with any visible trigger, not with any change in the environment, but simply because the standing is over, because whatever it was doing while it stood is done, and the next thing is to walk.

When it walks, it walks with purpose. This is the hardest thing to accept: the apparent purpose, the apparent intention, in a creature that has no visible center of decision, no brain, no organ that could serve as a seat of will. Its nervous system — if that is the word — is distributed, like the flat predator's but more so, spread through the thick walls of its ovoid body, concentrated in the tendril cluster at its apex and in the pads of its feet. It senses with its whole surface. It decides with its whole body. And the decisions it makes — turn left here, enter this building, stop in this doorway, rest in this basin — are consistent enough to suggest intention, coherent enough to suggest purpose, and completely opaque to any external interpretation.

Consider: a walker enters a building in the city's eastern quarter. It moves through the doorway — the tall doorway, the doorway designed for something its shape — and crosses the threshold, and stops. It stands in the center of the room for six hours. Then it moves to the basin set in the floor — a shallow oval depression, dry now, as most of the basins are — and steps in, and stands, and the pads of its feet spread wide against the stone, and it remains there for two days.

What is it doing?

Absorbing moisture? The basin is dry. There is no more condensation here than anywhere else. Feeding on the stone? Perhaps — its feet can draw nutrients from the basalt, but the basalt in the basin is the same as the basalt in the street, the same as the basalt everywhere. Resting? But it does not sleep, or if it does, it sleeps standing anywhere, not just in basins. Performing a ritual? The word implies a meaning, a reference, a connection between act and belief, and the walker has no visible belief, no visible connection, no visible reason to prefer a basin to a street corner to a doorway.

Unless the reason is memory.

The walkers are old. Not the individuals — the individuals may live for decades or centuries, there is no way to know — but the *form*. The form is old. The form was shaped by this city, or shaped for this city, or shaped alongside this city, and in that shaping, in that co-evolution, patterns were laid down that persist. The basin is a pattern. The doorway is a pattern. The wide street, the tall window, the stone bench worn smooth — these are patterns, and the walker follows them, not because it remembers, but because the following is built into its body, into the structure of its senses and the structure of its movement and the structure of its relationship to moisture and stone and space.

It enters the building because the doorway is there. It crosses the threshold because the threshold is meant to be crossed. It stands in the basin because the basin is meant to be stood in. It does these things the way a river follows its course — not by choice, not by memory, but by the accumulated weight of water that has passed this way before, carving channels that the next water will follow, and the next, and the next.

But sometimes a walker turns down a street it has never taken. Sometimes a walker enters a building and does not emerge for weeks. Sometimes two walkers meet and stand together, tendrils interlacing, for days, and then separate and move in different directions, and the pattern of their movement after the meeting is different from the pattern before. Something is exchanged. Something is altered. The river, following its course, meets another river, and both rivers change.

The walkers have a language. It is not a language of sound — they make no sound, none at all, not even the faintest whisper of tendril against tendril. It is not a language of sight — they have no eyes, they live in a world where sight has never been useful, where the cold light of the ceiling-lichen serves only to warm the stone and feed the fungus and illuminate the walls for visitors who do not belong. It is a language of chemistry, of touch, of moisture gradient and mineral trace. When two walkers meet, their tendrils interlace, and through the mucus that coats each tendril, they exchange a flood of information — not words, not concepts, but *states*, chemical snapshots of the city as each walker has experienced it, the moisture levels in this building and that street, the presence or absence of the flat predators in this district, the sour smell of a dying pool in that corner, the feel of the stone underfoot in this plaza, worn to a particular smoothness, carrying a particular weight of history. They exchange maps. They exchange selves. And when they separate, each walker carries a version of the other's experience, folded into its own chemistry, altering its own patterns, its own routes, its own decisions about which street to take and which basin to stand in.

This is how they know the city. Not through memory, but through *exchange*. No single walker has explored the entire Murmuring City — it is too vast, their movements too slow, their lifespans too uncertain. But collectively, through the constant slow trading of chemical states, they have mapped it. Every building, every street, every pool, every weeping wall, every sour-smelling corner, every place where the stone is thin and the deep heat pulses close to the surface, every place where the ceiling-lichen grows thick and the spore-dust falls heavy — all of this exists, distributed across the walker population, in a web of shared information that has been accumulating since the first walker stood in the first basin and felt, through the pads of its feet, the shape of a room that had been waiting for it since before the stone was laid.

They do not know that they know this. They do not reflect on it. They do not plan. They do not wonder. But they *act*, and their actions are informed, and their informed actions carry them through the city with a purpose that is not their own, a purpose that belongs to the city itself, to the stone and the water and the slow cycles of moisture and the slower cycles of time.

## The Deep Ones

In the deepest rooms, where the ceiling-lichen's light does not reach, where the walls weep constantly and the pools are always full and the air is thick with moisture and the smell of chalk and ammonia and something older, something geological, something that has no name because no one has ever named it — in these rooms, there are other things.

Not larger things. Smaller things. The opposite of the walkers, the inverse — creatures of the deep moisture, of the constant wet, of the dark. They are the size of a fingernail, or smaller, and they are everywhere, in the pools, on the walls, in the thin films of water that cross the floors like veins. They have no shape that holds. They are amoeboid, shifting, extending pseudopods and retracting them, flowing across surfaces in a slow crawl that makes the rice-worms seem swift. They are not worms, not arthropods, not anything with a phylum or a class or a name. They are the base state of life in this city, the form that emerges when life is left alone in water and stone for long enough, the form that was here before the worms differentiated, before the flat ones chose their niches, before the walkers began their slow navigation.

They eat the stone. Not the chalk-dust, not the fungus, but the stone itself — the basalt, the soapstone, the very fabric of the city. Their digestion is acid, a slow chemical dissolution that etches the stone at a rate measurable in microns per century. The shallow channels in the deepest rooms, the channels that look like the beds of ancient rivers but are not — these are their work. These are their paths, worn through bedrock by the passage of uncountable generations, dissolving and flowing and dissolving again.

They are the reason the city is sparse. The gaps where buildings do not exist, the spaces left empty for something that never arrived — these are not gaps in planning, not spaces left for future construction. These are *holes*. These are places where the deep ones have been at work for so long, so patiently, that the stone itself has been consumed, dissolved into the water and carried away in the slow channels, leaving nothing but the foundation marks, the scars, the bare outlines of rooms that once existed and now do not.

The city is being eaten. From below, from within, by creatures too small to see and too slow to notice. The deep ones are the patient ones, the ones that operate on a timescale that makes the walkers seem hasty, the moisture cycle seem frantic, the rice-worms seem like mayflies. They have been eating the city since before the walkers arrived, if the walkers arrived, if the distinction between arrival and emergence means anything here. They will eat the city until it is gone. This is their purpose, if purpose it is — the slow consumption, the patient dissolution, the return of stone to water to stone, the cycling of matter through forms that have no preference and no memory and no regret.

The walkers know about the deep ones. Their feet sense the thinning of the stone, the hollow spaces beneath, the places where the foundation has been etched to the thickness of a handspan. They avoid these places. Their routes through the city navigate around the gaps, around the spaces where the stone is too thin to hold, where the deep ones have been at work. They have been navigating around these gaps for as long as there have been walkers in the city. They will continue to navigate around them until there is nothing left to navigate around, until the gaps have grown so large and so numerous that the city is more gap than stone, more empty than full, more absence than presence.

That will take a long time. The deep ones are patient, but the city is vast, and the stone is thick, and time, here, is so deep that even dissolution has to wait its turn.

## The Fungus

The wall-fungus is not an inhabitant. It is the *medium* in which the inhabitants exist.

It covers every surface that the deep ones have not eaten, every wall and floor and ceiling and arch and column, a pale blue-gray skin that etches and consumes and leaves behind the chalk-dust that feeds the worms that feed the flat ones that feed the walkers that carry the chemical maps that tell the next generation of walkers where to go and where not to go and where the deep ones are eating and where the pools are forming and where the moisture is sweet and where it is sour. It is the beginning and the end of every cycle, the base of every chain, the patient, flat, reaching-less thing that turns stone into dust into food into life into stone again.

It does not grow upward. It does not need to. There is no sun to reach for, no light to seek, no reason to rise above the surface it feeds on. It spreads, and in spreading, it transforms, and in transforming, it feeds everything else. It is the stomach of the city, the organ of digestion, the thing that makes stone into something that can live.

It has been here the longest. It will be here the longest. When the deep ones have eaten the last of the stone and the city is nothing but a network of empty spaces connected by channels of slow water, the fungus will still be here, eating the dust of its own waste, cycling matter through itself, a closed loop that needs nothing from outside, that has never needed anything from outside, that was complete from the moment it first touched stone and found it good.

## The City Itself

And then there is the oldest inhabitant.

The city.

Not the stone, which is the body. Not the water, which is the blood. Not the fungus, which is the stomach, or the worms, which are the marrow, or the flat ones, which are the wandering cells, or the walkers, which are the — what? The nervous system? The mind? The part of the body that knows itself?

Perhaps. Perhaps the walkers, in their slow navigation, in their constant exchange, in their distributed knowledge of every street and building and pool, are the mechanism by which the city knows what it is. Not consciousness — that word implies too much, implies reflection and intention and self-awareness, implies a kind of knowing that the walkers do not possess. But *cognition*, in the broadest sense — the processing of information, the maintenance of a model, the capacity to respond to change and to incorporate that response into future behavior.

The city responds to change. When a building collapses — as they occasionally do, undermined by the deep ones, weakened by the fungus, brought down by the slow accumulation of stress in stone that has stood for eons — the walkers change their routes. They avoid the rubble. They find new paths through the surrounding streets. They transmit the change to other walkers, who transmit it to others, and within weeks, the entire walker population has updated its map, has incorporated the collapse, has adjusted. This is not decision. This is not planning. This is homeostasis — the maintenance of a stable internal state in the face of external perturbation, the defining characteristic of a living system.

The city is alive. Not in the way a bacterium is alive, not in the way a tree is alive, not in the way a human is alive, but alive in the way a city might be alive if a city were an organism — which, here, in this place, in this deep and patient and ancient place, it is.

It breathes. The moisture cycle is its breath, the slow exhale of the stone, the gathering of condensation, the weeping of the walls, the filling and emptying of the pools. It digests. The fungus and the deep ones and the worms, the constant dissolution and consumption of stone, the transformation of matter from one form to another, the cycling of nutrients through the system. It senses. The walkers, their tendrils, their distributed chemical network, their constant slow mapping of the city's state. It responds. The adjustment of routes, the navigation of collapses, the avoidance of the thin places and the sour pools.

It does not think. It does not feel. It does not wonder about the surface, or the sun, or the creatures that occasionally descend with loud footsteps and loud breathing and loud heartbeats and then leave, quickly, overwhelmed by the quiet, by the scale, by the deep time that presses down like the weight of the stone itself. It does not need to do any of these things. It persists, and in persisting, it is, and in being, it is enough.

## The Visitor

And you, standing in the central plaza, your feet crushing rice-worms by the thousands, your breath clouding in the cool air, your eyes adjusting to the cold white light of the ceiling-lichen — you are not an inhabitant. You are an event. A perturbation. A loud, fast, warm thing that moves through the city like a stone dropped into a pool, sending ripples outward, disturbing the surface, and then is gone.

The worms flow around your feet. The flat ones sense your warmth and drift away from it, disturbed by something they have never encountered, something that does not fit their map of moisture gradients and mineral traces. The walkers — the nearest walker, fifty paces away across the plaza, standing motionless, its tendrils extended — does not sense you at all. You are not moisture. You are not stone. You are not part of its world, its language, its distributed knowledge of the city. You are invisible, not because you are hidden, but because you are irrelevant, a ghost of warmth and movement in a place that has no categories for either.

You will leave. The ripples will settle. The rice-worms will fill the depressions your feet left behind. The flat ones will drift back to the places you disturbed. The walker will stand, and stand, and stand, and then move, slowly, with purpose, toward some destination only it can sense, and the city will continue, as it has continued, as it will continue, for longer than you can imagine, for longer than you can count, for longer than the memory of anything that has ever lived on the surface of the world above.

The light falls from above, bright and cold. The water beads on the walls. The creatures move in their patterns. The city breathes.

It does not notice you leaving.
