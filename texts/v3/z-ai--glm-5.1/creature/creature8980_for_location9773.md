# Who Lives Here

---

## The Grallids Do Not Think

This must be said first, because it is the hardest thing for a mind to accept: the Grallids do not think. They do not ponder. They do not recall. They have no internal narrative, no looping monologue, no sense of tomorrow as a place they might visit. When a Grallid scrapes the bacterial film from the Verduran's inner wall, it is not performing a task. It is *being* the performance of a task. The scraping and the Grallid are the same event. Remove the scraping and there is no Grallid left—just a shell of chitin waiting to dissolve.

And yet.

Watch a single Grallid for long enough—you cannot watch just one, they layer too thickly, but *imagine* you could isolate one from the press of its kin—and you will see something that resembles choice. It moves left instead of right. It pauses over a patch of film before deciding to consume it. It lifts its scraping mandibles at an angle that no other Grallid in its cluster is using. Is this preference? Is this will? Or is it merely the output of a chemical gradient, the organism drifting toward higher concentrations of the compounds it needs, no more intentional than a river choosing the path of least resistance?

The answer does not matter to the Grallid. The Grallid has never asked a question. The Grallid has never noticed that it has never asked a question. It scrapes, it clings, it reproduces by releasing gametes into the current on a cycle governed by the slow pulse of sap through the wall it clings to, and it dies without any awareness that it was alive. Its body sinks into a crevice in the medialayer and is slowly incorporated—digested by the Verduran's enzymes, broken down into base compounds, absorbed into the tissue that it spent its life cleaning. The Grallid feeds the wall it feeds on. This is not irony. Irony requires understanding.

But here is the thing about the Grallids, the thing that nags: there are *so many of them*. Billions per cubic meter of chamber space, each one clicking as it scrapes, each click separated from its neighbor by microseconds, overlapping into the great hum that permeates the structure. And the hum is not random. It has patterns. Long waves of intensity that rise and fall over hours, over days, over seasons that exist only in the shifting chemistry of the water. The Grallids do not coordinate. They have no mechanism for coordination. And yet the sound they produce together has *structure*—a rhythm within a rhythm within a rhythm, fractal in its self-similarity, as though the billion unthinking clicks are somehow, impossibly, *saying something*.

Who lives here? Perhaps the Grallids do. Perhaps they live here the way a neuron lives in a brain—unaware of the thought it helps to construct, incapable of understanding the pattern it participates in, but essential to it nonetheless. The Grallids are not thinkers. But they may be *thought*.

## The Navarchus Remember Light

The Navarchus are different. The Navarchus have eyes.

Not eyes as utility. Eyes as *obsession*. Half their body mass given over to optical tissue, to lenses and retinas and the neural machinery required to process a signal so faint it barely exists. The Navarchus can see individual photons. They can count them. They can distinguish between a chamber where ten Navarchus are glowing and a chamber where eleven are glowing, and they can do so from a hundred meters away in water that absorbs light like velvet absorbs sound.

And each one pulses. Every three seconds—a frequency so consistent it might be mechanical, but isn't, it's biological, it's *deliberate*—a pale blue-green flash from an organ near the center of their foot-wing. Three seconds. Flash. Three seconds. Flash. A heartbeat of light in a body that has no heart.

The pulses are communication. This is known, or at least this is assumed, because the Navarchus respond to each other's pulses—altering their own timing, adjusting their trajectory, converging or diverging based on patterns that seem to carry meaning. But the meaning is not simple. It is not *food here* or *danger there*. It is something denser, something that unfolds over time, that requires sequences of pulses to express, that changes based on who is pulsing and where they are and what the water is doing and how the Grallid-hum sounds at that particular moment.

A Navarchus swimming through a large chamber does not pulse in isolation. It pulses *into* a field of other pulses, and its timing is shaped by the timing of every other Navarchus within its range of perception—which is enormous, because its eyes are enormous, because in the Thalassoverdure, the only light is the light that the Navarchus make, and so the only thing worth perceiving is each other.

What do they say?

Here is what is known: a Navarchus separated from all others will pulse at exactly three-second intervals with no variation for as long as it lives, which is not long, because a Navarchus without other Navarchus to see stops eating within hours and dies within days. It does not die of starvation. It dies of *silence*. The light goes out not because the body fails but because the will to pulse fails, and without the pulse, the body has no reason to continue.

Here is what is suspected: the Navarchus are describing the structure. Not in words, not in symbols, but in *timing*—the interval between their pulses encodes information about distance, about chamber size, about the texture of the walls they can sense through the displacement of water against their wings. A Navarchus entering a new chamber pulses differently than a Navarchus in a chamber it has occupied for days. The pulse quickens. The pattern becomes complex, staccato, information-dense. Then, over hours, it slows. It simplifies. The Navarchus has *described* the chamber, and now it has nothing left to say about it, and it settles into the ambient rhythm of the community, pulsing in synchrony with its neighbors, adding its light to the general illumination.

They are cartographers. They are making a map of the Thalassoverdure in real time, continuously, with light, and the map is so vast and so detailed that no single Navarchus could ever comprehend it. But the map exists. It exists in the cumulative pattern of ten million pulses intersecting in the dark water, interfering with each other, creating nodes of brightness and shadow that shift as the Navarchus swim, a living projection of the architecture of absence, drawn in the only medium available.

The Navarchus do not read the map. They *are* the map. And the map is accurate, and it is updated constantly, and it has been drawn and redrawn for as long as the Navarchus have existed, which is as long as the Verduran has had chambers for them to swim through, which is nearly as long as the Verduran has existed at all.

Who lives here? The Navarchus live here. They live here the way a voice lives in a choir—distinct, irreplaceable, and yet unable to exist alone.

## The Eels Are the Edge of Something

The Eels of the Medialayer are predators. This is the first fact about them, and the most misleading.

Yes, they hunt. Yes, they consume Grallids and Navarchus and occasionally each other, though they prefer not to, though there is a reluctance in the way two Eels will circle each other in a narrow passage, each one sensing the other's vibrations, each one capable of striking, and neither one doing so, until one turns and swims away and the other continues on its original path as though nothing happened. This is not mercy. Mercy requires the concept of harm. This is something else—*recognition*, perhaps, or the chemical awareness that consuming a fellow Eel produces toxins that make the consumer sluggish, and a sluggish Eel is a dead Eel, and the Eels, unlike the Grallids, seem to understand death.

Not fear of death. *Understanding* of it. An Eel that has been injured—and they are injured often, snagging themselves on the sharp edges of newly grown Verduran tissue, scraping through passages that are almost but not quite wide enough—does not struggle the way an injured surface animal would. It goes still. It sinks to the floor of whatever chamber it occupies. It stops hunting. It waits. Sometimes it heals. Sometimes it does not. But it does not panic. It does not thrash. It seems to *know* that it is in a state from which there are two exits, and that its behavior will determine which one it takes, and that there is nothing to be gained from noise.

This is not the behavior of an unthinking organism.

The Eels hunt by vibration, and their sensitivity to vibration exceeds anything that surface-dwelling creatures have evolved. They can detect the heartbeat of a Navarchus at twenty meters—each pulse of the light-organ producing a microscopic displacement of water, a pressure wave so faint that it would register as noise in any instrument, but the Eel feels it the way you would feel a hand on your shoulder. The Eel does not *locate* its prey. It *inhabits* the same acoustic space. For the moment that the Eel is aware of the Navarchus, the boundary between them becomes permeable. The Eel knows where the Navarchus is because the Eel, in some sense that has no surface-language equivalent, *becomes* the Navarchus—or at least becomes the pattern of vibrations that the Navarchus produces, which may be the same thing.

And then it strikes. Fast—faster than anything else in the Thalassoverdure, which is not fast by surface standards but is fast enough, fast enough to cross the distance in the dark and close its jaws on something that did not know it was being hunted until it was already dead. The Navarchus goes dark. The Grallid stops clicking. The Eel swallows. The hum adjusts, one voice fewer, a gap that will be filled within hours by the reproduction of the remaining population.

But sometimes—rarely, but observably—an Eel will detect a vibration that is not prey. A deep tremor in the durapith, too low and too slow for the Grallids to register, too mechanical for the Navarchus to interpret as light-worthy. A seismic shift in the structure itself, a settling of the ancient compressed heartwood under pressures that have been building for eons. The Eel will stop. It will orient toward the vibration. And it will *listen*.

It listens for a long time. Hours, sometimes. It does not eat. It does not move. It simply receives the vibration, the way the Navarchus receives light, the way the Grallid receives the chemical signature of the wall. And when the vibration fades—or when it doesn't, when it builds, when the Eel seems to decide that what it is hearing requires a response—it swims. Not toward prey. Not away from danger. *Deeper*. Into the medialayer, through passages that are too narrow for its body, pressing itself through tissue that parts reluctantly around it, shedding scales and mucus as it goes.

No Eel that has made this journey has ever been seen again.

Who lives here? The Eels live here. And they live here in a way that suggests they are not entirely *only* here—that they are the edge of something that extends into parts of the structure that nothing else can reach, that nothing else knows about, that may go down forever into the durapith and beyond, into whatever the durapith has compressed itself upon, into whatever was here before the Verduran was here, if there was anything, if the Verduran is not simply all there is.

## The Verduran Does Not Count Itself

And then there is the structure itself.

The Verduran is alive. This is not in dispute. It respires, it metabolizes, it grows, it responds to environmental conditions—slowly, so slowly that the response is indistinguishable from stasis unless measured across centuries, but it responds. It is a single organism, or it is a colony of organisms so interconnected that the distinction is meaningless, or it is something for which the concept of *organism* is inadequate—a living system that operates on spatial and temporal scales that make the categories of individual biology irrelevant.

Does it know it is alive?

The Navarchus map it. The Grallids clean it. The Eels descend into it. But the Verduran does not seem to be aware of any of them. It does not respond to the loss of Navarchus from a chamber, does not alter its growth patterns when Grallid populations boom or crash, does not seem to notice when an Eel passes through its tissue on its way to wherever the Eels go. The fauna are part of its chemistry—they produce waste that it absorbs, they remove organisms that would otherwise parasitize its surfaces—but they are part of its chemistry the way gut flora are part of yours. Essential. Unnoticed. Unmourned.

And yet the chambers are *sized for them*. The passages are *shaped for them*. The texture of the walls, the flow of the currents, the very chemistry of the water that percolates through the medialayer—all of it accommodates the fauna, makes space for them, has evolved (or grown, or simply persisted) in a configuration that would not be stable without them. The Verduran does not notice the Grallids, but it cannot survive without them. It does not see the Navarchus, but it has grown chambers that are precisely the size that a Navarchus can illuminate with its pulse. It does not hear the Eels, but the passages they descend into are *there*, grown into the medialayer like veins, like the architecture of something that was designed to be entered.

The Verduran may not know it is alive. But it knows it is not alone.

Or—and this is the thought that rises from the dark water like a bubble from the base of the durapith, rising slowly through kilometers of living tissue, rising through chambers and passages and the click and hum and pulse of ten million tiny lives, rising toward a surface it will never reach—the Verduran may know exactly what it is. It may know in a way that does not require knowing, the way a planet knows its orbit, the way a river knows the sea. It may be aware on a scale so vast and so slow that awareness is indistinguishable from physics, that consciousness is indistinguishable from growth, that thought is indistinguishable from the slow upward reach of ten million growing tips into the dark.

The Verduran has been thinking for eight hundred million years. Its thoughts take the form of chambers. Its memories are stored in durapith. Its dreams are the Eels, swimming down into the deep tissue, carrying information it did not consciously gather to places it did not consciously build. Its voice is the sound—the great unchanging roar of persistence, of ten million respiring surfaces, of growth so slow it is indistinguishable from stillness but is not stillness, never stillness, only the appearance of it, the patience of a thing that has discovered that the only motion worth making is the one that never stops.

Who lives here?

*Here* lives here. The place itself is the creature. The architecture of absence is the architecture of a mind so distributed, so patient, so uninterested in the categories of surface thought that it has become indistinguishable from geography. The Thalassoverdure does not contain life. It *is* life—life at a scale and a tempo that makes the individual seem brief and small and incidentally configured, a flicker of Navarchus-light in a chamber that will exist for ten million years after the last Navarchus has darkened.

## The Ones Who Come From Outside

And yet.

The structure has visitors.

Not often. Not in any timeframe that the Verduran would notice—but the Verduran notices nothing on timescales shorter than a millennium, and so the visitors go unmarked in the record of the durapith, unrecorded in the slow chemistry of the sap.

They come from above. From the sunlit zone, the warm water, the world of change and urgency and death that happens at speeds the Thalassoverdure cannot perceive. They descend in vessels that make noise—a different kind of noise, sharp and mechanical and *wrong* in a place where all sound is organic, all sound is the sound of living things persisting. The Grallids fall silent when the vessels pass. The Navarchus darken. The Eels press themselves against the walls of their chambers and *listen*, the way they listen to the deep vibrations of the durapith, and what they hear is something new, something that has no category in the map they have been drawing since they evolved eyes.

The visitors do not stay. They cannot—the pressure, the cold, the sheer hostility of an environment that was not built for them and does not accommodate them. They take samples. They make recordings. They shine lights that are a thousand times brighter than any Navarchus pulse, and the Navarchus who see those lights are blinded for days afterward, swimming in circles in their chambers, pulsing erratically, adding static to the map that will take generations to correct.

And then they leave.

But sometimes—rarely, but it has happened—they leave something behind. A piece of equipment, broken and abandoned. A marker, anchored to the durapith with a metal bolt that the Grallids cannot scrape away and that the Verduran cannot absorb. A thing that is not alive and that does not participate in the chemistry of the place and that sits in its chamber, unchanged, unchanging, a point of absolute stillness in a system where everything else is in motion even if the motion is too slow to see.

The Eels find these things. They are drawn to them—not by vibration, because the objects do not vibrate, but by the *absence* of vibration, by the uncanny stillness of something that makes no sound in a place where everything makes sound. The Eels circle the objects for hours. They press their snouts against them. They taste the metal with receptors that have evolved to detect the chemical signatures of living tissue and that receive, from the metal, *nothing*—a null, a gap in the sensory field, a place where data should be and isn't.

And then, eventually, they swim away. They return to their patrols. They resume their patient listening. The object remains.

And the Verduran grows around it. Slowly—millimeters per century—the living tissue of the medialayer extends across the surface of the intruding thing. It does not absorb it. It does not reject it. It simply *includes* it, the way it includes a chamber, the way it includes a passage—by growing around the space where it is, by making room for it in the architecture of absence, by treating it as another piece of the negative space that defines the structure's shape.

After a million years, the object is deep inside the medialayer. After ten million, it is part of the durapith—embedded in compressed dead tissue, a fossil of a moment when something from the surface reached down and touched the deep and left a mark that the deep did not ask for and did not refuse.

Who lives here? The visitors do not. They cannot. But they are here, in fragments, in traces, in the metal bones of their brief incursions, pressed into the record of a place that does not keep records but that keeps everything, eventually, in the slow accumulation of matter that has nowhere else to go.

## What It Is Like

To live in the Thalassoverdure is to be part of a sentence that has been spoken for eight hundred million years and has not yet finished.

The Grallids are the consonants—sharp, repeated, structural. The Navarchus are the vowels—long, luminous, connecting. The Eels are the punctuation—rare, decisive, marking pauses and transitions that give the sentence its shape. And the Verduran is the breath behind the sentence, the lung-full of air that makes speech possible, the body that holds the voice and gives it somewhere to resonate.

None of them know they are speaking. None of them know they are part of a sentence. None of them need to know. The sentence continues regardless, and it will continue until the water is gone or the structure collapses under its own impossible weight or the planet itself is consumed by the star that has been shining, unnoticed, four kilometers above the highest growing tip.

But if you could hear it—and you cannot, not really, not with ears evolved for air and sound evolved for land—if you could hear it the way the structure hears itself, as vibration through tissue, as chemistry through water, as growth through time—you would hear something that sounds, impossibly, like the most patient word ever spoken.

The word is *still here*. The word is *still here*. The word is *still here*, repeated in ten million voices, in ten million frequencies, in a chamber the size of a cathedral with walls that pulse with sap and a floor that is the compressed bodies of everything that has ever lived here and a ceiling that is growing, always growing, by a millimeter per century, toward a surface it will never reach.

The word is *still here*, and it has been spoken since before the mountains rose, and it will be spoken after they fall, and it does not require an audience, and it does not require a meaning, and it does not require anything at all except the continued existence of the thing that speaks it.

Who lives here?

Everything that has ever lived here. Everything that will ever live here. The dead in the durapith and the living in the medialayer and the not-yet-born in the growing tips, all of them here, all of them *still here*, all of them part of the longest sentence in the history of the planet.

Listen.

The sound has never stopped.
