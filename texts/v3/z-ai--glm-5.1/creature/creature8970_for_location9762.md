What lives here lives against the walls, and what lives here lives in the walls, and what lives here *is* the walls — and these are three different things, though the Throat does not distinguish between them.

---

The springtails know the boundaries of their worlds with a precision no cartographer could match. Each colony — and they are colonies, family-groups extending across four or five generations of the tiny arthropods — occupies a single moss-cushion or fern-root mass and knows every millimeter of its territory. They can feel where the dampness changes. They can taste the mineral gradient where seep-water meets stone. They do not cross from one garden to the next because there is nothing for them in the bare stone zones — no food, no moisture, no shelter from the vibration that must feel to them like an earthquake that never ends.

But they know the bare zones are there. They know because when the condensation peaks in the cold months and the walls weep their heaviest, a springtail from one colony will occasionally — rarely, perhaps once in a generation — ride a rivulet of water across a stretch of bare stone and arrive at a new garden. If the garden is unoccupied, or if its colony is small enough to absorb a stranger, the traveler stays. Genes flow between the isolated gardens like whispers between rooms, carried on the Throat's slow breath.

This is how the moss-gardens remain viable. This is how life persists in isolation: by sending out travelers on the rare occasions when the conditions align, by accepting strangers when they arrive damp and trembling on the threshold of a new wall-garden. The ecosystems of the Throat are pocket worlds, but they are not sealed worlds. They communicate through the medium of the place itself — through water, through stone-seep, through the patience of waiting for a moment when crossing becomes possible.

The spiders understand this too. The sheet-web builders that anchor their silk across the dripping crevices are strategic about where they build. They choose the seams where condensation is reliable and where the springtails are most likely to leap or fall, and they rebuild after every heavy-damp period when the increased water flow damages their constructions. A spider in the Throat spends half its life building and half its life waiting, and it does not distinguish between the two activities. Building is waiting with silk. Waiting is building with patience.

The spiders are the only creatures in the Throat that seem to respond to the sound. Not to its presence — everything here is shaped by the presence of the sound — but to its variations, the ones so subtle that human ears cannot detect them. When the hidden river's roar shifts by a fraction, perhaps as the water finds a new path through the stone below, the spiders adjust the tension of their webs. It is not a startle response. It is a calibration. They are listening with their silk, reading the sound the way a sailor reads a sail, making small adjustments to account for changes in the medium they inhabit. What they learn from the sound, they do not share. But they remember. A spider in the Throat will rebuild its web in the same configuration after a collapse, restoring the tension to the same degree, unless the sound has changed — in which case the new web will be subtly different, tuned to the new acoustic reality.

---

The pale fungi are harder to understand.

They grow toward the sound — this is observable, documented, repeated across the accessible length of the gorge. The shelf-fungi on the walls facing the hidden river are larger and more numerous than those on the opposite walls, even when the organic substrate is identical. The vibration favors them, or they favor the vibration, or something in their metabolism has learned to use the mechanical energy of the roar the way surface fungi use sunlight.

But they also grow in patterns that do not correspond to any obvious environmental factor. A colony on a ledge near the gorge's narrow end will extend its fruiting bodies in a configuration that seems almost deliberate — radiating outward from a central point, each shelf angled slightly toward the next, creating a structure that catches and holds the condensation with an efficiency that pure chance could not produce. Another colony, ten meters further, will grow in a tight spiral. Another will form a row, precise as a shelf of books, each fruiting body exactly the same distance from its neighbor.

It is tempting to see intention in this. It is tempting to look at the spiral and the row and the radial array and think: *something is being said*. And perhaps something is. The fungi are processing the organic debris that falls into their ledges — moss fragments, fern spores, insect remains, the occasional feather that drifts down from the kittiwake nests above — and they are processing the sound that rises through the stone, and they are processing the dampness that varies with the seasonal breath of the Throat, and perhaps the patterns they produce are a record of what they have processed, a notation in a language that writes itself in growth and angle and spacing.

Or perhaps they are simply growing as fungi grow, and the patterns are the inevitable result of a simple set of rules encountering a complex set of conditions. The Throat does not clarify. The Throat is not in the business of clarifying. It presents phenomena and leaves interpretation to the observer, trusting — or not caring — that the observer will project meaning onto whatever the darkness reveals.

But the fungi are, undeniably, *listening*. Their cell walls are thinner on the side that faces the hidden river. Their growth rate increases during periods when the sound seems — to instruments more sensitive than human ears — to shift slightly upward in pitch. They are consumers of vibration, converting the roar into tissue, and if the patterns they produce carry information, that information is not for us. It is for the Throat. It is the Throat's way of speaking to itself, slowly, in the only language it has: the language of things that grow.

---

The one who built the shelters is dead.

This is almost certain. The weathering on the dry-stone walls suggests decades at minimum, perhaps centuries. The cordage on the hanging stone is brittle to the point of disintegration. The chert flakes have settled into positions that would require years of micro-vibrations to achieve. Whoever stacked the shale fragments and bored the hole in the river stone and left the antler piece wedged in its crack has been gone for a long time.

But someone has been here more recently. Not often. Not for long. But recently enough that the evidence is still fresh — if you know how to read it.

A shelter near the middle of the gorge, the one with the best view of both walls and the most even temperature, shows signs of use that the others do not. The interior platform has been swept clear of debris, though the sweeping was imperfect and left traces in the corners. A quantity of moss has been gathered and piled on the platform — not a common moss, but one of the star-shaped species that grows only in the dripping crevices, the kind that stays saturated longer than any other. It was cut, not pulled, and cut with something sharper than stone. The shelter's entrance has been reinforced with two new stones, placed on top of the existing wall, and these stones are less weathered than the others, their edges still sharp.

Someone slept here. Someone cut moss for bedding and reinforced the windbreak and swept the floor and then left, walking the length of the gorge in the constant roar, passing the wall-gardens and the pale fungi and the patient spiders, and departed through the fissure without leaving any other trace.

How long ago? Months, perhaps. A year at most. The cut moss is still green at its base, though the exposed surfaces have browned. The swept debris has not fully redistributed. The new stones have not settled into the wall's rhythm.

Who?

The Throat does not say. But the antler piece in the crack near the entrance — the one whose purpose is obscure, whose surface is polished by long handling — has been moved. It was not in that crack before. You know this because the crack is too narrow to have accommodated it naturally, and because the polish on its surface shows fresh marks, thin scratches where it was pushed into place. Someone found the antler piece somewhere else in the gorge and wedged it there, near the entrance, where it would be the first thing a departing visitor would see and the last thing a returning one would touch.

A marker. A memory. A *reminder*.

The Throat keeps what is placed in it. The dampness and the cold preserve what the sound does not destroy. The antler piece will be there for decades, perhaps centuries, slowly weathering, slowly absorbing the vibration, slowly becoming part of the wall's story. And the person who placed it may return, or may not, and if they do not return, the antler will remain, unanswered, a question pressed into stone — different from the claw marks only in the species of the questioner.

---

The thing that made the claw marks has not been seen.

The marks are old. The edges are soft. Whatever hauled itself up from the hidden channel — seal or other, marine mammal or something that found the underground river by a different path — it has not repeated the attempt in long enough that the stone has begun to heal around the wounds.

But the hidden river is still there. The cave system it flows through is still there. And the Throat connects to that system, however impenetrably, however narrow the passage where the floor drops away and the water's roar becomes absolute. The connection exists. The path exists, even if no human body could traverse it.

So the question is not whether something could come up from below. The question is whether anything down there knows the way.

The seals, if they are seals, are navigators of dark water. They find their way through cave systems by echo and by memory, and if one of them surfaced in the Throat once, it could do so again. But the Throat is not a useful place for a seal — no fish, no rest, only cold stone and a narrow sky-seam far above. A seal that surfaced here would be lost, would be confused, would be breathing air that smells of damp stone and green moss and the faint animal musk of the shelters, and it would retreat the way it came, leaving only the marks of its panic on the shale.

Unless it did not retreat. Unless it found something in the Throat that it was looking for. Unless it came up not because it was lost but because it was following something — a current, a chemical trace, a sound within the sound, a frequency that humans cannot hear but that resonates in the bones of a deep-diving mammal like a song heard from very far away.

The claw marks do not answer. They are only marks. But they are deep, and they are numerous, and they suggest a large body moving with urgency, and they are located at the point where the hidden river comes closest to the accessible world. Something came up from the dark water. Something felt the stone and the cold and the roar and the dampness, and it climbed, and it hauled itself into the Throat's final narrowing, and then—

And then the marks stop. The stone beyond them is smooth. Whatever made them either went back down or went further up, and if it went further up, it found a passage that the human visitors have not found, a route through the rock that leads somewhere else entirely.

The Throat does not clarify. But the moss near the claw marks is a different species than the moss elsewhere in the gorge — a darker, denser variety, one that seems to thrive on the specific moisture conditions found only in this deepest section. And the moss is healthy. It is growing. It has been undisturbed for a long time.

Whatever came up from below, it has not come back this way. Not recently. Not in the time it takes for moss to colonize stone and grow thick enough to lose a fingertip in.

But the hidden river is still moving. And the sound is still rising through the rock. And somewhere in the dark water below, something may still be listening to the frequencies that the Throat sends downward — the footsteps of rare visitors, the vibration of the wind at the sky-seam, the subsonic pulse of the seasons as the gorge breathes its slow annual breath.

---

What lives in the Throat lives in the margins.

This is the final thing to understand. The Throat is a place of absence as much as presence — a corridor of bare stone punctuated by improbable gardens, a channel of constant sound that contains almost no visible life, a wound in the land that has held its shape for longer than the land remembers and that has been visited by beings who leave only the lightest marks and then vanish back into the world of light and silence above.

The life here is marginal life. It is life at the edge of the possible, life that persists because it has found the exact conditions it needs and because those conditions, in this place, do not change. The mosses do not flourish *despite* the darkness and the cold; they flourish *because of* them, because the darkness and the cold create a stability that more variable environments cannot offer. The ferns do not struggle toward the light; they have abandoned light entirely and have learned to draw sustenance from the stone's slow sweating. The fungi have found a way to eat sound. The spiders have found a way to listen with silk. The springtails have found a way to send travelers across voids on threads of water.

And the visitors — the ones who built the shelters, the one who reinforced them, the one who left the antler in the crack — they have found a way to use the Throat that has nothing to do with survival. No one survives in the Throat for long. The cold and the damp and the sound see to that. But someone has found a reason to come here, to descend through the fissure, to walk the corridor of bare stone between the wall-gardens, to sleep on a platform of stacked shale with a bed of cut moss, to wake in the darkness with the roar filling their ribs, to touch the antler piece on their way out and then to climb back into the silence above.

Perhaps they come to listen. Perhaps the sound — which becomes a condition, which becomes a pressure, which becomes a presence — is something they need to feel for reasons that have nothing to do with the Throat itself. Perhaps they are the descendants of the ones who built the shelters, carrying on a tradition whose original purpose has been forgotten. Perhaps they are someone who found the Throat by accident and could not stop returning, drawn by something in the vibration that speaks to a part of them that has no name.

They are alive, this recent visitor. They are alive, and they know the way, and they may come back. And when they do, they will find the antler where they left it, and the shelters as they left them, and the wall-gardens growing their slow millimeters, and the sound unchanged, and the cold steady, and the dampness breathing its patient breath, and the Throat waiting — not with welcome, not with hostility, but with the vast indifference of a place that was here before life found it and that will be here after life has forgotten it, making its sound in the darkness, holding its wound open, being what it is: a throat, a channel, a voice that speaks in the language of water and stone and time, saying the same thing over and over, saying it to no one, saying it because saying is what it does, because the hidden river moves and the sound must rise and the walls must carry it and the life that clings to the walls must find its way in the margins of the roar, in the spaces between the sound, in the silence that the sound makes impossible and that exists nonetheless — a silence made of persistence, of patience, of the slow green tenacity of things that live against the walls and ask nothing of the voice that fills the channel except that it continue, that it never stop, that it go on speaking in the dark.
