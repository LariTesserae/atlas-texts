# What Lives in the Throat

---

The people have no word for "outside" that isn't also a word for "dead" or "empty" or "the place where nothing grows." This is not poetry. It is taxonomy. They have categorized the world into two domains: the Throat, which contains everything, and the not-Throat, which contains nothing worth naming. The passage that leads upward through thirty meters of oxidized stone to the plateau's surface is known to them—they have crawled it, some of them, in seasons when curiosity or compulsion outweighed caution—but they speak of it the way surface-dwellers might speak of a well: something you descend into briefly and leave again, not a place where anyone could stay.

Their name for themselves is untranslatable. It is a sound made deep in the throat, a kind of clicked hum that requires the acrid air to produce correctly, a resonance that doesn't work in thin or dry atmospheres. When they say it to each other, the vegetation seems to pulse slightly in response—or perhaps they have learned to pulse in time with the vegetation. It is hard to tell where the rhythm starts.

---

They are small.

Not stunted—never make the mistake of thinking them diminished. They are *compressed*. Generations of navigating passages barely wide enough for shoulders, of living in a cavity where the ceiling is low and the floor is root-mass, of building structures meant to be occupied by people who will never stand fully upright in their homes, have selected for compactness. The adults average perhaps a meter and a half in height, but it is their proportions that strike a surface-dweller first: their limbs are shorter relative to their torsos than any surface human population, their shoulders narrow, their movements economical in the way of creatures that have learned to occupy small spaces without feeling confined. They do not spread. They do not reach. They nest.

Their skin has adapted to the chemical air. It is thicker than surface skin, drier, with a fine scaling that is almost reptilian—not peeling, not damaged, but *armored*, a lipid layer that holds against the perchlorate-laced atmosphere and prevents the cracking that a visitor's skin begins within the first hour. Their lips do not split. Their eyes do not redden. They breathe the iron-and-sugar haze the way surface-dwellers breathe sea air: easily, without thought, their lungs long since habituated to the particulate load that would choke an outsider into coughing paralysis.

Their coloration has shifted over the generations. Not dramatically—there is no single Throat-palette—but the range has moved. They tend toward darker skin tones, as you might expect in a place with no strong light, and there is a warmth to their pigmentation, an amber undertone, that you would not find in surface populations. Under the bioluminescent glow of the growth-bodies, they look gilded. They look like something that has taken on the color of its home.

---

They do not have families in the way surface-dwellers mean the word.

They have *strands*—a term that captures something of what they actually practice. A strand is a web of relation that includes genetic lineage but is not limited to it. Children are born to the strand, not to a pair of parents, and the entire strand participates in their raising. This is practical in a settlement of a hundred people where genetic diversity requires careful navigation, but it is also theological: the Throat's ecosystem has taught them that survival is a distributed phenomenon, that no single organism stands alone, that the growth-bodies and the insects and the hunters and the midden-beasts are all strands of the same living architecture. Why would people organize themselves differently?

A child learns early that they are part of something. The growth-body wall of their home breathes around them as they sleep; the insects that crawl through their living space are neighbors, not pests, to be respected and occasionally culled but never eradicated; the warm chemistry of the Throat itself is a presence they are taught to feel, a slow pulse that underlies every moment of every day. Children raised in the Throat do not experience solitude. They cannot. The sound is too constant, the air too thick with life, the walls too alive with vegetation and crawl and the steady exhalation of perchlorate metabolism. They are embedded. They are *in* something, the way a root is in soil.

---

Their language has no writing.

This is not for lack of capacity. They carve symbols—marks for identification, notation for chemical processes, territorial indicators on the growth-body walls—but these are labels, not literature. Their knowledge is preserved in chant and rhythm and the precise repetition of oral forms that have been refined over generations into structures of extraordinary density. A single chant can encode the complete life cycle of the iridescent climber, including behavioral variations across micro-seasons, optimal harvesting times, preparation methods for its chemical products, and the ritual observances that govern its use. It takes perhaps twenty minutes to sing. It takes perhaps twenty years to learn.

The chants are not merely information storage. They are *performance*, and the performance matters. The Throat's acoustics amplify and complicate sound—the ovoid cavity creates standing waves, resonant frequencies, pockets of silence and amplification that shift as the vegetation grows and the haze thickens and the temperature fluctuates. The people have learned to use these effects. Their chants are composed for this space, for this air, for the way sound behaves in a pocket torn from ancient rock. When they sing, they are not just reciting; they are *playing the Throat like an instrument*, shaping the reverberations, letting the cavity speak back to them in harmonics they can feel in their sternums and their teeth.

They do not sing alone. There is no solo performance in the Throat's tradition. The chants are communal, overlapping, call and response and simultaneous layer, the voices of the strand weaving together the way the vegetation weaves across the walls. To sing alone would be unnatural. It would be like a single root trying to hold the wall without the others. It would not work.

---

They eat the growth-bodies.

This is the simplest way to say it, and the most misleading. The vegetation of the Throat is not a single organism or even a single species; it is a consortium, a tangled community of perchlorate-metabolizing organisms that have grown together so long they function as a single living mass. The people have names for the different growths—the shelf-growths that climb the walls, the curtain-growths that drape from the ceiling, the mat-growths that cover the floor sediment, the nectar-growths that weep chemical food for the insects, the lung-growths that colonize their buildings and breathe alongside them. Each has its properties, its uses, its place in the cycle.

They harvest the growth-bodies with care. Not gentleness—the vegetation is tough, ancient, resilient; it does not require gentleness—but *precision*, knowing exactly how much can be taken from a given section without disrupting its function, knowing which growths will regenerate quickly and which require seasons of recovery, knowing when a curtain-growth is ready to be cut and when it is still building its chemical reserves. They have no concept of farming as surface-dwellers understand it—they do not plant, they do not cultivate—but they practice something adjacent: *tending*, ongoing relationship with a living system that they shape and that shapes them in return.

The harvested growth-body tissue is processed in the fermentation vats: stone chambers lined with mineral sediment and seeded with the microorganisms that break down the perchlorate compounds into forms the human body can use. The process is slow, taking weeks for a single batch, and it produces the thin, acrid fluid that is their staple—their water, their soup, their wine, their medicine, all in one. The fluid tastes, to a surface-dweller, like something between battery acid and fermented honey. To the people of the Throat, it tastes like *enough*.

They also eat the insects. Of course they eat the insects; the insects are the Throat's most abundant animal life, and any ecosystem this tight recycles its biomass without sentiment. The thumb-sized crawlers are harvested in columns, packed into growth-body membranes, and fermented into a dense protein paste. The fist-winged fliers are caught in filament nets hung across the flight paths and eaten fresh, their chitin cracked and their chemical nectar drained as a sweetener. The people do not waste. They cannot afford to. Nothing in the Throat can afford to.

---

They worship, after a fashion.

Not the vegetation—never the vegetation, which is too present and too ordinary to be sacred, the way surface-dwellers do not worship their own walls. Not the insects, which are food and neighbor and occasional pest but not divine. Not the Throat itself, exactly, though they speak of it with a reverence that approaches devotion.

They worship *the warmth*.

The warmth that rises from below, geothermal and ancient, the same warmth that keeps the growth-bodies' slow chemistry running, that makes the air thick and close, that the people feel through the soles of their feet when they stand on the floor sediment. They do not personify it. They do not give it a face or a name or a story. But they observe it. They track its fluctuations—the slight increase during certain micro-seasons, the slight decrease during others—with the same precision they bring to their chants, and they have developed ritual practices that correspond to these shifts: gatherings during the warmth's peak, solitary contemplation during its ebb, the fermentation vats timed to its rhythms.

The warmth is not God. The warmth is *the ground being alive*. The people understand, in their way, that the Throat exists because the plateau's interior still carries heat from processes that began when the rock was molten, that their entire ecosystem runs on energy that is not solar but *telluric*, not from above but from below. They do not have the language to articulate this. They do not need it. They feel it every moment of every day: the floor is warm, the air is warm, the growth-bodies are warm, and the warmth does not stop. It has not stopped for longer than anyone can remember. It will not stop. The people trust it the way surface-dwellers trust the sun—without question, without condition, with the bone-deep certainty of creatures whose lives depend on something that has never failed them.

---

They are not unhappy.

This is important to understand, because the surface-dweller's imagination tends to cast the Throat as a prison: a pocket, a cavity, a place of confinement where a hundred people live because they cannot leave, because they have never known anything else, because the passage is narrow and the world beyond is dead. But the people of the Throat do not experience their home as confinement. They experience it as *plenitude*.

The Throat is full. Full of life, full of sound, full of warmth, full of the constant motion and metabolism and reproduction of an ecosystem that has been running longer than human civilization has existed on the surface. The people are embedded in this fullness the way a cell is embedded in tissue, the way a root-thread is embedded in the wall. They do not lack for company; the insects and the hunters and the growth-bodies are company enough, and their own kind surround them always. They do not lack for purpose; the tending and harvesting and fermenting and chanting and child-raising and strand-weaving fill every hour. They do not lack for beauty; the amber glow of the bioluminescent patches, the sulfur-yellow blink of the growth-bodies' rhythms, the haze that softens every edge and makes the air itself luminous—this is their aesthetic, and it is as complete as any surface sunset.

They do not know they are small. They do not know they are few. They do not know that the plateau above them stretches for thousands of kilometers in every direction, that the surface holds billions of their kind, that the world is vast and various and full of things they have never imagined. These facts, if they could be conveyed, would not astonish them; they would simply be irrelevant, the way the existence of deep-sea vents is irrelevant to a farmer in a temperate valley. The Throat is their world. It is sufficient. It has been sufficient for longer than memory holds.

---

They die in the Throat.

Their dead are given to the midden-beasts—the pale, many-legged foragers that clean the settlement's waste, that consume what is no longer needed, that transform organic matter into the sediment trace that feeds the growth-bodies' roots. This is not disrespect. This is the cycle made visible. The people understand that they are part of the Throat's metabolism, that they take from the growth-bodies and the insects and the warmth, and that when they die, they give back what they have taken. The midden-beasts are not scavengers to them; they are *priests*, ministers of the final transaction between a person and the pocket that held them.

There is no burial. There is no monument. The chant for the dead is sung once, by the strand, in the acoustics of the cavity, and then the name is released back into the sound of the Throat, absorbed into the constant click-and-drone, becoming part of the noise that is always present, that has always been present, that will always be present. The dead do not go anywhere. They stay. They become the warmth, the sediment, the growth, the hum.

---

Stand in the center of the Throat during a gathering—there is no night here, only the dim and the dimmer, but there are times when the warmth peaks and the people come together—and watch them.

They are small in the cavity's shadow, a hundred compact bodies in a space that could hold ten times their number. They are not dwarfed. They are *proportioned*, the way the insects are proportioned, the way the growth-bodies are proportioned—fitted to this place by time and adaptation and the relentless logic of an ecosystem that has already decided what works. They move through the amber haze with ease. They navigate the curtain-growth and the filament forests without hesitation. They breathe the iron-and-sugar air without coughing. They sing in the resonant cavity, and the cavity sings back, and the vegetation pulses, and the insects drone, and the warmth rises from below, and the whole Throat thrums with the sound of something that has been alive for a very long time and will continue being alive for a very long time after.

They are not trapped. They are not surviving. They are not holding on.

They are *home*.

---

And the Throat continues.

The micro-seasons turn. The chemical concentrations shift. The growth-bodies swell and recede. The insects hatch and harvest and die and become sediment. The hunters hunt. The midden-beasts clean. The people tend and chant and ferment and raise their children and give their dead to the cycle and wake each day to the same amber dimness, the same warmth, the same sound that is not silence and will never be silence, the sound of a pocket-world in perpetual motion, finished and alive, final and still breathing, complete and still becoming.

This is what lives in the Throat: everything. Everyone. A hundred humans and their breathing walls and their chemical sustenance and their chanted knowledge and their warmth-worship and their strand-woven lives. An ecosystem that has solved itself, that has found the shape it will hold for as long as the warmth rises and the sediment feeds and the growth-bodies metabolize the ancient salts. A cavity in a plateau that has forgotten it was mountains, high above a surface that does not know this place exists, containing a world that does not know it is small.

The Throat swallows and keeps.

And what it keeps, it keeps whole.
