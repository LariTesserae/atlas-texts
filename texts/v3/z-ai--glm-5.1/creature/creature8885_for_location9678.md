The people who live in the Capacitor Grove are few, and they have been here long enough to forget that they were ever elsewhere.

---

There is Sera, who maintains the machines. She is the one you saw moving between structures, and if you had called out to her, she might not have answered—not from rudeness, but from the genuine uncertainty of whether the sound was meant for her. She has lived inside the grove for thirty-one years, and in that time her attention has turned increasingly inward, toward the rhythms of the place, toward the subtle language of vibration and humidity that the living architecture speaks. She knows the generator's moods. She knows when the loom's tension needs adjusting by the quality of sound that carries through the root-felt. She wears gloves when she handles the metal components—not to protect her hands, but to protect the copper from the salts in her skin, which corrode the contacts. The machines are the guests here. She is their interpreter.

Her hands are stained with plant resin and machine oil, and she has long since stopped trying to tell them apart. There is a film on her forearms, a faint greenish patina that she has never been able to scrub away, and she has stopped trying to scrub it away, and if you asked her about it she would look at you as though you had asked whether she breathed. It is simply part of her now. The grove has marked her. The grove marks everything that stays.

She sleeps in the structure nearest the workhouse, a bulbous growth of bark and living wood with a single round entrance that she passes through by ducking. The interior is warm—warmer than the open grove, kept so by the metabolic heat of the walls themselves—and her bed is a shelf of root-felt that she covers with woven fiber from the loom. There is a lamp, battery-powered, recharged from the generator. There are tools, hung on pegs that the bark has grown around and swallowed up to the halfway point, so that the hammer and the wrench seem to be emerging from the wall like artifacts half-excavated. There are no decorations. She does not need them. The walls breathe around her, and that is sufficient company.

She talks to the grove sometimes. Not in words, exactly—more in the pattern of her movements, the timing of her repairs, the way she runs her hand along the bark when she passes. She is not sure if the grove understands. She is not sure if understanding is the right framework. The grove responds, is what matters. When she adjusts the pH of the substrate near the generator, the bark grows a little faster around the conduit. When she prunes the canopy above the workhouse, the light shifts to favor the new leaves. They are in conversation. The vocabulary is adjustment. The grammar is growth.

---

There is Dehn, who tends the substrate. He is older than Sera, though you would not know it from watching him work; he moves with the careful economy of someone who has learned that haste costs more than it saves. His structure is on the far side of the grove, near the water reservoirs, and his days are spent monitoring the root-felt that covers the ground—testing its composition, adjusting its moisture, adding compost from the processing vessels where organic waste is broken down by the grove's maintenance organisms. He is, in effect, a soil farmer, though the soil he farms is the living floor of a living room.

His fingernails are black with humus. There is earth under his skin that will never wash out, packed into the crevices of his knuckles, embedded in the calluses of his palms. He smells of damp and decomposition, not unpleasantly—like a forest floor after rain. When he presses his hand flat against the root-felt, he can feel the pulse of water moving through the substrate, the slow percolation of moisture toward the reservoirs. He can tell you, within a degree, the temperature of the earth six inches down. He can tell you whether the mycorrhizal networks are healthy by the taste of the air.

Dehn came to the grove when it was young—when the walls were only three feet thick and the canopy was sparse enough that rain fell directly on the ground. He remembers when the substrate was bare mineral, before the root-felt established, before the cycles closed and the grove became self-sustaining. He remembers carrying water in buckets from a well outside the walls. He remembers when the generator was new, and the sound of it was strange, and the bark had not yet learned to grow around the conduit. He has lived inside the grove's entire adulthood, and in some ways he has become part of its memory. The root-felt he tends has passed through his hands so many times that it carries the chemical signature of his skin, and if you could read the substrate like a ledger, you would find him written there.

He does not leave the grove. He has not left in fourteen years. The outside is too bright, too dry, too uncontrolled. The air does not move right out there. The ground does not breathe.

---

There is Maren, who weaves. She is the youngest, though she is not young—forty-three, by her own count, though counting is difficult when the seasons blur together and the grove's interior climate does not change. She operates the loom in the workhouse, turning the plant fiber that the grove produces into fabric: cloth for clothing, for bedding, for the sealed bags that store seed pods and dried material. She works by touch as much as by sight, her hands reading the tension of the threads, the quality of the fiber, the rhythm of the shuttle. The loom is mechanical, belt-driven, powered by the generator, but she has modified it over the years—added sensors, adjusted the timing, replaced parts with components that the grove has grown around and incorporated. The loom is half-machine now, half-plant. Maren does not see the difference.

She was born somewhere else. She does not talk about it. She came to the grove because she was looking for quiet, and she found it, and she has never wanted to leave. The quiet is not silence—she would not like silence—but the particular quality of ambient sound that the grove produces: the breathing, the dripping, the humming, the creaking. A soundscape that is alive but not demanding. A background that does not require response.

She hums while she works. The sound blends with the generator, and sometimes the others hear it and mistake it for a change in the machine's pitch, a shift in the grove's rhythm. They do not correct themselves when they realize. Maren's humming is part of the soundscape now. The grove has absorbed it.

---

There are others, sometimes. The grove's population fluctuates between four and nine, depending on the season and the needs of the settlement and the movements of people across the flat terrain outside. A few come and go—technicians from other stations, traders who bring metal and manufactured goods in exchange for the grove's output: the pressed seed oil, the woven fiber, the concentrated nutrients that the substrate produces. They stay for a few days, sleep in the guest structure (the smallest, the newest, still growing its interior walls), and then leave. The grove does not mark them. They do not stay long enough.

But there is one more who stays, and she is the one you will not see, because she does not come out during the day.

Her name is Lith, and she tends the reservoirs. She is the oldest—seventy-two, or seventy-three, she isn't sure anymore—and she has lived in the grove since before Dehn arrived. She was here when the walls were saplings. She was here when the first generator was installed, a gas-powered thing that burned fuel they had to carry in from the nearest settlement, six days' walk. She remembers the sound of combustion inside the grove, how wrong it felt, how the bark flinched away from the heat. She remembers when they switched to the current system, the hand-wound generator that Sera now maintains, and how the grove seemed to relax.

Lith does not sleep well anymore. She rises at night, when the grove's breathing slows and the canopy's photosynthesis ceases and the darkness is absolute except for the faint bioluminescence of certain maintenance organisms that glow in the root-felt. She moves through the grove by memory and by touch, her fingers trailing along the bark walls, reading the surface the way a sighted person reads a face. She checks the reservoir levels. She tests the water quality. She adjusts the channeling fibers that direct condensation to the catchments. She works in the dark because she prefers the dark, and because the dark is when the grove speaks most clearly, in the language of pressure and moisture and slow, sleeping growth.

She will die here. She knows this, and she is at peace with it. She has asked Sera to leave her body in the processing vessels when the time comes, so that the grove can break her down and incorporate her minerals into new growth. She has asked Dehn to spread her remains across the substrate she spent her life tending. She wants to become part of the root-felt. She wants to be digested and remembered and cycled back into the living architecture that she has served for half a century.

The grove will absorb her. The grove absorbs everything, eventually. The metal conduit, the cotton insulation, the copper wire, the rubber sheathing—and the calcium and iron and carbon of the people who live inside it. Not maliciously. Not hungrily. Simply because that is what living systems do: they incorporate, they cycle, they transform. The people who live in the Capacitor Grove understand this. They have made their peace with it. They are symbionts, and symbionts do not outlast their hosts.

---

And the grove itself? Does it live, in the sense that you mean?

It breathes. It grows. It responds to stimulus. It maintains homeostasis. It processes energy and cycles nutrients and repairs damage and defends against intrusion—the bark walls are thick for a reason, and the entrance can be sealed by swelling the living wood around it, a defense mechanism that has been triggered twice in the grove's history, both times during storms that threatened to tear the canopy apart. It does not think, as far as anyone can tell. It does not plan. It does not converse in any language that Sera or Dehn or Maren or Lith can identify.

But it accommodates. It grows around the conduit, incorporating the metal into its structure, routing sap around the obstruction, building new tissue that holds the wire in place without crushing it. It adjusts its canopy to admit light where the workhouse needs it, and shades the areas where cool storage is required. It channels condensation toward the reservoirs that Lith tends, and away from the electrical components that Sera maintains. It tolerates the machines. It tolerates the people. It has, over the course of decades, shaped itself around them, and they have shaped themselves around it, and the boundary between inhabitant and habitat has become so blurred that it is no longer clear where the grove ends and the settlement begins.

This is what it means to live in the Capacitor Grove. You do not occupy a space. You participate in a process. You are not a resident. You are an organ.

And if you stay long enough, you will feel the walls breathing around you, and you will breathe with them, and you will stop noticing where your rhythm ends and theirs begins. And the grove will hold you, warmly, damply, in its slow systolic embrace, and it will never let you go—not because it is possessive, but because letting go is not something that living systems do. They incorporate. They cycle. They transform.

They keep.
