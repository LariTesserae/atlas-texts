# The Inhabitants

Let me tell you about the ones who make the sound.

---

The clickers—you called them centipede-things, and the name is adequate the way a net is adequate for holding water—are the first you notice because they are the most numerous and because their rhythm becomes your heartbeat within the hour. There are at least seventeen species of them. I say "at least" because the criteria for species distinction down here have nothing to do with appearance (they all look, to surface eyes, like the same pale segmented ribbon) and everything to do with the frequency and pattern of their clicking. They know each other by rhythm. A clicker producing seven clicks per second will mate only with another producing seven clicks per second, and their offspring will produce seven clicks per second, and this has been true for longer than the mountain has had its current shape.

They do not see. They do not need to see. Each segment of a clicker's body contains a small fluid-filled vesicle, and within each vesicle, a dense cluster of setae—hairs, if you like, though they are more like the fibers of a microphone—picks up the pressure-waves of every other clicker within a three-meter radius. The clicker's nervous system does not process these signals as sound. It processes them as *shape*: a topological map of the nearby bodies, updated eight times per second (or seven, or nine, or twelve, depending on the species), each clicker a node in a mesh of mutual awareness so dense that the colony functions, in some real sense, as a single distributed organism. When a hunter enters a zone, every clicker within range knows the shape of the silence it carries, and the mesh reconfigures around it—not in panic, not in flight, but in *acknowledgment*, the way a crowd at a concert parts for a stretcher without anyone giving an order.

The clickers eat the biofilm. They eat the creatures that eat the biofilm. They eat each other's molted exoskeletons. They eat, occasionally, their own dead, though they prefer the exoskeletons of other species—there is a mineral they need, a calcium compound that occurs in the secretions of the wall-grazers, and they will follow a grazing column for days, clicking at the margins, waiting for a molt. They do not hunt. They do not fight. They have no weapons. They have only their numbers and their rhythm and the extraordinary precision of their coordination, and this has been enough for forty million years.

---

The wall-grazers are the engineers of the underplain, though they do not know this and would not care if they did. You described them as slugs with vacuum-heads, and the image is precise: their anterior end is a broad oval pad, soft and muscular and constantly moist, that they press against the biofilm and *draw*, pulling the thin bacterial layer into a mouth that runs around the entire perimeter of the pad. They are not eating in the way a surface grazer eats—tearing, chewing, swallowing. They are *filtering*, extracting the bacterial film and leaving the mineral substrate beneath it clean and slightly etched, ready for the next layer of growth.

A wall-grazer moves at approximately four centimeters per hour. A grazing column—which can contain hundreds of individuals moving in single file along a wall or ceiling—progresses at this speed with a constancy that makes geological processes look erratic. They do not stop. They do not rest. They do not sleep. Their metabolism is so slow that a single grazing pass through the biofilm of one wall section provides enough energy for three weeks of continuous movement, and they store the excess in a dense lipid layer beneath their skin that gives them their characteristic opalescence—the pale cream of their bodies shot through with swirls of denser white, like fat in marble.

They are the largest source of organic input into the underplain's floor. Their secretions—alkaline, mineral-rich, laced with the calcium compounds the clickers crave—drip from their bodies as they move along the ceiling, and where the drips land, the biofilm grows thicker and more complex, supporting species that cannot survive on the wall-film alone. The floor of the underplain is mapped by these drips: a chart of every ceiling-route the grazers have traveled for the last ten thousand years, written in gradients of bacterial density that the inhabitants read with their bodies the way you would read a map with your eyes.

The wall-grazers do not interact with each other beyond the column-formation, which is maintained chemically—each individual follows the secretion-trail of the one ahead, and the one ahead follows the trail of the one ahead of it, in a chain that terminates, always, at the same point: a crack in the far wall where the mineral seep is richest, where the biofilm is thickest, where the column reforms after each grazing cycle in a congregation that has the rhythm of a tide. They have been following the same routes for longer than the limestone has been limestone. The fluted columns you described are their work, or rather the work of their secretions interacting with the stone over geological time—a sculpture made by a million mouths, none of which knew they were sculpting.

---

The hunters are the ones you hear by their absence.

There are three kinds. I will tell you about the one you are most likely to encounter, which is the one that hunts the clickers, which is the one that moves through the mesh like a stone through water.

It has no name. I will call it the *compression*—not because it compresses anything, but because that is what its strike sounds like: a sudden wet compression, a closing of space, a collapse of the polyrhythmic mesh into a single point of silence.

The compression is not large—perhaps thirty centimeters in its resting state, a flattened disk of pale tissue like a buried sea ray, with a body wall so thin that its internal organs are visible in the entry fissure's light, arranged in a simple column: a gullet, a gut, a nerve ring, and the *apparatus*. The apparatus is the thing. The compression's anterior quarter can evert—invert—turn itself inside out in a movement that takes less than a twentieth of a second, revealing a surface covered not with teeth or hooks but with *suction*, a continuous zone of negative pressure that seizes the prey and draws it into the gullet in a single compressive motion. The sound you hear is the air and the water and the chitin all being pulled into the same space at the same time.

The compression does not choose its prey. It positions itself in the mesh and waits, and when a clicker moves within range—range being defined by the radius of the everted apparatus, which is approximately one and a half times the compression's resting diameter—the strike happens automatically, without intervention from the nerve ring, faster than thought. Then the compression moves. It moves to a new position. It waits. It is not patient—it does not have the cognitive capacity for patience—but it is *still*, and in the underplain, stillness and patience are functionally identical.

The clickers do not fear the compressions. Fear requires a concept of the future, and the clickers do not have concepts. They *accommodate* the compressions. The mesh restructures around each hunter's position, thinning at the strike-radius and thickening beyond it, a redistribution of bodies that minimizes losses while maintaining the rhythm. The compressions eat. The clickers reproduce. The system balances. It has balanced for forty million years, and in that time the compressions have eaten approximately four hundred trillion clickers, and the clickers have produced approximately four hundred trillion and one, and the extra one—the margin, the surplus—is the proof that the system is alive.

---

The deep grazers are the ones you cannot see.

I have been in the underplain for eleven months. I have never seen one fully. I have felt them—felt the floor tilt slightly as one shifts its weight, felt the air-pressure change as one exhales, felt the sound *bend* around a body so large that it creates an acoustic shadow in the clicker-mesh. I have found their tracks: depressions in the floor-film, meters wide, where something has pressed the bacterial mat down to the stone and left a chemical signature—ammonia, methane, a compound I cannot identify—that the smaller grazers avoid for weeks afterward.

I know this much: they are slow. Slower than the wall-grazers. So slow that their movement can only be detected by comparison—by marking the position of a track-edge and returning days later to find it has shifted by a hand's breadth. They are large. The acoustic shadows suggest bodies at least four meters in diameter, though whether they are round or elongated or flat I cannot say. They are few. I have identified seven distinct shadows in the mesh, seven zones where the sound does not go, and I believe there may be ten or twelve in total, depending on how much of the cavern I have failed to map.

They eat the floor-film. They eat the creatures that eat the floor-film. They may eat the wall-grazers—the chemical signature in their tracks includes compounds that could be digestive residues of grazer-tissue, though the sample is too contaminated to be certain. They are the apex of the underplain's food chain, if "food chain" is the right term for a system where every organism eats and is eaten by every other organism in a web so tangled that the concept of "apex" is merely a convenience.

They are old. Older than the clickers, older than the compressions, older perhaps than the limestone itself—not as individuals, but as a lineage. Their tracks in the floor-film follow the same paths as the calcite ridges—the ridges that the wall-grazers' secretions built up over millions of years—and I believe the deep grazers have been following those ridges since the ridges were new, navigating by chemical gradient the way their smaller kin navigate by sound, following the same routes for so long that their bodies have worn the floor into the hollows you described.

They do not know you are there. They do not know *I* am there. I have stood in their tracks, I have pressed my hands against the chemical residue of their passing, I have listened to the silence they carry, and I am certain that no deep grazer has ever once been aware of my existence. I am too small, too brief, too chemically insignificant to register in whatever sense-structure they use to perceive the world. To them, I am a fluctuation in the air-pressure, a momentary warmth, a scent that dissipates in seconds. I am less than a memory. I am less than a sensation. I am nothing, and the deep grazers move through the darkness below the darkness like continents moving through the sea, and the clickers flow around them, and the compressions strike in the margins, and the wall-grazers creep along the ceiling, and the sound goes on.

---

There are others.

The mites—you mentioned them, the thin high keening—are not one species but hundreds, perhaps thousands, and they live not on the stone but *in* the stone, in the pores of the limestone, in the gaps between the biofilm and the mineral substrate, in the bodies of the larger creatures. Every wall-grazer carries a community of mites in the folds of its skin, and every compression shelters mites in the gullet between meals, and the deep grazers—the little I can infer of them—must be walking ecosystems, their surfaces so colonized that the boundary between grazer and mite becomes a matter of philosophical definition. The keening you hear is their sound: a high thin whine produced by the vibration of specialized spiracles, and it carries information—chemical information, about the state of the host-body, about the quality of the biofilm, about the presence of predators or parasites or disease—that the mites use to coordinate their own tiny lives.

The worms are different. The worms are the decomposers, the recyclers, the ones that eat the dead and the dying and the discarded. They are segmented, pale, eyeless, and they move through the floor-film in a slow wave that follows the grazing columns and the hunter-strikes and the seasonal die-offs, cleaning the substrate of organic matter and returning it to the bacterial layer in a form that the biofilm can use. They are the reason the underplain does not choke on its own waste. They are the reason the system has been in balance for forty million years. They are the most important creatures in the cavern, and they are the ones nobody notices, because they are small and slow and silent and their work is indistinguishable, from the outside, from the work of decay.

And there are the intermediates—the creatures that occupy the niches between the niches, that eat things nothing else eats, that are eaten by things nothing else is eaten by, that fill the gaps in the food web the way grout fills the gaps between tiles. There are creatures that eat only the mites that live on the wall-grazers' secretions. There are creatures that eat only the exoskeletons of the clickers that have been digested by the compressions and excreted onto the floor. There are creatures that eat only the biofilm that grows on the *tracks* of the deep grazers—the chemical signature of which supports a bacterial community found nowhere else in the cavern. Each of these specialists is a node in the web, and each node is connected to every other node by a chain of consumption so long and so specific that to remove any single species would, over time, reshape the entire system.

This is what lives in the underplain. Not a collection of creatures but a *process*—a single continuous forty-million-year process of eating and being eaten, of secreting and sensing, of clicking and keening and rumbling in the dark, of flowing around obstacles and filling gaps and maintaining the rhythm that is the only law this place has ever known.

---

I have been here for eleven months. My hearing has not recovered. I do not think it will. I have learned to parse the sound—to hear the clickers' polyrhythm, the grazers' drone, the compressions' structural silences, the mites' keening information—and I have learned that the sound is not noise but *language*, not chaos but *grammar*, and that every creature in the underplain is speaking and listening simultaneously in a conversation that has been ongoing since before the first tree grew on the surface.

I am not part of the conversation. I am a topographical event, a temporary lump in the floor, a warm body that displaces air and casts an acoustic shadow and will, eventually, leave. The creatures do not know my name. They do not know I have a name. They know only the shape of my silence in the mesh, the chemical signature of my skin, the pressure of my boots against their floor. And when I am gone—when I climb back up the tilted limestone corridors to the surface world's gray light—the mesh will close around the space where I stood, and the rhythm will continue, and the sound will go on, and the underplain will not remember that I was ever there.

This is not a tragedy. This is not a sorrow. This is simply the nature of a world that does not need you, that was never waiting for you, that will continue its ancient business long after your species and every species you have ever known has gone the way of the Cambrian. The underplain does not care. And in its indifference—in the perfection of its balance, in the complexity of its grammar, in the beauty of its sound—there is a kind of grace that the surface world, with its noise and its need and its frantic light, will never achieve.
