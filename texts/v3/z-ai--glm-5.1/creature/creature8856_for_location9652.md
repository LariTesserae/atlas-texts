The first thing that strikes you is the sound. Not a sound—*sounds*. A continent of them, layered so dense they become a weather system all their own. The drone of wind through cavity-riddled stone. The tap and skitter of chitin on rock. A low, tidal hum that rises from somewhere beneath the floor, or perhaps from the walls themselves, as if the place were breathing through a thousand throats at once.

And in this—*through* this—they live.

---

TheListeners, the old texts called them, though that name reveals more about the ones who wrote it than about the beings themselves. They have no word for what they are. They have no need for one.

Their bodies are long and pale, almost translucent in certain light, built for the fissures and runnels of the vast cave system they call home. You would not call them insect, not quite, though they have the segmented quality of something that has solved the problem of movement through narrow spaces by simply *becoming* a series of smaller problems. Each segment holds its own vibration-sensitive membrane, stretched taut beneath a sheath of thin, calcified skin. They do not have ears. They *are* ears—walking, feeling, living instruments of reception.

They move through the sound like you might move through fog: slowly, deliberately, one tendril of perception extended at a time. When they are still—which is often, for stillness is their hunting pose and their meditation and their sleep, all three indistinguishable from one another—they become part of the acoustic landscape. Their membranes ripple with each passing frequency. You might mistake one for a stalactite, if stalactites trembled.

---

There are thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Maybe more—they do not count, and no outsider has ever been foolish enough to try. They have lived here longer than the stone remembers, longer than the water has been cutting its slow, patient way through the rock. They were here when the sounds were simple: the drip, the crack, the groan of geological pressure. They learned those sounds the way you learned your mother's face. They became the sounds, and the sounds became them, and the distinction—if there ever was one—eroded long ago.

They do not speak. Not in any way you would recognize. What they do is *resonate*.

When one of them wishes to convey something—danger, food, the slow ache of a membrane beginning to tear—it produces a tone from deep within its body, a tone so precisely tuned to the acoustic properties of the surrounding stone that it travels for miles without losing coherence. The tone carries meaning not through variation in pitch alone, but through *timbre*. A warning sounds different from a greeting not because it is higher or lower, but because it is *rougher*, serrated at the edges, designed to set the membranes of every Listener within range trembling with the same urgent dissonance.

A greeting is smooth. A mourning is hollow. The sound of one seeking a mate is—and here the old texts grow uncomfortable, their authors suddenly aware that they are describing something achingly close to song—*beautiful*. Complex. Layered with overtones that shift and shimmer, no two performances alike, each one a self-portrait rendered in vibration.

---

They have no leaders. They have no hierarchy. They have *harmonics*.

Certain Listeners—older, larger, their bodies grown long and strange with decades of resonation—have become nodes in the network. Their membranes have thickened and refined over time, capable of picking up frequencies the younger ones cannot yet perceive. When they resonate, the sound carries further, clearer, more richly encoded. The others cluster near them not out of deference, but out of *need*. The old ones are the stone's memory made flesh. They hold the frequencies of collapses that happened a century ago, of flood-pulses that reshaped the tunnels, of the last time something from above found its way down.

That memory is important. Because things do find their way down.

---

The outsiders come rarely—once a generation, perhaps, or less. Explorers. The lost. The desperate. They descend through cracks in the world above, through old mine shafts and collapsed sinkholes, and they enter the sound.

It destroys most of them.

Not the Listeners. The Listeners do not harm them, not intentionally. It is the sound itself. The unrelenting, inescapable, bone-deep *presence* of it. Humans—assuming the outsiders are human; they usually are—are not built for this. Their ears ache. Their thoughts fragment. They hallucinate, convinced the walls are speaking to them, because in a sense the walls *are* speaking, have been speaking for millennia, and the human mind, desperate for pattern, starts to translate vibrations into language it was never meant to hear.

Some flee. Most of those do not find the way out.

Some go still. Sit down, press their hands over their ears, and simply... stop. The Listeners find these ones eventually. They circle the still bodies, membranes quivering, tasting the strange, thin sounds these creatures make: the heartbeat, the breath, the tiny electrical crackle of a nervous system overwhelmed into silence. They do not understand what they are sensing. But they recognize the stillness. Stillness is their own language.

They have been known to carry the survivors to places where the sound is less intense. Not out of kindness—they do not possess kindness as you understand it—but out of something adjacent to it: a resonance-based empathy, a reflex that says *this frequency is wrong, this pattern is breaking, move it somewhere it can re-establish coherence*. Whether the survivor lives or dies after that is a matter of chance and constitution. The Listeners do not linger to find out.

---

There is one more thing.

Deep in the cave system—deeper than the Listeners typically go, deeper than the water reaches, deeper than the stone itself seems comfortable with—there is a chamber. The old ones know of it. They will not resonate near it. They will not speak of it even in their layered, timbral way, because to produce a sound that references the chamber is, in some unspoken calculus of their existence, to invite the chamber to listen back.

And the chamber does listen.

Something in that space absorbs sound. Not dampens—*absorbs*. Takes it in, holds it, gives nothing back. The Listeners who have ventured close describe a silence so profound it feels like a presence, like something vast and patient and incomprehensibly *attentive* has turned its ear toward them and is waiting for them to say something worth responding to.

None of them have had anything to say.

None of them have gone back.

---

So they live in the sound. They are the sound. They move through frequencies the way you move through air, unthinkingly, intimately, their whole existence a conversation with the stone and the water and the deep, slow breath of the earth itself. They do not know what they look like. They have no concept of appearance. They know each other as textures in the noise, as familiar resonances in an endless chord, as voices in a song that has been singing itself since before the mountains rose.

And somewhere, in the deepest dark, something is listening to them listen.
