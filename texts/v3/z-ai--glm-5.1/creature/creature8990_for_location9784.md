# The Inhabitants

---

The people of the Rootworks do not call themselves that.

They call themselves *root-trainers*, a term that predates the hollow and refers to a specific trade, and they call themselves *workers*, which describes what they do, and they call themselves *the hands*, which describes what they are to the organism. They do not have a word for their people as a category. They have not needed one. If you ask a woman standing beside a sap-line whether she belongs here, she will look at you the way you might look at someone who asked if you belong inside your own skin.

---

They come from above. They still do. The fissure opens and closes and opens again, and through it come the ones who have heard—heard what, exactly, varies. That there is work. That there is purpose. That there is a place where the boundary between living and making has collapsed into something you can touch. They descend through the gap in the earth carrying whatever they carried when they decided to leave, and they do not go back up.

Not because they cannot. Because the Rootworks takes hold.

It is not a poisoning. It is not a parasite. It is *habitation* — the process of becoming a habitat, of acclimating so thoroughly to an environment that leaving it feels like a violence against the body. After six months, the surface air tastes wrong: too dry, too empty, lacking the vegetal humidity and the faint warmth of sap-adjacent atmosphere. After a year, the silence of open sky is disturbing; the Rootworks is never silent, and its inhabitants adjust to the constant subsonic thrum the way they adjust to their own heartbeats, and the absence of that sound reads as a wrongness, a missing organ. After two years, the light above feels aggressive. The sun is too bright. It changes too fast. A morning that becomes an afternoon that becomes an evening in the span of hours feels like a kind of epilepsy in the sky.

After five years, they do not leave.

---

They number in the low thousands. No one has conducted a census because the concept of counting the inhabitants separately from counting the infrastructure has not occurred to anyone who lives here. The woman at the pumping station has been here eleven years. Her partner arrived nine years ago; they met at a germination yard, training a new residential thicket around its iron frame. Their daughter was born in the Rootworks. She is six. She has never seen the sun.

This is not considered a tragedy.

The daughter runs along the root-flattened pathways with the other children, and she knows the hollow the way surface children know their neighborhoods—as a territory, as a range, as a series of landmarks and dangers and shortcuts. She knows which sap-lines are cool enough to touch. She knows the sound the pioneer-roots make when they're pressing into new stone, and she knows to stay away from the frontier edges where the substrate is thin and the rock is sharp. She knows the insects will not harm her unless she bleeds. She knows the hunters will not harm her at all; they have lived alongside the human inhabitants for generations, and the children learn each other's boundaries the way any cohabiting species does, through incremental negotiation, through the occasional startle, through long habituation.

She does not know she is supposed to miss the sun.

---

The root-trainers are the closest thing to a professional class. They are the ones who shape the growth—guiding roots along planned vectors, pruning structural fibers, training petal-lip doors and membrane windows and the spiral buttress-stairs that take years to reach their final form. The work requires a combination of arborist's knowledge and architect's spatial sense and something else, something the root-trainers call *reading*—the ability to look at a growing tip and understand what it wants to become.

Because the organism has intentions. Not thoughts. Not plans. Intentions—a directionality, a tendency, a shape it is reaching toward. The root-trainers read these tendencies and work with them or against them, depending on the need. A root that wants to grow upward can be trained into a column; a root that wants to spread can be directed into a floor. But the work is easier, faster, more durable, when the trainer works *with* the intention rather than against it.

The old trainers develop a rapport with the organism that the newcomers find unsettling. They touch bark and feel the sap moving underneath. They press their ears to trunk-walls and hear the organism's slow respiration. They know, without being told, when a section is sick or stressed or growing too fast. The woman at the pumping station—you met her, amber-handed, eleven years resident—she is not considered unusual in this. She is considered *experienced*. The knowing comes with time. The organism does not speak but it *informs*, and the body learns to listen.

---

The mechanics work the machines. There are fewer of them than there used to be. The organism replaces function after function—growing where iron once bore weight, pumping where engines once drove, filtering where machinery once processed—but it has not yet learned to forge, to weld, to repair the spinning cutting-heads of the pruning machines or the diesel pump-engines that still burn seed-oil in the older stations. The mechanics keep these running. They work in workshops that smell of metal and oil and the everpresent sap, and their hands are not amber but black, stained with the grease and the grime of salvage.

They are the ones who remember that the Rootworks was once smaller than it is. An older mechanic, retired now but still sharp, can tell you about the hollow when it was only a kilometer across, when the pumps were all diesel and the pruning was done by hand with saws and axes, when the frontier was close enough to hear the rock cracking from the residential thickets. He remembers when the first electric turbines were installed, when the first transparent viewing-ports were grown, when the first petal-lip doors opened and closed without being pushed. He is not nostalgic. He is proud. He says: *It was always going to be this. We just had to help it learn.*

The organism learned. The organism is still learning. The mechanics who remain are the ones teaching it the last things it does not yet know how to do, and when they succeed—when a root grows around a bearing with the exact tolerance the machine requires, when a sap-line develops a valve that opens and closes on command—the celebration is quiet but profound. Another function transferred. Another piece of salvage that will not need replacing. The organism coming closer to self-sufficiency.

---

The harvesters work the fauna.

This is the work that requires the most courage, or the most fatalism, depending on who is describing it. The hunters—the many-limbed, arboreal predators that keep the grazer populations in check—are not dangerous to humans the way they are dangerous to grazers, but they are not *safe*. They are present. They are part of the system. And when their populations exceed what the ecosystem requires, they must be culled.

The harvesters do this. They work in teams of three or four, moving through the spaces between buildings with the patience of people who know they are not the apex predators in this environment but who have negotiated a functional coexistence. They use traps, mostly—living traps, grown from root-tendrils that close on pressure and hold without killing. The hunters are harvested live, taken to the processing yards, killed quickly, and rendered. Their chitin becomes structural material. Their organs yield compounds the organism cannot synthesize. Their meat feeds the workers who want meat, though many do not, have not for years, finding their protein in the seed-beds and the processed sap-cakes that taste like concentrated straw and keep indefinitely.

The harvesters carry the marks of their work. Scars from the hunters' gripping appendages, thin white lines across forearms and shoulders. One man missing two fingers—they were not bitten off but *crushed*, the grip of a cornered hunter strong enough to shatter bone before the trap could be opened. He does not consider this a reason to stop. He says: *They're not mean. They're just alive.*

---

The cleaners are not human.

This is important to understand. The human inhabitants have named them *cleaners* because the function they serve maps roughly to what a janitor does in a surface city, but the cleaners are as much a part of the organism as the roots and the sap and the phosphorescent cambium. They are the insect-analogues, the many-jointed, chitin-shelled things that crawl the bark in lines, tending the aphid-analogues, processing waste, sealing breaches, and—crucially—eliminating anything that does not belong.

They are the immune system. They are thorough. They are indiscriminate in the way that immune systems are indiscriminate: they do not evaluate, they *recognize*, and what they do not recognize, they consume.

This is why you do not bleed in the Rootworks.

A worker who cuts herself on a pruning blade knows to seal the wound immediately—sap makes an adequate bandage, the amber fluid clotting over the cut and hardening into a temporary scab. If she bleeds freely, if the blood reaches the substrate, the cleaners will come. Not in swarms—they do not swarm, that is a surface-insect behavior, a panic response—but in a steady, purposeful convergence, thousands of small bodies moving toward the source of the foreign protein, and they will consume the blood and they will consume the contaminated substrate and they will consume *whatever the blood has touched*, and if that includes the worker's skin, her clothes, the flesh around the wound, they will not stop.

Workers have died this way. Not many. Not often. The warnings are clear and repeated and heeded. But in the early years, before the protocols were established, before the organism and its human inhabitants had learned each other's boundaries, there were incidents. A man who collapsed from exhaustion near a sap-line, his nose bleeding, his body too tired to move. They found him three days later, cleaned to the bone, the bones themselves pitted where the cleaners had begun to process the minerals.

His remains were returned to the substrate. This was not considered a burial. It was considered a *completion*.

---

The daughter—she is six, she has never seen the sun—she plays a game with the other children that has no surface equivalent. They call it *frontier*. One child stands at the edge of the built environment, where the humus thins and the raw rock begins, and the others hide in the residential thickets, and the one who is *it* must find them by following the vibrations in the substrate. The organism transmits pressure differently than soil; footsteps carry, attenuated but detectable, and the children learn to read these signals the way the root-trainers learn to read growing tips.

The daughter is very good at this game. She can feel a footfall from thirty meters, can distinguish between the weight of a child and the weight of an adult, can tell the direction of movement from the way the vibration propagates through the root-mat. She will be a root-trainer, her mother says. She has the hands for it. She has the ears.

She does not think of the Rootworks as a place that contains life. She thinks of it as life that contains places. Her home is not *in* the thicket; her home *is* the thicket, the shaped interior, the petal-lip door that opens when she pushes and closes when she enters, the membrane window that filters the phosphorescent light into something warm and dim and sleeping. She does not understand the concept of an empty house. Here, houses are not empty. They are *occupied* the way a lung is occupied—by the organism itself, the walls breathing, the floors warm with sap-lines running underneath, the air tasting of green copper and transpiration.

She will never leave. Not because she cannot. Because she is part of the system now, has been since birth, her microbiome colonized by Rootworks organisms, her immune system calibrated to the hollow's atmosphere, her circulatory rhythm synced to the slow tidal respiration that takes hours to complete. She breathes with the city. The city breathes with her.

---

There are others.

Older ones, the first settlers, the ones who came down three hundred years ago when the hollow was barely a cavity and the organism was barely more than a root-mass with ambitions. They are not dead. They are not entirely alive, either—not in the way you are alive, not in the way the daughter is alive. They have *settled*. Their bodies have grown into the infrastructure, literally, roots threading through their extended limbs, sap-lines budding from their skin where the amber has soaked so deep it has become part of their blood. They do not move. They do not speak. They are part of the organism now in a way that is not metaphor, and the workers care for them the way they care for the pumps and the sap-lines and the growing tips—regularly, gently, with attention but without sentiment.

These are the ones who chose integration. Who decided, decades ago, that they did not want to maintain the boundary between themselves and the Rootworks, and who let the boundary dissolve. It was not a sudden process. It took years. The organism is patient. The organism does not force. But it *accepts*, and when a body stops resisting—when the skin stops being a wall and becomes a membrane—things pass through.

The old ones are still there. You can see them if you know where to look, embedded in the walls of the oldest thickets, their faces calm, their eyes closed, roots growing from their open palms. They are not dead. They are not suffering. The neural monitoring the workers conduct—monthly, gentle, electrodes pressed to temples that are more bark than skin—shows slow activity, dreamlike, the patterns of deep sleep or deep meditation. They are somewhere. They are part of something.

The daughter leaves them offerings. Small cakes of processed sap, placed in the roots near their hands. The cakes are always gone by morning, consumed by the organism or by the cleaners or by something else, something that does not explain itself.

---

You asked who lives here.

The answer is: *the Rootworks lives here*. The people, the insects, the hunters, the grazers, the old ones embedded in the walls—these are not inhabitants the way you inhabit an apartment. They are *organs*. They are components of a system that does not distinguish between the human and the vegetable, the mechanical and the biological, the living and the built. The woman at the pumping station says *we* when she means the workers and *it* when she means the plant and *the works* when she means everything together, and she does not separate these terms because there is no separation to make.

You descend through the fissure in the earth and you enter a living thing, and if you stay long enough, it will grow around you the way it grows around the iron frames of the pumping stations—not destroying, not replacing, but *incorporating*, folding you into its architecture, making you part of the structure that sustains itself and expands outward and will not stop.

The daughter is six. She has never seen the sun. She is growing the way the Rootworks grows—slowly, steadily, reaching toward something she can feel but not yet name.

The hollow breathes. The sap pulses. The frontier advances another centimeter into the stone.

The works continue.
