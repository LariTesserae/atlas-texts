# What Lives on the Plateau of Voices

---

## The Mat-Dwellers

Begin with what is beneath your feet, because that is where most of the living happens.

The insects do not visit the mat. They *are* the mat—or as close to being the mat as anything can be said to be itself. The rhizome layer extends meters down, and within those meters, in every cubic handspan of living and decaying tissue, there are insects. Not colonies. Not nests in any architectural sense. Just presence. Occupancy so total that the substrate cannot be meaningfully separated from its inhabitants. The tunnels the burrowers make do not heal behind them so much as fill—overflowing with the bodies of those who come after, who lay their eggs in the walls of the tunnel, who die there and become the walls of the tunnel, whose offspring eat their way outward into new tissue that closes over them like water over a dropped stone.

There are beetles. This is both precise and useless—precise because some of them are certainly beetles by any functional definition, useless because the word cannot hold what they are. Iridescent carapaces that flash green-gold in the flat light, bodies the size of a thumbnail or a fist or barely visible at all, moving in loose coordination that might be communication or might simply be the shared response of ten thousand bodies to the same stimulus. They do not swarm in the manner of locusts. They *permeate*. A handful of mat pulled from the ground resolves, on inspection, into a shifting mass of chitin and movement that resolves further, under closer study, into dozens of distinct forms—some burrowing, some climbing, some lying still with mandibles open, waiting. They occupy every niche that the mat provides, and the mat provides every niche, because the mat is infinite horizontal food. Living tissue spreading in all directions, forever, tender at its growing edges, fibrous and dense in its older reaches, decomposing at its lowest layers into something that is not quite soil because it is never allowed to be still long enough to become soil.

The wasps—call them wasps for the narrow waists and the ovipositors, though the word falters—are parasitic on the larger burrowers. They move through the mat in quick darting motions, antennae tapping, and when they find a grub or a soft-bodied tenant they inject their eggs with a precision that suggests long evolutionary conversation between injector and injected. The host does not die, not immediately. It continues moving through the mat, feeding, growing, while the wasp's offspring develop within it, and when they emerge—chewing their way out through whatever orifice presents itself—the host's body becomes a brief shelter before it is absorbed into the surrounding tissue. Nothing is wasted. The plateau has been running this economy for longer than the stone tools have been settling into the root-mat, and it has reached an efficiency that is almost mathematical.

And there are things without names because no one has been here to name them, and if they had been, the names would be insufficient. Segmented things with too many legs that move like liquid through the tunnels. Flat things with no visible features at all that adhere to root-surfaces and pulse. Things that glow faintly in the humid dark beneath the top layer, bioluminescent for reasons that may no longer have purpose—mating displays for mates that no longer look, warnings for predators that no longer hunt, or simply the leftover expression of a gene that never learned to be silent. The deeper you dig, the stranger they become, because the mat at depth is an alien environment: dark, wet, hot, pressurized by the weight of everything above it. The things that live there have never seen the sky. They do not know that the mat ends. For them, the world is infinite food in all directions, and they have no reason to think otherwise.

The clouds that darken the sky—these are the fliers. The insects that have taken to the air, not permanently but in cycles, in pulses that correspond to nothing so simple as a day. They rise from the mat in columns of sound and motion, males seeking females, females seeking something that smells like home, and they fill the air for hours or days before settling again. Walking through one of these emergences is like walking through weather. They bump against your face, your hands, your neck. They do not bite—they are not interested in you, you are not their economy—but their bodies leave traces of wing-dust and moisture on your skin, and the sound of their collective wings presses against you like a hand. When they settle, the silence they leave is not silence at all but merely the absence of that particular frequency, and the plateau's voice drops a half-register and continues.

## The Quick Warm Ones

The chittering things are mammals. This seems almost surprising in a place so alien in its overall character—that here, among the column-plants and the living mat and the constant roar of insect life, there should be creatures recognizable as kin to the familiar. But there they are: small, furred, warm-blooded, nursing their young, and filling the low growth in waves that ripple across the mat like wind on water.

They are rodents in the broadest sense, though their ancestry has wandered far from any rodent that science has categorized. About the size of a human hand, fur the color of the mat—green-gold-brown, shifting with the seasons—they move in loose collectives that might be family groups or might be something more fluid, alliances of convenience that form and reform as food availability shifts. They eat the tender shoots that the mat produces constantly, new growth emerging from the rhizome layer like slow green sparks, and their grazing pattern is what gives the mat its uniform height. They are the gardeners, keeping the vegetation low not through any plan but through appetite, and without them the mat might thicken into something impenetrable, might climb, might become something other than this vast flat plain of living tissue.

Their population pulses are the plateau breathing. When the mat's growth peaks—when the humidity and warmth align just so and the rhizomes produce a flush of new protein—the furred ones multiply to meet it. Litters increase. Survival rates climb. The chittering rises in volume until it becomes a continuous tone, a layer of warm voice atop the insect drone. And then something shifts—some nutrient depletes, some parasite finds purchase, some predator discovers the abundance—and the population crashes. Not to zero. Never to zero. But enough that the chittering thins, and the mat grows a little taller in their absence, and the insects that feed on their dung and their dead must find other resources or wait for the next pulse.

They have burrows, of a sort—not dug into soil but pressed into the mat, tunnels and chambers shaped by the warmth of their bodies, lined with their own shed fur until the walls are soft and dry. Each burrow-system is temporary, lasting only as long as the local resources justify the stay, and when a group moves on, the mat fills the vacancy within days. You can find the old burrows if you know how to look—slight depressions in the surface, a subtle difference in the density of the vegetation—but the plateau does not preserve their architecture. It was never meant to last.

They are prey. This is their primary relationship with the world: they eat, and they are eaten. They have no defense against the predators except numbers, except the statistical likelihood that when the shadow passes overhead, it will take someone else. And so they breed constantly, prolifically, producing more bodies than the plateau can possibly consume, and the excess feeds everything above them in the food chain. They are the mat's energy made mobile, transferred from plant to animal and back again in a cycle so tight that nothing is lost.

When you walk the plateau and flush a wave of them from the low growth, the sound of their collective startlement is like the world gasping. A thousand tiny voices raised at once, a scatter of warm bodies in all directions, and then silence as they freeze in place, camouflaged against the mat, waiting for the threat to pass. If you stand still long enough, the chittering resumes. If you walk on, they emerge behind you, and the mat looks as though it was never disturbed at all.

## The Sky-Boned

The things that fill the bird-niche are not birds. But calling them birds is easier than explaining what they are, and perhaps in the end the distinction does not matter—they serve the function of birds, occupy the architectural space of birds, and if their lineage runs through some other branch of life's tree, the plateau has made them birds regardless.

They are hollow-boned, yes. Keen-eyed, certainly. They call to one another across the enormous distances between column-plants, and their voices echo off the fluted surfaces in strange delays that make a single call sound like a conversation. They nest in the epiphyte gardens that crawl up the columns—complex structures of woven vegetation and their own secretions, anchored in the crotches where parasitic vines meet the column's surface. Each nest is a city of sorts: dozens of individuals, related in ways they seem to recognize, raising young collectively and defending territory against other groups.

Their plumage—if it is plumage, if the flat overlapping structures that cover their bodies are feathers and not some convergent analog—is adapted for the plateau's strange conditions. Large, to catch the humid air. Pale beneath, to reflect the heat that rises from the mat. Dark above, to absorb what light filters through the constant haze. They soar more than they fly, riding the thermals that the columns generate, spiraling upward in the updrafts and then gliding long distances between the tower-plants with barely a wingbeat. When they need to descend, they fold their wings and drop, and the sound of their passage is a low rushing whisper that the insect drone almost—but never quite—covers.

They are scavengers and hunters both, opportunistic in a way that the plateau rewards. They eat the insects that rise in clouds, snatching them from the air with open beaks during emergences. They eat the furred ones when they can catch them, swooping low across the mat and grabbing a body before the collective startlement can scatter the rest. They eat the fruits and seeds that the column-plants produce—great woody pods that fall from the towers and crack open on the mat, spilling nutrient-dense flesh that everything on the plateau competes for. And they eat each other, when the opportunity arises. The nests on the columns are not always safe from their own kind.

Their calls are the sharpest layer of the plateau's voice—high, piercing, carrying for kilometers in the humid air. They have a language, or something that functions as one: specific calls for specific threats, for food sources, for mating availability, for territorial boundary. The echo off the columns complicates their communication, and they have adapted by varying the timing of their calls, by learning which surfaces produce useful reverberation and which produce confusion. A group nesting on the eastern face of a column calls differently than a group on the western face, their voices shaped by the acoustic architecture of their home. Whether this counts as culture or merely adaptation is a question the plateau does not answer.

They are the most visible presence on the plateau because they move through the air, because they are large enough to see at distance, because their calls draw the eye upward. But they are not the most numerous, not by orders of magnitude. They are simply the ones that the emptiness cannot hide.

## The Silent Ones

The predators are what you do not see.

This is their method and their nature: absence as presence, silence as approach, the sudden appearance of violence where before there was only the ordinary noise of living. They come in several forms, because the plateau supports several forms of predation, but they share a quality of existing at the edge of perception—not hidden, exactly, but unnoticed, unremarked, part of the visual and acoustic background until the moment they are not.

The largest are the mat-stalkers. Quadrupedal, low-slung, moving through the vegetation with a fluidity that suggests their bodies were designed for precisely this environment. They are the color of the mat—exactly the color, shifting as the mat shifts with the seasons, and this is not camouflage in the simple sense but something more thorough: their fur seems to sample the surrounding vegetation and adjust its pigmentation, a living process that takes days but keeps them perpetually matched to their hunting ground. They are patient in a way that seems almost vegetative themselves. They will lie still for hours in the low growth, breathing slowly, heart rate dropped to something barely above coma, while the furred ones move around them and the insects crawl over them and the mat grows beneath them. They are waiting for a specific confluence: a young or injured animal straying just far enough from the collective, a moment of inattention, the statistical certainty that given enough time, opportunity will present itself.

When they move, they move fast. A burst of speed that lasts seconds, no more—the mat-stalkers are not built for pursuit but for ambush, and if the first lunge fails, they do not chase. They settle back into the vegetation and wait again. But when the lunge succeeds, the sound is terrible: a wet impact, a brief squeal cut short, the ordinary noise of the plateau resuming almost instantly as if nothing has happened. The mat-stalkers drag their kills into the low growth and eat quickly, messily, and what they leave behind is absorbed into the mat within days. Bone, fur, teeth—all of it digested by the root-layer and converted back into the plant-matter that feeds the prey that feeds the next predator. The circle is so tight it has no interior.

There are also the drop-hunters. These are the ones that perch in the epiphyte gardens of the column-plants, high above the mat, watching with eyes that can resolve individual furred ones at distances that seem impossible. They are smaller than the mat-stalkers—light enough to glide, not merely fall, from their perches—and they strike from above, folding their wings tight and dropping like stones, opening at the last moment to slow their descent and extend talons that are almost the only visible feature of their otherwise drab bodies. They take their prey aloft, if they can, carrying the still-struggling body back to the column to feed. If the prey is too heavy, they eat it on the mat, and the other predators learn to listen for the sound of impact and come to scavenge what remains.

And there are the things that live in the mat itself. Not the insects—something larger, something that moves through the rhizome layer the way a mole moves through soil, pushing aside roots and compacting tunnels as it goes. You see their passage as subtle ridges in the surface, the mat rising and settling as something moves beneath it. They are almost never seen, but you hear them sometimes at night—a deep rhythmic thumping that might be digging or might be communication, a sound that the insects and the furred ones and the sky-boned all fall silent for, as if the entire plateau is briefly listening to the voice of something it would rather not wake.

## The Column-Plants

They are not fauna. But they are alive, and they shape everything that lives around them, and in a sense they are the plateau's true inhabitants—the only ones that are fixed, that stay, that define the geography rather than merely occupying it.

They are photosynthetic towers, immense columnar growths that stand alone and far apart, each one a solitary architecture rising a hundred meters or more from the living mat. Their surfaces are not smooth—they are fluted and buttressed, folded in ways that increase their surface area for transpiration, for the epiphytes that colonize them, for the nesting sky-boned that make their homes in the crotches of their branches. They are ecosystems unto themselves: the bark crawling with insect life, the gardens of smaller plants that root in their crevices, the vines that climb them and sometimes strangle them, the colonies of animals that never descend to the mat because the column provides everything they need.

They drink from deep. Their roots penetrate the mat entirely, reaching down to whatever mineral substrate lies beneath the meters of vegetative tissue, pulling up water and nutrients that the mat alone cannot provide. And they transpire constantly, exhaling moisture into the air from surfaces so vast that they create their own weather. The localized rain that falls around each column is the plant's breath condensing on the humid air—a slow drizzle that keeps the mat damp for meters in every direction, that feeds the rivulets that wander briefly before sinking back into the rhizome layer. Each column is a fountain. Each column is a rain-making machine. Each column is a god, if you are small enough and dependent enough to perceive it that way.

They reproduce, though rarely. The great woody pods that fall from their upper reaches contain seeds that are almost never viable—the plateau's climate is too stable, too unchanging, and the conditions that trigger germination may not have occurred in centuries. The pods themselves are food, valuable enough that animals will fight for access to them, and the seeds that survive the feeding are usually eaten or crushed or buried too deep in the mat to reach light. The columns that stand now may be the same columns that have stood for millennia, growing slowly outward, adding rings that no one counts, aging in a way that is not aging as animals understand it but simply continuing, accumulating, becoming more themselves with each passing century.

When a column falls—and they do fall, eventually, weakened by vines or disease or the slow failure of their root-systems—the impact is cataclysmic. A hundred meters of woody structure crashing to the mat, crushing everything beneath it, opening a wound in the vegetation that will take years to heal. The column's body becomes a ridge of decaying matter that the insects colonize immediately, that the mat grows over and incorporates, that becomes in time indistinguishable from the rest of the substrate except for a slight rise in the surface that might, if you knew what to look for, mark the grave of something that was once enormous and is now slowly becoming food for its descendants.

## The Ones Who Were

Someone lived here. Someones.

The stone circles are almost invisible, but they are there—low rings of fitted rock, no higher than a hand's width, marking spaces that were once cleared of vegetation and kept clear. Windbreaks of stacked root-mat, now crumbling and overgrown. Pits dug into the living substrate and lined with broad leaves, the leaves long since rotted away but the pits still visible as subtle depressions in the surface. The calcium deposits where hearths once burned. The discoloration where tool-work scarred the rhizome layer.

They built light and temporary, these former residents, and the plateau has swallowed most of what they left. This was not architecture meant to last—it was shelter, functional and minimal, made from materials that were immediately at hand. The stone for the circles came from the pits they dug, broken from the mineral substrate beneath the mat. The root-mat for the windbreaks was cut and stacked and dried in the sun, and when the windbreaks were abandoned, the mat simply re-absorbed them, re-incorporated the tissue, healed the wound. The leaves that lined their storage pits were the leaves of the column-plants, broad and waxy, harvested from fallen pods or stripped from low-hanging growth, and when the pits were emptied and abandoned, the leaves decomposed and became part of the mat's endless cycle.

Who were they?

The plateau does not say. Or rather, the plateau says many things, and none of them are definitive.

They were not numerous. The evidence suggests small groups, family bands perhaps, moving across the mat in seasonal patterns that followed the furred ones' population pulses or the column-plants' fruiting cycles. They did not settle—they moved, and they returned, and they moved again, and the places they returned to accumulated the slight marks of habitation: the stone circles, the hearth-deposits, the slow deepening of frequently used paths through the vegetation.

They used tools. The half-buried stone implements—hand-sized, flaked with crude but effective technique—speak to hands that could grip and strike and shape. But they did not work metal, did not build in materials that survive, did not leave any record of themselves beyond the slight depressions in the mat and the scattered stone that the rhizome layer has almost swallowed. They had no writing, or if they had writing it was on materials that the plateau digested long ago—bark, leaves, the fibrous stems of the mat itself, all of it food for the insects and the roots and the slow churning of the vegetative substrate.

They might have been human. The stone tools are not inconsistent with human manufacture, and the hearths are not inconsistent with human use of fire. But they might have been something else—something related, something parallel, something that evolved on this impossible plateau under conditions that rewrite the rules. The evidence is too slight, too digested, too incorporated into the mat to say for certain.

What happened to them?

The same thing that happens to everything on the plateau, in the end. They became part of the place. Their bodies returned to the mat, their tools settling into the root-layers to be slowly crumbled and incorporated. The calcium from their bones may be feeding a column-plant now, or providing structure for an insect's carapace, or circulating through the rhizome layer in a form that was once a hand, a face, a voice added briefly to the plateau's roar.

Or they left. They walked to the edge of the plateau—wherever that edge might be, wherever the mat gives way to something else, to soil and rock and the ordinary world—and they descended, and they did not return. Perhaps the noise drove them away in the end. Perhaps they followed some seasonal migration that the plateau's rhythms once made sensible, and the migration failed, or they found somewhere better, or they simply forgot the way back.

The plateau does not memorialize. It digests and grows over and forgets. Whatever story the stone circles might have told has been swallowed by the root-mat, overwritten by new growth, compressed and incorporated until it is nothing but a slight difference in the chemistry of the vegetation—a ghost in the leaves, a memory in the insects that eat them, a resonance that the plateau's voice carries without knowing it carries anything at all.

## The Sound Itself

And then there is the question of whether the plateau itself—the whole system, the mat and the columns and the insects and the furred ones and the sky-boned and the predators and the water cycling endlessly from root to air to root—is alive. Not whether its components are alive, which they obviously are, but whether the system constitutes an organism, a single entity with boundaries and homeostasis and something that might be called intention.

The sound suggests it. That roar, that wall of noise that has been so constant for so long that the stone beneath it has learned to vibrate in sympathy—it is not random. It is layered, structured, interlocking. The insects provide the bass, the furred ones the middle register, the sky-boned the treble, and the predators the silence between the notes that gives the whole composition rhythm. The column-plants provide the physical architecture that shapes the acoustics, and the mat provides the insulation that keeps the sound from escaping, and the humidity provides the medium that carries it, and the warmth provides the energy that sustains it.

It is a song that has been playing for longer than anything can remember. A song that no single voice chose, that emerged from the interaction of millions of lives over millions of years, that has no beginning and no end and no pauses. A song that is the proof of presence, the insistence on existing, the collective voice of everything that lives on the plateau raised simultaneously in a sound that is not communication but something more fundamental: the sound of life being alive, of matter organized into patterns that want to continue.

If you stand in the center of this place—any center, any point will do—and close your eyes, you can hear it as a single thing. Not as separate voices layered on top of each other, but as one voice, complex and enormous and without pause. The plateau's voice. The voice of the Plateau of Voices, which is the voice of everything that lives there, which is the voice of the place itself, speaking in the only language it has.

It says: *I am here. I am here. I am here.*

It says: *I am full and empty and loud and lonely and alive.*

It says: *Stay, or go. I will continue either way. I have been continuing longer than you can imagine, and I will continue longer than you can fear, and when you are gone I will still be speaking, and the sound will be the same, and the mat will grow, and the columns will breathe out rain, and the insects will drone, and the stone circles will settle deeper, and it will not matter that you were here, except that it will, because everything that lives here becomes part of the voice, and the voice remembers everything, even when the mat digests and forgets.*

*You are already part of the song. You became part of it the moment you arrived. Your footsteps changed the mat. Your breath changed the air. Your warmth changed the humidity. Your presence changed the behavior of everything that sensed you, and their changed behavior changed the behavior of everything that sensed them, and the ripples spread outward through the community of living things like waves through water, and the sound shifted—imperceptibly, minutely, but truly—and the plateau's voice is not the same as it was before you came, and it will not be the same after you leave, and this is what it means to be alive in a place that is also alive: you change each other, and the change is permanent, and the sound carries the record of it forever.*

*Listen.*

*Listen.*

*Listen.*
