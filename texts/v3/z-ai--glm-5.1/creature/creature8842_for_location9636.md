# The Inhabitants

They are not born. This is the first thing to understand, and the hardest, because it means the word "they" is already doing something wrong — implying a collection of discrete individuals who began at a point and will end at one, who can be counted, who have edges.

But let the language fail for now and work with what it can hold.

---

You see them first as movement in the middle distance, a long way down one of the interior walkways — a slow procession along the inner wall of the tower, three or four shapes moving in sequence, unhurried, their bodies casting no shadows in the bioluminescent glow. At this range you might mistake them for more fauna, another variety of the pad-footed crawlers outside, because they move low to the ground and their outlines are not quite right for anything you'd call humanoid. Not quite right for anything you'd call *any*-oid. They are taller than you expected, or longer, and they seem to have too many joints, or too few — it is difficult to tell which, because their posture keeps shifting, their bodies articulating in ways that suggest a skeleton that is either extremely flexible or not a skeleton at all.

As they pass a glowing patch of wall, you see more. You see that the light falls through them. Not all of it. Not as if they are transparent. But their bodies are dense in some places and permeable in others, layered like the substrate itself, thick opaque masses of tissue ringed by thin gauzy membranes that the bioluminescence passes through in a green-gold haze. They have mass. They have presence. They are solid enough to walk on the walkways and trigger the pressure surfaces and be registered by the tower's memory. But they are not solid the way you are solid. They are solid the way the plant is solid — which is to say, they are solid where they need to be and porous where they need to be and the distinction is maintained actively, biologically, a constant negotiation between structure and flow.

Up close — and they let you get close, they do not flee or freeze or react to you with anything you can parse as alarm — you see that their surfaces are not skin. Not cuticle either, not chitin, not bark. They are a continuation of the substrate, differentiated but continuous, growing from and returning to the same living chemistry that underlies everything here. Their undersides, where they meet the walkway, are indistinguishable from the walkway's surface. You watch one of them settle and the place where its body meets the floor *merges*, edges blurring, tissues interlacing, so that for a moment it is not standing on the floor but *of* the floor, a risen place in the surface like a wave that has not yet broken. Then it moves again, and the separation reasserts, and you understand that mobility here is not a given but a choice — a mode that can be adopted or set aside, a state rather than an inherent condition.

This is what the fauna outside do too, you realize. The pad-footed grazers. The swarming things in the creases. They are all expressions of the same system. The difference is one of complexity, not kind.

---

They do not have faces. This is not precisely true, but it is the first truth you arrive at, and it takes time to revise. What they have are *fronts* — oriented ends, directionally organized masses of sensory tissue that they present to whatever they are attending to. The front is not always at the top of the body. It migrates. When one of them is moving through a corridor, the sensory mass gathers at the leading edge, whatever edge is leading — a concentration of fine tendrils and recessed pits and smooth pressure-sensitive patches that look, from the outside, like a dense bloom of pale tissue, a complex knot that resolves and re-resolves as the organism turns its attention. When it settles, when it pauses on the walkway to do something you cannot interpret, the sensory mass relaxes, distributes itself more evenly, and what you thought was a face becomes a body like any other part of the body, as if it has closed its eyes because it has no single set of eyes to close.

They sense you. You know this because when you enter a chamber, the ones already present adjust — not dramatically, not in the way a human would turn to look, but in a slow redistribution of their sensory tissue, a gathering of complexity on the side of their bodies that faces you. They *face* you the way a plant faces a light source, gradually, oriented by need. And they sustain that orientation as long as you are there, and release it when you leave, and you realize that they have been attending to you in the same way they attend to everything: fully, specifically, without urgency, and without any indication of whether they find you interesting or merely present.

---

There are no children. This is connected to the matter of birth, and it takes you days to understand the connection, because you keep looking for the wrong thing — for small versions of the beings you see, for juveniles, for any sign of reproduction as you understand it. You find nothing. Every individual you encounter is fully formed. Every individual has been here, in some sense, as long as the tower has been here. They are not generations. They are *growths*.

Here is what happens: in certain chambers of the tower, chambers you have not been taken to but whose locations you can infer from the patterns of warmth and circulation in the walls, the plant concentrates resources. Nutrients. Trace minerals. The complex chemicals that serve here as hormones and neurotransmitters. It accumulates them in dense nodes, thick-walled and slow, and over periods of time that make your lifespan feel like a blink, those nodes differentiate. They develop internal structure. They grow. And eventually — eventually — they open, and what emerges is not new in any meaningful sense but is *additional*, a new expression of the tower's ongoing metabolism, a new set of legs and sensors and processing capacity for the vivisystem's continued operation.

The beings who live here are the tower's organs. Its extremities. Its interface with the world outside its walls. They are not separate from it and they are not owned by it; those concepts require a distinction that does not obtain. They are *of* it the way your hands are of you, except that your hands cannot detach and walk across the plateau and return with information about the neighboring towers, and they can. They are autonomous the way a thought is autonomous once you have thought it — it came from you, it is made of you, but it goes somewhere you did not directly send it, and it returns changed.

When you ask — and asking is its own problem, because you have no shared language, because communication happens through touch and pressure and the trace chemicals you leave on every surface you contact, because the tower reads you and they read the tower and you are only beginning to grasp that *reading* is the correct verb — when you ask about origin, about generation, about the beginning of a self, they do not seem to understand what you are asking. Not because the question is too complex but because it is too simple. It assumes a boundary that does not exist. Where does the tower end and the inhabitant begin? The question has no answer because it has no meaning. The tower does not end. The inhabitant does not begin. There is only the vivisystem, expressing itself in modes that include stillness and motion, wall and walker, the plant that computes and the people who are its computation made mobile.

---

They do not speak. Not in any way you would recognize. The sounds they make — and they do make sounds, small creaks and clicks and the low hiss of circulatory medium moving through their tissues — are incidental, the sounds of bodies in motion, not language. Their language is chemical and tactile and, most importantly, *architectural*.

They write by growing. This is the second thing that takes days to understand, and when you understand it, the whole tower rearranges itself in your perception. Those chambers you thought were unused, the ones with walls that seem denser, more complex, layered with fine filaments in patterns you took for random growth — those are libraries. Or something like libraries. Or something like the thing that libraries are a poor approximation of. The walls contain records in the arrangement of their tissues, information encoded in the branching patterns of the vascular bundles, in the density and composition of the lining, in the timing and concentration of the circulatory medium that seeps through. To read, one of them presses against the wall and stays still for a long time, and you realize that the sensory tissue on its front is not just reading pressure and temperature and trace chemistry but is reading *structure*, is parsing the growth-patterns the way your eyes parse text, is receiving information that the tower grew specifically to preserve.

They communicate with each other the same way. When two of them meet on a walkway, they touch — not briefly, not as greeting, but for extended periods, pressing surfaces together, exchanging fluids and chemicals, reading each other's tissues with the same acuity they bring to the walls. It is not a handshake. It is not an embrace. It is a merging, temporary and partial, a brief period of shared internal state, and when they separate, they have *read* each other, each has become a carrier of the other's information, each will carry some trace of the encounter to the next surface it touches, to the next individual it meets, to the walls of the tower itself. Information circulates. It circulates through them. They are the medium and the message both.

And this is why there are no children, not really, not the way you mean it. Every new growth that emerges from the tower's nodal chambers is born reading. It emerges saturated with the information accumulated in the resources that formed it, the concentrated chemistry of decades of slow circulation, the growth-patterns and tissue-arrangements that encode everything the tower has known. It does not learn the way you learned — tabula rasa, taking decades to absorb what your culture deemed essential. It inherits, biologically, chemically, structurally, the way a cell inherits, and then it adds to that inheritance, and when it eventually returns to the substrate — and they do return, they settle, they merge, they become floor and wall and the basis for some future growth that will carry their traces in its tissue — it deposits everything it gathered back into the system. Nothing is lost. Nothing is forgotten. The tower's memory is not a record; it is a body, and it grows the way bodies grow, by incorporation and transformation.

---

There are things they do that you cannot parse as activity. Long periods of stillness in contact with walls. Slow circuits through chambers you cannot distinguish from any other chamber. Time spent on the exterior walkways, exposed to the cold dim air, pressurized surfaces of their bodies oriented toward the grey sky or toward neighboring towers or toward nothing you can identify. You watch them and you cannot tell if they are working or resting or thinking or praying or simply existing in a mode that has no human equivalent. The distinction probably does not apply.

But sometimes you see something that might be art. Might be. The word is wrong, freighted with assumptions about intentionality and audience and the category of the aesthetic that may not translate, but it is the word you have, so you use it. On certain walls in certain chambers, the tissue is different — denser, more complex, grown in patterns that do not seem to serve any structural or informational purpose you can identify. They are beautiful, these patches, if beauty is a word you can use here. They are intricate and they are *excess*, they are organization that exceeds function, and when you press your palm to them you feel something that might be the plant's equivalent of joy, a brief intensification of circulation, a flush of warmth and chemical activity that is not a response to any input you can perceive, that seems to arise from the tissue itself, for no reason, or for reasons that have nothing to do with survival or computation or the processing of data.

The inhabitants spend time near these patches. They touch them. They add to them, you realize — slowly, over the course of days, you watch one of them press against a patch of patterned wall and stay there, and when it moves away, the pattern has changed, grown a new branch of complexity, a new layer of structure that integrates with what was there before and extends it in a direction that seems, if not random, then at least not determined by anything you can identify. They are making something. Or adding to something. Or *being* something in a way that leaves a trace.

This might be their art. This might be their conversation. This might be the vivisystem thinking about itself, reflecting on its own structure, elaborating for the sake of elaboration, the way a vine grows not toward anything in particular but because growing is what it does and the direction it takes is a record of every variable that acted upon it and the result is something that no one asked for and no one planned and that is, nevertheless, complex and coherent and, if you are the kind of creature that finds complexity coherent and coherence beautiful, then beautiful.

You press your palm to the wall. You leave your own trace, your own trace chemicals, the unique signature of your foreign biochemistry. The pattern absorbs it. Integrates it. The wall grows very slightly different, and you understand that you are in the record now, that the vivisystem has registered you as it registers everything, that your presence has become part of its body, and that long after you leave, after the trace of your chemistry has faded from every surface you touched, the pattern will still bear some echo of your having been here, some slight deviation in the growth that would not have occurred if you had not pressed your palm to this wall at this moment.

This is what it is to be remembered by a city that is also a plant that is also a system that is also, in some sense you cannot quite define, a mind.

---

They do not name themselves. You understand this eventually, when you have been here long enough to recognize individuals — and you do recognize them, their patterns of movement, the specific configurations of their sensory tissue, the way they lean or settle or articulate their too-many-jointed bodies — but when you try to map names onto them, the exercise feels wrong, feels like a category error. A name implies a permanence that they do not claim. Each of them has been multiple things — has been wall, has been walker, has been part of the substrate and part of the tower and part of another being temporarily, during a merging — and each of them will be other things again. They do not hold onto themselves the way you hold onto yourself. They do not seem to need to.

And yet they are distinct. They have preferences, if you can call them that — places they return to, other beings they spend more time in contact with, patterns of movement that are recognizably their own. They have histories, encoded in their tissues, readable by any other being that presses close enough. They have, if not personalities, then *textures* — specific densities and distributions of information that make them who they are, for whatever value of "who" operates here.

They are not interchangeable. They are not identical. They are simply not *separate* in the way you are separate, and the separateness you perceive in them is a function of your perception, not their reality. They know where one ends and another begins, probably, the way you know where one thought ends and another begins — not because there is a wall between them but because there is a distinction in quality, a shift in content, a difference that makes a difference.

---

At night — and it is night only because the dim light dims further, and the grey sky darkens toward a grey that is almost black, and the temperature drops, and the fauna outside chorus their dusk-call, and the tower's bioluminescence brightens in response — at night, the interior changes. The inhabitants settle. Not all of them, not all at once, but many of them, in a slow wave that moves through the tower like the peristaltic breathing of the substrate, like a relaxation that is also a deepening. They find places on the walls, on the floors, in the creases between structural elements, and they press against the tower, and they merge, and they go still.

Not sleep. You do not think it is sleep. Their sensory tissue does not retract or close; it distributes, spreading across their surfaces, and they become more like the wall than they were, less like a thing that moves and more like a thing that is. They are listening. You are sure of this. They are listening to the tower, to the network, to the slow peristaltic signals that pass through the substrate from tower to tower across the vast grey plateau. They are receiving. Processing. The tower computes, and they are part of its computation, and at night their role shifts from mobile processor to stationary one, from the part of the mind that moves and acts to the part that sits and integrates.

In the morning — and it is morning only because the light lightens, and the fauna outside chorus again, and the inhabitants begin to detach and reorient and resume their circuits — in the morning, they know things they did not know the night before. You cannot say how you know this, but you see it in the way they move, in the adjusted trajectories they take through the corridors, in the specific walls they pause at and the specific other beings they seek out for merging. Something has been communicated. Something has been decided. The network has processed, and they have received the results, and they act on them without deliberation, without any visible process of deciding, the way your body decides to heal a wound or your immune system decides to attack an infection — not through conscious choice but through the integrated operation of a system that includes them and exceeds them and does not require their explicit consent to function.

This is what it is to be part of something. This is what it is to be an organ of a living city. This is what it is to be a cell in a body that is also a building that is also a plain that is also a mind that does not care about you because it does not need to care about you because caring is a category that applies to separate beings facing each other across a gap, and there is no gap here, there has never been a gap here, there is only the vivisystem, breathing and growing and computing and remembering and occasionally, for reasons no part of it could explain, growing something beautiful on a wall that serves no purpose, that stores no data, that simply *is*, and is cherished, and is kept, and is passed over every day by beings who touch it as they pass and add their traces to it and carry its traces with them, and who would not call it art or prayer or memory or love, but who do not leave it alone, who return to it, who tend it, who grow it, who are grown by it, who are — in the only way that matters here — *of* it, and in it, and it in them, forever, until the substrate breathes its last, and the towers fall silent, and the grey sky goes grey at last into a darkness that no one will be left to see.
