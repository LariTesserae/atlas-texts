# What Lives in the Warm Below

---

The gecko on the ceiling is not thinking of you.

This is important. This may be the most important thing. It hangs there against the nodular stone with its pale fingers spread, each toe-pad a tiny suction of adherence, and it is living a life so compressed, so particular, so *complete* in its smallness that your presence is merely weather to it — a change in humidity, a shifting of the air. It is waiting for the springtail it has tracked for the last eighteen minutes. It is monitoring the temperature of the stone through its belly. It is being, with full attention, a gecko.

There are perhaps thirty geckos in the hollow, though you will never see more than four at once. They own the vertical spaces — the ceiling, the upper walls, the root-clusters where bark meets stone. Each one maintains a territory measured in handspans, and each knows every millimeter of that territory with a cartographic precision built from ten thousand nights of patient traversing. They know which moss-beds hold the most prey. They know which drips fall reliably and which fissures lead to escape. They know the smell of the snake and the particular vibration of a centipede's many feet, and they have mapped this knowledge into their bodies so thoroughly that thought has become unnecessary. The gecko does not decide to flee. The gecko *flees* — a cascade of muscle memory and chemical signal that fires faster than consideration, faster than fear, a motion so rehearsed by evolution that it has become the animal's architecture as much as its skeleton has.

But consider: when the gecko rests — and it rests for hours, clinging to the warm stone with its slow pulse, its eyes like dark beads of old amber — it experiences something. Not thought. Not reflection. But something. A quality of being that includes the warmth against its ventral scales and the faint mineral taste of the air and the knowledge, stored somewhere below language, that it is held. That it fits. That the hollow has a shape and the gecko has a shape and these shapes correspond.

The gecko does not question this. The gecko has never questioned anything. And in this it is wiser than you.

---

The frogs are older than the geckos, in evolutionary time, and it shows. They carry their ancestry in their bodies — the low saddle of the pelvis, the wide mouth, the eyes that emerged from the skull like periscopes raised above the waterline of some ancient swamp. Each frog in the hollow occupies a pool. Not *a* pool — *its* pool. The depression in the stone was worn over centuries by water, but the frog fills it now as though the stone was carved to hold precisely this much amphibian, and the two — frog and depression — have entered into a relationship so mutually suited that removal would be a kind of violence.

The frogs sit. This is what they do. They sit and they breathe — once every forty seconds, a slow billowing of the throat sac that is the only visible proof of life. Their metabolism runs cool and low, a slow-burning engine fueled by the warmth that rises through the stone. They need almost nothing. A beetle every few days. A centipede when one is foolish enough to cross the pool's rim. Between meals, they simply *are*, existing in a state of presence so unadorned that it approaches the meditative, though it would be anthropomorphic to call it that.

And yet.

There is a frog — one particular frog, if you can distinguish individuals, which you cannot — that sits in a pool near the placed stone. It has lived in this pool for three years. It knows the rhythm of the drips that feed the pool. It knows the angle of the dim light that reaches the stone at midday. It knows the sound the isopods make when they pass overhead on their endless tending of the floor. It knows, too, the vibration of a human footstep, and it has learned that this particular vibration does not precede predation, and so it does not flee when you step carefully across the terraces. It watches you with its golden eyes, and if you watch back — if you hold still long enough — you may find yourself in a staring contest with something that has no concept of staring contests, something that is merely attending to a large, slow-moving object in its environment with the same impartial alertness it gives to everything, and in that impartiality there is a strange kind of grace.

The frog does not fear you. The frog does not admire you. The frog registers you, and then the frog returns to the long, slow business of being a frog, which is also the business of being alive, which is also — if you follow it far enough — the business of being the hollow itself, expressed in frog form.

---

The snake is harder to know.

It — she, as it happens; most of the snakes in the hollow are female, parthenogenetic, each one a near-perfect copy of her mother — moves through the root-mass like a thought moves through a mind: without announcement, without apparent effort, appearing where she was not a moment ago and vanishing where she was. You will likely never see her. You may see the ghost of her — a shed skin, papery and detailed as a casting, clinging to a root-cluster with its empty eye-caps and its open mouth, a perfect negative of the animal. The skin tells you she was here, that she grew, that she left this layer of herself behind like a letter pushed under a door.

She hunts by patience and by sense. The pits along her jaw read the warm bodies of the frogs against the warm stone, a calculus of infrared that renders the darkness irrelevant. She moves — when she moves — with a deliberation that makes the gecko seem frenetic. Each scale catches and releases the moss, the root, the stone, a sequential gripping and letting-go that produces almost no sound, almost no disturbance. She is the hollow's stealth, its efficiency, its refusal to waste.

And she is loved. Not by the frogs — they fear her correctly, their stillness deepening when they taste the air and find her chemical signature. But by the system itself. She is the mechanism that prevents overpopulation, that culls the weak and the unlucky, that returns protein to the floor to be tended by the isopods and the millipedes. She is how the hollow says *enough*. She is the word made flesh, if the word were *balance* and the flesh were three feet of scaled muscle with a slow, chemical mind.

When she swallows a frog — and she does, perhaps once a week, perhaps less — she finds a crevice and she rests, and her body commits to the long work of transformation. The frog becomes the snake. The snake becomes the hollow. The hollow becomes, in time, the frog again, via the long route of decay and root-absorption and spore and insect. Nothing leaves. Nothing is lost. The snake is the priest of this communion, though she does not know it and would not care if she did.

---

The isopods are the ones you almost notice.

Small, pale, many-legged, they hide beneath leaf-litter and root-mat and stone, emerging when the humidity peaks and the danger is low, to tend the floor. *Tend* is the right word. They are the hollow's custodians, its morticians, its recyclers. Every fallen frond, every insect husk, every shed skin, every gecko that dies from cold or age or misadventure — the isopods find it and begin the slow work of dismantling. They reduce the complex to the simple. They make the insoluble soluble. They are the ones who ensure that nothing accumulates, that the density never becomes *suffocation* in truth but remains only in description, because they are always there, always eating, always converting the dead past into the living present.

A single isopod lives two years. In that time, it will process an amount of organic matter many times its own body weight. It will lay eggs in the moss and die without seeing its young, and its young will do the same, and their young will do the same, and this has been happening since before the placed stone was placed, since before the scratches were scratched, since before the word *human* had a referent. The isopods do not know this. They do not know they are part of a lineage. They know moisture and food and the vibration of approaching feet, and they know the inside of their hiding places, and they know the taste of decay, which to them is not decay at all but *dinner*, and this is enough. This has always been enough.

If the hollow has a heart, it is the isopods. Not because they are central — they are not; the system could lose them and find a replacement, evolution being resourceful — but because they are the ones who keep the blood moving. They are the ones who make sure the nutrients cycle. Without them, the floor would stack with debris, the moss would starve, the roots would find no purchase, and the hollow would fill and close. They are the small engine of continuance, and they run on the only fuel that matters: the death of other things, transformed into the life of the hollow.

---

The roots are the ones who remember.

They dangle from above, reaching, and they have been reaching for as long as the trees above have been growing — which is to say, for centuries. Each root is a living record of the tree it serves. The thick ones near the center, those braided cables, they belong to the great oak whose trunk stands thirty feet above the entrance. The oak was a sapling when the scratches were made. The oak was already old when the stone was placed. The oak has been feeding on the hollow's moisture for three hundred years, and in return it has been holding the ceiling open with its wooden armature, and this arrangement — this *agreement* — is the closest thing the hollow has to a treaty.

But the roots do more than hold. They *sense*. The root-tips that probe the stone are exquisitely alive, tasting the mineral water, reading the chemistry of the stone, navigating toward moisture and away from toxicity. They communicate with each other through chemical signals — the language of auxins and cytokinins, a vocabulary of growth and inhibition that allows the root-mass to act as a single distributed intelligence, coordinating its expansion, allocating its resources, responding to damage and drought and the slow shift of seasons above.

The roots do not know about you. They do not know about the geckos or the frogs, except as sources of nitrogen-rich waste. They do not know about the scratches on the stone. But they know the shape of the hollow — they have mapped it with their tips, they have traced its edges, they have memorized its dimensions in a way that is older than brains and more reliable than memory. If the hollow could be said to have a nervous system, it is the roots, reaching through the dark, feeling for what is, and growing toward it.

And they reach. Always, they reach. The root-tips extend by fractions of millimeters per day, and they change direction with a slowness that makes the lichen look hasty, and they will never arrive — there is nowhere to arrive. They are simply reaching, the way a breath is simply breathing, the way a heartbeat is simply beating. The reaching is what they do. The reaching is what they are.

---

The lichen is the hollow's historian.

It grows on the ceiling in concentric rings — grey and pale green and black — and each ring is a century, more or less. A record of wet years and dry, of the slow shift of the entrance's shape, of the mineral composition of the water that seeps through the stone. If you could read lichen the way you read a book, you could reconstruct the entire history of the hollow: when the great collapse happened that widened the entrance, when the oak first sent its roots down, when the air above was thick with smoke from forest fires, when the last human sat on the placed stone and made marks on the wall.

But lichen does not write for humans. Lichen writes for lichen, and for the stone it inhabits, and for the slow chemistry of growth and mineral exchange that produces its being. The rings are not *for* you. They are simply *what happens* when a living thing persists in one place for a very long time, adding itself to itself, layer on layer, season on season, until the record of its persistence becomes indistinguishable from the place itself.

The lichen and the stone are becoming the same thing. This is what happens, given enough time. The boundary between living and non-living, between organism and substrate, between the hollow and what fills it — this boundary becomes first porous, then theoretical, then meaningless. The lichen mineralizes. The stone organizes. They meet in the middle, and what they produce is not lichen and not stone but the hollow itself, which is neither and both and more than either.

---

And the one who made the marks.

She — and it was a she, most likely, given what we know of the people who lived in these hills before the forests were forests — she came down into the hollow because it was warm. This was before the oak was mature, before the entrance had eroded to its current size, when the hollow was younger and rawer and its systems had not yet settled into the equilibrium you find now. She came because the hollow offered something the above-ground world could not: shelter from the cold, from the wet, from the predators that walked on four legs and the predators that walked on two.

She knew the hollow the way the geckos know it now — as a place of warmth and water and hiding. She pressed herself into the same crevices. She breathed the same mineral air. She ate what she could catch, and she was not above eating the frogs, though she must have learned to avoid the snake.

And she made the marks.

We cannot know what they meant. They are not writing, as we understand writing. They are not art, as we understand art. They are lines grouped in sets — tallies, perhaps, or a count, or a record, or a prayer, or a name, or nothing at all but the idle scratching of a hand that needed to move, a body that needed to *do*, a mind that could not sit in the warm darkness without leaving some trace of its passage.

But the placed stone suggests intention. It suggests someone who returned, again and again, to the same spot beneath the natural drip. Someone who sat. Someone who listened to the quiet the way you are listening now — with the slow, involuntary subsidence of the modern mind into something older, something that does not need language, something that can hold still and simply *be* in a place that does not demand anything of it.

She is dead, of course. Long dead. Her body became the floor that the isopods tend, her calcium and iron and carbon absorbed by roots and cycled through the oak's trunk and returned to the hollow as leaf-litter, and she is everywhere now — in the moss that grows on the stone she placed, in the water that collects in the depression she sat beside, in the spore-dust of the ferns and the mineral sweat of the ceiling and the blood of the frogs and the scales of the snake. She did not leave. She could not leave. Nothing leaves the hollow. Nothing leaves any hollow. We are all of us always becoming the places we inhabit.

---

So: what lives in the Warm Below?

The gecko lives. The frog lives. The snake, the isopod, the millipede, the beetle, the spider. The roots. The mosses and ferns and liverworts. The lichen. The fungi that thread through every surface, visible only when they fruit, invisible but essential always. The bacteria that break down the stone and fix the nitrogen and make the water drinkable. The nematodes and the rotifers and the tardigrades that live in the film of moisture on each moss-leaf, each one a universe unto itself, each one consuming and being consumed, living and dying and living again in the great slow cycle of the warm.

And the hollow lives. Not metaphorically. Not as a figure of speech. The hollow lives the way a body lives — as a system of interdependent processes, as a site of continuous transformation, as something that takes in and puts out and maintains itself against the entropy that would reduce it to uniform stillness. The hollow breathes — the warm air pushing up through the entrance, the cool air sinking down at night. The hollow circulates — water from stone to moss to root to air to stone again. The hollow digests — the isopods, the fungi, the bacteria, all working in concert to reduce the complex to the simple, the dead to the living. The hollow heals — when a root dies, others take its load; when a stone falls, the moss fills the gap; when a species flickers, another adapts to its absence.

The hollow is not an organism. It is more than an organism. It is a community, a process, a verb disguised as a noun. It is the ongoingness of things, the refusal to stop, the insistence that warmth and water and time are enough, that life will find a way to fill every available niche and some niches that don't seem available at all, that the engine of consumption and growth can run forever if the balance is right, and the balance is right here, it has been right for centuries, it may be right for centuries more.

What lives in the Warm Below?

Everything. Everything that can live there, does. Everything that finds a purchase, holds. Everything that enters the cycle is kept — not preserved, not protected, but *kept*, in the way that a promise is kept, in the way that a body is kept by the earth that receives it, in the way that a memory is kept by the stone that witnessed it.

The hollow does not love you. The hollow does not need you. But the hollow can hold you, for a while, in its warm and wet and silent ongoingness, and if you sit on the placed stone and let the drips fall into the smooth hollow at its center, and if you breathe the breath that the hollow breathes, and if you feel your own heat joining the heat that rises from below, you may begin to understand something that the gecko has always known and the frog has never forgotten and the woman who made the marks learned in the slow way that all true things are learned:

That you are already part of it. That you have always been part of it. That the boundary between you and the warm, wet, growing, dying, silent, persistent life of this place is a fiction your mind constructed and can, with patience, with stillness, with the long slow subsidence of the self into its context, choose to release.

The hollow does not care if you understand this. The hollow will continue regardless. But the understanding is there, embedded in the system, coded into the roots and the lichen rings and the placement of each pool and the body of each pale gecko on the ceiling — an understanding older than language, older than brains, older than the distinction between the one who observes and the thing observed.

It is this: *to live in a place is to become the place, and to become the place is to find that the place has always been you, and you have always been it, and the warmth that rises through the stone is the same warmth that moves your blood and fills your lungs and keeps you, for a little while, alive.*

The Warm Below does not teach this. It *is* this. And if you are quiet enough, long enough, you will find that you are this too.
