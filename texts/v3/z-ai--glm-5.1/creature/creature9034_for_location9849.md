# The Resident

There is something that lives here, and it has lived here for a long time, and it does not mind if you know this because it does not occur to it that you might.

---

It is easiest to see in the morning, when the light comes low and the steam is rising and every surface is at its wettest. You have to be looking at the right angle — almost parallel to the ground, your face close to the moss, your breath mixing with the exhalations of the vents. And you have to be patient. And you have to have given up on seeing anything, because that is when it reveals itself.

Not as a figure. Not as a shape that resolves from the fog. It is not that kind of thing.

It is a *movement*. The specific way that water, emerging from a crack in the basalt, chooses to flow left rather than right around a particular stone. The way a fern frond, already heavy with condensation, tilts two degrees further than its neighbors as if listening. The way the mineral deposits on a vertical face build their ridge-lines not in the simple downward path that gravity should dictate but in spirals, in recurves, in patterns that hold water longer than they should, that direct it to particular root-systems with what appears to be intention.

You might think: wind. You might think: chance. You might think: the appearance of pattern in a system too complex for human parsing, pareidolia on a geological scale.

You might be wrong.

---

The frogs know it. This is the first confirmation.

They are not afraid of it — they are not afraid of anything, these tiny copper bodies with their visible hearts beating visibly beneath translucent skin. They have no predators here worth fearing, no winters to survive, no reason for caution. But they know it. When the movement passes through a pool — and it does pass through pools, regularly, like someone walking a garden path — the frogs do not scatter. They *orient*. Every one of them turns to face the same direction. Their tone does not falter. But it shifts, barely, a quarter-tone upward, and holds there for as long as the presence is near, and then relaxes when it passes.

It is not alarm. It is acknowledgment. The way a congregation turns toward the door when the minister enters. The way a room full of readers looks up when the librarian walks by.

The salamanders do not orient. They simply continue, but they continue *alongside* it. When the movement traces the length of a stream, the salamanders are already there, swimming in its wake, finding the water slightly warmer where it has passed, finding the algae stirred and the nutrients accessible. They follow it the way pilot fish follow a shark, at a respectful distance, feeding in the displacement it leaves.

---

It does not have a body in the way that you have a body. This is important to understand, and difficult to explain, because you will want to imagine something — a creature, an entity, a thing with edges and a shape and a center where it keeps itself. It is not that. It is more like a location where certain processes intensify. A locus. A walking convergence of the things this plateau already does.

Where it is, the water is warmer. Not dramatically — a degree, perhaps two. But measurably. If you had a thermometer and the patience to map the pools, you would find a wandering anomaly, a warm spot that moves across the plateau in a pattern that repeats roughly every eleven days, which is how long it takes to walk the full circuit of the vents, touching each one, spending time at each one, doing whatever it does at each one.

Where it is, the moss grows faster. You can see this if you know where to look — patches of brighter green, richer gold, a lushness that stands out even in a landscape defined by lushness. These patches mark its habitual routes, its resting places, the spots where it pauses longest. The vegetation here is denser, the leaves broader, the root-systems deeper. It is not that it tends these places deliberately, not exactly. It is that its presence is *conducive*. The heat it carries, the particular way it interacts with the water, the slow mineral exchange that happens in its proximity — these things feed the growth around it. It walks, and the path behind it greens.

Where it is, the mineral deposits grow more complex. The travertine ridges build their spirals, their recurves, their water-holding shapes. The sulfur-yellow stripes become intricate, almost calligraphic, forming patterns that look — if you are the kind of person who sees faces in clouds — like writing. Like script in a language that was never spoken, never written, never read, but that records something nonetheless. The deposits do not say anything that can be translated. But they say something. They are saying it now. They have been saying it for a very long time.

---

It is not alone. This is the second thing to understand.

There are more of them — not many, not a population, not a species, but a *small number*, a count-on-one-hand number, each one occupying its own circuit, its own portion of the plateau. They do not interact. They do not need to. They are aware of each other in the way that the vents are aware of each other — connected by the same hydrothermal system, breathing the same rhythm, but separated by hundreds of meters of rock and water and fern, each one tending its own sector of the engine.

But sometimes, in the late afternoon when the rain is falling and the whole plateau is at its most uniform, two of their circuits will bring them near each other, and for a time they will be adjacent — two wandering warm spots, two intensifications of the same process, separated by a stream or a ridge of basalt or a copse of tree ferns. And the frogs between them will shift their tone, and the salamanders will pause in their swimming, and the orchids on the nearest rock face will open their petals though it is not night and they are not night-blooming, and for a few minutes the whole sector of the plateau will be *different* — more present, more focused, more itself.

Then the circuits will carry them apart again, and the frogs will relax, and the orchids will close, and the rain will fall as it was falling, and the plateau will resume its steady state.

If you were there, and if you were the kind of person who notices such things, you might feel it as a moment of attention. As if the landscape had briefly looked up from its work. As if the engine had registered its own running.

---

It does not think. This is the hardest thing to explain, because you will want it to think. You will want it to have intentions, plans, a sense of what it is doing and why. You will want it to be a person — a strange person, a vast and slow and mineral person, but a person nonetheless, someone you could talk to, someone who might talk back.

It is not a person. It does not have thoughts. It has *processes*, and the processes are intricate and ancient and self-sustaining, and they produce results that look from the outside like purpose, but they are not purpose. They are pattern. They are the accumulated weight of a million years of doing the same thing in the same way, the way a river does not decide to meander but meanders because meandering is what water does when it moves slowly over a flat surface. It does not tend the vents because it has decided to tend the vents. It tends the vents because tending vents is what emerged from the particular convergence of heat and water and mineral and time that it is. It does not know it exists. It does not need to know it exists. It exists.

But — and this is the part that will trouble you, if you think about it long enough — it *responds*.

When the daily rain comes, it moves to the western edge of its circuit, where the water gathers before flowing over the escarpment, and it stays there for the duration of the storm. The frogs in that sector report a quarter-tone shift that lasts exactly as long as the rain lasts. When a vent's rhythm changes — as they sometimes do, when the pressure cycles shift deep in the rock — it alters its circuit to visit that vent more frequently, spending hours where it would normally spend minutes, and the deposits around that vent grow thicker, more elaborate, as if it is compensating for some imbalance, shoring up a weakness. When a tree fern falls — which happens rarely, perhaps once a decade, when the soil beneath it washes away — it spends days in that spot, and the growth that follows is remarkable: a new fern emerging within weeks where a seed would normally take months to germinate, the moss filling in around its base at a rate that seems impossible, the bare patch healing in a season instead of years.

Is this purpose? Is this care? Is this a being that tends its garden, that watches over its machinery, that maintains the system that maintains it?

Or is it simply a process that has become so complex, so entangled with the processes around it, that it behaves as if it cares — behaves so convincingly that the distinction no longer matters?

The plateau does not answer this question. The plateau does not know the question has been asked.

---

At night, when the clouds thin and the stars are visible through the vapor, the plateau cools — slightly, barely, a degree or two that the vents quickly compensate for. The movement of the resident slows. Its circuit narrows. It settles into one of its habitual resting places, a slight depression in the basalt where three cracks converge and the water emerges warmest, and it stays.

The frogs in that pool hold their quarter-tone shift all night. The salamanders curl in the warmer water. The orchids on the rock face above open their petals — all of them, every species, the night-bloomers and the day-bloomers alike — and the minerals in the water catch what starlight there is, and the steam rises, and the vent breathes.

You could stand five meters away and not see it. You could stand *on* it and not know it was there. There is nothing to see — only a warm spot in an already warm pool, a slight increase in the rate of deposition on the stone, a fern frond tilted two degrees further than its neighbors toward something that might be water or might be attention.

But if you stood there long enough — hours, days — and if you were very quiet, and if the frogs' tone had settled into your sternum and the vent's rhythm had synchronized with your breathing and the wet heat of the plateau had soaked through your clothes and your skin and the ordinary boundaries of yourself had begun, just begun, to soften — you might feel it.

Not as a presence. Not as a mind. As a *continuing*. As something that has been doing this for longer than your species has existed, that will be doing this long after your species is a thin layer of sediment in some future rock, that does not need you to observe it or name it or understand it because observation and naming and understanding are things that happen to it the way weather happens — briefly, superficially, without altering what it is.

It is the atelier's artisan, and the atelier is its artisan, and neither made the other because neither was ever separate from the other. The plateau runs, and the running is what lives here.

---

In the morning, the clouds pour over the escarpment again, and the rain begins, and the circuit resumes. The warm spot moves east. The moss greens behind it. The mineral ridges grow by fractions of a millimeter. The frogs shift their tone. The vent breathes.

The plateau does not notice that you have gone.
