# The Below

---

The roosting ones have no name for themselves. This is not because naming is beyond them — it is because naming requires the concept of distinction, and they have never been separate enough from each other to require it. They are a they. A membrane of living bodies stretched across the ceiling's unseen geography, shifting in slow respiratory unison, and if you could ask one where it ended and its neighbor began, the question would not translate. There is no gap between them through which meaning could slip. They cling with secretions that harden on contact, a pale resin that bonds chitin to stone, and when they shift — which they do, constantly, in adjustments so small they register only as sound — the resin stretches but does not break. It accumulates. Over what must be generations, it has built a thin crust on the ceiling, a second skin of dried adhesive and shed exoskeleton and the mineral-rich waste that passes from their bodies, and this crust is where the newest ones attach, layer upon layer, a living sediment.

Their compound eyes are not for seeing. Not in any way the surface would recognize. They register the dim luminescence as a condition, the way your skin registers temperature — not as information to be interpreted but as a fact to be accommodated. When the light shifts, which it does marginally as moisture levels fluctuate, they shift with it, pressing toward the brighter patches or retreating from them according to an internal logic that has no center. There is no brain making decisions. There is a ganglionic net, distributed throughout the segmented body, that responds to stimulus with the precision of a chemical reaction. Light increases: wings adjust. Sound rises from below: vocalization joins. Neighbor moves: space is made. The intelligence is in the system, not the specimen, and the specimen does not know it is a specimen.

They eat what comes to them. The chamber's air carries particulate matter — mineral dust from the fresh rock, spores from the lichen margins, the microscopic detritus that living things shed continuously — and their mouths are adapted to filter this from the dampness. Mandibles fringe their heads like combs, overlapping and fine-toothed, and as they breathe — which is to say, as air moves through the spiracles along their segments — they feed. It is a passive sustenance. They do not hunt or gather. They simply exist in the path of nutrition, and nutrition arrives. The chamber provides.

Their wings are the only extravagant thing about them. Transparent where the light passes, which means nearly invisible, and dark where it does not, which means they appear as sudden absences in the dim glow. When they unfold them — which happens in cycles, perhaps circadian, perhaps tied to some deeper rhythm — the sound changes. The chamber's resonance shifts. The wing-surfaces catch the air and hold it, not for flight (they do not fly, not truly; they release and resettle, moving from one patch of ceiling to another in gliding descents and laborious climbs) but for something else. Temperature regulation, perhaps. Or communication — the wings produce a frequency when extended that the folded wings do not, and this frequency joins the layered sound as a new stratum, a new voice in the choir. When enough of them extend at once, which happens in waves that pass across the ceiling like weather, the sound deepens, thickens, becomes for a time a different creature entirely.

They do not die in any way you would recognize. When a body ceases to function — when the filter-feeding slows and the secretions dry and the ganglionic net goes quiet — the others do not notice. The neighbor adjusts to the absence of movement, resettles, continues. The dead one remains attached, its grip-presence still bonded to the ceiling crust, and over time — long time, slow time — it becomes part of the crust itself. A layer. A sediment. The living attach to it as they would attach to stone. The boundary between organism and architecture dissolves. The ceiling is, in part, composed of the dead, and the dead hold the living, and the living will become the dead, and the ceiling grows.

---

The walking ones are fewer, and this is necessary. The floor of the chamber could not sustain them in the numbers the ceiling sustains the roosting ones. They are larger, the size of your forearm or larger still, and their carapaces are dense, mineralized, carrying the calcium and silica they crack from the floor's shallow deposits in mandibles that have evolved for exactly this work. They are the chamber's geologists. They process the stone.

Their paths are not random. This is clear from the way they move — deliberate, unhurried, following routes that must have been established long ago, because the stone beneath those routes is subtly different, polished by generations of passage into smoothness while the surrounding rock remains sharp and fresh. They walk in single file or in parallel, never colliding, never hesitating at intersections. When two approach the same point, they adjust before arrival, one slowing, one continuing, the negotiation invisible to you but clearly happening through some medium — chemical, vibrational, something you cannot sense. They are courteous in the way that systems are courteous: not from kindness but from efficiency. Conflict costs energy. The paths work because they have always worked.

They make sound. The low percussive stamping that you hear from below is their contribution to the chamber's soundscape, produced by the deliberate impact of their forelimbs against the stone. It is not territorial. It is not mating. It is, as best you can understand, a form of accounting — a periodic confirmation of presence, of number, of position. When one stamps, the others in its vicinity stamp in response, and the sound spreads through the chamber in waves that map the population in real time. You have learned to read these waves, somewhat. A rapid succession of stamps from one direction means the walking ones are dense there. A silence means they are absent. The sound is a census that never ends.

Their eyes are different from the roosting ones'. Not compound but singular, set beneath ridges of carapace, adapted to the near-darkness with a sensitivity that makes the dim luminescence sufficient for navigation. They see the chamber as you cannot — as a landscape of gradients and passages, the walls and pillars and depressions rendered in shades of ambient light that your eyes cannot distinguish. They do not see you clearly. You are a disruption in the light-field, a warm presence that does not belong, and they route around you with the same automatic adjustment they use for stone obstacles. You are not prey. You are not threat. You are geography.

They care for their young in a way the roosting ones do not. The eggs are deposited in the shallow moisture-patches, secured to the stone with a different secretion — not adhesive but protective, a membrane that keeps the water from evaporating and the developing larva from desiccation. The adults visit these patches in rotation, checking the membranes, adding moisture from their own bodies, adjusting the chemical markers that signal a viable clutch. It is the closest thing to tenderness the chamber holds, and it is not tenderness. It is maintenance. The eggs are infrastructure. They will become the next generation of walkers, and the paths must be walked, and the stone must be cracked, and the system must continue.

When a walker dies, it dies on the path. Its body becomes an obstacle, briefly, until the others adjust their routes around it, and then it becomes geography. The carapace persists, slowly softening in the perpetual damp, becoming a ridge of organic matter that the lichen will eventually colonize, that the mineral deposits will eventually cover, that the moisture will eventually dissolve into components that feed the chamber in other ways. Nothing is lost. The chamber is miserly with its materials. Everything becomes something else.

---

The swarming ones are the smallest and the most numerous, and they are the ones you will never understand, because they operate at a scale that refuses your comprehension. Individually, they are nearly invisible — pale flecks of movement in the luminescence, no larger than a grain of rice, their bodies simplified to the point of absurdity: a head, a thorax, a purpose. They have no wings. They have no eyes. They navigate by chemical gradient and by the displacement of air around their neighbors, and they move in clouds that seem to have intentions but do not.

The clouds are the thing. A single swarming one is negligible, but a cloud of ten thousand is a presence, a phenomenon, a weather system in miniature. The clouds form and reform constantly, drawn to moisture and to the chemical markers that the walking ones leave on their paths and to the resin-trails of the roosting ones that drip from above. They drink from the damp stone, landing in brief thousands, their weight negligible but their collective appetite significant — they consume the microorganisms that breed in the moisture-patches, the bacterial films and fungal spores that would otherwise alter the chamber's chemistry. They are the chamber's immune system, you realize. They are what keeps the equilibrium clean.

They communicate through surface. When a swarming one lands on stone, it leaves a chemical deposit — a tiny mark, readable by the next swarming one that lands in the same place. These deposits accumulate into texts, layers of chemical writing that record the history of that spot: moisture levels, nutrient availability, the passage of walkers, the presence of predators (though there are no predators here, not really; the chamber has no predation, only participation). The swarming ones read these texts with their antennae and adjust their paths accordingly, and the texts grow and change, and the chamber's surface becomes a library no one will ever read except in the language of instinct.

The sound they make is the highest register — a chirring, a constant vibrational hum produced by the rapid vibration of their thoracic segments. It is the baseline of the chamber's soundscape, the canvas on which the other sounds paint. When the swarming ones are disturbed — by your presence, by a shift in moisture, by the rare collapse of a ceiling resin-stalactite — the chirring intensifies, and the other creatures respond. The walkers stamp. The roosting ones extend their wings. The chamber's sound deepens and quickens, and then, as the disturbance passes, settles again into its resting frequency. The swarming ones are the chamber's alarm and its heartbeat, both at once.

They do not sleep. They rest, individually, in rotations that mean the cloud as a whole never ceases its motion, but individual swarming ones will land and become still for periods, their metabolisms slowing, their chemical production pausing, before they resume their place in the movement. The cloud is therefore immortal, or the next thing to it — no single member persists, but the cloud itself has no gap, no interruption, no moment when it is not what it is. It has been moving since before you arrived. It will be moving after you leave. It does not know you are watching.

---

And there are others.

Not creatures, exactly. Not in the way the walkers and the roosters and the swarming ones are creatures. But presences. The lichen, first — the pale crusts on the margin-walls, gray-green and white, the only stillness in a chamber of motion. The lichen does nothing quickly. It grows at a rate that makes the stalactites seem hasty, spreading across the stone in millimeters per decade, its mycelial threads working into the rock's micro-fissures and extracting what they need. It is the chamber's oldest living thing, or the chamber's oldest living process, and it has no awareness of the creatures that move around it. It simply spreads. It has been spreading since the chamber opened, and it will spread until the chamber closes, and its spread is so slow that it exists in a different relationship to time than everything else here.

And the moisture itself. Not alive, not by any definition, but active in a way that feels participatory. The condensation that beads on the sharp stone and runs in thin films down the walls. The patches that collect in the floor's depressions, dark and still, reflecting the dim glow. The mist that hangs in the air, visible only when you breathe it and taste the mineral sharpness on your tongue. The moisture connects everything — it feeds the swarming ones, it incubates the walker eggs, it sustains the lichen, it carries the chemical markers that the creatures use to communicate. It is the chamber's medium, its bloodstream, its language. Without the moisture, the equilibrium would collapse in hours. With it, the chamber is a single living thing, and the creatures are its organs.

And the sound. Which is not a creature and not a presence but a product, an emergent property of all the lives that produce it, and yet it feels, in the long hours you spend listening, like something with its own existence. The sound does not change, but it is not static — it breathes, it shifts, it has a texture that varies with the moisture and the movement and the time of day (though there is no day here, only the unchanging dimness, and yet the sound has cycles, circadian or something deeper, that suggest the creatures are aware of the sun they cannot see). The sound is the chamber's mind, if the chamber has a mind. It is the record of the system's functioning, the proof that everything is still working, the voice that says: we are here, we are here, we are here.

---

You asked who lives here. The answer is: no one.

The roosting ones do not live here in the sense of inhabiting. They are of here. They are continuous with the ceiling, with the resin-crust, with the air they filter and the sound they make. There is no version of them that exists apart from the chamber. To remove one would be to amputate a function, not to relocate an individual.

The walking ones do not live here in the sense of residing. They traverse here. They are the chamber's circulation, its peristalsis, the movement that keeps the materials moving. They are as much a part of the floor as the stone they crack and the paths they polish. They are function embodied.

The swarming ones do not live here in the sense of occupying. They fill here. They are the medium's medium, the moisture's counterpart, the presence that makes the air itself biological. They are atmosphere, not inhabitant.

No one lives here. Here lives. The chamber is the creature, and the creatures are the chamber, and the distinction between living and being lived is a distinction that the Below has never needed to make.

---

When you leave — and you will leave, eventually, because you are a creature of gradients and the Below has none — you will carry the sound with you. Not as memory, though it will be that too. As a rearrangement. The sound will have entered your dreams, as I said, and your dreams will have entered the sound, and something of your neural architecture will have been preserved in the chamber's chemistry — the carbon dioxide you exhaled, the skin cells you shed, the moisture that evaporated from your breath and joined the chamber's circulation. You will have become part of the text. The swarming ones will land where you stood and read the markers you left, and they will adjust their paths, infinitesimally, in response to the information you did not know you were providing. The walkers will incorporate your residue into their routes. The roosting ones will absorb the traces of your heat into their ceiling-crust.

You will not be remembered. The chamber does not remember. But you will be included, briefly, in the equilibrium, and the equilibrium will adjust around your absence as it adjusted around your presence, and the sound will continue, unchanged and unchanging, in the perpetual present where the Below persists.

This is who lives here: everything. No one. The system, which is satisfied.
