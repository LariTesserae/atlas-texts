# Who Lives in the Transpiration Quarter

---

The workers have a word for themselves that does not translate cleanly. It means something like *the ones who stay while it grows around them*. It carries an acknowledgment that they were not first—were not, in the strictest sense, necessary—and that they may not be last.

---

There are twelve on a full shift, but twelve is not a stable number. The Quarter employs what it needs, and what it needs shifts like its floor-plans. There have been seasons with nine. There have been months with fifteen, when the fungal bloom came early and the presses could not keep pace with the harvest and extra hands were borrowed from the drying-yards downstream. The workers come from families that have always worked wet infrastructure—dyke-tenders, peat-cutters, reed-processors, people whose ancestry is written in the calluses on their palms and the particular way they walk on uncertain ground. They learn the Quarter the way a child learns weather: by immersion, by consequence, by the gradual accumulation of predictions that become instincts.

Maret has been here longest. Fourteen years. Her hands are stained a permanent amber-brown from handling the fungal shelves, the pigment driven into the grain of her skin like dye into wood. She can tell the mineral content of a trough by touching the water—calcium makes it slick, iron makes it sticky, and the rare manganese trace that signals a productive bloom makes it tight, almost dense, like a liquid that has learned to resist. She does not remember learning this. Her hands learned it before her mind could name what they knew.

Maret sleeps in the wall-cavity during long shifts. The hammock there has conformed to her shape. The bark-lining has worn smooth where her shoulder brushes it each time she turns. A small shelf-fungus—accidental, not a cultivar—has established itself on the inner wall near her head, and she has not removed it, and now it is part of how she finds her way in the dim-lit hours: the faint smell of its spores, damp and slightly sharp, means *here*, means *rest*, means *the place that has learned the shape of your body*.

She is thirty-nine. She moves through the Quarter with a gait that is not quite a walk—more of a weight-shifting glide on the clogs, keeping her center of gravity low, never committing her full weight to any single deck-board because she knows which ones have softened with rot beneath and which have root-pressures building underneath that will give without warning. She does not think about this. Her body thinks about it the way a bird's body thinks about wind.

---

Collum is new. Eight months. He is still learning the grammar of the place, which is not a single grammar but several—the language of sounds (the compressor's knock means something different at the start of a shift than at the end), the language of smells (the sweet-rot edge in the air means the root-troughs need circulating; the metallic sharpness means the copper gutters are shedding oxide), the language of temperatures (if the air feels thicker on the left, the eastern press is running hot). He makes mistakes. He steps on the wrong deck-plate and his foot goes through and the water below is warm and shocked and closes around his ankle like something alive, which it is, in a sense, because the water here is never just water.

The other workers do not laugh at him. They remember their own early months, the slow process of becoming a creature that can live in the Quarter without fighting it. They know that fighting it is the first instinct—the urge to clear the mold, to straighten the pipe, to pry the burl away from the compressor, to impose on the building the logic of a machine. This instinct must be unlearned. The Quarter is not a machine. It is a process, and you do not command a process; you participate in it.

Collum is learning to participate. He has stopped flinching when the condensation falls on his neck. He has started noticing when the fig-membrane is shedding heavily—the small dry scales of leaf that drift down from the ceiling like snow in a warm country—without being told. He has not yet learned to sleep in the wall-cavity. The breathing of the building keeps him awake: that slow, deep pulse of sap in the southern wall, a rhythm too vast and too slow for a heart but too regular for geology.

He will learn. It took Maret four months. It took the others longer.

---

There is a worker named Dael who speaks to the compressor. Not in the way a person speaks to a machine—cursing it, coaxing it, the ventriloquism of frustration. Dael speaks to it the way you speak to something that might be listening, because after three generations of being fed seed-lubricant and having its gaskets replaced with inner-bark, after having a burl close around its base like a fist around a stone, the compressor is perhaps no longer entirely a machine. Something has crept into it. The workers do not name this something. They do not discuss it. But when Dael talks to the compressor at the start of each shift—murmuring a greeting, sometimes a question about its aeration cycles—the others do not react, because they have all, at some point, felt the impulse themselves.

The compressor does not answer. But it has not died. And there is perhaps a difference between a machine that will not die because it is well-maintained and a machine that will not die because it has, in some obscure way, decided not to.

---

The workers are not old. Maret at thirty-nine is the oldest, and she will not see fifty in the Quarter. The work does not kill—it is not dangerous in the way mining is dangerous or smelting is dangerous—but it ages. The humidity and the heat and the constant presence of spore-laden air do something to the lungs over time. The workers develop a cough that is not quite illness—a thick-productive cough that brings up mucus tinged amber and faintly sweet, and when they spit it into the culverts it dissolves into the process-water and becomes part of the system, the same as everything else.

They do not consider this a tragedy. It is a term. The Quarter gives them work and meaning and a place in a functioning whole, and in exchange it takes from them slowly what they have slowly given. This is, Maret has said, a fair contract. She was not being stoic. She was describing something she understood to be true.

---

There is a child. This is not typical. But one of the workers, Saelne, had a daughter, and there was no one to take the daughter, and so Saelne brought her to the Quarter, and the Quarter accepted her in the way it accepts everything: by growing around her.

The daughter is called Riet. She is seven. She does not go to school. She knows more about water-crop and fungal blooms than most adults will ever learn, and she cannot read, and she can identify seventeen cultivars of lacustrine grass by touch alone, and she has never seen a sky that was not glimpsed through the maintenance access in the southern wall, a small frame of light that is slowly closing as the cambium grows back.

Riet plays in the root-troughs. The water-beetles have learned to avoid her quick hands, and she has learned not to crush them, and there is a détente between the child and the insects that neither party could articulate but both respect. She talks to the gourd-tanks. She gives them names. She is not wrong to name them—they are alive enough to sweat, alive enough to have individual shapes and particular flaws and lifespans that end when they soften and must be replaced, and if a thing is born and lives and dies and can be named, then naming it is not fantasy but courtesy.

Riet will grow up here. She will absorb the Quarter's rhythms the way the fig-membrane absorbs water—through every surface, without trying, until she is saturated with it. She will probably not leave. There is a word for the workers who leave—*the ones who grew elsewhere*—and it is spoken with a particular resignation that does not judge them but acknowledges that they were, finally, not of the Quarter's ecology, and the Quarter knew it before they did.

---

At night—or what they call night—some of the workers dream, and their dreams are wet. Not nightmares. Not the drowning-dreams that come from sleeping in damp. Something else: dreams of circulation, of being a channel through which something moves, of being necessary not for what you hold but for what you pass through. Maret has dreamed of being a root-hair in the intake filter, drawing water upward. She has dreamed of being the fig-membrane, expanding and contracting with the temperature, feeling the copper sheathing above her like armor, feeling the rain strike it and run. She has dreamed of being the compressor, knocking and knocking and knocking, fed and maintained and un-dying, the burl around her base like a hand that would never let go.

She does not find these dreams strange. They are the dreams of a body that has learned it is part of a system that does not end at its skin.

---

There is something else in the Quarter. Not a person. Not an animal. The workers know it by its absence more than its presence—a quality of attention that has no source, as if the building itself has developed, over generations of breathing and growing and processing, a kind of awareness that is too slow and too distributed to be consciousness but is also too coherent to be accident.

You feel it most in the night-shift, when the work-lamps dim and the human presence thins and the Quarter's own sounds rise. The creak of the hornbeam. The gurgle of the culverts. The drip from the ceiling, irregular but persistent, a rhythm that has no meter but also no randomness—a pattern too large to grasp, like the pattern of rain on a roof that knows where the leaks are.

The workers do not worship this. They do not name it or pray to it or leave offerings. But they are gentle with the building in a way that goes beyond maintenance. When they cut the maintenance access in the southern wall, they seal the wound with resin. When they replace a gourd-tank, they thank the old one before they harvest it. When the compressor knocks and they cannot find a mechanical cause, they oil it and leave it be, and the knocking stops, eventually, on its own, which proves nothing and confirms nothing but which they note without comment.

This is not superstition. It is etiquette. You are living inside something that is alive. You behave accordingly.

---

The workers age. The building ages. The machines age, each at their own pace, and the compressor does not age at all, or ages so slowly that its aging cannot be distinguished from its being claimed by the wood. The water does not age. The water cycles, which is a kind of immortality that is also a kind of death—no self, no memory, just the endless passage through root and trough and gourd and culvert, carrying the same trace salts, depositing the same compounds, being drawn up again.

In the dim amber of the night-shift, Maret stands near the distribution manifold and feels the pulse in the southern wall. It takes a full minute to complete one cycle. She has counted. She has counted many times. It is slower than her heartbeat. It is slower than her breathing. But it is there—reliable, vast, the rhythm of something that does not know it is alive and therefore cannot die.

She places her hand flat against the hornbeam. The wood is warm. The grain runs in strange directions, trained and retrained over decades. Beneath her palm, sap moves.

The Quarter is in balance. Maret is in balance. The workers, the beetles, the gourds, the compressor, the child playing in the root-troughs with her hands in the warm water—all of them are in balance, the way a spinning wheel is balanced: moving, always moving, returning to the same point, the same configuration, the same slow product of patience and pressure and the unending willingness of living things to process what they are given.

This is who lives here: the ones who stay while it grows around them, and the thing that grows, and the thing that neither stays nor grows but simply cycles, carrying trace minerals through every living surface, and the not-quite-awareness that holds them all in its slow, warm, unceasing attention.

They are not separate from the Quarter. They are its organs, its microflora, its necessary guests. And when they leave—if they leave—the Quarter will close around the spaces they made the way it closes around the maintenance access: slowly, livingly, without grief, because the Quarter does not grieve, because the Quarter does not end, because the Quarter is a process, and a process does not mourn the water that has passed through it. It simply draws up more.
