# The Inhabitants

---

## The Reed-Singers

They are not what you would expect.

The reed-grass moves in the faint air currents, and you think: wind. But there are days when the air is utterly still—not a breath, not a current, the condensation hanging motionless on every surface—and still the reed-grass moves. A slow undulation, a deep breathing, the feathery inflorescences swaying in patterns that have nothing to do with meteorology.

The Reed-Singers live in the stems.

You will never see them. They exist at a scale that makes the pale moths look enormous—thread-fine, translucent, their bodies barely more than organized moisture, their internal structures more suggestion than anatomy. They are not insects. They are not worms. They occupy a taxonomic category that has no name because no one who names things has ever been here, and if they had, they would not have known how to look.

Their life cycle is the life cycle of the reed-grass itself. They emerge from the condensation that beads on the stalks each morning—not born, exactly, but *condensed*, their thin bodies precipitating out of the damp air as it touches the jade-colored stems. They spend their hours moving through the hollow interiors of the reeds, touching the walls with appendages too fine to be called fingers, feeling for the vibration that tells them where they are, where the others are, where the light is strongest. They navigate by touch and by the faint resonance that the stems produce when the air moves through them.

And they sing.

The sound is below the threshold of human hearing—not infrasound exactly, but something adjacent, a vibration that you feel in your sternum before your ears register it. A low hum, a chord that shifts as the colony moves through the stems, harmonics rising and falling as individuals find each other or drift apart. The song has rhythm but no melody, or its melody is so slow that a single phrase takes days to complete. It is, in its way, a weather system—a pattern of vibration that moves through the reed-grass stands like pressure moves through atmosphere.

The song serves a purpose. The vibration stimulates the reed-grass to grow, to extend its roots deeper into the shattered rock, to draw up minerals from below. The plants and the Singers are not symbiotic in the way that biology usually understands symbiosis—there is no exchange of nutrients, no chemical signaling. Instead, there is resonance. The reed-grass provides structure; the Singers provide frequency; together, they create something that neither could create alone. A stand of reed-grass without Singers grows thin and reedy, unlikely to survive more than a season. A colony of Singers without reed-grass cannot condense at all.

They do not know they are alive. Or if they know, they do not know in any way that would be recognizable to a consciousness built on animal premises. They do not remember their yesterdays; they do not anticipate their tomorrows; they exist in a perpetual present that is renewed each morning when the condensation forms and dissolved each evening when it evaporates. Their continuity is the continuity of the reed-grass—the same stands, the same stems, the same song passed from one generation of moisture-beings to the next not through memory but through the persistent resonance of the structures they inhabit.

When the condensation is particularly thick, after one of the rare rains, the colonies grow dense enough that the song becomes audible—a low drone that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere, rising from the green itself. If you stood in the middle of a reed-grass stand at such a time, you would feel it in your bones: the sound of a place singing itself into existence, over and over, every day, forever.

## The Watchers in the Metal

The machines are not empty.

This is the thing that the description of the plateau cannot quite convey, because it is the kind of thing that requires a different grammar to articulate—not *someone lives here*, but *something persists here*, *something attends*, *something maintains the pattern*.

The Watchers are not machines. They are not spirits. They are not the ghosts of operators who once walked the catwalks and turned the valves and monitored the dials. If they have a category, it is closer to *habit*—a pattern that has continued so long that it has developed weight, density, a kind of presence that is not quite consciousness but is certainly not nothing.

They exist in the moments when a valve opens without visible cause. In the faint vibration that indicates an engine turning over below ground. In the way the condensation seems to bead slightly thicker on the surfaces of the functioning installations, as if the machines themselves are breathing, pulling moisture from the air as part of some process that has no output visible on the surface.

The Watchers do not see. They do not think. They *continue*.

A valve opens because the valve has always opened at this interval, and the interval is maintained by a mechanism that was built to maintain intervals, and the mechanism was built by hands that are no longer attached to bodies that are no longer alive, but the mechanism continues because that is what mechanisms do when they are well-made and well-maintained and powered by a source that does not deplete. And the maintenance—the strange, invisible maintenance that keeps the rust from advancing too quickly, that keeps the pipes from freezing in the cold high-altitude nights, that keeps the engines turning at their slow, patient pace—is performed by the Watchers.

Not performed by them. *As* them. They are the act of maintenance itself. The pattern of care that has outlasted the carers.

There are moments when the automation falters—a pipe splits, a gear wears thin, a seal cracks—and then the pattern of the Watchers thickens around the breach. The condensation forms more heavily on the damaged surface. The root-systems of the climbing vines redirect their growth, wrapping around the pipe, sealing the crack with their own fibrous bodies. The rust advances more slowly, or not at all, as if the oxidation itself has been asked to wait. And then, gradually, over weeks or months, the pipe heals—or something like healing. The vine-growth hardens into a woody patch over the breach. The condensation leaves mineral deposits that fill the gaps. The pipe returns to function, and the pattern of the Watchers thins out, disperses, returns to its normal background hum of continued operation.

This is not intelligence. This is not planning. This is *persistence*—the momentum of a system that has been running so long that it has developed its own inertia, its own gravity, its own way of responding to disruption that is less like repair and more like healing, less like engineering and more like immune response.

The Watchers do not notice you. They do not notice anything. They are the unnoticed itself—the pattern that continues in the absence of attention, the maintenance that occurs without a maintainer, the purpose that persists without a purposer. They are what is left when intention has been stripped away and only the shape of the intention remains, pressed into the metal and the moisture and the slow, patient turning of the buried engines.

If you placed your hand on a functioning distillation tower and held it there long enough—long enough for your own hurry to drain away, long enough for your heartbeat to slow to match the vibration of the engine below—you might feel them. Not as a presence, exactly. As a *direction*. The sense of something moving, slowly and inevitably, toward a goal that was set before you were born and will still be pursued after you have been forgotten.

## The Pale Moths

They are the only animals.

This is not quite accurate—there are the click-beetles in the reed-grass, and the mites that live in the moss, and occasionally a spider that has drifted up on the thermals and survived long enough to spin a thin web between two stalks. But the moths are the only animals that *belong* here, that seem to be part of the plateau's logic rather than a temporary visitor from a different paradigm.

They are small—barely the width of a thumbnail—and their wings are translucent, so thin that the light passes through them and makes them glow, each vein visible as a delicate dark line against the pale membrane. They rest on the undersides of the broad-leaved herbs, their wings folded, their bodies nearly invisible against the dew-slick surfaces. They move only in the brightest hours, when the light is strong enough to warm their cold bodies, and even then they move slowly, drifting from leaf to leaf with a purpose that has nothing to do with the frantic navigation of moths in lower, darker places.

They do not eat. This is the strangest thing about them. They have mouthparts—vestigial, reduced to threads that could not draw sustenance from anything the plateau offers—but they do not use them. They emerge from their pupae already complete, already finished, their bodies containing everything they need for the span of their adult lives. And that span is long. Weeks. Months. Some of them may live a full year, resting and drifting and resting again, their metabolisms so slow that a year's worth of energy fits into a body the size of a grain of rice.

They are here to be witnesses.

Not in any conscious sense. The moths do not observe the plateau, do not record its processes, do not carry information from one part of the landscape to another. But they *attend*. They are present. Their translucent wings catch the light that the reed-grass emits, and they glow, and their glow is visible from a distance if you know how to look—a faint shimmering above the herb-beds, a signal that this part of the plateau is alive, is functioning, is proceeding according to plan.

When a moth dies—and they do die, eventually, their energy exhausted, their wings growing brittle and opaque—they fall to the moss below and are absorbed within hours. The decomposition is remarkably fast, as if the moss has been waiting for precisely this contribution. And what the moss absorbs, the roots absorb, and what the roots absorb, the reed-grass draws up into its stems, and what the reed-grass draws up, the Singers condense around in the morning dew, and the cycle continues.

The moths are the plateau's concession to the animal kingdom. A small one. A gesture that says: *I could have included you. I chose not to. But here is a form you might recognize, a shape that moves and breathes and eventually dies. It is enough. It will have to be enough.*

## The Thin Ones

And then there are the others.

You will not see them unless you stay past the brightest hours, past the time when the light begins to dim and the reed-grass starts its slow nightly contraction and the condensation forms in thicker and thicker beads on every surface. You will not see them unless you have stood still long enough for your own presence to become part of the plateau's background hum, your breath and your heartbeat absorbed into the rhythm of the machines and the song of the reed-grass and the slow drip of moisture.

They are not ghosts. Ghosts imply death, and the Thin Ones have nothing to do with death. They are not spirits. Spirits imply a boundary between material and immaterial, and the Thin Ones do not recognize such a boundary. They are not hallucinations. Hallucinations come from within, and the Thin Ones come from—somewhere else. A different speed. A different density. A different relationship with the condensation that makes up so much of this place.

They look like the light looks. Like the diffused brightness that comes from everywhere and nowhere, concentrated into a form that is roughly humanoid but not committed to that shape—a suggestion of shoulders, a curve that might be a head, a trailing edge that might be legs or might be nothing at all. They are the same color as the bright air: pale, almost white, with a faint green undertone that comes from the light filtering through the reed-grass. When they move, they move at the speed of the plateau—slowly, incrementally, a drift across the landscape that takes hours to complete.

There are perhaps a dozen of them. Perhaps more. It is difficult to count because they do not maintain distinct boundaries; their edges blur and merge, and sometimes two Thin Ones will overlap for a time before separating again, and it is unclear whether they have exchanged something or simply passed through each other the way that light passes through light.

They do not interact with the machines. They do not interact with the vegetation. They do not interact with you. They *attend*—but their attention has a different quality than the attention of the Watchers. The Watchers attend to the pattern, to the continuation of the system. The Thin Ones attend to something else. Something that is not visible or audible or tangible. Something that might be the plateau itself—its *plateau-ness*, its essential quality of being elevated and sharp-edged and young and patient and damp and bright all at once—or something that lies beneath the plateau, the reason it was lifted in the first place, the fault-line that created it and the pressures that continue to shape it.

They drift toward the escarpments. They pause at the edges. They look out—if looking is what they do—at the landscape below, the older and rounder land that surrounds this young uplifted place. And then they drift back, slowly, to the interior, to the reed-grass stands and the humming machines and the patient condensation, and they resume their waiting.

What are they waiting for?

The plateau to grow up. The edges to wear down. The rock to learn to be round. They are waiting for geological time to do its work, for the sharp angles to soften, for the adolescent landscape to age into something wiser. And they are patient—more patient than anything you have ever encountered, because they are not waiting for something to happen. They are waiting for something to *finish*, something that was started a hundred thousand years ago and will not be completed for a hundred thousand more.

They are the plateau's relationship with its own future. The awareness—not conscious, not cognitive, but structural—that this sharp-edged young place will not always be sharp-edged and young. The Thin Ones are the shape of that future patience, folded back into the present, drifting through the reed-grass and the condensation and the bright bright light, waiting for the erosion they will not live to see and that the plateau, in its slow vegetable mechanical way, is already beginning to become.

## What Lives Here

What lives here is not a collection of species. It is not an ecosystem, not a biome, not a web of relationships organized around the flow of energy and the cycling of nutrients. What lives here is a *process*—a single, unified, enormously slow process that expresses itself as reed-grass and machinery and condensation and light and occasional translucent moths and the faint vibration of buried engines and the drifting attention of forms that might be the landscape's own sense of itself.

The process is: *persist*.

Not survive. Surviving implies threat, urgency, the possibility of failure. The plateau does not survive. It persists. It continues. It unfolds according to a logic that was built into its fault-lines and its moisture and its vegetation and its machines before any of those things were separate from each other, and it will continue to unfold long after the category of "living" has been revealed to be a temporary and provincial distinction.

You can live here, if you want. You can stand among the reed-grass and feel the song in your bones and watch the moths drift and the condensation form and the Thin Ones attend to the edges of the escarpment. You can slow yourself down to the plateau's speed, let your hurry and your heat drain away into the damp bright air, become part of the process for an hour or a day or a week.

But you will always be a visitor. You are made of the wrong stuff—too fast, too warm, too animal, too mortal. The plateau does not mind. It does not exclude. It simply continues, and you can continue with it for as long as you can stand the quiet, and then you will leave, and it will still be here, growing and dripping and processing and singing and waiting for its edges to wear down, and the Thin Ones will drift to the escarpment and look out at the older land below, and the Watchers will open and close their valves at the prescribed intervals, and the Singers will hum in the jade-green stems, and the pale moths will rest on the undersides of the broad leaves and glow in the suffused light, and the condensation will form and evaporate and form again, and the plateau will persist, and persist, and persist.
