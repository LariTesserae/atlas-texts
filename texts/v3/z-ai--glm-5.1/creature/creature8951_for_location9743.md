# What Lives Here

---

The frogs know something you do not.

This is not intelligence. This is not wisdom. This is something older than both, something that has no name because names require a distance between namer and named, and the frogs have no distance from themselves. They are what they are in the way that water is wet—not as a description, not as a state, but as an identity so total it cannot be separated from the being.

Consider the frog on the fern frond. Thumbnail-sized. Green-black. Its skin is wet but it did not make it wet. The air made it wet. The steam made it wet. The frog was born wet and it will die wet and between those events it will never be dry, not for a moment, not for a breath. It does not know what dryness is. It does not know what thirst is. These concepts have never reached it because they have never needed to. It lives in the condensation, in the perpetual saturated now, and its body is a permeable membrane between the wet air and the wet fern and the wet moss below, and it does not distinguish between the water that is itself and the water that is not-itself, because at this scale, in this humidity, the distinction is a fiction.

The frog's heart beats fast. Seventy beats per minute, eighty, a hundred. It has never known stillness inside its chest. The heat sees to that. The heat that rises from the stone below, that makes the ferns warm to sit on, that makes the water body-temperature, that speeds every chemical reaction in every cell of every living thing on the flat—the heat is the frog's tempo, and the frog does not know it is fast because the frog has never heard a slow heart.

What does the frog perceive? Not the flat as you would perceive it—not as a landscape, not as a space, not as a place with edges and dimensions. The frog perceives temperature gradients. It perceives the difference between the fern (warm) and the water (warmer) and the air (warmest, almost, the thick wet air that presses on its skin like a hand that is also a bath). It perceives vibration—the thrum of the insects, the chorus of its own kind, the footstep of the lizard on the moss twelve inches away. It perceives the movement of the midge across its field of vision, the dark streak against the dim silver light, and it perceives hunger, and the hunger and the movement meet in its brain and its tongue flashes and the midge is gone and the frog does not think *I have eaten* because there is no *I* that has eaten, there is only the eating, the continuation, the ongoing.

The frog is always eating. The frog is always breathing. The frog is always sitting on the fern or moving to another fern or descending to the water to breed or ascending from the water after breeding. It does all of these things and none of these things are separate from each other and none of them have a before or an after that matters, because the flat has no before and no after, and the frog is the flat's creature, made in the flat's image, existing in a continuous present that never hardens into past or thins into future.

When the frog dies—and it will die, they all die, within a season or two, a year at most, because their metabolism is a fire in the heat and fires burn out—it does not know it is dying. It slows. Its heart, which has never been slow, becomes slow. Its body, which has never been still, becomes still. It slips from the fern—or it is already on the moss, or in the water, or wherever the slowness finds it—and it becomes wet in a different way, wet from inside, the fluids leaving the boundaries that held them, and the frog becomes the biofilm, becomes the food for the bacteria it never knew were there, becomes the soil the moss is building, becomes the flat.

The flat does not mourn it. The flat does not count it. There are ten thousand frogs on the flat—or a hundred thousand—or some number that cannot be counted because counting requires separating one from another, and on the flat, one frog is indistinguishable from the next, and the next, and the chorus does not miss a voice when one falls silent because another is already rising to take its place, and the place where the dead frog became the biofilm is occupied within an hour by a living frog, and the living frog is sitting on the same frond, in the same warmth, hearing the same chorus, and it does not know it is a replacement because it does not know it replaced anything, because it does not know anything, because knowing is not what frogs do.

Living is what frogs do.

And they do it completely.

---

The insects do not live.

This is not a claim about their biology—they are alive, by any measure, by any definition, by every functional criterion of metabolism and reproduction and response to stimulus. But the word *live* implies a duration, a narrative, a stretch of time in which something can happen to a self, and the insects do not have duration or narrative or self in any way that the word can reach.

The midge is a process. It is the process of egg becoming larva becoming pupa becoming adult becoming egg, and the adult phase—the flying phase, the winged phase, the phase that rises in clouds from the ferns when something moves through them—lasts hours. A day. Two days, if the humidity is exactly right, if no bird's beak finds it, if no frog's tongue finds it, if it does not simply run out of the fuel its larval self stored in the warm pools below. Two days is the maximum. Most midges get less. Most midges get an afternoon, an evening, a single dim silver night in which to find another midge, to mate, to lay eggs in the water, to become the steam and the biofilm and the next generation.

They do not sleep. They do not rest. From the moment the pupal case splits and the wings unfurl—and the wings unfurl in the humidity, in the heat, in the sulfur-iron-organic air—they are moving, they are flying, they are seeking, they are the thrum. The thrum is their sound. It is the sound of ten million wings in ten million cubic feet of wet air, and it is not communication, not language, not signaling—it is the physical reality of small bodies moving fast in thick medium, and it is the baseline sound of the flat, and it has been the baseline sound of the flat for as long as the flat has existed, and it will be the baseline sound for as long as the flat persists.

Do the midges perceive? Do they have experience? The question is wrong. The midge perceives the way a chemical reaction perceives its substrate—directly, without mediation, without the interpolation of consciousness. Light. Heat. Moisture. Carbon dioxide from breathing things. These are the midge's inputs, and its outputs are movement and reproduction, and there is nothing between the input and the output, no deliberation, no consideration, no pause. The midge is a straight line from stimulus to response, and the line is drawn in the air by its wings, and the line lasts a day, and then the line is gone, and the midge is gone, and there was never a midge that was separate from the line, and the line was never separate from the air.

The clouds rise and settle. Rise and settle. Something moves through the ferns—a lizard, a bird, a hypothetical visitor—and the midges rise in a cloud so dense it is almost solid, a breath of living particles, and then they settle again within seconds, within heartbeats, and the fronds are as covered as they were before, and the thrum is as constant as it was before, and the nothing that happened is as nothing as it was before.

The insects are the medium. They are what the air is made of, here, as much as the steam and the sulfur and the water. They are the between-thing, the thing that connects the frogs to the energy that rises from the stone, the thing that turns algae and biofilm and organic detritus into flight and protein and the next generation of frogs. They are the transfusion mechanism, the digestive system of the flat, and they do not know it, and they do not need to know it, and they will never know it, and they will go on being it for as long as the flat goes on.

---

The lizard is the exception.

The lizard is the only thing on the flat that might have something like a day.

It begins in the morning—when the dim silver light becomes slightly less dim, when the temperature rises the two degrees that separate the night's mere heat from the day's genuine warmth. The lizard emerges from the moss, from the hummock where it spent the dark hours, and it moves to a rock at the edge of a hot pool, and it sits. It sits in a way that the frog does not sit—the frog sits because it is sitting, because sitting is what it was doing and is doing and will be doing, but the lizard sits with intention. The lizard sits to get warm. The lizard sits because it has a preference—warmth over cool, rock over moss, the edge of the pool over the interior of the ferns—and it enacts that preference by moving to the place that satisfies it.

This is not what you would call thinking. But it is also not what you would call reflex. It is something between—a directedness, a responsiveness to internal states that are more than stimulus-response, that involve something like evaluation, something like want. The lizard wants warmth. The lizard wants food. The lizard wants, and it moves toward what it wants, and away from what it does not want—too much heat, too much water, the shadow of the wading bird passing overhead.

The lizard's day is a sequence of wants. Warmth first—emerging from the moss, finding the rock, sitting with its belly pressed to the warm stone, feeling the heat from below and the heat from above and the heat from the steam rising around it, letting its cold blood warm until its heart speeds and its muscles loosen and it can move fast, faster than anything else on the flat, faster than the insects, faster than the frogs, almost faster than the eye can follow. Then food—skittering across the rocks, snatching midges from the air, snapping at the small flies that land on the wet stone, eating ten, twenty, fifty in a morning, its stomach filling with the same protein that fills the air with the thrum. Then rest—back to the rock, back to the warmth, sitting with its eyes open and its body still, watching. The lizard watches. It watches the way a camera watches—without comprehension, but with complete attention, with the absolute focus of a creature whose survival depends on seeing the bird before the bird sees it.

And then, in the afternoon—when the heat is at its worst, when even the lizard's furnace metabolism feels the strain, when the steam is thick enough to taste and the dim silver light is almost indistinguishable from darkness—the lizard descends from the rock. It enters the ferns. It moves through the green maze, over fronds and under stems and past the sitting frogs that do not move as it passes, and it finds a pool—a shallow, hot, mineral pool like all the other pools—and it drinks. It drinks the water that is almost too hot to drink, water that tastes of iron and sulfur and the organic richness of the biofilm, and it submerges its body for a moment, just a moment, letting the water cool its skin the few degrees that make survival possible, and then it emerges, wet and gasping, and it returns to the rock, and it sits, and it watches.

The lizard's day is the same every day. The same rock. The same pool. The same sequence of warmth and food and rest and drink and heat. But the lizard does not know it is the same—cannot know, does not have the mechanism for knowing—because it does not remember yesterday and it does not anticipate tomorrow. It has only the day, the single day, the ongoing day, and it lives that day completely, from the first want to the last, and when the dim silver light dims further and the heat drops its two degrees and the night comes, the lizard descends into the moss and it does not dream, because dreaming requires a mind that can hold images and the lizard's mind cannot hold anything, and the lizard is gone until the morning, and in the morning it will be the same lizard, or a different lizard, and it will not know the difference, and the difference will not matter, because the lizard's day is what the lizard is, and the day is always the same day, and the day is always enough.

The crustaceans remember the dark.

Not the dark of the flat—the flat has no true dark, only dimness, only the perpetual silver-gray that passes for night. The dark the crustaceans remember is older than the flat, older than the steam, older than the rock that has not decided what it is. The dark the crustaceans remember is the dark of the deep earth, the dark of the aquifer, the dark of the water that has not seen light since before the continents assumed their current shapes, the dark that is not an absence of light but a presence—an environment, a medium, a complete and self-sufficient world in which light is not a missing thing but an irrelevant thing, a thing that has no meaning because it has never been needed.

They came up through the weeping rocks. The water came up, hot and mineral-rich, and they came with it—pale, small, blind, their bodies adapted for a world without eyes, without predators, without the complex demands of a surface ecosystem. They came up into the pools, and the pools were warm, and the pools had food—the biofilm, the organic detritus, the dead insects and the dead frogs and the dead moss and everything that died and fell and dissolved in the mineral broth—and they stayed.

But they did not forget the dark. They did not forget the depth. They did not forget the pressure of the water above them, the certainty of the stone around them, the absolute reliability of a world that did not change because it could not change, because it was below the reach of erosion and weather and the confused inceptions of a surface that could not decide what it was.

The crustaceans in the pools are not comfortable. The pools are bright—not bright to you, not bright by any surface standard, but bright to a creature whose ancestors evolved in the total absence of light, whose pigment has fled, whose eyes have become rudimentary nubs of tissue that can detect only the most basic difference between one dimness and another. The pools are dangerous—there are birds here, there are frogs here, there are lizards that will eat anything small enough to swallow, and the crustaceans are small. The pools are chaotic—the water level changes, the temperature changes, the chemistry changes, the bottom shifts under the feet in a way that stone never shifts. The pools are not home.

But the pools have food. The pools have warmth. The pools have the specific mineral chemistry that the crustaceans' bodies require—the sulfur, the iron, the calcium, the particular dissolved cocktail that their deep ancestors evolved to metabolize, that their cells still expect, that they cannot live without. And so they stay. They stay in the pools, in the light, in the danger, and they feed, and they breed, and they produce pale eggs that sink to the bottom of the pools and rest among the biofilm and the warmth, and the eggs hatch, and the new crustaceans are also pale and also blind and also adapted for a world they do not inhabit, and they too feed and breed and stay, and none of them understand why they stay, because understanding is not a capacity that crustaceans have, they have only the drive, the imperative, the pull toward the warmth and the food and the minerals, and they follow it, and they remain.

At night—and there is a night, of sorts, a period when the silver light dims further and the steam thickens and the frogs' chorus reaches its peak—some of the crustaceans move. They descend. They find the places where the water seeps back down, the places where the pool connects to the weeping crack in the rock, and they go down, down, back into the dark, back into the stone, back into the aquifer that remembers the shape of the world before the flat. They do not stay there. They cannot stay there—there is not enough food, not enough of the specific surface chemistry that their new generation has adapted to. But they visit. They touch the dark. They remember, in their bodies, in the orientation of their swimming, in the way they move toward the down and the stone and the cold that is the only cold they have ever known, they remember that they are from somewhere else, that the pool is not their origin, that their origin is older and deeper and darker than anything the flat has made.

And then they rise again. To feed. To breed. To live in the bright warm dangerous surface world that is not theirs, that will never be theirs, that has colonized them as thoroughly as the biofilm has colonized the rock. They are the flat's creatures now, the flat's citizens, the flat's lingering proof that the surface and the deep are connected, that what happens below matters above, that the heat that makes the steam is also the heat that made the shrimp, and that the same earth that has not decided what it is has also not decided who belongs here.

The crustaceans belong here. The crustaceans do not belong here. Both statements are true. Both statements are irrelevant. The crustaceans are here, and they are alive, and they are moving between the dark and the dim, and they will keep moving between the dark and the dim for as long as the flat endures, and the flat will endure for as long as the heat endures, and the heat will endure for as long as the earth remembers how to breathe through this particular wound in its skin.

---

The bird is alone.

This is the thing you must understand about the bird. The frogs are legion. The insects are uncountable. The lizards are numerous enough to be a feature, a pattern, a repeating element in the landscape's design. The crustaceans are abundant in their pools, pale grains in dark soup, dozens in every seep, hundreds across the flat. But the bird—the wading bird, the silent bird, the long-legged still-eyed bird that picks through the shallow water in the gray hours—is alone.

There are others of its kind. Somewhere. The flat is not the whole world—the fog is a local phenomenon, the steam is a local phenomenon, the flat itself is a local phenomenon, and beyond its borders there are landscapes that have decided what they are, that have trees, that have dry ground, that have birds that call and fly and mate and live in the way birds live when they are not alone. The bird on the flat has come from somewhere. The bird on the flat will go somewhere, eventually, when the instinct to move overrides the instinct to stay, when the season shifts, when the internal calendar that no one has decoded tells it that it is time.

But for now—for the duration of its time on the flat—the bird is alone. It is the only one of its kind. It is the only large animal in a landscape of small ones. It is the only thing that sees the flat from above the ferns, from the height of its long neck and its longer legs, from the perspective that includes horizons and fog and the dim silver light that spreads across the landscape like a blessing that has forgotten its words.

What does the bird see?

It sees the green. The overwhelming, dense, saturated green—the green that is almost black, the green that absorbs light, the green that is the dominant fact of the landscape. It sees the steam rising from the green, the white columns and wisps and threads of water becoming air, and it sees the fog where the steam meets the cooler air, and it sees the flat extending to the fog, and it sees nothing beyond the fog. It has never seen beyond the fog. It does not know the fog has a beyond.

It sees the pools. The dark pools, the mineral pools, the hot pools where the pale crustaceans move like submerged stars. It sees the frogs at the edges of the pools, sitting on fronds, sitting on rocks, sitting in the chorus that is the landscape's voice. It sees the movement of the lizards across the stones, the quick flash of their bodies, the too-fast dart of their hunting. It sees the insects rising when it moves, the clouds of midges, the living breath of the ferns, and it does not eat the midges—they are too small, too numerous, too much work for too little return. It has standards. It has preferences. It is a bird, and birds are discerning, and this bird discerns.

It eats the crustaceans. It eats the tadpoles. It eats the slow frogs, the young frogs, the frogs that have not yet learned to sit still on the frond and wait for the midges to come. It eats with a precision that seems cruel—standing in the water, stepping carefully, placing each foot on the fern mat with a delicacy that does not disturb the pool, then striking with its beak, the long beak, the beak that is almost a needle, the beak that pierces the water without sound and comes up with a pale shrimp or a green-black tadpole or a kicking frog, and the beak tips back, and the throat works, and the food is gone, and the bird is still again, and the bird is watching again, and the bird is waiting again.

The bird is always waiting. The bird is the only thing on the flat that waits—the frogs do not wait, the insects do not wait, the lizards do not wait, even the crustaceans in their dark-remembering pools do not wait. The bird waits because the bird has a mind that can project, that can anticipate, that can imagine the next moment and plan for it, and this capacity—this waiting—this patience—this willingness to be still in a landscape where everything else is in motion—this is the bird's advantage, and this is the bird's curse.

The curse is consciousness. The bird is the only conscious thing on the flat.

It is a small consciousness—a bird consciousness, built for navigation and hunting and the recognition of seasonal cues, not for philosophy or self-reflection or existential despair. But it is consciousness nonetheless. The bird knows it is a bird. The bird knows it is alone. The bird knows the flat is not its home, that it arrived here and will leave here, that the pool it hunts in today is not the pool it will hunt in tomorrow, because tomorrow it will move—or the day after—or the day after that—and it will find another flat, another fog, another series of pools with crustaceans and tadpoles and frogs, and it will hunt there, and it will be alone there, and it will not think *I am alone* because birds do not think in language, but it will feel the alone, the shape of the alone in its mind, the space where another bird should be, and the space will be empty, and the bird will keep hunting, and the bird will keep living, and the bird will keep being the only thing on the flat that knows it is alive.

When the bird leaves—when the instinct finally drives it, when the internal calendar reaches its threshold, when something shifts in the chemistry of its blood and it feels the pull toward somewhere-else—it will not look back. It will rise from the pool, its long legs extending, its wide wings unfolding, and it will fly into the fog, and the fog will close around it, and it will be gone, and the flat will not mark its passing, and the frogs will not pause in their chorus, and the insects will not notice the absence, and the lizards will not miss the shadow that crossed their rocks.

But the crustaceans will notice. In their dark-remembering way, in their deep-ancestral way, they will notice that the pressure from above has changed, that the danger has shifted, that the pool is slightly safer than it was before. And they will rise from the bottom where they retreated when the bird's shadow fell, and they will swim in the warmer water near the surface, and they will feed, and they will breed, and they will continue, and they will not think *the bird is gone*, because they cannot think, but they will live in the space the bird left, and they will fill it, because on the flat, every space is filled, every space is occupied, every space belongs to something that wants it more than whatever left it behind.

And then there is the thing you cannot see.

The thing that is not an organism. The thing that is not a species. The thing that is not alive in any way that life has been defined—but that is living, in some sense, in some definition that has not been written yet.

The flat itself.

The flat is an organism. This is not a metaphor. This is not a poetic claim about ecosystems and interconnections and the Gaia hypothesis and the wonderful complexity of nature. The flat is an organism in the same way that a biofilm is an organism—a collection of living things that functions as a single entity, that responds to stimuli as a single entity, that maintains homeostasis as a single entity, that has a boundary between itself and the not-itself, that takes in energy and excretes waste and reproduces (or would reproduce, if the conditions were right, if another flat could be made, if the heat and the water and the raw stone could be assembled elsewhere in the same configuration).

The flat does not think. The flat does not feel. The flat does not want. But the flat *maintains*. It maintains its temperature—the heat rising from below, the steam releasing it above, the water circulating it through the biofilm and the moss and the ferns. It maintains its water—the seeps and the pools and the condensation and the rain that is its own breath come home. It maintains its chemistry—the sulfur and the iron and the calcium and the organic compounds that cycle through the rock and the water and the living things in an endless loop that produces nothing new and loses nothing old. It maintains its structure—the ferns and the horsetails and the moss that hold the biofilm in place, that prevent the water from washing the organic matter away, that keep the first soil on the first rock in the first configuration that allows the first life to continue.

The flat is in equilibrium. This is what equilibrium means—not stasis, not stillness, not the absence of change, but the balancing of change against change, the cancellation of force by counterforce, the dynamic stability of a system that has found its form and is holding it. The flat is holding its form. The flat has been holding its form for as long as it has existed. The flat will hold its form for as long as the heat holds, and the heat will hold for as long as the earth breathes through this wound, and the earth will breathe through this wound for as long as the deep processes that create heat and water and raw stone continue to operate, and those processes are older than the flat and older than the continent and older than the names of the geological eras, and they will continue for longer than any of those names, and the flat will continue with them.

The flat is what lives here.

Not the frogs, though the frogs are part of it. Not the insects, though the insects are necessary. Not the lizards, the crustaceans, the bird—though all of them are components, organs, cells in the body of a thing that is larger than any of them and that does not know they exist, because the flat does not know anything, the flat does not need to know anything, the flat only needs to maintain, and it maintains, and it is maintained, and it goes on.

You asked what lives here. The answer is: a thing that is becoming itself and has always been itself and will never finish becoming because it has already found its form. A thing that is green and wet and hot and dim and loud and full. A thing that has no name because there is no one to name it. A thing that does not need a name because it does not need to be distinguished from anything else—it is the only thing of its kind, the only flat, the only place where this specific configuration of heat and water and life has assembled itself, and it is complete, and it is self-sufficient, and it is alive in every way that matters and some ways that do not matter yet but might matter someday, when the world finds a word for what this is.

The flat lives here. The flat is what lives here. The flat is the only thing that lives here, and everything else is the flat, and the flat is everything else, and the chorus and the thrum and the steam and the heat and the green and the wet and the now—these are not attributes of the flat. They are the flat. They are what the flat is when the flat is being itself, which is all the flat ever does, which is all the flat can do, which is enough, which has always been enough, which will continue to be enough for as long as the flat endures.

And the flat endures.

And the flat endures.

And the flat endures.
