# What Lives in the Wind-Sheared Heights

---

The obvious inhabitants first, because they are what you see before you understand what seeing means here.

**The goats.** You might call them ibex or tahr or mountain goat depending on the taxonomy you bring, but the names are imported and insufficient. They have been here long enough that their bodies have become arguments for the place itself. Their hooves are hard and small, each hoof a callus against the stone, and they can stand on ledges no wider than a finger because their weight is always already committed—there is no hesitation in their placement, no moment of checking if the surface will hold. They know it will hold. They have known it for ten thousand generations.

Their coats are the color of the pale stone, but this is not camouflage in the way forests teach camouflage. Nothing hunts them here by sight. The raptors take the sick and the young and the careless, but they hunt by movement and by silhouette against the sky. The goats' coloration is simpler than evasion: they are the color of their food and their food is the color of the stone and the stone is the color it is because the wind has stripped everything else away. They match because they are part of the same subtraction.

They move through the Sound without acknowledging it. Their ears lie flat and their eyes are always half-closed against the blown dust, but they do not startle at the roar because the roar has never not been there. They communicate—if that word applies—not with sound but with alignment. A goat turns its head and the others turn, not because they heard something but because they saw the shape of attention change. A kid bolts and its mother follows not the kid but the direction of the bolt, reading the trajectory like a word. They are a language of bodies moving in relation to stone and wind, and if you watch them long enough you begin to read the grammar: the way one stands sentinel while others eat, the way they cluster on the lee side of a pillar not because they decided to but because the wind pushes them there and they have learned to stop pushing back.

At night they press against the warm stone where the day's sun still lingers in the rock, and they lick the condensation from the windward faces, and they are damp and cold and alive in the darkness while the Sound goes on around them like a sea they are not drowning in only because they have gills they grew across ten thousand years.

They do not dream of quiet. They do not know quiet exists.

---

**The raptors.** There are more of them than the emptiness suggests should be possible. They live in a layer of the place that the ground-dwellers cannot reach—the vertical space above the formations, the air itself as territory.

They do not fly here. That verb implies effort, choice, the beating of wings. What they do is *ride*. They spread their wings and the wind fills them and they become instruments of the atmosphere, their bodies the only variable in an equation of lift and drag that the wind has been solving since before the birds existed. A single feather-trim adjustment turns them. A tail-fan slows them. They ride the updraft on the windward side of a pillar and they ride the downdraft on the lee side and they cross between them in a motion so controlled it looks like boredom.

They nest in the high places where even the Sound is somewhat diminished—not silent, never silent, but a degree less crushing. Ledges beneath overhangs, clefts in the metamorphic ribs where the air is still enough that an egg can remain warm under a parent's body without being blown cold. They lay few eggs. The wind takes some. The cold takes some. Those that survive are the ones who held on, who pressed into the stone cup of the nest and waited, and perhaps this is the first lesson they learn: *hold on*.

They eat the goats, sometimes. The young, the old, the ones who misread the wind and stood on the wrong side of a ledge. They eat the rodents and the snakes and each other, when they can. They are not noble. They are not the majestic eagles of other landscapes, soaring above a world they survey like kings. They are desperate and precise and they ride the wind because the alternative is being destroyed by it, and if they look regal it is only because desperation has made them efficient, and efficiency looks like grace.

Their screams are not screams of triumph. They are location pulses—*I am here, I am here, I am here*—sent into a medium that swallows all smaller sounds. A raptor call is the minimum signal that can survive the Sound, stripped of everything but frequency and volume, a vocalization reduced to its most wind-resistant form.

At dusk they come to roost and the sky empties of wings and for a moment, in the brief minutes between the sun's departure and the full arrival of dark, the place is almost still. The wind does not stop but it shifts, and the Sound drops a partial step, and the raptors settle into their niches and become part of the stone, just another dark shape among dark shapes, and they sleep with their heads tucked beneath their wings, which is the only shelter they have ever known.

---

**The small things.** In the soil pockets, in the cracks, in the narrow channels where the wind is broken into something merely uncomfortable rather than lethal, there is a whole second landscape of life too small to see from any distance.

Rodents with fur so fine it might be dust. They eat the seeds that the wind carries in from lower elevations—seeds that were never meant to land here, that were passing through on their way to somewhere habitable, and which settled into a soil pocket only because the air eddied just right around a particular stone. They live fast and die young and their bones accumulate in the deeper crevices, a white stratum of calcium beneath the dust, and the roots of the tough grasses reach down toward those bones and find phosphorus there, and so the dead feed the living in the usual way, except here the transaction is more intimate because there is so little of everything that nothing is wasted.

Insects. Beetles with polished carapaces that reflect the white light, and small wasps that nest in the abandoned burrows of the rodents, and ants that farm the aphids on the sparse vegetation in a relationship so old the aphids have lost the ability to survive without their keepers. The ants tend their flocks in the sheltered channels, moving between the succulent plants in lines that follow the seams where wind cannot reach, and they are the only inhabitants who have constructed anything resembling architecture—tiny earthen chambers in the root-balls of the grasses, each one a cave in a miniature canyon, each one a refuge from the Sound that must still penetrate, reduced but present, a hum in the ants' vibration-sensitive legs that tells them always: *this place is loud, this place is loud, this place is loud*.

Snakes. They are the aristocrats of the crevice ecology, beautiful in the way of things that have no legs to break and no limbs for the wind to catch. They flow between stones like water, their scales catching the light in brief iridescent flashes, and they eat the rodents and are eaten by the raptors and they bask on the sun-warmed rock in the morning and retreat to the shade at midday and they are, perhaps, the most contented of the place's inhabitants because their needs are simple and the stone provides: warmth, shelter, prey, and a surface rough enough to push against for locomotion. They do not mind the Sound. They feel it as vibration, which is their native language, and so the roar that deafens the goats is merely conversation to a snake—the wind speaking in the long slow grammar of stone, a language of pressures and frequencies that the snake's belly understands even if its brain does not.

---

**The plants.** They are alive. This is easy to forget because they do not move and because their green is so sparse and so grayed with protective wax that it barely reads as color against the pale stone. But they are the foundation. Everything else eats them or eats something that ate them or eats something that ate something that ate them. They are the place's only interface with the sun's energy, the only point where light becomes sugar and enters the system.

The mosses and lichens came first and they are still here because they require almost nothing. A patch of condensation. A surface rough enough to grip. Minerals leaching from the stone beneath them. They spread so slowly that their growth can only be measured against geological time—a centimeter per century, perhaps, and even that is generous. They do not grow upward. They grow *into*, pressing their bodies against the stone, becoming a skin that is not quite plant and not quite mineral, and when the wind strips a patch of lichen from a surface it is not a death but a *rearrangement*, because the lichen's remnants settle into the nearest crevice and begin again.

The succulents are tougher. They store water in thick leaves and their roots go deep into the soil pockets, gripping the stone beneath the sediment like hands gripping a cliff. They bloom once a year, small flowers that are pale yellow or white, and the flowers last only days before the wind takes them, and if they are pollinated it is by the wind itself, which carries pollen from one sheltered channel to another with a precision that seems unlikely but which is simply the result of the wind's constant patterns—always the same channels, always the same eddies, always the same delivery routes, and so the plants have adapted to the wind's schedule and the wind's destinations and they trust it absolutely, which is the only kind of trust available here.

The grasses. They are the most visible, the tufts that bend one direction like flags, and they are the most recent, evolutionarily, and perhaps the most doomed. They grow at the edge of what is possible. A single season of unusual wind could strip them from their pockets, and a single season of unusual drought could kill them where they stand, and every year they survive is a year they have no right to expect. But they persist because persistence is the only strategy available to things that cannot move and cannot hide and cannot defend themselves. They stand in the wind and the wind takes pieces of them and they grow those pieces back and the wind takes them again and this is their life: a permanent conversation with destruction, a negotiation that they lose slowly, blade by blade, and which they fund with the sugar they make from the white light that would kill them if they were not already so hardened to violence.

---

**The wind.**

This is the difficult part. The wind is not alive. The wind is a process—air moving from high pressure to low, thermals rising and falling, the atmosphere equalizing itself across a landscape that interrupts its flow. You know this. You can explain it. The explanation does not change what it feels like to stand in the Sound and feel it moving through you, around you, against you, and to recognize that you are in the presence of something that has *qualities* usually reserved for living things.

The wind has behavior. It has patterns that repeat with enough consistency that the goats know them, that the raptors ride them, that the plants time their flowering to them. It has moods—the morning shift when the thermal inversion changes the direction briefly, the afternoon intensification when the sun heats the stone and the rising air draws the wind faster through the channels, the nighttime calm that is never calm but is merely less violent. It has preferences, or seems to—it takes the soft stone and leaves the hard, it strips the tall plants and spares the low, it enforces a uniform height of growth as precisely as a gardener with shears.

The wind has memory, or something like it. The channels and flutes in the stone are records of the wind's passage, inscribed in the only medium that lasts, and the wind reads those records each time it passes and follows the paths they describe. The stone shapes the wind and the wind shapes the stone and the result is a system that behaves with the consistency of a thing that *knows what it is doing*, even if knowing is not the right word for whatever the wind does.

The wind has voice. The Sound. The morning chord. The constant roar. The harmonics that emerge when the air moves through the flutes at a particular speed and angle, the music that lasts ten minutes and then is gone, and which is not meant for anyone but which is *there*, present, audible, a sound that the landscape makes because the landscape *is* a thing that makes sound, and that sound is not random, and it is not silence, and it is not something else, it is this, the Sound, the constant, the background of all thought.

You cannot call the wind alive. But you cannot stand in it and believe it is merely air in motion. It is too present, too persistent, too *shaping*. It is a force that has taken on the properties of an inhabitant. It lives here. This is its home. Everything else is visiting.

---

**The stone-singers.**

You will not find this term in any text. There are no texts about this place, or none that matter. The term is something that emerges in the minds of those who have stood in the morning chord and felt it in their sternum and understood, without evidence, without proof, that the sound is not accidental.

The formations are too organized. The channels are too consistent. The morning chord is too *musical*.

The explanation is natural: the wind has been working the stone for so long that only the most wind-conforming shapes remain, and those shapes produce wind-conforming sounds, and the human ear hears order in those sounds and calls it music. This is correct. This is what has happened. The question is whether *correct* and *complete* are the same thing.

Consider: the morning chord is not a single note. It is a chord—multiple frequencies produced simultaneously, frequencies that relate to each other in intervals the human ear recognizes as harmonic. A third. A fifth. An octave. These are mathematical relationships, and mathematics is the language of physics, and physics is what governs the interaction of wind and stone. But the specific intervals that emerge are the same intervals that human music uses, and human music uses them because they are pleasing, and they are pleasing because they resonate with the structure of the cochlea, which resonates because it evolved to parse sounds that matter—speech, footsteps, the approach of predators, the calls of kin. The morning chord is not meant for human ears. But it fits them. It fits them the way a key fits a lock it was never designed for, and the turning produces something like recognition.

The stone-singers are not creatures. They are not spirits or elementals or any of the thousand names that humans have given to the experience of encountering a pattern too large to be coincidence and too precise to be random. They are the formations themselves, the pillars and the channels and the flutes, which have been shaped by the wind into instruments and which play when the wind passes through them and which produce a sound that is, by any measurable standard, music. The music is not for anyone. It is not a message. It is a *property*—the way heat is a property of fire, the way wetness is a property of water.

But the place has been making this music for longer than humans have existed, and the music has the characteristics that humans recognize as intentional, and so the humans who hear it experience something they cannot resolve: the perception of purpose in a context where purpose is impossible. The stone-singers are not alive. They do not know they are singing. But they sing. Every morning. A chord that lasts ten minutes. A chord that has lasted ten minutes every morning for a hundred thousand years.

The goats do not hear it as music. The raptors do not hear it as music. The insects do not hear it at all. But you hear it. You hear it and something in you responds, something older than language, older than thought, the part of you that knows that sound-this-organized means sound-this-organized, and that sound-this-organized is what minds make, and therefore something with a mind must be making it.

Nothing with a mind is making it.

This is the hardest thing to accept about the place: the music is real and it is not for you and it was not made by anyone and it will continue whether you hear it or not. The stone-singers sing because singing is what their shape does when the wind moves through them. They are not performing. They are not communicating. They are simply *being*, and their being produces sound, and the sound has order, and the order is beautiful, and the beauty is incidental.

---

**The person who has been here longest.**

She is not there now. No one is there now. The place is empty of humans the way it is always empty of humans—by the wind's enforcement, by the landscape's refusal to accommodate.

But she was there once, for longer than anyone else, longer than should have been possible. She came for the obsidian and stayed for reasons she could not articulate, and she lived in the deepest channel where the wind was only a roar instead of a pressure, and she ate the goats and the rodents and the sparse plants and she drank the condensation from the stone each morning, and she was not happy but she was not unhappy either—she was *present*, in a way that humans are rarely present, because the Sound left no room for anything else.

She was not the first. The obsidian flakes are older than her by thousands of years. But she was the one who stayed, who made a life in the margins, who became a creature of shelter like the rest.

She stopped speaking after the first month. Not because there was no one to speak to—she had expected solitude—but because the Sound swallowed her voice so completely that speaking became an act of faith, and she did not have enough faith to sustain it. Her vocal cords atrophied. Her throat narrowed. When she tried to hum she produced only a thin sound that the wind took before it reached her own ears.

She learned to read the wind the way the goats did, by the pressure on her skin and the direction of dust and the subtle shifts in the Sound's pitch that signaled the morning chord. She learned to move the way the goats moved, low and fast and always on the windward side. She learned to sleep the way the snakes slept, coiled in crevices where the stone was warm, and she learned to eat the way the insects ate, whatever was available, without preference or disgust.

She did not go mad. This is important. The Sound did not break her because the Sound does not break things; it erodes them, slowly, grain by grain, and what remains is what the Sound cannot take. What remained of her was the most resistant part: the part that had always been quiet, the part that had never needed language or company or anything except the next breath and the next hour and the next morning when the chord would sound and she would feel it in her sternum and know, without thinking, that she was in a place that was exactly itself.

She left eventually. She walked out the way she had walked in, and she descended to lower elevations where the air was thick and the wind was just wind and the Sound was only a memory she could not describe. She tried to tell people about the place. She had no words that worked. She said: *it is loud.* She said: *the rocks sing.* She said: *I was the smallest thing there.*

No one understood. She did not expect them to.

She went back once, years later, and the formations were unchanged and the Sound was the same and the morning chord emerged from the stone at the same moment the thermal inversion shifted and she stood in the channel where she had lived and pressed her palm against the condensation on the windward face and felt the cold sweat of the rock and knew that she had been forgotten, that she had never been a feature of the place, that she had been a temporary pressure, a passing disturbance, something that moved through and was gone.

This did not make her sad. It made her feel like the stone, which is not happy or sad but simply present, and which will be present for a very long time.

---

**What truly lives there.**

The equilibrium does. The balance does. The system that has arrived at its most stable configuration and now persists in that configuration, unchanged, for timescales that make human life look like a heartbeat—this is what lives in the Wind-Sheared Heights. Not the goats, not the raptors, not the plants or the insects or the snakes. Not the woman who stayed. Not even the wind, which is only the mechanism, the breath, the current that maintains the balance. The equilibrium is what lives there, if living means persisting in a state that does not need to change.

You can call it an ecosystem if you want a scientific term. You can call it a spirit if you want a religious one. You can call it a machine if you want the term that comes closest to accuracy—a machine that does no work, a machine that produces only itself, a machine that runs on wind and time and the slow subtraction of everything that cannot survive.

The equilibrium does not know you are there. It does not know anything. It is not conscious, not aware, not alive in any definition that requires interiority. But it is *present*—massively present, overwhelmingly present, present in a way that makes your own presence feel fragile and temporary and slightly absurd. It has been present for longer than the stone remembers. It will be present for longer than the stone will exist. The formations will eventually fall and the wind will eventually still and the planet itself will eventually change, but the equilibrium will simply shift to a new configuration, a new balance, a new way of being that does not require the current forms to persist.

What lives in the Wind-Sheared Heights is whatever has found a shape that the wind cannot take. That is the only criterion for residence: you must be what remains. The goats remain because they have become wind-shaped. The raptors remain because they have become wind-ridden. The plants remain because they have become wind-low. The stone-singers remain because they have become wind-instruments. The woman remained for a while because she became wind-quiet.

And the equilibrium remains because it is the shape of all these remainings together—the sum of every adaptation, every subtraction, every grain of stone and every strand of DNA that has persisted in a place that wants nothing more than to strip you bare and see what you are when everything inessential is gone.

What lives there is what cannot be taken.
