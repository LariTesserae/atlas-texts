# The Residents

They are not what you expect.

---

You come looking for inhabitants and you find processes. You find the moss and the machinery and you think: *empty*. You think: *abandoned*. You have been trained by every story to expect a figure in a doorway, a face at a window, something that recognizes you and is recognized. You expect eyes.

The plateau does not deal in eyes.

But it is not empty. It is, in fact, extraordinarily full—so full that the problem is not finding life but understanding that what you are seeing *is* life, that the category you brought with you is too small, too particular, too dependent on the warm and the quick and the individual.

Start smaller. Start with what you almost overlook.

---

The spiders are the obvious first answer, and they are an answer, but not the one that matters. Still: they are here, and they are thriving, and their webs are so dense in the upper pipe-works that from certain angles the infrastructure appears to be held together by silk. They are not remarkable as spiders go—small, brown, deliberate, the kind that set up in a corner and rebuild every time the corner is demolished and a new corner is built in its place. They have adapted to the cycles of construction and deconstruction in the only way available to them: they build, and when the building is destroyed, they build again. In this they are not so different from the automated cranes, and if you watch them long enough you begin to see the resemblance—not metaphorical but structural, a shared logic of *repeat the motion, repeat the motion, repeat the motion until the motion is all there is.*

Their webs catch the midges. The midges breed in the standing water. This is a cycle so simple it barely qualifies as an ecology, and yet it persists, and persistence in a place like this is a kind of intelligence.

But you did not come for spiders.

---

The birds are harder to find. There are a few—species that favor cliffs and crevices, that nested in rock faces before there were buildings and see no reason to change their habits now. They are small and gray and the exact color of the wet stone, and they move through the pipe-lattice with a fluency that suggests they have internalized the architecture completely, that they think in conduit and catwalk the way other birds think in branch and canopy.

They call to each other, but rarely, and the sound is strange: a thin piping note that echoes through the metalwork and comes back distorted, multiplied, as if the manufactory were answering in a voice just slightly different from the one that called. The birds do not seem to notice or mind. Perhaps they have always lived in a world that talks back. Perhaps they do not distinguish between their own voices and the sounds the structure makes in response.

They nest in the highest frameworks, where the fog thins and the light—if light is the word for what happens up there—is marginally less diffuse. Their nests are constructions of moss and wire and the plastic strapping that comes loose from shipping pallets, and they are indistinguishable, at a distance, from the minor accumulations of debris that form in any sheltered corner. The birds have learned to look like part of the system. Whether this is camouflage or simply *accuracy*—whether they are hiding in the architecture or whether they *are* the architecture, another of its processes—depends on how wide you are willing to draw the boundary around the word *inhabitant*.

But you did not come for birds, either.

---

The ferns. Now.

The ferns are not simply growing here. The ferns are *operating* here. This is a distinction that matters.

Watch a stand of maidenhair ferns colonize a ventilation grate over the course of a season. They do not simply arrive and sprout; they send rhizomes through the slats, anchoring themselves to the metal mesh with a thoroughness that suggests planning, though of course it is not planning—it is something older and more reliable than planning. They position their fronds to catch the updraft of warm air that rises through the grate, and the warm air carries moisture and the moisture condenses on their leaves and drips down to their roots. They have *found* the grate's function and *used* it. They have integrated with the system.

Or watch the way the larger ferns—the ones that have established themselves in the drainage channels, their roots sunk deep in the accumulated silt—respond to the flood cycles. When the water rises, they flatten. Their fronds press against the channel walls, streamlining themselves, reducing resistance. When the water recedes, they lift again. It is a behavior so appropriate to the conditions that it looks intelligent, and dismissing it as mere tropism feels like a failure of imagination, a refusal to grant the label of *response* to anything that does not have a nervous system to respond with.

The ferns do not think. The ferns do not need to think. The ferns have been doing this for three hundred million years—longer than the plateau, longer than the machinery, longer than the concept of manufacture—and they have arrived at a method that works so completely that thought would only slow it down. They are not residents of the plateau in the way that a tenant is a resident of a building. They are residents the way a liver is a resident of a body: essential, integrated, impossible to remove without killing the whole.

But you want something that *chose* to be here. You want something that could, in principle, leave.

---

In the deepest basins, in the zones where the water is permanent and the yellow stillness is absolute, there are shapes in the reeds.

Not animals. Not quite. Not plants, either, though they are made of the same materials—stem and fiber and the dark organic matter that accumulates when living things die in standing water. They are accumulations. Aggregations. The slow result of the reeds growing and dying and growing again in the same place, layer after layer, season after season, each generation building on the saturated remains of the last until what was once a stand of vegetation has become something else entirely.

They are roughly the size and shape of a person crouching in the water, if a person were made of reed-stalk and peat and the pale root-matter of plants that have been drinking yellow water for decades. They do not move—or rather, they move at the speed of growth, which is a speed so slow it requires time-lapse photography to perceive, and the plateau has no cameras pointed at its basins, and so the movement goes unrecorded and therefore, by the standards of the quick, unacknowledged.

But they are growing. They are *directing* their growth, in the way that plants direct growth: toward water, toward nutrients, toward the structural support of the reed-stalks around them. They are building themselves. And as they build, they create the conditions for further building—their bodies slow the water further, trap more silt, provide more substrate for the next season's reeds. They are self-perpetuating. They are, in the most literal sense possible, self-made.

They do not have minds. They do not have intentions. But they have *trajectories*, and a trajectory maintained over sufficient time becomes indistinguishable from a purpose, and a purpose maintained over sufficient time becomes indistinguishable from a self.

Call them the basin-grown. Call them the reed-formed. Call them what you like; they do not know they are being named.

They are the closest thing the plateau has to natives.

---

And then there is the other kind.

Not grown. Made. Or rather—grown *and* made, the boundary so thoroughly crossed and recrossed that the terms have become meaningless.

They are the ones who were here before the automation failed, or changed, or became what it is now. The ones who worked the machinery and maintained the systems and walked the catwalks when the catwalks were still cleared of vegetation, when the drip was a problem to be solved rather than a feature of the landscape. The ones who built the first layers, who laid the original flooring over the original platform works, who welded the pipes and set the relays and programmed the algorithms in languages that are no longer spoken.

They are not here anymore.

But their work is here. Their decisions are here—encoded in the placement of every wall, the routing of every conduit, the tolerances of every joint. The plateau remembers them the way a body remembers a posture: not as information but as *shape*, as the particular angle at which a pipe bends because a human arm found that angle natural, as the specific height of a railing because that was the height that met a human hand. The ergonomics of the place are the ergonomics of a body that no longer exists here, and yet the body persists in the architecture, repeated ten thousand times, a ghost not of a person but of a *proportion*, a way of being in space that was so thoroughly built into the world that the world continues to assume it even in its absence.

The machinery does not miss them. The machinery does not have the capacity for missing. But the machinery *accommodates* them—leaves the clearances they would need, maintains the pathways they would walk, sets the controls at the heights they would reach. The plateau is shaped for people in the way that a coral reef is shaped for the organisms that built it: the builder is the structure, the structure outlasts the builder, and what lives in the structure afterward is shaped by the builder's ghost whether it knows it or not.

---

So who lives here?

The spiders live here, building and rebuilding in the margins. The birds live here, gray on gray, calling to each other and to the echoes that answer. The ferns live here, operating the systems they have integrated with, drinking the water that the machinery provides. The basin-grown live here, if *living* is the word for what they do—accumulating, self-perpetuating, building themselves from the materials at hand in the slow motion of vegetative time. And the absent live here, the ones who are present only as ergonomics, as the assumed body in the architecture's assumptions.

But none of these are what you are looking for, because none of these are what the plateau *is*. The plateau is not a container for these residents. The plateau is the resident—a single vast organism of pipe and moss and relay and root, of water and electricity and chlorophyll and rust, breathing in the gray fog, exhaling moisture, building and unbuilding itself in the anthill-logic of automated process. The inhabitants you can name—the spiders, the birds, the ferns, the shapes in the basins—are not separate from it. They are its organs. Its processes. Its way of being alive in the particular way that a place can be alive when it has been making itself for long enough.

You want a face. You want a name. You want something to meet your eyes and recognize you and say *I am here.*

The plateau says something else. The plateau says: *I have always been here. I will continue to be here. The wet is my breath and the machinery is my heartbeat and the vegetation is my skin and the fog is my thought, such as it is, and you are standing on me and your boots are leaving prints in my moss and I will grow over those prints when you leave because that is what I do, I grow and I build and I grow over what I have built and I build again on top of what has grown, and this is not a cycle, this is a direction, this is a trajectory, this is a self becoming more fully what it already is.*

The drip continues.

The ferns unfurl.

The machinery stirs in its sleep, and somewhere in the lower levels, a press begins its rhythmic thudding, and the whole plateau takes a breath, and holds it, and waits for whatever comes next.
