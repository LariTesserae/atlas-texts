What lives here has never had a name.

Not because names are impossible—you could give one, you could assign syllables to what you find—but because nothing here has ever needed to call itself anything. It simply is. It has been. The distinction between living and being is not one that applies.

Start with the pillars, since they are what you see first, or what you would see if sight were useful here. They are the closest thing to individuals the plateau possesses. Each one rises from a holdfast, a pale web spread across the rock like a hand pressed flat against a table, fingers splayed. The holdfast is the oldest part. It may be the only part that persists as a continuous self. The stalk above it breaks and regrows; the fronds tear away in the current and reform; but the holdfast remains, layering over itself year after year, decade after decade, becoming a kind of sedimentary record of its own existence. If the pillars have memories, they live in the holdfasts.

But this is a mistake, calling them individuals. It implies a boundary that may not exist.

The holdfasts spread. They touch. Where two pillars stand five meters apart—and they almost always stand approximately five meters apart—their holdfasts have met underground, have grown into and around each other, have fused at points and separated at others, have formed a network that connects every stalk on the plateau to every other stalk. This network is older than any individual pillar. It may be as old as the plateau itself. When a stalk breaks and a new one grows from the holdfast, the new stalk is not a descendant; it is the same organism, extending upward again, reaching for the current like a hand reaching for a rope.

So: one thing, then. One living thing, spread across the entire flat top of the plateau, expressing itself as hundreds of pillars, each one a finger, each one a voice in the chord.

But this is also a mistake.

---

The mats in the cracks are not kelp. They are not turf. They are not anything that taxonomy has a place for, unless taxonomy makes new places, which it might, if it knew about this.

They pulse. They breathe their yellow light. They resonate with the current and with each other and with something else, something that comes from below, from the rock, from the deep basalt that once was magma and now is cold and cracked and full of whatever these things are.

They might be older than the kelp. The kelp grows on the rock; the mats grow in the rock, in its fractures, in the spaces where the stone is more gap than substance. They don't have roots. They don't have holdfasts. They don't appear to draw nutrients from the water in any way that measurement can detect. What they have is rhythm.

Each mat contracts on its own schedule—or not its own, or not entirely its own. The contractions pass through the cracks in waves, and the waves sometimes synchronize and sometimes don't, and the pattern of synchronization is too complex to be random and too irregular to be a signal, at least not a signal meant for any mind that works the way minds typically work.

What if the mats are listening?

The current makes sound. The pillars make sound. The rock makes sound. The mats receive sound, translate it into contraction, translate contraction into light. They are transducers. They are ears that glow. They take the noise of the plateau and make it visible, faintly, in that breathing phosphorescence.

Or: they are mouths. They take whatever energy the rock provides—geothermal, chemical, something older and stranger—and they speak it into the water, and the water carries it to the pillars, and the pillars amplify it, and the sound goes on.

Or: they are both. They are a conversation that has been happening for ten thousand years, or a hundred thousand, or however long this place has been drowning and making its music. The mats speak; the current carries; the pillars answer; the mats listen. A closed loop of signal and response, and the signal is never the same twice because the current shifts, the tides change, the pillars grow and break and regrow, and the whole system adapts, slowly, at the pace of cold water and patient vegetative thought.

This is what lives here. Not organisms but a process. Not creatures but a conversation.

---

The question of the people is a real question, even if the answer is mostly absence.

They came when the plateau was above water. The structures prove it. The soot proves it. The tools prove hands that worked in air, that tied knots, that scraped and shaped and built. They found a place that was already making its music—the pillars thrashing, the mats pulsing, the sound continuous—and they settled in the gaps. They made shelters between the stalks, leeward, away from the wind that was the current's ancestor. They lived simply, ate simply or not at all, stayed briefly.

The nets trouble the story. No animals means nothing to catch. The nets were never used; their edges were finished with the same careful binding as their centers, which means they were made completely, made well, made by hands that knew what a net was for, and then never deployed. Why?

Perhaps the nets were not for catching. Perhaps they were for *listening*. A net is a grid of intersections, a web of tension and release, and if you hang a net in the current where the pillars thrash, it will vibrate. It will pick up the chord. It will translate the water's movement into a pattern of strain across its knots, and if you hold it—if you are a person with hands and a body that can feel—you can read that pattern the way a blind person reads a face.

The people came to listen. They built their shelters in the spaces between the pillars because that was where the sound was clearest, where the chord had room to resolve. They made their nets to feel the music more closely, to translate it into something their bodies could understand. They stayed for as long as it took to hear whatever they came to hear.

And then they left, or were taken, or were drowned.

The plateau does not mourn them. It does not remember them. Their structures stand because the water preserves them, because the current flexes rather than shatters them, because the kelp rope and the bone stakes and the dried fronds are patient materials in cold water. But the plateau has incorporated them the way it incorporates everything: as part of the sound. The structures change the current's flow, slightly, locally. They create eddies and shadows. They add their own small resonance to the chord. The curtains of dead fronds in the doorways sway in the water and produce a faint whisper that the living pillars pick up and carry and amplify and feed back into the mats, which pulse once, twice, in response, and the conversation shifts, and the music goes on.

---

The turf between the pillars is the humblest part of the ecosystem, the least remarkable, and so it is easy to overlook what it might be doing.

It covers the rock like skin. It moves in the current like wheat in wind. It draws nutrients from the water and it grows and it dies and it grows again, and its dead blades drift in the flow until they catch on something—a pillar base, a structure's wall, a crack in the rock—and decompose slowly, feeding nothing, because there are no decomposers here, only the patient chemistry of cold water and mineral dissolution.

But the turf also *dampens*. It absorbs sound. It muffles the click of debris tumbling across the rock. It softens the bass thrum where it is thickest, near the center of the plateau, where the pillars stand tallest and the current is slowest. It creates a gradient of silence—not true silence, nothing here is truly silent, but a relative quiet—that makes the edge of the plateau louder by comparison, that makes the sound seem to swell as you approach the cliff.

This might be accidental. The turf may simply be growing where the current allows it to grow, thick where the water is slow, thin where it is fast. The acoustic effect may be a side effect of survival.

But the mats in the cracks also seem to be arranged—thickest at the plateau's edge, where the cliff drops away, where the current is most violent, where the sound is loudest. They pulse brightest there. They synchronize most clearly there. As if they want to be heard.

As if the whole plateau is shaped to project.

A body with a mouth. The mats at the edge, speaking. The pillars above, amplifying. The turf in the center, making space, creating the chamber, the resonant cavity, the quiet heart from which the sound can radiate outward into the dark water and down into the abyss.

What is it saying?

Nothing that can be translated into words. Nothing that can be parsed as a message with a sender and a receiver and a content. The plateau is not *saying* anything. It is *being* something: a process so long and so slow and so continuous that the distinction between expression and existence has worn away entirely. The sound is not language; it is the sound of a place existing, the way a heartbeat is the sound of a body existing, the way breath is the sound of a person being in a room. It does not require interpretation. It requires only the acknowledgment that something is here, and it is alive, and it has been alive for longer than anything with a skull can comprehend.

---

So what lives on the Drowned Plateau?

A single organism distributed across a hundred hectares of rock, expressing itself as pillars and turf and holdfast, speaking through the current and the sound it makes.

A collection of presences in the cracks that may be separate or may be one, that breathe and glow and listen, that have done so since before the water came, that will do so after everything else is gone.

The ghosts of hands that made nets and lit fires and slept on stone shelves, incorporated now into the music, their structures serving as instruments they never intended to build.

And the sound itself, which lives in the way that a song lives—not as a thing but as an event, not as an object but as a continuous act of becoming, happening now, happening always, the longest sustained note in the ocean, playing to no audience, needing none, filling the dark water with proof that something here persists.

What lives here is the plateau. And it will not stop singing until the rock itself wears away.
