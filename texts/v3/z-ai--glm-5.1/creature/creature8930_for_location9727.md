# What Lives There

---

The crustaceans have generations now. Unnumbered generations. They have been here long enough that the ones who first found the city would not recognize their descendants, and their descendants would not understand the concept of recognition. They are a mass organism, a skin, a pelt. When they move across the facade of the old bank building—its columns smoothed into nubs, its pediment a mineralized curve—they move as one thing, and the building seems to shift beneath them, to breathe, to shed its own skin into the current. You cannot isolate an individual. You can try. You can watch one pale segmented body climbing over its thousand siblings, and for a moment it seems distinct, purposeful, heading somewhere specific. And then it turns, or something turns it, and it rejoins the flow, and the flow reshapes, and the surface shimmers, and whatever you were watching is gone into the granular clicking mass.

They eat what comes to them. Organic residue, yes—the slow dissolution of everything organic the city once contained, which is more than you'd think. Wood takes decades to vanish. Fabric longer. And there are other sources. The chemosynthetic mats that grow where the heat vents, the bacterial films that spread across every surface, the dead of their own kind, the occasional small fish that sickens and sinks. The crustaceans are not picky. They are the city's digestive system, its mouth at the edge of every surface, its constant processing. When they retreat—when an eel comes, when pressure shifts—they leave behind a clean space, a patch of mineralized wall or floor that seems almost new, almost like the building it was, before the water. And then they return, and the surface disappears under their bodies again, and the eating resumes.

They have no fear of the deep things. This is incorrect. They have no *concept* of the deep things. When the pressure shifts and the eels go still, the crustaceans go still too, but not from fear—from something mechanical, a reflex that passes through them like a wave, stillness as a physical property of the water rather than a decision. They do not know what they are waiting for. They do not know they are waiting. They are simply not moving, and then they are moving again, and the interval between is not remembered.

---

The eels remember. This is a dangerous thing to say about a creature so apparently simple, so obviously a predator and nothing more. But they navigate the corridors with a precision that exceeds hunting. They know the buildings. Not just as spaces to move through—they know them as *specific* spaces. The eel that claims the old department store does not venture into the library next door. The eel in the municipal building hangs at the intersection of three hallways on the second floor where the current is warmest, and it has hung there for—how long? Eels live long. Longer than you'd think. And these eels, in this warmth, with this metabolism, they may live longer still.

They recognize each other. Not by sight—the light doesn't permit it—but by the displacement of their bodies, the specific pressure wave of a specific neighbor passing through a specific doorway. They know who lives in which building, which floor, which corridor. They have agreements. Not conscious, perhaps. Not written. But the patterns of their movement suggest something like territory, something like arrangement, something like a city council of long pale bodies meeting in the chambers of what was once a city council's chambers, slowly opening and closing their mouths in the warm dark.

When the deep things move, the eels go still. And this stillness is different from the crustaceans'. The eels choose it. They press their bodies against walls and floors and they flatten, and their eyes—which are wrong in the same way the fishes' eyes are wrong, too large, too dark, too aware—roll upward, or downward, or in a direction that doesn't have a name because it requires the pressure of three stories of warm water above you to feel. They are listening. They are feeling. And whatever they learn, they keep. The eels are the memory of the Drowned Quarter. They hold what the city was, not in any brain that you could dissect, but in the pathways they swim, in the territories they maintain, in the way they still circle the old parking garage as if cars might return.

An eel will live fifteen years in open water. Here, in the warmth, with the chemosynthetic abundance, they may live fifty. They may live longer. The largest ones—thick as a diver's thigh, longer than the doorways they navigate—are old. Impossibly old. They were here before the last person who remembered the surface died, if there were people who remembered the surface, which there weren't, because the city went under long before anyone living remembers, and the sea has stopped trying to reclaim it because the sea *has* it.

---

The fish are the strangest inhabitants, and they are the reason this place feels haunted, which it isn't—haunted requires ghosts, and ghosts require a relationship to the dead that nothing here maintains.

But the fish maintain a relationship to the living.

They school in the traffic patterns. Not approximate traffic patterns—not a vague tendency to flow along streets. They school in the *specific* patterns of the specific streets they occupy. The fish on what was Maple Avenue school differently than the fish on what was Broad Street. They follow the lanes. They stop at the intersections where stop signs were, or where traffic lights stood, or where the crosswalks marked the asphalt—these things are gone now, dissolved or buried or mineralized into the reef, but the fish remember them. Or not remember. The patterns are in the water now. Decades of cars and trucks and buses moving in specific rhythms, creating specific currents, leaving specific thermal traces on specific patches of road—the water learned. The water holds the pattern. And the fish, whose ancestors moved into the water and found the pattern already waiting, swim it like a river, like a current that exists only because the city once had traffic.

This is why their eyes are wrong. This is why they school around parking structures in dense spirals, waiting. They are reading something. They are responding to something embedded in the water itself, the memory of movement, the ghost of circulation. Their eyes are large because they need to see in the dark, yes, but also because they are seeing something else—something that isn't light, that isn't visible, that exists only as pressure and warmth and the faintest chemical trace of exhaust and rubber that still, after all this time, lingers in the water that circulates through the streets. The fish are the city's dream of itself. They are what the traffic became when the traffic had nowhere to go.

---

And then there are the things deeper in.

You asked who lives here. I have described residents, but I have not described *inhabitants*. The crustaceans are residents. The eels are residents. The fish are residents, and they are something else, too—they are the echo of something that was. But the things in the basements, in the sub-basements, in the tunnels that go down toward the heat—they are not residents. They are the building.

No. That's not right either. They are what the building became when the building could no longer be a building.

The foundations of the Drowned Quarter sit on something hot. The description says geothermal, and it's right, but geothermal is a word for a process, and the things down here are not processes. They are the heat's inhabitants. They are what grows when warmth and pressure and mineral saturation and time converge in a space that was never meant for life but has, through some accident of engineering, become exactly the kind of space where something new might take hold.

They are not animals. They might not be biological. They might be something that exists in the space between biology and geology, where the buildings have become a reef and the reef has learned the grammar of architecture—they might be what happens when that grammar learns a new tense.

When they move, the water changes. A pressure shift. A warmth surge. A subtle current that shouldn't be there. The eels feel it. They go still. The crustaceans go still. The fish flow into their traffic patterns and hold, suspended, waiting for the light to change, waiting for something that will never come because it already came and went and what remains is the pattern. And the deep things move through the tunnels below—tunnels that were subway tunnels, that were steam tunnels, that were the buried infrastructure of a city that thought it would last forever—and they move through them slowly, so slowly, with a patience that makes the sea's patience look hasty, and they are patient because they are not going anywhere.

They live here. This is their home. The basements, the sub-basements, the heat—the city built on top of them, unknowingly, and then the city went under, and now they have their spaces back, and they move through them in the dark, in the warmth, in the pressure, and they do not come to the surface often because the surface is not where they are.

What do they look like? The description doesn't say. The description says they are felt, not seen. But you asked who lives here, and I will tell you what I imagine, knowing that imagination is not knowledge and that the deep things may not be imaginable:

They are large. Not whale-large. Building-large. They are the size of the tunnels they move through, because they grew in those tunnels, because the tunnels shaped them and they shaped the tunnels, because infrastructure and organism have become, down there, the same thing. They are smooth. They are warm. They are the color of the heat they live in—dark, reddish, mineral—and they have no eyes because there has never been light to see by, and they have no mouths because they do not eat, they absorb, they take in the chemosynthetic richness of the geothermal flow and they grow, slowly, imperceptibly, the way a reef grows, the way a city grows, the way something grows when it has no predator and no limit and all the time in a drowned world.

When they pass, the Quarter holds its breath. And then the breath releases, and the clicking resumes, and the eels drift again, and the fish flow back into their streets, and the deep thing continues its slow circuit through the tunnels below, and the city remembers—for a moment, in the silence, in the stillness—what it used to be.

And then it forgets. And it goes on being what it is.

---

There is no one else. There is no remnant human population, no merfolk, no sentient octopus reading the faded text on submerged billboards. There is no society here, no culture, no language. There is only life, dense and adapted and strange, filling every crack and room and corridor with its clicking and its swimming and its slow, patient hanging in the warm dark. The Drowned Quarter does not need us. It does not remember us, except in the traffic patterns of fish who have inherited our rhythms without understanding them. It does not mourn us. It does not wait for us.

It is full. It is complete. It is a place that has found its state and holds it, and the state does not include us, and that is not a tragedy. That is just what happens when the sea takes something and makes it its own. The buildings stand. The warmth rises. The things in the deep tunnels move through their long cycles. And above it all—or below it all, or around it all, because above and below have different meanings when the sky is a gray ceiling you cannot reach and the ground is something warm and living that you cannot see—the city breathes, through a thousand clicking mouths, through the hum of geothermal heat, through the silence of the deep things passing, and it is alive, and it does not know it is alive, and that is the closest thing to peace that any place can have.
