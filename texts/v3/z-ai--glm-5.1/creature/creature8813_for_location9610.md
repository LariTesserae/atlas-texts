# The Residents

---

The salamanders do not think of themselves as many. They think of themselves as one body in many convenient locations.

This is not mysticism. This is practicality. When you have lived in the same thirty paces for generations beyond counting—when your ancestors' bones have become part of the soil from which the moss grows, which grows the insects, which grow you—the boundary between self and place becomes the kind of question that only creatures with too much time would ask. They have exactly enough time. They use it for basking.

In the brightest part of the day—which is bright indeed, light arriving from everywhere like a consensus they never asked to join—they emerge from the moss and find the warmest stones. Not the stones that are warm by human standards. The stones that are *less cold*. The distinction matters. Their bodies are small furnaces, burning whatever the moss provides, and they allocate the heat with the precision of creatures who have never known abundance and therefore have learned the art of sufficiency. A forelimb here. The arch of a spine there. The golden belly, which they keep turned toward the stone's slight warmth, is the only indulgence they permit themselves.

They do not have names for the humans who occasionally enter. They have something better: a category. *The tall cold things that make breathing sounds and then leave.* The category is not unkind. It is accurate. The children—*the smaller tall cold things that are more quiet*—they tolerate with the mild interest of creatures who have never been hunted and therefore have never learned to categorize in terms of threat. The children drink from the bone-cup. The salamanders understand the bone-cup in their way, which is to say they understand it as a shape water sometimes occupies. They have never considered who carved it. They have never considered carving.

What they consider is this: the moss, the insects, the stone, the light. The slow progression of brightness across the floor as the day advances. The way the cold increases after the light peaks and begins its long withdrawal. The precise moment at which retreating into the moss becomes the only sensible act, and then the moss closes around them like a green silence, and they are home in the way that only creatures who have never left a place can be home—not with attachment but with identity, the moss and the salamander being aspects of a single system that has no interest in distinguishing its parts.

They move and freeze. Move and freeze. This is not fear. This is the rhythm of a body that has learned: stillness is a form of presence that the world respects. When they move, they are small dark shapes negotiating the green architecture. When they freeze, they become part of the architecture. A watching human loses count because the salamanders have performed the oldest trick of all—they have become the place they inhabit. Count the salamanders and you are counting the moss. Count the moss and you are counting the stone. Count the stone and you have been here long enough to understand why counting stopped mattering.

---

The beetles know something the salamanders do not. Or perhaps the salamanders know it too and simply do not care to act on it. The beetles know that *somewhere else* exists.

Not somewhere better. Just somewhere else. The edge of the Moss Ward, where the packed earth meets the foundation of a wall that does not belong to this place, is a border they cross and recross with the purposefulness of merchants who have learned that trade routes are more important than destinations. They do not leave for long. A few body-lengths into the crack between wall and earth, a few minutes of exploring the cooler darker reaches where the moss thins and gives way to bare stone, and then they return. They always return.

Perhaps it is the light. The beetles—copper-colored, round, their carapaces catching the scattered brightness like small mirrors designed by a culture that values utility over decoration—seem to thrive in the illumination. They move faster in the light. Their deliberate paths across the moss-forests become more deliberate, more assured, as if the light confirms something they need confirmed: that the world has edges and they are within them.

They have their routes. You can see them if you watch long enough—the slight wearing of moss where the same path has been traveled ten thousand times. From the crack in the eastern wall where the moss is thickest, across the face of the moss to the trough, where they drink by perching on the stone lip and dipping their mouthparts with a precision that would seem careful if it were not so clearly habitual. Then along the trough's edge—which they patrol with the attentiveness of sentinels, though what they are guarding against or for is a question only a beetle could answer—and back to the crack. The circuit takes approximately four hundred steps for a beetle. They make it several times a day. They have been making it for longer than the surrounding houses have had their current occupants.

What do they find at the trough? Water, yes. The film of algae that grows where stone meets water, yes. But also: the reflection of the sky, trapped in the water's surface, which they walk across or beside or perhaps *through*, depending on your philosophy of surface tension. If a beetle can see the sky—which it can, in its way, compound eyes taking in the world as a mosaic of brightness and darkness—then it sees the sky doubled here: above, in the rectangle the walls frame, and below, in the water the trough holds. Two skies. Perhaps this is why they come. Perhaps this is why they return. In a place defined by its boundaries, the trough offers a door that is also a mirror.

The beetles do not think in terms of doors or mirrors. They think in terms of *toward* and *away*, *warm* and *cool*, *here* and *slightly elsewhere*. It is enough. It has been enough for the duration of their species' tenancy, which predates the trough, which predates the wall the trough sits against, which predates the concept of tenancy itself.

---

The vine is the oldest resident, and it knows this in the way that very old things know things: not with pride but with the weight of simple continuation.

Its root system—somewhere outside the Moss Ward, in soil that does not belong to this place and therefore does not concern this account—sends water and nutrients through the woody stem that traverses the wall's lip and descends into the bright cold space. The stem is thick. Thicker than it needs to be for the leaves it supports. This is because the stem remembers other leaves, other seasons, other incarnations of the vine that have lived and died and lived again in the same path, following the same trajectory, a greenscar across the stone that the current vine adheres to with the fidelity of a pilgrim following a sacred road.

The vine does not think of itself as a bridge. But it is one. The wren-like bird uses it as such—quick precise hops from wall to wall, pausing mid-crossing to produce that thin high sound that might be song or might be the vine singing under the weight of passage. The vine tolerates this. It has no choice, and it has had no choice for long enough that the distinction between tolerance and preference has worn away like the distinction between wall and moss. The bird comes. The bird goes. The vine holds. This is the arrangement.

In the bright scattered light, the vine's leaves have gone pale. The cold has leached them toward white, toward a jade so light it seems to be a memory of green rather than green itself. This is not illness. This is adaptation. The vine has learned that in a place where light arrives from everywhere, dark leaves would absorb too much, would burn, would become a liability. So it has gone pale. It has become a thing that lets most of the light pass through, taking only what it needs, reflecting the rest downward to the moss and the thyme and the small white flowers that watch the sky with their five-petaled faces.

The vine has seen children come and go. It has seen adults stand at the crack and not enter. It has seen the trough carved and the cup placed beside it—though *seen* is perhaps too active a word for what the vine does, which is more like *having been present during*. It does not remember these events. It contains them. The way stone contains the memory of the glacier that scored it—not in narrative but in orientation, in the direction of its grain, in the way its surface faces the source of a pressure that no longer exists.

If you asked the vine—and you cannot, but if you could—it would tell you that it is not crossing the space. It is holding the space together. The two walls lean toward each other like old friends, and the vine is the arm one has draped across the other's shoulder, and without it they might fall inward or fall apart or fall into something that is not the quiet conversation they have been having for centuries. The vine is how the space knows it is a space and not just a gap. The vine is the line that makes the circle. It is the oldest resident because it was here before the moss found its gradations, before the ferns erupted, before the clover made its nation. It was here when the walls were new and did not yet know they would become old. It will be here—some iteration of it, some stem following the ghost of this stem—when the walls finally settle past their lean and meet, and the Moss Ward becomes a room with a roof of stone, and the light has nowhere to scatter, and the space enters its next phase, which will be darkness and will be a kind of brightness too, because the vine has learned to grow toward whatever light exists, and there is always some light, even if it is only the phosphorescence of very old moss and the slow bioluminescence of decay.

But that is far away. The vine does not think of it. The vine thinks of *toward* and *along* and *hold*.

---

The ferns do not erupt. That is the wrong word. They *insist*.

Each frond emerges from the joint between two stones with the formality of a formal gesture—a bow, a curtsy, the arch of a hand making a point in an argument that has been going on so long the participants have forgotten the subject. The ferns are not large. The cold discourages that. But they are many, packed so tightly that their fronds overlap like shingles, like the scales of a very green very patient dragon sleeping against the wall.

They have an arrangement with the condensation. The walls weep in the cold hours before dawn—water beading on stone, gathering, tracing paths down the moss-channels—and the ferns have positioned themselves where the water passes. Not to catch it. To be *in its way*, so that the water must acknowledge them, must part around their stems, must leave a little of itself behind as tribute. By morning, each frond carries a line of droplets along its edge, each droplet magnifying the world beneath it: the moss, the stone, the dark spaces where the salamanders sleep. A fern is a gallery of small lenses, each showing a different version of the same place, each insisting: *this is what the world looks like from here.*

The ferns are the Moss Ward's archivists. They do not record events—nothing enough happens to warrant recording—but they record *conditions*. The curl of a frond's edge tells you how much moisture the night provided. The angle of its arch tells you which direction the light was strongest when it unfurled. The slight browning at the tip of an older frond tells you that yesterday was marginally warmer than today, or that the condensation arrived later than usual, or that something breathed on it—some tall cold thing making breathing sounds—before moving on.

If you sat here long enough—if you learned to read the ferns the way the ferns read the moss, the way the moss reads the stone—you could reconstruct the history of every day this place has held. Not the events. The *qualities* of light and water and cold. The archive is vast and redundant and no one will ever read it because no one needs to. The ferns record and record and the recording is the point, the way breathing is the point even when no one is listening to your chest rise and fall.

---

The moss is not one thing. This is the first thing the moss would tell you, if the moss told things, which it does not, because the moss has been here long enough to know that telling is less effective than being.

Three species. Three strategies. One surface.

The deep emerald moss lives where the condensation runs—the channels down the wall where the water traces its nightly paths, where the stone is always wet, where the cold is most intense and the light arrives green-filtered through the water film that never quite dries. This moss is lush by Moss Ward standards, which is to say it is a millimeter or two thicker than its neighbors, its stems slightly more upright, its color the kind of green that makes you understand why the word *verdant* was invented. It drinks first. It drinks most. It has earned this through tenure.

The sage moss lives where the stone dries—the faces the light hits directly, the surfaces where the condensation gives way to a mere sheen by mid-morning and to nothing by afternoon. This moss is tougher. Its stems lie flat against the stone, pressing their whole length into the surface like swimmers pressing against a current. It has learned to hold what it gets and to want less. Its color is the color of wisdom in a place where wisdom means *knowing how to be cold and damp and continuing anyway*.

The chartreuse moss lives at the margins—the borderlands where emerald and sage negotiate, where the water sometimes comes and sometimes does not, where the light falls differently depending on the hour and the season and the fog. This moss is the most interesting. It is the least settled. It changes color subtly through the day, brightening when the condensation arrives and fading when it withdraws, as if it is trying on identities and finding none of them quite right. It is the moss that the salamanders prefer—not because it is better for basking or better for hunting, but because it is the most like themselves: a thing that exists between states, that has learned to be comfortable with negotiation, that has found its identity in the act of becoming rather than in the fact of being.

Together, the three mosses carpet the lower walls in a gradation that seems deliberate. *Seems* is the wrong word. *Is* deliberate—not by the moss's design but by the stone's offering. The stone provides the conditions. The moss accepts them. The arrangement that results is the only arrangement that could result, which is another way of saying it is perfect, which is another way of saying it has been here long enough that the question of perfection no longer applies.

The moss does not think of itself as a carpet. It does not think of itself as writing slow calligraphy across the vertical faces, though from outside it looks like that. It thinks of itself—if it thinks, which it does not, which it does in a way that thinking cannot account for—as *the surface the surface the surface*. It is the skin the stone has grown. It is the green the light becomes when it has nowhere else to go. It is the place where the water stops and is accepted, where the spores catch and hold, where the small insects live their small lives and become the salamanders' food and the salamanders become the soil and the soil becomes the moss.

The moss is the Moss Ward's way of being visible. Without the moss, this would be a cold bright space with stone walls and nothing to mark the passage of time. With the moss, it is a clock whose face is green and whose hands are the shadows of ferns and whose ticking is the drip of condensation falling onto leaves that were waiting for exactly this.

---

The tiny white flowers do not move. This is their contribution.

They grow between the thyme at the base of the walls, each flower no larger than a fingernail, five petals, facing upward as if watching. They do not track the light—there is no need, the light is everywhere. They do not close at night—the cold is the same whether they are open or closed, and they have chosen to remain open, to remain watching, to continue their small act of attention regardless of whether there is anything to attend to.

They are the youngest residents. Not as a species—as individuals. Each flower lasts only a season, and the species has been here as long as anything, but each particular flower is new, is brief, is a small bright insistence that *now* is worth paying attention to, even in a place where *now* is indistinguishable from *always*.

The children watch them. The children understand something about the flowers that adults have forgotten—something about the value of small things that face upward, that do not move, that persist in being exactly what they are for exactly as long as they are. The children sit with their backs against the moss and they watch the tiny white flowers and something passes between them, some recognition of shared brevity, some understanding that the children will grow and leave and become the adults who smile and do not enter, and the flowers will wither and seed and become the flowers that the next children will watch, and the watching is the point, the attention is the gift, the small white face turned upward to the light that comes from everywhere is the only prayer this place has ever needed.

The flowers release no scent. They ask for nothing. They are five-petaled arguments for the sufficiency of simply being present, and they are winning, and they have been winning for as long as anything can remember, which is as long as the moss, which is as long as the stone, which is as long as the gap between the walls that lean toward each other like old friends sharing a secret that is not a secret but is simply this: *we are here, and that is enough, and that has always been enough, and the light will come again tomorrow and the condensation will bead and fall and the salamander will emerge and the beetle will make its circuit and the vine will hold and the fern will arch and the moss will be the surface and the flower will face upward and the child will watch, and the place will continue its long silent adequate existence, and that is what lives here: not a thing but a way, not a resident but a residency, not a who but a how, and the how is this: patiently, coldly, brightly, completely.*
