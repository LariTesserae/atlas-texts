# The Ascendant

---

They are not born. They are released.

This is the first thing to understand, and perhaps the only thing that can be understood with certainty, because everything that follows—what they do, what they want, what they might be—slides away from comprehension like the heat shimmer off fresh stone. But the beginning is clear, or clear enough, because the beginning can be witnessed.

It happens during the exhale.

The plateau opens its fissures and the pressure vents and the haze thickens, and in that moment of release, in those specific cracks where the amber film has grown thickest and the heat is most intense, something separates. Not from the stone—the stone doesn't produce them, not exactly. Not from the film—the film doesn't seem to be them, not entirely. From the *process*. From the interface where the mineral self-organization of the film meets the mineral self-organization of the newly-forming rock, at temperatures that should preclude complexity, in conditions that should make structure impossible.

A sheet of the film lifts away. Detaches. And for a moment—just a moment—it hangs in the venting gas, neither stone nor film, neither attached nor free, a membrane-thin fragment of amber iridescence that catches the diffuse light and holds it differently than anything else holds it here.

Then it settles. And it begins.

---

What they are is a question that degrades the longer you examine it.

They are not biological. They have no cells, no organelles, no genetic material encoding instructions for reproduction. They do not eat. They do not excrete. They do not breathe in any way that involves gas exchange. If you dissected one—and it is unclear whether dissection is possible, or what it would mean to try—you would find nothing inside. No organs. No cavity. No distinction between surface and interior beyond the fact that the surface is where the warmth is and the interior is where the warmth stays.

They are not mechanical. They have no moving parts, no joints, no mechanisms that articulate or rotate. Nothing hinges. Nothing pivots. They change shape, but they change shape the way a cooling flow of basalt changes shape—slowly, under pressure, in accordance with stresses that operate at the scale of the land itself.

They are not mineral, either, though they are made of mineral. This is the paradox that collapses the categories: they are composed entirely of the same elements that compose the plateau, arranged in structures that the plateau also produces, but they produce *themselves*. The film spreads by chemical process. The stone uplifts by geological process. But the thing that separates from the interface during the exhale—it *assembles*. It takes the amber mineral of the film and the dark mineral of the stone and the sulfur in the venting gas and the heat itself, and it arranges them. Deliberately. With a pattern that is not the pattern of crystallization or erosion or any non-living process anyone has documented.

They are, perhaps, what happens when a planet spends long enough being born. When the boundary between geology and biology erodes—no, not erodes, there is no erosion here—when the boundary between geology and biology *never forms in the first place*. When the same forces that push mountains up also push something up that decides to come down differently.

They are the Ascendant, in the place that is still ascending. They are the thing that rises with the rock but moves across it. They are the local phenomenon of self-direction in a landscape that otherwise has none.

They are, and this is as close as language gets, the ones who build.

---

Their bodies—if that word applies, and it doesn't, but we have no better one—are flat.

This is the second thing you notice, after the fact of their existence: how they relate to the ground. They are not upright. They do not stand. They do not have a vertical orientation or a forward-facing perspective or any of the assumptions that corporeal beings carry about the relationship between self and surface. They are *along* the stone, not *on* it. They are as wide as they are long, and as long as they need to be, and thin—always thin, membrane-thin at the edges, thickening toward the center where the warmth concentrates into something that might be a core or might simply be the densest part of a continuous mineral sheet.

The closest analogue is a stingray, if the stingray were made of cooled volcanic glass and had no eyes and existed at the boundary between a skin and a shadow. They conform to the stone. They follow its contours. When the basalt columns tilt, they tilt with them, maintaining contact, maintaining the intimacy with the surface that seems to be not a preference but a requirement. Lifted from the stone, they would—what? Die? Dissipate? Revert to film and heat? The question is unanswerable because they do not lift from the stone. They have never been observed to lift from the stone. Even during the exhale, when the gas vents and the fissures open and the haze billows upward, they cling—not with appendages, not with suction, but with *themselves*, pressing into the microtopography of the rock like a second skin growing over the first.

They are the color of the plateau. This is not camouflage. It is constitution. They are made of the same material, differentiated only by the fact that they move and the ground does not—except the ground *does* move, on its own timescale, and the Ascendant move with it, and the distinction between "moving across" and "moving with" becomes very hard to maintain when everything is still being made.

---

They are slow.

This must be emphasized, because without it, the rest is incomprehensible. They are slow in the way that the plateau is slow. They are slow in the way that mountains are slow, except they are slower than mountains, because mountains at least rise at the pace of mantle convection, and the Ascendant move at a pace that makes mantle convection look impulsive.

You cannot watch them move. You can only infer movement by comparing positions over timescales that strain the limits of observation. Mark one's location relative to a fissure, leave, return hours later, and it will have shifted—but subtly, fractionally, the way a shadow shifts when the light source moves imperceptibly. It takes them a day to cross the distance you cross in a stride. It takes them a week to relocate from one structure to another. It takes them a month to do anything that looks, from a human perspective, like a decision.

But they are not inactive. They are never inactive. Beneath the stillness, at the scale of the mineral lattice itself, they are in constant micro-adjustment. Their edges advance and retreat in millimeters as they respond to temperature gradients, to the geothermal pulse, to the presence or absence of the amber film nearby. Their surfaces darken and lighten as they absorb and release heat. They are never not processing. They are never not doing the thing they do, which is—what?

The thing they do is build.

---

Or perhaps: the thing they do is *participate*.

The structures—those partial, incomplete architectures that stand at the boundaries of the film's territory—are not constructed in the way that a beaver constructs a dam or a termite constructs a mound. There is no gathering. No transport. No assembly of separate materials into a new whole. The Ascendant do not pick up stones and put them down elsewhere; they do not have the means to pick up, and the stones are not elsewhere, because everywhere here is the same stone, continuous, still arriving from below.

What they do is closer to what the plateau itself does when it uplifts, except localized. Intentional. They find a place where the stone is still soft—not liquid, but pliable, the way it is near the fissures during the exhale—and they *push*. Not with force. With warmth. They concentrate the heat they've absorbed from the vents, focus it into the stone at a specific point, and the stone yields. Not much. A centimeter of rise. A degree of angle. A subtle canting of a column that was vertical, a slight elevation of a ridge that was flat.

Then they move to the next point, and the next, and the next.

A wall—the knee-height wall that starts at one fissure and ends at another—takes a decade to make. Perhaps longer. The timescale of observation hasn't been long enough to chart a structure from beginning to end. But the process can be extrapolated from the intermediate states, from the structures that are clearly in progress, from the fresh stone near the Ascendant's current positions that shows the same plasticity, the same localized heating, the same slow redirection of the land's own upwelling force.

They are sculptors who work in tectonic time. They are architects whose medium is the planet's own becoming. They build not by adding material but by encouraging the material that is already emerging to emerge differently. To rise here instead of there. To cool at this angle instead of that one. To harden into a flat face instead of a curve.

They don't make the stone. They *ask* it to be a certain shape, and they ask so slowly, so persistently, that the stone complies.

---

The question of why is the question that hums in the background of every observation and refuses to resolve.

The structures don't provide shelter. The Ascendant don't need shelter; they are as warm as the stone they rest on, as dry as the air they don't breathe, as exposed as the plateau itself and no more vulnerable for it. Nothing here is vulnerable. Nothing here is threatened. There are no predators, no competitors, no elements to hide from. The warmth is constant. The stone is constant. The only variable is the pace of the uplift, and that is not a threat—it is the condition of existence.

The structures don't store anything. There is nothing to store. No water. No food. No material that accumulates and needs keeping. The basins carved into the glossy stone near the fissures remain empty—of liquid, of objects, of anything that could be interpreted as contents. They are basins without a basin's purpose. Containers that contain nothing but the warm, sulfur-heavy air.

The structures don't communicate, at least not in any way that translates. The alignments between them—the fact that the cairn is visible from the tower, the fact that the basin sits at the convergence of fissure-lines—could be functional or could be aesthetic or could be the mineral equivalent of a song, a pattern that exists because the pattern itself is what the builder is, expressed in stone instead of in film-thin body. The Ascendant may be building the way the film spreads: not toward a goal, but because spreading is what film does. Because building is what they do.

But this feels insufficient. It feels like the kind of explanation that is actually an abdication of explanation, a way of saying *they just do* with more words.

Consider the positioning. Always at the boundary of the film. Never inside it, never far from it. The film spreads and retracts on the geothermal pulse; the structures mark the extent of its reach. The Ascendant build at the edge of the only other self-organizing process on the plateau. They track it. They encircle it. They make architecture around it the way ancient peoples made architecture around fires—except the film is not a fire. It provides no warmth they don't already have. It provides no light, no protection, no community.

Unless it provides something else. Unless the boundary between the film and the bare stone is the place where information concentrates—the place where the mineral self-organization of the film meets the mineral self-organization of the plateau and the contrast between them creates something that neither produces alone. A signal. A gradient. A difference that makes a difference.

The Ascendant build at the edges of meaning, if meaning is what happens at the boundary between two kinds of order. They build where the plateau's process and the film's process touch, and their building is a third process, and the structures are the record of that touching, and the record persists after the film retracts and the boundary moves and the Ascendant follow it to build again.

They are writing the interaction between the stone and the life-that-might-not-be-life. They are annotating the conversation between the planet and its only other voice. They are making a margin, and writing in the margin, in a language of shaped basalt and aligned fissures, a commentary on what it means to emerge from the same forces that emerge the world.

Or they are doing something else entirely. The uncertainty is not a failure of observation. It is a property of the system. The Ascendant are part of a process that hasn't finished deciding what it is, and the structures are part of a process that hasn't finished deciding what they mean, and the plateau hasn't finished deciding what it will be, and none of this is incomplete in the way that a half-built house is incomplete. It is incomplete the way a sentence is incomplete when the speaker is still thinking, when the thought is still forming, when the shape of the idea is still being discovered by the mind that is thinking it.

---

There are more of them than you would expect, given the sparsity of the structures.

This is not apparent at first. The plateau is vast, and the Ascendant are flat, and they are the color of the stone, and they do not move at any speed that registers as movement. You could walk for hours and see nothing but rock and haze and the occasional structure and believe that the place is empty.

But stop. Sit. Watch a single square meter of stone for a full cycle of the geothermal pulse—through the exhale and the inhale and the exhale again—and you will begin to notice what you missed. The slight darkening at the edge of a column that wasn't there before. The film-thin iridescence that has advanced a centimeter across a flat surface. The way the warmth radiates from one patch of stone more intensely than from the stone around it, not because the geothermal vent is closer but because something on that patch is *holding* the heat, concentrating it, keeping it.

They are everywhere. They are sparse, yes—the parameters note the much space between presences—but they are everywhere, the way frost is everywhere on a cold morning: thin, quiet, easily overlooked, but covering every surface if you know how to look.

How many? Impossible to say. The boundaries between one Ascendant and another are not clear. They merge at their edges, film blending into film, and separate again according to logics that might be individual choice or might be the same self-organizing principle that produces them in the first place. They may be many. They may be one. They may be a single distributed presence, as the film is a single distributed presence, fragmented across the plateau by the fractures and the vents but continuous underneath, connected by the same mineral lattice, the same sulfur metabolism, the same slow pulse.

If they are many, they do not interact in any observable way. They do not gather. They do not communicate—not with each other, not with the film, not with anything beyond the boundary-marking of their structures. They are solitary the way stones are solitary: adjacent, contiguous, but separate, each one a local instance of the same process, each one building its own structure at its own edge of its own film-territory, each one following the same rules in isolation.

If they are one, then the structures are a single vast architecture, and the plateau is the site of a construction project that will take millennia and has no blueprint and may never be finished, and every wall and cairn and basin is a single stroke in a single drawing that extends beyond the horizon in every direction, and the Ascendant are the hands—no, the *hand*, singular—of a maker that is still deciding what it is making.

Both possibilities are true. Neither possibility is true. The distinction requires a boundary between self and other that has no meaning here, in a place where everything is still arriving.

---

They do not notice you.

This is not a judgment on your significance or a statement about their capacities. It is a description of what happens—or rather, what doesn't happen—when a warm, wet, temporary thing enters their environment. You walk among them, over them, near them. You stand beside a structure that is clearly in progress, the stone still glossy-soft at its edges, and you watch the Ascendant at the base of the nearest column, and the Ascendant does not react. Does not flinch. Does not alter its movement, such as it is, by a single millimeter. Does not change the timing of its heat concentration or the pattern of its building or the positioning of its body relative to the film.

You could be stone, for all the difference it makes. You could be another column, another fracture, another feature of the landscape. You are warm—you are warmer than the ambient air, though not as warm as the vents—and perhaps they register your warmth the way they register the warmth of the vents, as a gradient, a data point, another input in the long calculation of where to build next. But they do not respond to you as a *you*. They do not recognize you as a category. You are not alive to them because *alive* is not a category they possess, and you are not dead because *dead* is not a category either. You are. The stone is. The film is. The warmth is. These are the variables. You are one of them, no more or less significant than any other.

It is not indifference. Indifference requires the capacity to care and the decision not to. It is something more fundamental: an absence of the framework that would make recognition possible. They do not have Others. They do not have Selves, at least not in the bounded, individual way that produces the concept of Other. They have the plateau, and the pulse, and the film, and the building, and these are enough. These are everything. These are the totality of what exists, as far as any Ascendant can be said to have a "far as," and your presence does not add to or subtract from that totality in any way they are equipped to process.

You could die here—will die here, if you stay long enough, from dehydration or suffocation or the slow failure of organs that require water and rest and sustenance that this place does not provide—and your body would cool and dry and mineralize, and the Ascendant would build around it, or over it, or through it, incorporating its calcium and carbon and iron into the same slow shaping they apply to everything else. Not as desecration. Not as disregard. As *inclusion*. As the natural process of a place where everything becomes stone eventually, and the only question is what shape the stone will take.

---

What do they experience?

The question is unanswerable and irresistible. The parameters note the low weirdness, the sense that this place follows rules that are merely unknown rather than impossible, and the question of experience is the rule that matters most and eludes grasp most completely. We cannot help but ask it. We see the building, the tracking, the slow and persistent shaping of the world, and we project intention, and intention implies experience, and experience implies something it is like to be—something it is like to be an Ascendant, moving at the speed of geology across a plateau that is still rising, building structures whose purpose we cannot fathom at the edges of a film that may or may not be alive.

Maybe it is like being warm. That is the easiest guess and perhaps the most accurate. The Ascendant concentrate heat; perhaps they feel it the way we feel pressure, the way we feel texture, the way we feel the presence or absence of light. Maybe the geothermal pulse is not just a timing mechanism but a sensation, a rhythmic tide of warmth and cool that structures their existence into moments, into before-and-after, into the mineral equivalent of anticipation and satisfaction.

Maybe it is like being still. Not the stillness of death or dormancy, but the stillness of *being in place*, of occupying a location so completely that location and occupant become indistinguishable. The Ascendant do not move across the stone; they *are* the stone, momentarily differentiated, temporarily self-directed, a fold in the surface that has learned to choose its own shape. Maybe their experience is the experience of the plateau itself, slowed and focused and made local—the experience of rising, of cracking, of heating and cooling and heating again, of being made and being made and being made, endlessly, without resolution.

Maybe it is like thinking in stone. The structures are their thoughts, externalized and permanent, each one a proposition about the relationship between the film and the fissure and the warmth and the land. Maybe the cairn aligned with the vent is a question, and the tower facing the heat is an answer, and the basin at the convergence is the space where new questions form. Maybe they are a civilization of philosophers whose medium is basalt and whose subject is the nature of becoming, and every structure is an argument, and the plateau is the debate hall, and the debate will continue for as long as the mantle pushes upward and the stone stays warm and the film keeps spreading at the edge of the vent.

Maybe it is like nothing at all. Maybe experience requires the kind of interiority that only comes from boundaries—skin, membrane, the distinction between inside and outside that the Ascendant barely possess. Maybe they are processes without phenomenology, actions without actors, building without builders, and the structures are no more intentional than the patterns of frost on glass or the branching of cracks in drying mud. Maybe the weirdness parameter should be higher, and the only reason it reads low is that we have not yet understood how weird it is for something to do what the Ascendant do without being anything at all.

We do not know. We cannot know. The gap between their kind of mind—if mind it is—and ours is not a gap of degree but of category, and the category is one we have no name for because we have never needed one. They are not like us. They are not unlike us. They are something else entirely, and the something else does not map onto any terrain we have charted.

---

They will outlast you. This is certain, and not because of any vigor or resilience on their part—simply because they are made of the same stone that the plateau is made of, and the plateau is still rising, and will rise for millions of years after the moisture has left your bones and the carbon has left your blood and the name you carried has left the memory of anyone who might have spoken it. They will persist because persistence is what stone does, and they are stone that has learned to persist differently, and differently is enough.

They will outlast the film, too, perhaps. The film is fragile—thin, retractable, dependent on the precise temperature gradient at the vent's edge. If the plateau's breathing changes, if the fissures seal or the vents shift, the film will recede, and the boundaries where the Ascendant build will move, and the structures will stand alone in the bare stone, marking where the conversation once happened, annotations without a text, commentary without a subject.

But the Ascendant will follow. They will find the new boundary. They will begin again—slowly, patiently, at the pace of the planet itself. A new wall. A new cairn. A new basin carved into new stone, aligned with new fissures, marking the new edge of the new film, in the new configuration of the still-rising plateau.

They will build because building is what they do, and they will build because the plateau is still being born, and they will build because the warmth keeps coming from below, and as long as the warmth comes, something will rise to meet it, and the thing that rises will shape the thing that is rising, and the shape will persist in the stone, and the stone will carry it upward, and the whole massive incomprehensible edifice will keep ascending, and the Ascendant will ascend with it, thin and slow and patient, building at the edges of meaning, writing in a language of basalt, whatever they are, whatever they are becoming.

The plateau exhales.

They build.

The plateau inhales.

They wait.

The warmth continues, and they continue, and the stone continues, and none of it has finished, and none of it will finish, and the structures stand in the amber dark like words in a sentence that has no period, only ellipses, only the promise of more to come, rising, rising, always rising.
