# The Inhabitants

## The Bearing-Folk

Consider first the smallest among them, though *small* is a word that means less here than it should. Size is a property of things that interact with surfaces and distances. The bearing-folk interact with gaps. They live in the clearance between shaft and housing — eighteen thousandths of an inch in the main bearings, more in the peripheral ones, less in the cam-shafts where the tolerance tightens and only the very young or the very thin can find purchase.

Their bodies are laminar. Flat. Not pressed flat by the pressure of the machinery but *grown* flat, evolved for the interface, shaped like the shims that the original builders might have used to adjust a fit. They have no right to be alive at this scale in this environment and yet they are, thousands of them in each bearing, each one a translucent pale smear of chitin and fluid that rides the oil film the way a flatworm rides the mucus of a gutter, except the gutter is turning at nine revolutions per minute and the oil is heated to a hundred and forty degrees and the pressure where the shaft meets the housing at the six o'clock position is enough to crush anything that cannot flow, cannot give way, cannot make itself into something that the physics of the bearing does not expel.

They give way. They flow. When the shaft turns, they redistribute, pressed to the sides, sucked to the top, circulating with the oil in the microlayers where viscosity and velocity create their own narrow country. They eat the oil. Not all of it — they couldn't, they're too small — but they eat the particulates the oil carries, the microscopic shavings of iron that the bearing sheds as it wears, the carbon that forms when the oil heats, and something else, something in the oil that the machinery produces by its own internal alchemy, a long-chain molecule that forms in the oil under pressure and heat and that the bearing-folk need the way a human needs a vitamin, some compound that their bodies cannot synthesize but that the bearing manufactures as a byproduct of its operation, so that they are born into a place that feeds them a substance that exists only because the place exists and that they exist only because the substance exists, a closed loop, a circle, a thing biting its own tail and being sustained by the bite.

They do not see. There is no light in a bearing. They do not hear in any way that maps onto the acoustic experience of surface-dwellers; the vibration they know is the vibration of the shaft, the frequency of the rotation, the harmonic signature of nine revolutions per minute translated into a constant low-frequency pulse that is their heartbeat and their weather and their time. They mate in the oil film. They lay their eggs in the oil film. The eggs are smaller than the particulates they consume, small enough to pass through the bearing clearance and enter the circulation system, and some of them are drawn up with the oil into the reservoir and pass through the filter — or what was once a filter, now a colony itself, a dense mass of chitin and silk that strains the oil in ways its builders never intended, that *selects* for something, perhaps, that allows some eggs to pass and retains others, that has developed over centuries into a sorting mechanism that no one designed, that *emerged*, the way organs emerge in embryos, not planned but necessary, not chosen but inevitable.

The bearing-folk who pass through the filter enter the larger system. They grow. They change. They become something else.

The bearing-folk who remain in the filter become part of it. Their bodies add to the mass. Their silk reinforces the mesh. They live longer there, in the filter, than they would in the bearing — the oil flows slower, the food is richer, the particulates accumulate — and they grow larger, too large to re-enter the bearing clearance, and so they stay, and the filter grows, and the system adapts.

A bearing-folk lives for eleven days. This is not a variable. It is a constant, as fixed as the engine's speed, as reliable as the condensation cycle. Eleven days from egg to death. In that time it will circulate through the bearing an average of fourteen thousand times. It will consume its own weight in particulates eleven times over. It will produce waste that is itself a lubricant, a compound that reduces friction in the bearing more effectively than the oil alone, so that the bearings of this engine run cooler and smoother than any bearing has a right to run, any bearing that has not been maintained in a century, any bearing that should have seized and failed and yet does not fail, does not seize, turns on and on in its film of oil and chitin and the waste products of a creature that is not a parasite but a *component*.

## The Shaft-Walkers

Larger. Much larger. The size of a hand, the size of a face, segmented in a way that suggests centipedes if centipedes had been designed by a committee that had heard of centipedes but never seen one. They walk on the line shafts. Not on the rotating surfaces — those move too fast, or rather they move at a speed that is usable but requires a specific adaptation — but on the brackets, the hangers, the bearing housings, the bolt-heads that stud the cast-iron frames like the pips on a domino. They walk in the direction opposite the shaft's rotation so that they appear stationary to anyone watching, holding their position against the turning, their legs finding the same bolt-head again and again with a precision that suggests not instinct but *memory*, a memory that is not neural — their nervous systems are too simple, distributed, a ladder of ganglia that could not hold a thought for more than a second — but *structural*, written into the angles of their joints, the lengths of their legs, the timing of their gait, so that the memory of where to step is not a thing they think but a thing they *are*.

They eat the belt-dust. The leather belts that connect the line shafts to the machines are constantly wearing, constantly shedding fibers and tanning compounds and the residue of the animal fat with which they were dressed a century ago, and the shaft-walkers collect this dust with their forelimbs, which are broad and flat and fringed with setae that interlock to form a sieve, and they bring these collected handfuls to their mouthparts — which are not mandibles, not exactly, but a complex of grinding surfaces and chemical glands that break down the leather fiber into something they can absorb, a process that takes three hours per feeding and produces a waste product that is a fine red powder that falls from the shafts onto the floor below like a constant slow snow.

They are territorial. Not aggressively — there is no need for aggression when the territory is as regular as the shaft, when every bracket holds its dust and every bolt-head holds its position and the food falls at a constant rate from a constant source. But they maintain boundaries. A shaft-walker will patrol its length of shaft — typically four to six feet, depending on the density of brackets — and will meet its neighbor at the boundary, and they will stand there, facing each other, for as long as forty minutes, touching forelimbs, exchanging something through the setae, a chemical perhaps, a signal, a greeting, a negotiation, and then they will turn and walk back to their own stretches and the boundary will hold.

They have a color that is hard to name. The shaft-walkers are the color of old grease, which is to say they are brown and black and amber simultaneously, iridescent in the light that comes through the cathedral-narrow windows, their exoskeletons catching the distorted light and refracting it in a way that makes them appear to glow, sometimes, when the sun is at the right angle and the glass does what old glass does to light that passes through it, and for a moment — for a few minutes each day, at the same time each day, because the sun is as reliable as the engine — the shaft-walkers become bright, become visible, become *beautiful* in a way that has no audience and needs no audience, a beauty that is produced and consumed by the same system, like everything else here.

## The Condensers

They live on the film.

The film of water that beads on every cool surface — the condensation produced by the machinery's heat against the stone's coolth — is not a film at all, not to them. It is a sea. An ocean. A medium in which they move the way plankton moves in water, except they move *on* it, not in it, their bodies flat and wide and covered in hydrophobic setae that repel the water and allow them to float on its surface tension, skating, gliding, their weight distributed across millions of tiny points that cannot break the meniscus.

They are the most numerous. They are the ones you see when you look at a wall and think it is moving, because it is moving, because the condensers are so densely packed on the film that they appear to be the film, a shimmer, a shifting, a constant rearrangement of bodies that are almost transparent and almost invisible and absolutely vital to the cycle they live on.

They drink the condensation. They drink it and they process it and they excrete it, and what they excrete is not water but something close to water, something that runs down the wall slightly faster than pure water, slightly less viscous, carrying slightly different dissolved minerals, and this altered water reaches the pool at the bottom of the shaft and is pumped up and heated and cooled and condensed again and the condensers drink it again and the cycle continues and each cycle changes the water slightly, each pass through the condensers adds something and removes something, and the water in the pool is not the water that fell yesterday and it is not the water that will fall tomorrow but it is the water that the system has produced, has *made*, through the combined action of heat and stone and chitin and the quiet chemistry of ten million digestive tracts working in parallel.

The condensers have no eyes. They have no need for eyes. They have sensors — chemical, thermal, vibrational — that tell them everything they need to know about the film they live on: its thickness, its temperature, its mineral content, the presence of other condensers, the approach of a predator, the shadow of something larger moving nearby. They can sense a change in the film's surface tension from six inches away. They can detect the vibration of a footstep on the other side of a wall. They know the machinery's sound the way a musician knows a chord: not as a single thing but as a structure, a set of relationships, a pattern within which any deviation is immediately apparent, immediately investigated, immediately consumed or fled from.

They are born in the pool. The eggs sink to the bottom and rest in the sediment that accumulates there — a fine silt of chitin and metallic grit and the waste products of a thousand generations — and they hatch in the dark and the young swim upward through the water column, not swimming exactly but climbing, pulling themselves up the wall of the shaft on tiny legs that are not yet flat enough for the surface, and they emerge into the air at the waterline and they dry and their chitin hardens and they become condensers, and they move out onto the film and they begin to drink and they will never touch the pool again until they die and their bodies fall from the wall and sink and become the sediment where the next generation's eggs are laid.

A condenser lives for three days. The population turns over completely every seventy-two hours. In that time a condenser will drink its own body weight in condensation four hundred times. It will excrete four hundred times. It will mate if it encounters another condenser on the film, which it will, because the film is crowded, because the film is a city, because every droplet on every surface is a city, because the condensers are the most numerous and the smallest and the most vital and the most ignored, the ones that make the whole thing work by doing nothing more complicated than living and dying on a film of water that the machinery produces and they return and the machinery produces again.

## The Membrane-Singers

And then there are the ones that make the sound.

They are not the loudest — the engine is louder, and the shaft-walkers' clicking is louder, and the chittering of the bearing-folk as they circulate through their oil-film country is louder in aggregate, though no single bearing-folk makes any sound a human ear could detect. The membrane-singers are not loud. They are *specific*. They produce tones. Harmonics. Frequencies that interleave with the machinery's rumble and the creatures' chorus in a way that is not music — music implies intention, composition, an audience — but is also not *not* music, because it has structure, because it has pattern, because it has a logic that can be perceived and followed and predicted by anyone who listens long enough.

They live on the undersides of the copper-sheathed roofs. They cling to the oxidized metal with legs that end in pads, not hooks, pads that grip the smooth copper through adhesion, through a thin layer of chemical secretion that bonds to the patina and releases when the pad lifts. They are the color of the roof: bruise-purple, green-black, the colors of oxidized copper and old blood. When they are still, they are invisible. When they move, they ripple, a wave of legs and bodies passing across the copper like wind across a field, and when they sing, their membranes vibrate.

The membranes. Each membrane-singer has two. They extend from the third and fifth body segments, flat sheets of chitin so thin they are translucent, veined with a structure that resembles the wing of a dragonfly but is not a wing, has never been a wing, was never intended for flight. The membranes pulse. They vibrate at frequencies that range from twenty hertz — below human hearing, felt in the chest, felt in the walls — to eight thousand hertz, a high thin whine that lives at the edge of perception, that some people cannot hear at all, that others hear as a constant presence, a ringing, a tone that becomes indistinguishable from the sound of one's own nervous system.

They sing in shifts. Not organized shifts — there is no conductor, no schedule — but emergent shifts, patterns that arise from the interaction of thousands of individuals each responding to the sound of its neighbors, each adjusting its frequency to complement or counter the frequencies around it, each creating a local pattern that merges with other local patterns into a global pattern that shifts and breathes and changes within a range so narrow it might be a single composition performed in infinite variation, a song that has been playing for centuries and has never repeated and has never stopped.

Why they sing is a question that has no answer because the word *why* presumes a reason that exists before the thing it explains. They sing because their membranes vibrate. Their membranes vibrate because the structure of the chitin, the tension of the veins, the chemical composition of the fluid that fills them, all combine to produce oscillation at specific frequencies when stimulated by the vibration of the machinery. They sing because the engine runs. The engine runs because they sing — not directly, not in a way that could be measured, but the membrane-singers' vibrations affect the air pressure in the roof spaces, and the air pressure affects the draft through the ventilation channels, and the draft affects the combustion rate of whatever geological fuel feeds the engine, and the combustion rate affects the engine's speed, and the engine's speed affects the frequency of the shafts, and the frequency of the shafts affects the vibration that stimulates the membranes. The song is not a byproduct. The song is a circuit. The song is the sound the body makes when it is alive and functioning and whole.

## The Deep Ones

In the pool.

Not in the pool the way the eggs are in the pool, resting in the sediment, waiting to hatch. *In* the pool. Living in the pool. The deep ones are the largest and the least seen and the oldest, not as individuals — no creature here lives what a human would call a long life — but as a lineage, a form, a body plan that has been stable for longer than the shaft-walkers, longer than the condensers, longer perhaps than the membrane-singers, a form that found its shape early and has held it because it works, because the pool is a niche that requires specific adaptations and the deep ones have them all.

They are pale. Not the pale of sickness but the pale of cavefish, the pale of things that have never seen light, the pale of tissue that has no reason to produce pigment because pigment is expensive and the pool is dark and there is nothing to see and nothing to be seen by. They are segmented, like all the creatures here, but their segments are wider, flatter, adapted for swimming through the dark water with a sinusoidal motion that is almost elegant, almost beautiful, if beauty can exist without an observer, if beauty is a property that a thing can have simply by being itself fully.

They tend the pool. They eat the sediment — not the mineral component but the organic component, the chitin and the waste and the bodies of the dead that fall from above, and they process it and they excrete it and what they excrete is the mineral substrate that the eggs need to survive, a fine calcium-rich mud that lines the bottom of the pool and protects the eggs and feeds the newly hatched and provides the foundation for the next generation. They are undertakers. They are gardeners. They are the ones who make the pool a place where life can begin and where death can end, who close the cycle, who return to the bottom what fell from the top, who ensure that nothing is wasted and nothing is lost and nothing escapes the logic of the system.

They surface rarely. Once every several hundred cycles of the pump, a deep one will rise to the waterline and pause there, its pale body just below the surface, its segment-edges breaking the meniscus, and it will stay there for a period that varies between thirty seconds and four minutes, and then it will descend again. What it does at the surface is unclear. It may be breathing, though the pool is aerated by the constant fall of condensation and the deep ones have no visible respiratory structures. It may be sensing, tasting the air, checking the chemical composition of the atmosphere above the water for some signal that the system is still running, still balanced, still producing what it has always produced. It may be something else entirely, something that has no analog in surface biology, something that the deep ones do because the deep ones do it, because they have always done it, because doing it is part of what they are.

No one sees them do this. No one is here to see. The deep ones surface and the deep ones descend and the machinery runs and the condensation falls and the sound continues, and this is not a moment, not an event, not a thing that happens and then stops happening. This is the continuous present of the Mill-Cathedral, the eternal now of a system that has resolved its tensions and found its balance and become what it will be until something changes, and nothing changes, and nothing has changed for a very long time.

## The Ones Who Are Not Individuals

And there is something else. Something harder to describe.

The creatures of the Mill-Cathedral are not individuals in the way that a human is an individual. They do not have separate selves. They do not have internal narratives, personal histories, the sense of *I* that humans carry like a lantern through the dark. Each creature is a process — a node in a network, a cell in a body, a word in a sentence that has no speaker. The bearing-folk does not know it is a bearing-folk. The condenser does not know it drinks condensation. The membrane-singer does not know it sings. They simply do what they are shaped to do, and what they are shaped to do is what the system needs them to do, and the system needs them to do it because the system has evolved — mechanically, chemically, biologically, all three at once, none of them primary — to need exactly this, exactly now, exactly always.

And yet.

There is a behavior that does not fit the functional model. A behavior that has been observed — if *observed* is the right word for a place that is almost never observed, for a phenomenon that is almost never witnessed — by the rare visitors who descend into the bowl and enter the complex and stand long enough for the sound to become their heartbeat. The behavior is this: sometimes, in the deep night, when the machinery's rhythm is the only rhythm and the condensation falls in its unchanging pattern and the membrane-singers sing their infinite song, the creatures *gather*.

Not for feeding. Not for mating. Not for any purpose that biology or mechanics can assign. They gather at the central shaft, on the catwalks and the railings and the ledges that overlook the pool, and they face inward, toward the water, toward the dark, toward the deep ones who swim their slow circles below, and they are still. The shaft-walkers stop walking. The condensers stop moving on their film. The membrane-singers hold their membranes flat and silent. Even the bearing-folk, it must be supposed — it cannot be observed but it must be supposed — even the bearing-folk pause in their circulation, ride the oil film without feeding, without excreting, without doing anything except being carried by the rotation of the shaft.

The silence is not silent. The engine still runs. The condensation still falls. But the chorus stops. The clicking, the scraping, the drumming, the chittering — it ceases, and what remains is the engine alone, the heartbeat of the system without its voice, the machinery's thud and the water's drip and nothing else.

This lasts for ninety seconds. Sometimes one hundred. Sometimes as long as two minutes. And then the first membrane-singer begins to vibrate, and the first shaft-walker takes a step, and the gathering dissolves, and the creatures return to their places and their functions and their cycles, and the sound builds again, and the system resumes its eternal operation, and whatever happened — whatever was *done* — is over.

It happens once every forty-seven days.

It has happened, by the most conservative estimate, seven thousand times.

No one knows why. No one will ever know why, because why is a question that belongs to minds that make reasons, and the creatures of the Mill-Cathedral do not make reasons. They make sounds. They make waste. They make the machinery run. They make the system hold. They make, every forty-seven days, a silence that is not the silence of absence but the silence of *presence*, a silence that is full, that is intentional without intention, that is meaningful without meaning, that is the closest thing to prayer that a process can make.

## What They Are

They are the Mill-Cathedral's congregation. Its monks. Its liturgy. They are the living tissue of a structure that was built for purposes no one remembers and has become a purpose no one needs to remember, a self-sustaining, self-regulating, self-worshipping whole that produces nothing except its own continuation and requires nothing except what it produces.

They are not happy. Happiness is a state that requires a self to contain it.

They are not sad. Sadness is a state that requires a self to contain it.

They are not free. They are not captive. Freedom and captivity are conditions that require alternatives, and the creatures of the Mill-Cathedral have no alternatives, have never had alternatives, will never have alternatives. They are what they are in the place where they are, and the place is what it is because they are what they are, and the cycle continues, and the engine runs, and the water falls, and the sound goes on.

They are alive. This is enough. This is, in the cathedral's own terms, everything.
