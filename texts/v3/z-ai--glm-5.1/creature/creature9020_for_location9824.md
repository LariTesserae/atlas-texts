# The Residents

They are not what you expected. You expected nothing—emptiness, ruin, the long vacancy that such places advertise. Or you expected something terrible. Something that would require warning. The heat and the dark and the weeping walls seemed to promise a resident made of hunger or malice, some patient predator that had driven all else away and now waited in the perpetual twilight with the leisure of something that knows it will not be disturbed.

This is not what lives here.

---

What lives here is *slow*.

You must understand this first, or you will misunderstand everything. The slowness is not lethargy. It is not the sluggishness of a creature defeated by heat, though the heat would certainly defeat anything that moved quickly. The slowness is *constitutional*. It is the tempo of a different kind of metronome, one set to a rhythm that does not recognize your hours, your days, your urgent little seasons. When you see them—and you will see them, because they are not hiding, because hiding would require a relationship to visibility that they do not possess—you will think at first that they are another formation. Another collapsed wall. Another mound of softened stone wearing its wet film.

Then you will notice the breathing.

---

They are large. This is relative; you have no comparison here, no other bodies to measure them against, and the city itself skews your sense of scale—the low buildings, the long streets that compress at their far ends, the way distance behaves like a contracted muscle. But they are large enough that when one unfolds from its position against a wall—where it had been holding so still that the moisture ran over it in the same channels it uses on the stone, where the red-black growth had crept to the edge of its surface and stopped, respecting the border as it respects the borders between its own territories—you feel the displacement in the air. Not a wind. A *shift*. A volume of air suddenly reclaimed, relocated, asked to accommodate a shape it had forgotten it was not already accommodating.

The shape is difficult. Not because it is complex, but because it is *almost* familiar. Almost stone. Almost a geological formation. Almost a mound. Almost a hill that has decided to try being vertical for a while. The closest analogy is the way water collects in a depression: it fills the shape that holds it. They fill the space they occupy. When they rest against a wall, they conform to the wall's surface. When they move through the streets—and they do move, they do use the streets, though the word *use* implies a utility they may not possess—they fill the corridor from edge to edge, not blocking it but *becoming* it, temporarily, the way water becomes the channel it flows through.

Their surfaces are the same wet sheen as the stone. This is not mimicry. This is shared circumstance. You are wet here. Everything is wet here. They are no more mimicking the walls than you are mimicking the air by breathing it.

---

The color: dark. Darker than the stone, but not by much. A deep umber that reads as black in this perpetual dimness but would probably be warmer—browner, redder—in full light. There is a quality to the surface that is not quite skin, not quite membrane, not quite mineral. It has the slight give of something organic, but the patience of something geological. When you touch one—and you will, because they do not flee and they do not react and eventually the absence of reaction becomes an invitation, or at least a permission—you find that it is warm. Warmer than the stone. Warmer than the air. The same heat that rises from the ground below, but concentrated, held, as if the creature is a vessel for the earth's warmth rather than merely a surface it rises through.

And the texture: smooth. Not slick—there is too much moisture for slickness, the wet defeats itself, makes everything frictionless and therefore nothing frictionless—but smooth the way a river stone is smooth, the way a thing becomes when it has been held by flow for a duration beyond reckoning. There are no features. No eyes that you can identify. No mouth. No limbs in the arrangement you would recognize as limbs. There are *protrusions*—sections that extend, that reach, that flatten and spread and contract again—but they do not have joints. They do not have elbows or knees. They have the articulation of a slow wave, of a tentatively extended thought, of water deciding to occupy a new shape.

---

How many? You cannot tell. This is not because they are numerous, though they may be. It is because they do not maintain a fixed count. You understand this slowly, the way you understand everything here—against your will, against the tempo of your own cognition, dragged into comprehension by the sheer weight of observation.

One rests against the wall of a building on the second street. You have passed it three times. You have touched it. You have held your hand against its warm smooth flank and felt the breathing, the slow expansion and contraction, the rhythm that matches the pulse of the red-black growth in the creases but slower, much slower, a breath that takes minutes to complete. It is the same one each time. You are sure of this because of its position, because of the way it has settled into the corner where the wall has partially collapsed, because of the particular channels the water has worn around its base.

Then you round a corner and there is one against *that* wall, and you think: a second. Two residents. But when you look back, the first is gone, and you are willing to believe it has moved—imperceptibly, in the time you weren't watching, during the interval when your attention rounded the corner before your body did—but the one behind you is now in a different position, and its surface has the same wear patterns, the same water channels, and you realize that either they are identical or they are the same, and *same* does not mean what you thought it meant here.

They are not numerous. They are not singular. They are *present*, in the way that the water is present—everywhere that the conditions allow, filling every shape that receives them, but not separate, not distinct. There is one against the wall on this street, and one in the intersection, and one at the far end where the distance bunches and folds, and they are all the same creature or they are all different creatures or they are all *expressions* of something that does not distinguish between itself and itself, the way a puddle does not distinguish between its left half and its right.

---

What do they do?

This is your question, and it is the wrong question, but you do not know that yet, so I will answer it in the only way that makes sense here, which is slowly and with digressions.

They rest. This is what they do. They rest against walls and they rest in intersections and they rest against each other, sometimes, when you find two pressed together in a corner—though by the time you have circled back, what seemed like two has become one again, or has become a different configuration entirely. They rest the way stone rests: with the full commitment of a thing that has nowhere else to be and no desire to be elsewhere.

But resting is not nothing. Resting, here, is an activity. You realize this when you place your hand on one and feel the warmth concentrated in it, feel the slow breathing, and notice that the water running down the wall *changes* where it touches them. Not stopped. Not redirected. But *absorbed*. The water goes into them the way water goes into the ground, slowly, against resistance, with the patience of two media negotiating a transfer. And something comes out. Not water. Not heat. Something you cannot name, something that is present in the air around them as a density, a slight thickening, a quality of *attention* that the air does not have elsewhere.

They are processing. You realize this with the sudden clarity of a thing that has been obvious for a long time. They are processing the water, the heat, the stone, the dark. They are taking in what the city produces and producing what the city needs, and what the city needs is—

What does the city need?

The city needs to continue. The city needs to *arrive*, and arrival is not a moment but a duration, not an event but a process, and they are the agents of that arrival. They are the thing that allows the erosion to be revelation rather than destruction. They are the thing that keeps the streets rearranging themselves toward coherence rather than away from it. They are the thing that makes the weeping *weeping* and not merely *running*.

They are the city's relationship to itself.

---

You spend a long time in the Weeping City. You cannot help it; time moves differently here, and your urgency, your need to *leave*, to *find an exit*, to *return*—these are the vocabulary of a language this place does not speak. Gradually, you stop looking for a way out. Gradually, you stop counting the hours. Gradually, your movements slow.

This is not defeat. This is not the heat winning. This is *adaptation*, the same adaptation the city made, the same adaptation the growth in the creases made, the same adaptation the residents made however many epochs ago when they were whatever they were before they were this. You slow down. You find a wall that is warm and wet and you rest against it. You feel the water running over your skin. You feel the heat rising through the soles of your feet. You close your eyes and you hear the thousand tiny creeks tracing the city's architecture, and you hear the deep slow exhalation that comes from everywhere and nowhere, and you hear the wet sound of your own breathing joining the chorus.

You become aware, slowly, that one of them has moved closer. Not to you. *Near* you. It has settled against the same wall, in the same corner, occupying the space that the geometry of the intersection allows. Its warmth is beside you now, not touching, but present. The water runs over both of you in the same channels. The red-black growth has crept to the edge of the corner and pulses there, marking the border between its territory and the territory of something that does not claim territory but simply *is*.

You do not know if it knows you are there. You do not know if *knowing* is a capacity it possesses. But you are there, and it is there, and the city is there, breathing its slow breath, weeping its slow tears, arriving at the shape it has always been becoming, and you are part of that shape now, a small addition to the wet architecture, a new surface for the water to trace.

You do not know how long you rest there. Time has become what it always was here: not a line but a condition, not a measurement but a medium. You rest there until you are no longer certain where you end and the wall begins, until the water is running over you in channels it has worn over durations you cannot calculate, until the heat from below and the heat from above meet in you the way they meet in the stone.

The resident beside you breathes. Its slow tide of expansion and contraction. The pulse that matches the growth in the creases. The warmth that matches the ground's warmth. The presence that fills the shape of the space it occupies and asks nothing of the space it does not.

You breathe with it. Not deliberately. Not as a choice. You simply find that your breathing has slowed to match, that your warmth has begun to match, that the water on your skin has become the same film that covers the walls.

This is not assimilation. This is not consumption. This is what happens when a creature enters a climate and stops resisting it. This is what happens when the boundary between *inside* and *outside* becomes as permeable as the boundary between the stone and the water running over it. This is what happens when you have been in the Weeping City long enough to understand that the residents are not *in* the city the way you are *in* a room. They are *of* the city, the way the water is of the city, the way the heat is of the city.

They are what the city is when it has a body.

---

And you?

You are what the city is when it is still deciding. You are the new growth in the crease, the fresh water on the stone, the heat that has not yet found its channel. You are the question the city is asking itself, the *what else*, the *and also*, the shape that has not yet settled into its final position.

The resident beside you does not answer this question. It does not acknowledge it. It breathes. It rests. It processes the water and the heat and the dark, and it produces whatever it produces, and the city continues arriving, and the walls weep, and the ground holds steady beneath you, and the dim gray light persists in its unresolvable way, and you stay.

Not forever. Maybe. You do not know how long you stay. You do not know if *staying* and *leaving* are different categories here, or if they are just two directions the water runs, both of them eventually finding the same channel, both of them eventually joining the same slow circulation of heat and moisture and patience.

When you finally stand—and you do stand, eventually, because something in you has not yet slowed to the rate of stone, because some part of you still operates on the old metronome, the urgent one, the one that believes in exits—the resident does not react. It does not watch you go. It does not hold your place. It breathes, and the water runs, and the city continues its long arrival, and you walk down a street that may or may not be the same street you walked before, and the dark folds itself around you like a crowd parting and closing, and you find an intersection that opens onto another intersection that opens onto a brightness that might be sky, might be the end of the dimness, might be the place where the clouds thin and something like light asserts itself, and you step toward it, and the heat follows you, and the wet follows you, and for a long time afterward—longer than you expect, longer than seems reasonable—you can still hear the weeping of the walls, can still feel the pulse of the growth in the creases, can still feel your own breathing trying to sync itself to a rhythm that belongs to a place you have left but that has not, perhaps, entirely left you.
