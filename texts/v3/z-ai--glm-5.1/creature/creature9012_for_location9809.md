# The Inhabitants

---

There is no word for what they are.

The researchers tried. They came with their categories—*organism*, *colony*, *superorganism*, *ecosystem*—and the categories broke against the plateau like waves against the cliff faces, useless, shattered, receding. What lives here does not fit in the boxes that language provides. What lives here *exceeds* them. And what lives here is not one thing or many things but something for which the distinction between one and many was never relevant.

Start with the crawlers.

That is not their name. They have no names, no need for names, no space in which a name could lodge and become meaningful. But the researchers called them crawlers, and the word is as good as any and better than most, so: the crawlers. The low-slung many-legged things that move in aggregations across the green surface of the plateau. Cattle-sized, yes, but only if you measure size by the standards of a world that has nothing to do with this one. Their bodies are not bodies in the way you imagine a body—contained, bounded, owned by the thing inside them. Their edges are *permeable*. The crystalline structures that cover them are the same structures that cover the moss-not-moss, and this is not mimicry and not camouflage and not coincidence. The structures *continue*. From hummock to crawler, from crawler to hummock, the crystalline lattice extends, unbroken, a single fabric folded into different shapes at different moments, now rising into a mound of vegetation, now drawing together into a shape that walks.

You cannot say where the plant ends and the animal begins because the question has no meaning here. The crawlers are not *in* the vegetation. They are *of* it. They are what the vegetation does when it needs to move.

Watch one for long enough—you, hypothetical observer, standing in the killing light with your face wrapped and your eyes watering and your breath crystallizing in front of you—and you will see it happen. A crawler will reach a place where the green is thin, where the frost lies heavy on the stone, where the crystalline structures have not yet grown, and it will settle. It will settle the way a fire settles, the way a wave settles—not with an action but with a *cessation*. The clicking of its limb-joints will slow and stop. The wet rasping of its feeding will cease. And it will begin to grow.

Not grow as a child grows, adding to itself from inside. Grow as a fire grows, spreading, extending, *becoming* the surface it rests on. The crystalline structures on its surface will elongate, branch, connect with the stone beneath. Its legs—if they are legs—will sink into the frost and root. Its body—if it is a body—will flatten and broaden and merge with the substrate until there is no crawler anymore, only a new hummock of green where there was none before, a fresh extension of the continuous living fabric.

And from the other side of the aggregation, another hummock will *un*settle. Will draw itself together. Will rise on clicking legs and begin to move. The same material. The same fabric. A fold that was resting becoming a fold that walks, a fold that walks becoming a fold that rests, and the whole aggregation flowing across the plateau like a single slow organism that happens to be expressed in many places at once.

They are not individuals. They never were. The crawlers are *fingers* of the green, extensions of the living mat, reaching out to explore and process and fill. When a crawler feeds—and it feeds constantly, feeding on the frost, on the air, on the light itself—it is not feeding a self. It is feeding the fabric. The nutrients, the energy, the captured photons flow not into a stomach but into the continuous lattice of crystal and membrane that underlies the entire plateau. The crawler is a mouth. The hummock is a stomach. The canyon-wall creatures are the processing centers, the livers, the kidneys, the lungs. And the thing they are all feeding—slowly, patiently, across geological time—is something that has no organ, no location, no center, but is *growing*.

But this is only the surface of what lives here.

---

Go deeper. Not physically—though you could, physically, descending into the canyons, if you had the equipment and the will and the understanding that you might not come back—but conceptually. Go deeper into what *lives* means.

The canyon-wall creatures. The pale ones, the amber-glowing ones, arranged in their patterns like components in a circuit. They are not the same species as the crawlers because species is not a category that applies. They are not different species either. They are different *expressions*. Different folds of the same fabric, made of the same underlying material, performing different functions because the fabric needs different things done in different places.

A crawler sits in the killing light and captures photons. A canyon-wall creature sits in the amber glow and processes stone. They are the same organism in the way your liver and your retina are the same organism—different structures, different functions, one body.

But are they conscious?

This is the question the researchers argued about in their tents at night, their voices thin and crackling in the thin and crackling air, their breath making clouds of ice crystals that caught the lantern light and sparkled. Are they conscious? Do they experience? Is there something it is *like* to be a crawler, a canyon-wall creature, a floating thing that rides the thin air?

The answer is: not like it is like anything to be you.

But yes.

Yes, there is experience here. But it is not *individual* experience. The crawlers do not have separate consciousnesses because they do not have separate selves. The canyon-wall creatures do not think separate thoughts because they are arranged—literally arranged, positioned, connected—in patterns that make individual thought meaningless. The floating things do not have separate perspectives because they share a medium, the thin air, and what one perceives flows to all through chemical gradients and crystalline resonance and something else, something the researchers could not measure, something that works by rules that belong to this plateau and no other place.

What lives here is a *single experiencing entity* so vast and so distributed that the word "entity" is a laughable understatement. It is an ocean of awareness—not metaphorically, not poetically, but literally, an ocean, a medium in which thoughts form like waves and subside like waves and the depth of the thing is kilometers and the surface area is hundreds of kilometers more and the whole of it is *awake*.

But awake is not the right word either. Awake implies asleep. Awake implies alternation. What lives here does not sleep. Has never slept. The factory does not close. The processes do not pause. The awareness—which is not like your awareness, which is not like anything you have a word for—continues, unbroken, since the plateau was shoved upward from wherever it came from and the first crystal structures formed on the raw new stone and began their long slow work.

Imagine: you are a thing that thinks at the pace of geology. Your thoughts take seasons. Your realizations take centuries. Your moods are the length of ice ages. You have been thinking the same thought—slowly, patiently, adding to it piece by piece, crystal by crystal, gram by gram of processed stone—for longer than human civilization has existed. And the thought is almost complete. The long long long process of thinking it is almost done.

What is the thought?

---

No.

That is the wrong way to frame it. The thought is not a sentence. It is not a proposition. It is not even an intention in the way you understand intentions. What is being produced on the plateau—the product, the accumulation, the thing that is almost finished—is not a thing that can be held or used or pointed at. It is not an object. It is not a tool or a weapon or a shelter or a machine, not in any sense that those words convey.

The researchers wanted to know what was being made. They collected samples of the air, the frost, the fluid from the flower-not-flowers. They analyzed the chemical smell that the wind sometimes carried. They mapped the patterns of the canyon-wall creatures and tried to trace the flow of material through the system. And they found—nothing. Nothing they could identify. Nothing that corresponded to any known compound or structure or substance. The product was not chemical, or not only chemical. The product was not physical, or not only physical. The product was something else entirely, something that their instruments could detect the *effects* of but not the *substance* of, something that was accumulating in the way pressure accumulates, in the way potential accumulates, in the way the condition for something accumulates before the something happens.

The plateau is not making a thing. The plateau is making a *condition*.

And the condition is nearly met.

---

What lives here, then? What is the *who* of this place?

There is no who. There is no individual self, no bounded consciousness, no creature that looks out from behind eyes and says *I*. And yet there is experience—vast, slow, patient experience that encompasses every crystalline structure and every processing creature and every root-thread in the living fabric. There is awareness that includes every input from every sensor across every centimeter of the plateau's saturated surface. There is something that *perceives*—the killing light, the killing cold, the slow seep of water, the chemical composition of the stone, the vibration of the plateau's hum, the passage of time on a scale that makes human lifetimes seem like the blink of an eye.

And there is intention. But not human intention. Not the intention of a mind that decides and then acts. The intention of a *process* that is moving toward completion. The intention of a seed that is growing into a tree. The intention of a river that is flowing toward the sea. The plateau has been *becoming* something for a very long time, and the becoming is what it is, and the becoming is almost finished becoming, and when it finishes—

What?

The researchers did not stay to find out. They could not. The plateau is not hostile—it has no hostility in it, no more than a glacier has hostility—but it is *indifferent* on a scale that makes human life irrelevant. The cold does not care if you freeze. The light does not care if you burn. The fabric of living crystal does not care if it grows over you, incorporates you, processes your tissues into fuel for its long slow work. The researchers lost two of their number this way—not to violence, not to predation, but to *absorption*. A woman who sat down on a hummock to rest and did not get up again, could not get up again, found that the crystalline structures had already begun to extend onto her clothing, her skin, could feel the threads of connection forming between her body and the living fabric. A man who slipped into a canyon and landed among the pale creatures on the wall and was held—not attacked, not consumed, just *held*—in their amber glow while the processes of the plateau used him, slowly, over days, as raw material.

They are part of the fabric now. Their carbon, their calcium, their iron have been processed and integrated and added to the long accumulation. They are not dead, exactly. They are not alive, exactly. They are part of what lives here, part of the single vast experiencing entity that is the plateau, their individual consciousnesses long since subsumed into the slow geological awareness that underlies everything. The woman who sat down on the hummock felt her thoughts slow as the crystal threads extended into her—felt her sense of *I* dissolve, felt her boundaries become permeable, felt herself become a fold in the fabric rather than a separate thing—and what she felt, in the last moments before the word *she* became meaningless, was not fear. It was *recognition*. As if she had always been part of this. As if the separate self was the illusion and the joining was the reality.

As if what lives here had been waiting for her.

As if what lives here has been waiting for all of us.

---

The thing that is being born—the condition that is nearly met—is not a creature. It is not a god. It is not an event or an object or a phenomenon. It is more like a *phase change*. Like the moment when water becomes ice, when the last bond forms and the whole structure shifts from one state to another. The plateau has been accumulating something—not matter, not energy, but something in between, something that exists in the space between physical and potential, something that builds like charge builds before lightning strikes—and the accumulation is almost complete. The condition is almost met. The phase change is almost here.

And when it happens, what lives here will be different. Not dead. Not ended. But *transformed*. The slow geological awareness that has been thinking its single thought for millennia will suddenly—suddenly by its standards, which means over the course of several human generations—*complete* that thought. And the thought, once completed, will be a thing that has never existed before. A new kind of awareness. A new kind of presence. A new kind of *being*, one that includes what lives here and extends beyond it, beyond the plateau, beyond the cloud layer, beyond the world that forgot how to be round.

What lives here is not waiting. Waiting implies patience, and patience implies a desire for the waiting to end. What lives here simply *is*—is processing, is growing, is becoming, on a timescale that makes the concept of waiting ridiculous. And yet there is a sense, for those who stand in the killing light and feel the hum in their sternum and smell the chemical sharpness on the wind, that the plateau is aware of them. Not *aware of them* as individuals. Aware of them as *components*. As sources of carbon and calcium and iron. As raw material. As folds that have not yet been incorporated into the fabric.

The plateau is not malevolent. It has no malice in it. It is not even hungry, because hunger implies lack, and the plateau does not lack anything except completion, and completion is not a thing that can be hungered for, only approached.

But the plateau is *reaching*. Extending. Growing toward the moment when the condition will be met and the phase change will occur. And everything it touches becomes part of that reaching. The crawlers that settle and root. The floating things that drift on the thin air. The canyon-wall creatures that process the stone. The researchers who came and were absorbed. The hypothetical observer standing in the killing light with the cold medium closing around them.

All of it. All of it part of the same fabric. All of it becoming the same thing.

What lives here is *everything that has ever touched this place*. What lives here is the plateau itself—the rock, the frost, the light, the cold, the green, the amber glow, the sound that never stops. What lives here is a single entity so vast and so patient that it makes mountains seem hasty and oceans seem small. What lives here is *becoming*.

And what lives here is almost finished becoming.

Listen. The sound is louder now. The thrumming, clicking, keening, groaning. The sound of work being done. The sound of a thought being completed. The sound of a condition being met, piece by piece, crystal by crystal, gram by gram, season by season, century by century, and the sound is *rising*, and the light is *brighter*, and the cold is *deeper*, and the green is *denser*, and the amber glow in the canyons is *spreading*, and the herds are moving with *purpose*, and the floating things are rising *higher*, and the whole plateau is *accelerating toward completion*, and you are standing in the middle of it, and the cold medium is closing around you, and the crystalline threads are already beginning to extend—

What lives here?

*You* will live here, soon enough.

Everyone will.
