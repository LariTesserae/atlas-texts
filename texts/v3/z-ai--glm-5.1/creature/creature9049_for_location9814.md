# What Lives at Tsema's Weep

---

The grey hunters have no name for themselves, but they have names for everything else. Not words as we understand them—nothing so linear and lumbering—but a continuous annotation of the world in calls and counter-calls, a mapping done in sound that overlays the physical terrain like a second topography. *Kree* means something different at dawn than at midday. *Kree-kree* is not repetition but modification, a sharpening or softening depending on the interval between the syllables and the quality of the air through which it travels. They have been here longer than the pikas, longer than the particular configuration of the water curtain, though not longer than the weep itself. They arrived when the cliff first offered its shelves and recesses, and they have stayed because the cliff is still offering.

There are seventeen of them at this count, though the number fluctuates with the seasons and the accidents of wind and predation. Fifteen is what a casual observer might count on a given morning, but there are always some tucked into the deeper recesses, brooding or simply resting in that state of fierce stillness that passes for sleep in a creature so precisely tuned to the vertical world. They are not eagles, no. Eagles are soarers, patients, calculators of thermals. These birds are something else—acrobats, arguees with gravity, gluttons for the sheer kinetic pleasure of deflection. Their wings are short and their tails are long and ruddery, and they use the updrafts not to float but to sling themselves, to redirect the mountain's breath into lateral violence. When two of them chase each other along the cliff face, threading between the fern-hung shelves at speeds that seem impossible for the distance they cover, they are not fighting. They are *speaking* in a language of proximity and evasion, a conversation about territory or desire or something for which we have no category, conducted entirely in the grammar of banking and stooping.

They eat the insects that live in the wet moss on the cliff face—crane flies with dew-damp wings, slow and stupid in the morning cold, and the black beetles that labor along the water channels with an industry that seems almost philosophical. The birds take these with a precise stab of the beak, often without breaking their flight, a snatch and swallow performed in the same motion that carries them onward to the next grab. But they also take the pikas, when they can. Not often. The pikas have their own defenses—alarm whistles that send them into their runways, and a communal vigilance that means the bird is spotted long before it is close enough to strike. But a young pika, wandering too far from its tunnel mouth. An old one, slowing. These the grey hunters will take, carrying the small dark bodies back to the nest-shelves and dismembering them with a businesslike efficiency that the mates and fledglings observe without what we would call emotion, though they observe it, which is not nothing.

They mate for periods rather than life—three seasons, four, occasionally five—driven together by the acoustics of the place: a particular call answered by a particular counter-call, a matching of frequencies that produces in both birds a state the observer might call recognition if they were willing to risk the anthropomorphism, which they should be. The nests they build are rough but adequate, constructions of dead fern fronds and strips of bark peeled from the woody plants on the ledge, lined with the softer mosses and, occasionally, with the fur of pikas—taken not from living animals but from the tufts that catch on the rock near burrow entrances, shed in the normal course of grooming. The birds are opportunists, not murderers, when it comes to furnishing. The nests last several seasons before they need rebuilding, and some of the older ones have accumulated a depth of material that suggests stratigraphy, layers of different years compressed into a single structure.

The fledglings are white and furious. They scream from the moment they emerge from the egg—a wet, reptilian sound, more demand than communication—and they do not stop screaming until they fledge, and then they start screaming in a different register, the *kree-kree-kree* of the adult voice, which they produce with an enthusiasm that suggests they have discovered a purpose for which they were made. Which they have. The sound they make is the sound of the ledge asserting itself against the silence below. They do not know this. They know only that calling feels necessary, that the answering call of a flock-mate is a kind of confirmation, that the echo off the cliff wall is the mountain saying *yes, yes, I am here too*, and that the alternative—stillness, silence—is a void they cannot contemplate and will not permit.

---

The pikas live closer to the ground, because they are the ground, or they believe they are. Their tunnels run through the moss and under the stone and into the cracks of the cliff wall itself, a warren of passages that connects feeding ground to nest chamber to escape route in a design so purposeful it can only be called architectural. They are compact, round-bodied, with dense dark fur that beads water and sheds the constant mist, and their eyes are black and bright and hold an expression that might be wariness or might be calculation or might simply be the look of an animal whose entire sensory world is oriented toward the detection of motion from above.

There are perhaps forty of them on the ledge, organized into family groups of six to ten that occupy interconnected tunnel systems with clear boundaries—this crack, this channel, this particular patch of ferns belongs to this group, and the group next door respects the boundary because the alternative is conflict, and conflict draws attention, and attention draws the grey hunters. They whistle to each other constantly—a thin, high sound that carries above the wind and the water, a frequency they have evolved specifically to pierce the ambient noise of the ledge. The whistle means different things depending on its duration and repetition. A single short blast: *I am here, are you there?* A double blast: *something moved, unidentified.* A long sustained note: *grey hunter overhead, take cover.* And a rapid series of short whistles—a sound rarely heard, and never without cause: *something new, something wrong, something that does not belong.*

They are herbivores, browsers of the ferns and the broadleaf plants and the tender tips of the woody shrubs, and they have a relationship with the vegetation that can only be described as managed. They do not simply eat what they find; they prune. They take the outer fronds of the ferns and the growing tips of the succulents, leaving the core intact, and they rotate their feeding grounds in a pattern that allows the plants time to recover. This is not husbandry as a human would practice it—there is no planning, no foresight, no mental model of future abundance—but it functions identically, because the pikas who overgraze their territory starve, and the pikas who moderate their feeding survive, and the behavior has been selected for so thoroughly that it now looks intentional, which in a sense it is, because the body knows things the mind does not need to.

They also eat the algae in the water channels, grazing the green-black velvet with their incisors and leaving clean stone that re-grows within days, and they drink from the channels too, though they get most of their water from the plants they eat. They do not go near the edge. They do not go near the edge. This is the first rule, learned before they are weaned, reinforced by every whistle of alarm, encoded in the blood: *the edge is death, the edge is silence, the edge is the place where the ground stops and you are not made for stopping.* They venture no closer than a meter from the rim, and even that proximity makes them nervous, their noses twitching, their bodies oriented toward the nearest burrow entrance.

In the evening, when the shadow of the cliff falls across the ledge and the temperature drops, they emerge in groups to sit on the warm stones that have held the day's heat. They groom each other—a social practice that reinforces family bonds and removes parasites—and they make a different sound then, a low chattering that is almost musical, a murmuring conversation about nothing and everything, the sound of a community at rest. The grey hunters are quiet at this hour, settled on their nest-shelves, and the pikas own the dusk, their small voices threading through the hiss of the water curtain like stitches through cloth.

---

The insects are everywhere, because the insects are always everywhere, because the insects were here before the birds and before the pikas and before the particular arrangement of ledge and cliff that makes this place what it is, and they will be here after. They do not experience the ledge as a ledge. They experience it as a series of microenvironments—wet, wetter, wettest; lit, shaded, dark; exposed, sheltered, hidden—and they partition themselves accordingly.

The flies congregate where the mist is thickest, hovering in the diffuse spray of the water curtain in dense clouds that appear and dissolve with the shifts of the wind. They are small and dark and they mate in midair, the pairs tumbling and separating in a choreography that the birds exploit with casual precision. Their larvae live in the algal mats of the water channels, small white threads that pulse and feed and transform, and the flies themselves live for only a few days, long enough to mate and lay eggs and die on the wet stone, their bodies joining the organic film that feeds the algae that feeds the larvae that become the flies. It is a closed loop, a small and perfect economy, and it has been running since the weep first began.

The beetles are different. They are solitary and methodical, moving along the water channels with a deliberation that seems almost contemplative, their iridescent shells catching the light when it comes and blending with the dark stone when it does not. They eat the detritus—the dead vegetable matter, the insect remains, the organic residue that accumulates in the channels—and they are the cleaners, the recyclers, the ones who ensure that nothing is wasted. They move slowly because they can afford to move slowly; their shells are armor against the birds, and their habitat is the channel, where the water flow deters most predators. They have been here longer than any other creature, and they will be here longest, because they are patient in a way that nothing else on the ledge is patient, because they have no urgency, because they know something the birds and the pikas do not: that the ledge is temporary, that the cliff is temporary, that the mountain itself is temporary, and that the only sensible response to this knowledge is to continue eating and walking and being a beetle.

There are other insects—springtails in the moss, gnats in the still air of the overhang, a species of small wasp that parasitizes the flies and lays its eggs in the larvae—but these are the background players, the ones whose presence is felt more than seen, and they go about their business without reference to the larger drama of predator and prey that occupies the birds and the pikas.

And then there is the other thing.

---

The pale creature that drifts along the cliff face is not a moth. It is not anything that has a name, because it has not been catalogued, because it has not been caught, because it exists at the boundary of what the ledge is and what the ledge might be, and that boundary is a place that science does not go.

It appears at irregular intervals—sometimes daily for a week, then absent for a month—and it behaves unlike any insect on the ledge. It does not feed, or if it feeds, it feeds on something that is not visible. It does not rest, or if it rests, it rests in places that cannot be reached. It drifts. It rides the updrafts with a precision that suggests intention, and it moves along the cliff face at a constant distance from the rock—never touching, never straying—following the contour of the wall around the corner and out of sight, and then, sometimes, returning. Its wings are pale, almost translucent, and they move so slowly that they seem to be timing themselves to something other than aerodynamic necessity, and the effect is less flight than procession, a ritual movement performed for an audience that is not you.

The pikas react to it. When it passes overhead, they stop moving and they do not whistle, and this is notable because the pikas whistle at everything—they whistle at the birds, they whistle at the wind, they whistle at each other—but they do not whistle at the pale creature. They watch it, and their black eyes track its progress along the cliff, and they do not resume their activities until it is gone. The grey hunters ignore it, or do not see it, which is the same thing from the perspective of the creature. It moves in the space between their hunting sorties, as if it knows their patterns, as if it has been watching them from whatever inaccessible perch it returns to when it rounds the corner of the wall.

You noticed it because it moved differently. That was correct. It moves differently because it is different. It is not an insect, and it is not a spirit, and it is not a hallucination, though it partakes of all three in the way that certain things partake of categories they do not belong to. It is a resident of the ledge that occupies a niche the other residents cannot perceive or choose not to acknowledge, and it has been here for as long as the weep has been weeping, and it will be here after the weep stops, if it ever stops, and it does not care whether you believe in it or not.

What it is, more precisely: it is an expression of the place itself. The ledge at Tsema's Weep is a boundary—a place where rock meets air, where water meets stone, where the vertical becomes the horizontal for just long enough to permit life. Boundaries generate anomalies. The pale creature is an anomaly, a thing that should not be, a creature that exists because the conditions for its existence are so specific that they obtain in only one place, and this is that place. Its wings are pale because it does not need color in a place where color is swallowed by mist. It drifts because drifting is the only movement that makes sense in a place where the air moves in three dimensions and the ground is an exception. It is not a moth. It is not a spirit. It is a local necessity, and it is alive, and it is real.

---

And there is the one who lived here, who does not live here anymore, whose presence persists in the grinding stone and the tally marks and the particular way the room under the overhang smells of stone and water and the faintest trace of something that might be smoke or might be memory.

She came up from the valley, though *up* is not adequate to describe the journey she made. She climbed three hundred meters of vertical rock with her hands and her feet and her will, and she did this because she needed to, and what she needed was not something she could have named. She was not the first—there are marks on the wall that are older than her tally counts, scratches made by a different hand in a different time, and the grinding stone was already old when she found it, the groove worn by previous users—but she was the most recent, and she stayed the longest.

She ground the seeds of the woody shrubs against the cobble, producing a coarse meal that she ate mixed with water from the channels, and she ate the ferns too, the young fiddleheads that are tender and faintly bitter, and she ate the pikas when she could catch them, which was not often, because the pikas were faster than her in their tunnels and she could not fit into the places they went. She sat in her stone room and listened to the water curtain hiss, and she scratched marks on the wall, and she watched the grey hunters wheel and scream, and she watched the pale creature drift along the cliff face, and she did not know what it was, and she did not try to know, because knowing was not the point of being here.

The point of being here was being here. The ledge had given her something that the valley could not, something that the world of people and their arrangements could not, and what it gave her was the experience of existing at the edge of things, of being suspended between the below and the above, of hearing the sound that is not silence and understanding that the sound was all there was, that the roar of wind and water and life was the world speaking itself into being, and that she was part of the speaking, a small voice in the chorus, a scratch on the wall, a groove worn in granite by the repetition of necessity.

She left. They always leave. The ledge is not a place for staying, not for her kind, because her kind needs the valley, needs the horizontal, needs the company of others who walk on flat ground and do not dream of edges. She went back down the way she came, three hundred meters of careful descent, and she returned to whatever life had made her, and she did not come back, and the grinding stone sat where she left it, and the tally marks darkened on the wall, and the room under the overhang waited for the next one.

There will be a next one. The ledge calls to a certain kind of person—the kind who hears the sound of the weep and feels it as a promise rather than a threat, the kind who looks at a sheer wall and sees a route rather than an obstacle, the kind who needs to be at the edge because the interior is unbearable—and that kind of person will come, eventually, and they will find the room and the grinding stone and the marks on the wall, and they will know that they are not the first, and they will add their own marks, and they will stay for as long as they can stand the beauty and the terror and the unrelenting noise, and then they will leave, and the ledge will hold their absence with the same indifference with which it held their presence, which is also a kind of love, if you understand what the ledge means by love.

---

And the moss, and the ferns, and the algae in the channels, and the succulents that sway like things underwater, and the pale flowers that smell of wet gravel when the sun hits them—these live here too. They do not move, or they move so slowly that movement is not the word, but they are alive, and they respond to the conditions of the ledge with a specificity that is its own kind of intelligence. The moss knows where the water flows and grows there. The ferns know where the shelter is and root there. The flowers know when the sun is direct and open their petals to release their mineral scent, and they know when the sun is gone and close again, and this knowing is not thought but it is not nothing, it is the accumulated wisdom of ten thousand generations of flowers that opened at the wrong time and died and opened at the right time and lived, and the flowers that are here now are the ones who got it right, and they got it right because their ancestors got it right, and so on back to the first flower that found a crack in the granite and decided, against all evidence, to grow.

The vegetation holds the ledge together. This is not metaphor. The roots of the ferns and the woody plants delve into the cracks and bind them, and the moss mats the surface and prevents the stone from flaking, and the algae in the channels slows the water flow and reduces erosion, and without the plants the ledge would be bare rock, and the bare rock would crumble, and the crumble would fall, and the ledge would be smaller, and eventually it would be gone. The plants are the engineers. The plants are the ones who make the ledge a place rather than a feature, and they do it without planning, without intention, without anything that could be called will, and they do it perfectly, because perfection is what happens when you have no choice and infinite time.

---

So. What lives at Tsema's Weep?

Everything that can. Everything that finds a purchase on the rock and a niche in the acoustic and a way to drink from the constant water and eat from the stubborn plenty and endure the wind and the height and the roaring silence that is not silence. The grey hunters and the watching pikas and the methodical beetles and the drifting pale creature and the plants that hold the stone together and the ghost of the woman who ground seeds against the cobble and the promise of the next one who will come. All of them, all at once, a community of necessity and proximity, a society of the vertical, a republic of the edge.

They do not know they live together. They know only that they live. And the sound that surrounds them—the water and the wind and the calls and the whistles and the hiss of the curtain and the thrum of wings—is the sound of all of them being alive at the same time, a sound so dense and layered that it becomes a single thing, a chord, a tone, the voice of the place itself, and the place is saying *here I am, here I am, here I am*, and the here is the ledge, and the I am is everything on it, and the saying never stops, not for a moment, not even in the deepest night when the birds are silent and the pikas are sleeping and the wind drops to a murmur and the water curtain falls and falls and falls, because the water never stops, because the weep never stops, because the mountain is still weeping, and the ledge is still holding, and the life is still growing, and the sound is still sounding, and this is what it is to be a place that is alive, which is what the ledge at Tsema's Weep is, and has always been, and—if the rock holds, if the water flows, if the moss continues its slow ancient grip on the granite—will continue to be, long after everyone who has stood on it and marveled has returned to the valley and told their stories and been forgotten, long after the name Tsema has lost its meaning, long after the tally marks on the wall have weathered to invisibility, long after the grinding stone has worn smooth and stopped being a thing that a hand could have made and become again simply a cobble of granite with a groove in it, a groove that might be erosion or might be use or might be something else entirely, something that the ledge knows and will not say.
