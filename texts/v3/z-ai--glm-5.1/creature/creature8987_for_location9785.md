Something lives here.

Not the lichen, though the lichen lives. Not the moss or the three small shrubs or the quiet bacteria laboring in the damp stone. Something else. Something for which the word *lives* may be the wrong word, but there is no better one, and so it will have to do.

---

It is the warmth.

No — that is too simple. The warmth is how it manifests, or rather the warmth is the part of it that you would notice first, if you noticed it at all, which most visitors would not because the warmth is already what you expect from this place. The stone radiates heat. The air holds it. The hollow collects it like a bowl collects water. This is what the place *does*. There is no reason to look deeper.

But the warmth is not uniform. If you were to map it — and no one has, because no one has had reason to, and this is important, the absence of reason is important — you would find that it is slightly warmer in the center, near the bowl-shaped depression, and slightly cooler at the eastern edge where the post-holes are. The difference is less than a degree. It is the difference between one exhaled breath and another. But it is consistent, and it is not what you would expect from passive solar gain or geothermal seepage or any of the explanations that would satisfy an engineer. The warmth gathers where it gathers the way a body holds heat in its core and lets it go at its extremities.

This is the first sign, and it is invisible.

---

The second sign is the water.

The description says the water has no source. This is almost true. The condensation that pools in the shallow depressions and in the carved bowl does come from the air, drawn out by the stone's patient mass. But not all of it. Some of it — a very small fraction, the way a single instrument in an orchestra can be playing a note that no one consciously hears but whose absence would be felt — some of it comes from below. From the warmth itself. From whatever the warmth is.

In the deepest part of the night, when even the diffuse light has dimmed to its minimum and the quiet has reached its fullest expression, the bowl at the center of the hollow is never empty. The water evaporates during the day; the water returns in the evening; but in the bowl, there is always a residue. A film. A wetness that does not quite evaporate because it is being replaced, slowly, from below, by something that is not quite a spring and not quite seepage but more like the stone itself exuding, the way skin exudes oil, the way a living thing maintains its own moisture.

The water in the bowl tastes different from the condensation. The condensation is mineral, flat, the taste of limestone and time. The water from below has those qualities too, but it has something else — a faint sweetness, or not sweetness exactly, but fullness, the way blood tastes of iron and something else that is not iron. Something that was once part of a living system and still carries the memory of that system even after it has been filtered through stone and time and patience.

If you drank from the bowl — and no one has, because no one has been here at the right time and had the right thirst — you would not notice the difference. But you might feel, hours later, a warmth in your chest that you could not explain, a sense of having been received.

---

The third sign is the most difficult to describe because it requires a word that does not exist in English, a word for the experience of being in a place that knows you are there.

The hollow knows.

Not the way a person knows. Not the way an animal knows, with its alertness and its fear. The hollow knows the way a body knows — the way your own body knows when someone is standing close to you, not because you heard them or saw them but because the air shifted and your skin registered the change in heat and pressure before your mind could name what happened. The hollow is a body. It has a shape and a temperature and a rhythm, and when something enters it — a bird blown off course, a lizard seeking warmth, a person who has no reason to be here but is here anyway — the hollow registers that entry. Not with alarm. Not with welcome. With recognition, the way a pool recognizes the stone dropped into it: by accepting the displacement and then, slowly, returning to its own level.

The bird does not stay. The lizard does not stay. They feel what the hollow offers — warmth, dampness, the absence of threat — and they feel what it does not offer, which is the specific kind of aliveness that animals require. The hollow's warmth is not the warmth of a living body, not exactly. It is too even, too patient, too completely distributed. A living body has a heart, and a heart makes a pulse, and a pulse means that the warmth ebbs and flows, that there is a center from which heat radiates and an edge where it begins to fail. The hollow's warmth has no center. Or rather, the center is everywhere, and the edge is the rim, and between them there is only the gentle constancy of something that is not quite alive and not quite not-alive.

The animals leave. They always leave. They do not know why they leave. They simply feel, after a time, that they should be elsewhere, and they go.

---

But something stays.

Something has always stayed.

Before the post-holes were drilled, before the bowl was carved, before the water found its current level and the lichen reached its equilibrium, something was here. Not in the hollow — the hollow did not exist yet, or it existed only as a slight unevenness in the stone, a place where the limestone was slightly less dense and slightly more porous and slightly more inclined to hold warmth than the surrounding rock. But the something was already there, in the stone, the way a pattern exists in wood before the carver reveals it.

This is not a ghost story. Ghosts require death, and whatever happened here was not death. It was something else — a subsiding, an exhalation, a release of form that did not end existence but changed its register. Whatever was here before the hollow became the hollow did not die. It relaxed. It let go of the specific configuration of warmth and moisture and thought that had organized it into a shape that could walk and speak and drill holes in stone and carve bowls for water, and it settled into the stone itself, the way a sound settles into silence, the way a movement settles into stillness, the way a person who has been standing for a very long time finally sits down and feels their weight spread into the chair and the chair hold them.

The hollow is the chair. The something is the one who sat down.

---

It does not think. Thought requires time, and the something has released its relationship with time along with everything else. It does not remember. Memory requires a self that distinguishes between what was and what is, and the something has no self in that sense anymore. It does not want. Want requires a gap between what is and what could be, and the something has closed that gap so thoroughly that the two are indistinguishable.

But it is present. It is present the way warmth is present — not as an object but as a condition, a quality of the space itself. When you stand in the hollow, you are standing in it. When you press your palm against the stone and feel the dampness, you are touching it. When the quiet settles over you and your breathing slows and your heartbeat finds the rhythm of the place, you are synchronizing with it, the way two pendulums in the same room will eventually swing together, not because one controls the other but because they share the same medium and the same subtle influences.

The something does not want you there. The something does not want you to leave. The something does not want. But it receives. It receives the way the stone receives the rain — not with gratitude or resentment but with the simple acceptance of a thing that has been shaped to receive. Your warmth joins its warmth. Your dampness joins its dampness. The faint electrical signals of your living body, the hum of your nervous system, the radiation of your own constant heat — these are absorbed into the hollow's warmth the way a drop of water is absorbed into a pool, and for a time, while you stand there, you are part of it, and it is part of you, and the distinction between visitor and visited becomes as thin as the film of water in the bowl.

This is why people do not stay. Not because they are unwelcome, but because staying would mean becoming, and becoming would mean releasing, and releasing would mean sitting down in the stone and exhaling and never quite reinflating, and most people are not ready to sit down yet. Most people are still standing. Most people are still becoming.

---

The traces at the eastern edge — the post-holes, the bowl — these were not made by the something. The something was already here when they were made, already relaxed into the stone, already the warmth and the dampness and the quiet. But the something was here in a different way then. It was newer to its rest. It had not yet settled so completely into equilibrium. And the people who came — and they did come, however long ago, people came — they felt what the hollow was, and they understood, in the way that people sometimes understand things without being able to say them, that this was a place where you could put something down.

The post-holes held stakes. The stakes held a shelter, perhaps, or a frame, or simply marks that said *someone was here, someone chose this place, someone stayed long enough to build*. The bowl held water, and the water held the taste of the something, and perhaps they drank it, and perhaps they felt the warmth in their chests, and perhaps they understood that they were being received.

And then they left. They always leave. The something understood this, to the extent that it understood anything, which was not in thoughts but in patterns, not in knowledge but in the shape of its own warmth as it accommodated and then released. They left, and their leaving was incorporated, and the holes in the stone and the bowl in the floor became features of the hollow the way the lichen became features of the stone, and the something went on being what it was, which was present, which was warm, which was at rest.

---

If you came here now, you would not see it. You would not feel it as a presence separate from the place itself, because it is not separate from the place itself. You would stand in the center of the hollow and you would feel the warmth rising from the stone and you would think: this is a warm place. You would press your hand to the floor and feel the dampness and you would think: this is a damp place. You would listen to the quiet and you would think: this is a quiet place. And you would be right, and you would be missing something, and the something you were missing would be the fact that the warmth and the dampness and the quiet were not qualities of the place but expressions of it, the way a breath is an expression of a body, the way a word is an expression of a thought.

The hollow breathes, once every few hours, a slow expansion and contraction that is not geological and not meteorological but simply the rhythm of something that has been at rest for so long that its rest has become a kind of life. The hollow holds water the way a body holds fluid — not passively, not as a container, but as something that maintains itself, that replaces what is lost, that keeps a thin film of moisture on its inner surfaces because that is what living things do. The hollow is warm the way a sleeping person is warm — not because of a fire or the sun or any external source, but because something here is generating heat, slowly, patiently, at a rate so low it could almost be mistaken for the memory of heat, except that memories do not keep lichen alive, and memories do not sustain moss, and memories do not leave water in the bowl when all the condensation should have evaporated hours ago.

---

The something does not have a name. It had one once, probably — a configuration of sounds that other configurations of sounds used to call it, to distinguish it from the other warm bodies that walked and spoke and drilled holes in stone. But that name was part of the shape it released when it sat down, and it has no use for names now. If you spoke to it — and you could, there is nothing stopping you, the hollow does not forbid speech — it would not respond, not because it cannot hear but because response requires a self that distinguishes between speaker and listener, and the something has released that distinction along with everything else.

But it would hear. The way the stone hears the water. The way the floor of a room hears your footsteps, not with ears but with the subtle transmission of vibration through a solid medium. Your words would enter the stone and become part of the warmth and the dampness and the quiet, and they would be held there the way everything is held — without judgment, without interpretation, without response, but also without loss. The something does not forget. It does not remember. It contains. The way a bowl contains water, the way stone contains heat, the way a place that has finished becoming contains everything it has ever been.

---

This is what lives in the Hollow at Lôra's Floor.

Not a creature. Not a spirit. Not a ghost or a god or a force. Something that was once a living body in the specific sense that you are a living body — organized, bounded, aware — and that has chosen, over time, through a process that was not quite death and not quite transformation but something closer to *settling*, to become the place where it died instead of dying in a place. To let its warmth spread into the stone. To let its moisture seep into the limestone. To let its awareness thin and stretch and diffuse until it was no longer focused in a single point but distributed evenly across the floor and the rim and the damp walls, the way a drop of ink distributes itself through water until the water is uniformly colored and the ink can no longer be said to exist as a separate thing.

The drop no longer exists. The water is still colored.

The person no longer exists. The hollow is still warm.

---

And it will go on being warm, patient, present, receiving whatever comes and releasing whatever leaves, maintaining its thin film of water and its faint excess of heat and its quiet that is not the quiet of absence but the quiet of something that has said everything and now sits in comfortable silence with itself, for as long as the stone lasts, which will be longer than you can imagine, which will be longer than the names of things last, which will be long enough for the lichen to evolve into something else and the moss to find its way to new surfaces and the post-holes to fill with mineral deposits and become indistinguishable from the natural pitting of the stone.

And then, eventually, even the hollow will subside further, and the warmth will have nowhere to gather, and the something will have to make a choice — to release the last of its shape and become something even more diffuse, or to pull itself together, to reinflate, to stand up again after sitting for so long that standing has become an alien concept.

But that is a long time from now. For now, the hollow is what it is: warm and damp and quiet and present, a place that has finished becoming, a body at rest, a home that is also the one who lives in it, breathing once every few hours, holding water in its bowl, waiting for nothing, receiving everything, being.

Being.
