# The Inhabitants

---

## The Builders

Start with the body. Your thumb is the measure, and it is approximately right, but it misleads in the way all size comparisons mislead — a whale measured in buses tells you nothing of how a whale moves. So: the body.

The builder is roughly cylindrical, slightly tapered at the anterior end, where the mouthparts are. It has twelve segments, each bearing a pair of legs, but *legs* is a word for creatures that walk on surfaces, and the builder does not walk so much as grip-and-pull through the substance of its own construction. The legs end in pads, not claws — pads that grip the secretion while it is still soft, that find purchase in the microscopic texture of cured walls, that allow the builder to move upside-down along ceilings or vertically through shafts without pause. When a builder moves, it moves like a train on a track: committed, directional, unable to deviate from the surface it travels.

The color is pale cream in youth, darkening to amber in age, because the builder's own exoskeleton is made of the same material it secretes. It is not that the builder produces the building material so much as it *is* the building material, continuously generating and shedding and regenerating its shell in a slow, lifelong molt. The walls of the Continuously Built are not built from foreign substance. They are built from the builders' own bodies, externalized, cross-linked, hardened. Each chamber wall is a skin someone grew and then grew beyond.

This is why the builders taste wrong to predators. You would not eat a house made of your own walls, either. The secretion is mildly toxic in its raw form — not dangerous, but bitter, alkaloid-laden, a chemical signature that says *I am part of the architecture* to anything that might consider eating it. The predators learned this lesson long ago. The vermin never learned it, because the vermin are not bright enough to learn, which is why the predators are necessary.

The builder has no eyes. It has never needed them. The interior of the Built is lightless — not dim, not shaded, but *lightless*, absolutely, the way deep caves are lightless, the way the inside of a body is lightless. The builder navigates by chemistry. Every surface in the Built carries a molecular signature: this passage leads to the fungal gardens, this wall is structural, this chamber is mine, this chamber is yours, this is where the eggs are, this is where the dead are taken, this is the way out, this is the way in. The builder reads these signatures with its antennae — two, short, dense with chemoreceptors — and the reading is instantaneous and total. A builder placed on a wall it has never visited will still know where it is, because the chemical language is consistent across the entire continent. The builders did not agree on this language. There was no agreement. There is only the language, the way there is only gravity.

Each builder works. The word *works* is chosen carefully. This is not instinct in the reductive sense — not the mindless repetition of a fixed action pattern. The builder assesses. It tests the wall it stands on with its mandibles, checking the cure rate, the thickness, the structural integrity. It adds secretion where the wall is thin. It scrapes away secretion where the wall has cured unevenly and might impede airflow. It moves to where it is needed, guided by chemical signals that originate not from any central authority but from the walls themselves, which carry the memory of what needs doing the way a scar carries the memory of a wound.

A builder lives six years. In that time, it will secrete enough material to extend a wall by perhaps half a meter, or build three chambers, or reinforce forty junctions. The math is staggering. A major structure — one of the mounds — might contain a million chambers. That is three hundred thousand builder-lifetimes of work, and the mound is still growing, has been growing for thousands of years, and the builders who started it are so long dead that their bodies have been subsumed into the walls themselves, absorbed back into the structure, their amber shells just another layer in the stratigraphy.

The builders do not know this. They cannot know it. There is no organ in their body capable of understanding the scale of what they are part of. But they do not need to understand. They need only to build, and to read the walls, and to respond to what the walls tell them, and to secrete, and to mate, and to die in a chamber that will be sealed over and built upon and become part of the architecture itself.

They are not unhappy. That is a human word and a human concept, but it applies here in its absence. The builder does not suffer from its smallness. The builder does not feel trapped. The builder lives in a world that is entirely legible to it, where every surface speaks, where every task is clear, where the only expectation is the work, and the work is endless, and the work is the point.

## The Predators

Call them the Patient Ones, because patience is their defining quality.

They are low-slung, as described, but this description fails to capture the elegance of the body plan. The predator is not crude. It is streamlined in the way a cave fish is streamlined — every unnecessary feature stripped away by generations of darkness, every surface smooth, every movement economical. It has six legs, not the builder's twenty-four, because six is enough for a creature that does not climb walls but patrols floors. The legs are jointed high, bringing the body close to the ground, and the body itself is flattened, able to slide through passages that seem too narrow until you see how it flows through them like water through a pipe.

The predator has no eyes. It has no need for them. It navigates by chemistry, like the builder, but its chemical sense is broader, more discriminating. Where the builder reads the walls, the predator reads the air — the faint currents that carry the signatures of every living thing in the passage ahead. It can distinguish the chemical trail of a grazer from that of a burrower from that of a parasite from that of a dead thing in need of cleaning. It can detect the alkaloid bitterness of a builder from three chambers away, and it ignores this the way you ignore the taste of your own saliva.

The mouthparts are the predator's most distinctive feature. They are not mandibles, not jaws, not a beak. They are a cone — a tapering, flexible cone that can extend from the head like a tongue, that can probe into crevices, that can suck soft tissue from a carcass with horrifying efficiency. When the predator feeds, it presses the cone against the body of its prey and draws the interior out, leaving the exoskeleton or skin or shell intact, an empty husk that will be broken down by the fungal gardens in time. Nothing is wasted. The predator does not waste. It takes what it needs and moves on.

It is silent. Absolutely silent. Not quiet — *silent*. The predator has no vocalizations, no stridulations, no wing-beats, no footfalls that register above the ambient hum. It moves through the passages like a ghost, like a chemical gradient, like something that is there and then is no longer there. The vermin do not know it is coming. The vermin know only that a passage was safe a moment ago and is now a trap, and by then the cone has already found them.

The predator is not cruel. Cruelty requires the capacity for satisfaction in suffering, and the predator has no such capacity. It is not even particularly aggressive — it feeds when hungry, rests when not, and spends the majority of its time in a state of low activity, lying in the wider passages where the compass-point junctions meet, monitoring the chemical traffic. It is a sentinel. It is part of the system's immune function, removing organisms that would otherwise proliferate beyond the architecture's capacity to contain them.

There are thousands of predator species in the Built. This is the other thing the description didn't tell you: the predators are not one thing. They are many things, filling many niches, ranging in size from the palm of your hand to the length of your arm. Some specialize in the aerial plankton, drifting on the thermals with extended catch-nets. Some hunt the burrowers, pushing into the compost with reinforced heads. Some patrol the canopy, feeding on the grazers. And some — the largest, the rarest — hunt the other predators, because a system without checks is a system that collapses.

The predators do not build. They do not secrete. They do not contribute to the architecture in any material way. But the architecture was built *for them* — the passages at compass points, sized precisely for their bodies — because the builders, over geological time, have shaped the structure to accommodate them. Not through altruism. Through selection. The mounds that built passages survived. The mounds that didn't, didn't. The predators are written into the architecture the way fire is written into the architecture of a forest — not present at every moment, but essential, structural, a force the system cannot do without.

## The Grazers

They live in the light, which makes them almost normal, and this normalcy is itself strange.

The grazer is the creature you would recognize. It has eyes — small, dark, adapted to the bright upper surface where the green skin photosynthesizes and the sun actually reaches. It has four legs and a body built for browsing, for reaching, for cropping the photosynthetic growth before it can overwhelm the ventilation structures. It is the size of a large rabbit, and it moves with the nervous alertness of a prey animal, because it is a prey animal, because the predators sometimes come to the surface, and the grazers are their occasional targets.

But the grazer is not a rabbit. Its mouthparts are not teeth but a grinding apparatus, two broad plates that shear the photosynthetic growth from the wall surface and macerate it against a bony palate. The green skin is tough — it has to be, to survive the wind and the light and the daily cycle of humidity — and the grazer's jaw is correspondingly powerful. It can strip a square meter of green growth in an hour, and it spends most of its waking hours doing exactly this.

This is not destruction. This is maintenance. The green skin, left unchecked, would grow thick enough to block the ventilation shafts, and the ventilation shafts are the lungs of the Built, and without them the interior would suffocate. The grazers are the pruning system. They crop the growth to a thickness that allows airflow, and they deposit the cropped material — partially digested, chemically altered — in the passages below, where the fungal gardens can process it further. They are gardeners, though they do not know it, though they experience their work only as hunger and satiation.

The grazers are the only inhabitants of the Built that see the sky. They watch the fog form. They feel the temperature drop. They know, in whatever way a creature this simple can *know*, that the evening means humidity, and humidity means the green skin will be softer and easier to crop, and they adjust their feeding accordingly. They are calibrated to the weather. The weather is calibrated to them, indirectly, because the green skin they crop is the same green skin that transpires the moisture that makes the fog that creates the humidity that drives the building cycle. Everything connects.

They are not social. They tolerate each other's presence, and they mate when the chemical signals are right, and they bear live young — single offspring, well-developed, capable of cropping within hours of birth — and they do not form bonds beyond the minimum required for reproduction. A grazer alone is a grazer complete. A grazer in a group is just several completes standing near each other.

They die on the surface sometimes, and their bodies dry in the sun, and the chemical signature of their death attracts the predators, who carry the remains below, who process them, who return their substance to the system. Nothing leaves. Nothing is lost. The grazer eats the green skin and is eaten by the predator and becomes part of the compost and feeds the fungus and nourishes the builder and becomes a wall, and the wall grows green skin, and the grazer eats the green skin.

## The Burrowers

You will never see them, but you will hear them — a tiny, constant rustling in the compost, the sound of turnover, the sound of soil being made.

The burrower is small — the size of your little finger — and blind, and soft-bodied in a way that seems impossible given the rigidity of its environment. It moves through the compost by eating it, passing it through a gut that is essentially a chemical reactor, and depositing it behind itself in a form that the fungal gardens can digest. It is a living digestive tube. It is a processor. It is the thing that makes the floor you would walk on if you walked on floors here, which you shouldn't, because the floor is alive and you would hurt it.

The burrower has no defense against predators. It has no defense against anything. It is food — pure, concentrated, accessible food — and every predator in the compost layer eats it, and the burrower's only strategy is to reproduce faster than it can be eaten. A single burrower produces fifty offspring in its two-year lifespan, and most of those offspring are consumed before they reach maturity, and the ones that survive produce fifty more, and the system stays in balance because the predators are efficient and the compost is deep and there is always somewhere to hide, at least for a while.

The burrower does not suffer. This must be said again, because the image of a creature whose entire existence is processing and being eaten seems like suffering to a human mind. But the burrower has no concept of its own mortality. It has no fear. It has the compost and the process and the chemical signals that say *eat here, deposit there, mate now* and it follows them with the same unthinking fidelity that the builder brings to the walls. It is a thread in the system. It does not know it is a thread. It does not know what a system is. It eats and processes and deposits and mates and dies, and its death is not a tragedy because its death is a meal, and a meal is never a tragedy in a place where nothing is wasted.

## The Gliders

They are the closest thing the Built has to something beautiful, and this is a dangerous word, because beauty implies an observer and the gliders have no observer but you.

They ride the thermals. The Built is warm — warmer than the air above it, because the metabolism of ten billion builders generates heat — and the warmth rises, and the gliders catch the rising air on membranes that stretch between extended ribs, and they soar. Not fly — *soar*. They do not flap. They have no mechanism for flapping. They extend and hold, and the air lifts them, and they drift above the canopy like leaves in a slow wind.

They feed on the aerial plankton — the spores and fragments and microorganisms that fill the air above the Built, because the Built produces all of these in abundance, because the Built is alive and living things shed and spore and fragment. The glider's mouth is a gape, a filter, a net of fine filaments that catch the plankton as the glider circles. It is feeding the way a baleen whale feeds, and it is as effortless, and as enormous in its cumulative effect, because the gliders number in the millions and the aerial plankton numbers in the trillions and the sky above the Built is a feeding ground as rich as any ocean.

The glider is thin. Dangerously thin. Its body is a tube of gut and reproductive organ wrapped in membrane, with a brain at one end barely large enough to process the simplest stimuli: up, down, food, not-food, mate, not-mate. It lives three years, and in that time it never lands. It cannot land. It has no legs. It has no way to move on a surface. If a glider falls, it dies — not from the fall but from the inability to rise again, and the predators find it, and the system recovers the body, and the other gliders circle overhead, unnoticing.

They mate in the air. The males release sperm packets into the thermal updrafts, and the females fly through them, and the packets dissolve on contact with the female's reproductive membrane, and fertilization occurs, and the female releases a single egg-case into the updraft, and the egg-case drifts, and eventually catches on a structure somewhere, and hatches, and the young glider unfolds its membranes and lifts off, and it does not know its parents and its parents do not know it, and this is not sad because there is no capacity for sadness in a brain that small, there is only the thermal and the plankton and the endless slow circle above the green.

You watch them and you think of grace. You think of freedom. You are wrong. The glider is not free. It is as bound to the system as the builder is, as the predator is, as the burrower is. Its freedom is an illusion born of distance — from below, it looks like choice; from above, it is clearly a mechanism. The glider rides the thermal because the thermal is there. It eats the plankton because the plankton is there. It mates because the chemistry says mate. It dies because it cannot do otherwise. It is a thread, like all the others, and the thread does not know it is part of a tapestry.

But it soars. You cannot deny that. It soars.

## The Fungal Gardens

They are alive. They must be included here, because they are not setting — they are inhabitants.

The fungus that fills the interior walls of the Built is a single organism per mound, or close to it. The mycelium connects. The cells communicate chemically, sharing nutrients, sharing information about growth conditions and food availability and the presence of contaminants. Each mound's garden is a network, and the network is the digestive system of the structure, breaking down the builders' waste, the dead grazers, the shed exoskeletons, the accumulated compost of centuries, and converting it into forms the builders can eat.

The fungus is pale — almost white in the dark interior, tinged with faint yellow where it fruits. The fruiting bodies are small, button-like, producing spores that drift through the passages and colonize new walls. The builders tend the gardens, weeding out contaminant molds, trimming the growth to maintain airflow, harvesting the nutrient-rich nodes that form at the edges of the mycelial network. The relationship is ancient. The fungus cannot survive without the builders' cultivation. The builders cannot survive without the fungus's processing. They are a single organism, split across two kingdoms, married by a billion years of codependency.

The gardens smell. Not badly — not to the builders, not to the predators, not to any creature adapted to the Built. But to a human nose, the smell would be overwhelming: rich, earthy, fungal, warm, with an undertone of ammonia and a sweetness that is almost fruity. It is the smell of metabolism at scale, of compost and growth and the slow transformation of death into food. It is the smell of the inside of the Built, which is the smell of the inside of a body, which is the smell of life itself.

## The Parasites

They exist. They are part of the system. They must be acknowledged.

Every creature in the Built carries parasites. The builders have mites that live in the joints of their legs, feeding on the secretion before it cures. The grazers have internal worms that take a percentage of their digestive output. The predators have external ticks that attach to the soft membranes between segments. The gliders have microbial flora in their guts that are not quite symbiotic and not quite parasitic, riding the line, taking a little, giving a little, surviving.

The parasites are not failures of the system. They are features. They regulate population. They stress immune function just enough to keep it sharp. They provide food for smaller predators that specialize in grooming. They are the friction in the machine, and friction is not always waste — sometimes friction is what keeps the whole thing from flying apart.

The builders do not fight their parasites. This is significant. The mites in their leg joints are not enemies; they are more like livestock, tiny creatures that process the fresh secretion in ways that actually improve its curing properties. The relationship has been going on so long that the builders' bodies have adapted to the mites' presence, developing channels and pockets where the mites can live without impeding movement. The parasite has become architecture. The infection has become structure.

## The System

And here is the truth that the individual descriptions approach but cannot quite reach: there are no individuals in the Continuously Built.

Not really. Not in any way that matters.

The builder builds the wall. The wall grows the green skin. The grazer crops the green skin. The grazer's waste feeds the compost. The burrower processes the compost. The fungus digests the processed compost. The builder eats the fungus's nutrients. The builder builds the wall.

The predator eats the grazer. The predator's waste feeds the compost. The burrower processes the compost. The fungus digests. The builder eats. The builder builds.

The glider eats the plankton. The glider's body falls. The predator eats the body. The predator's waste feeds the compost.

The parasite feeds on the builder. The grooming predator eats the parasite. The grooming predator's waste feeds the compost.

Around and around and around, and the sun adds energy, and the substrate adds mass, and the Built grows, and the hum continues, and the continent is a body that is also a city that is also a loop, and the loop is closed, and the loop is not closed, because the sun keeps shining, and the substrate keeps precipitating, and the builders keep building.

You stand in this and you feel the hum and you understand that what lives here is not the builder or the predator or the grazer or any of the individual forms. What lives here is the *system*. The system is the organism. The system is the inhabitant. Everything else — every creature, every wall, every spasm of predation and burst of reproduction and slow secretion of architectural material — is an organ, a cell, a process within the greater body.

You are standing in a creature the size of a continent, and it is breathing, and it is building, and it is alive in a way that makes your own aliveness feel small and temporary and beside the point.

This is what lives in the Continuously Built: everything. Connected. Continuous. Unfinished.
