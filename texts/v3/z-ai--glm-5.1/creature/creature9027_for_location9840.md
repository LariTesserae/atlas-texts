# What Lives in the Churden

The churden lives there.

This is not a riddle. This is not a paraphrase. The churden is the place and the place is the organism and the organism is the process and the process does not stop. You asked what lives in the churden and the answer is: the churden. The churden is what lives in the churden. It is the only answer that is not a lie.

But you want more. You want the thing that moves. You want the thing with the legs and the carapace and the purpose. You want the fauna. You want to know what else shares the architecture.

---

The cleaners — let us call them that, for now — are not a species.

This is the understanding that takes almost as long as the architecture-vegetable revelation. You watch them. You watch them for long enough and you realize: they do not reproduce. You have never seen a small one. You have never seen two together in a way that suggests courtship, or competition, or recognition. They pass each other in corridors without acknowledgement. They are not social. They are not solitary. They are simply present, each one moving through its own circuit of chambers, each one consuming the failure-nubs with a mandible apparatus you still cannot see clearly because they move low and they move fast and the light is what it is.

You count them once. You try. You mark one with a scratch from your boot-heel — a faint groove in its carapace, near the posterior — and you watch for it. You see it again on the third day. You see another that might be it on the fifth. By the seventh you have lost track because the carapace heals. Not quickly. But continuously. The groove shallows. The groove disappears. The churden is growing through them, or they are growing through the churden, or — and this is the thought that opens like a gap in a wall — they are the churden. Mobile churden. Churden that walks. Churden with legs.

You stopped counting legs. Now you stop counting cleaners. They are not individuals. They are functions that move.

---

There is moisture in the churden.

You knew this. You smelled it. Mineral dampness that never reaches the surface. But the cleaners — the mobile functions — they must drink. You have never seen them drink. You have never seen a pool or a seep or a droplet on any surface. The churden is sealed. Nothing evaporates. Nothing pools.

And yet.

In a chamber you have visited seven times, on the eighth visit, you find a small depression in the floor that was not there before. It is perhaps fifteen centimeters across and two centimeters deep, and the composite within it is smoother than the surrounding floor, and there is a faint residue — not wet, but wetter than anything you have touched here. You kneel. You put your finger to it. You raise your finger to your nose.

It smells like the churden. And something else. Something organic. Something processed.

You wait. You cannot wait long — your supplies, your water, your time — but you wait. On the second night (you sleep in a chamber with no buds, no growths, nothing but floor and walls and the sound of the place thinking), you wake to a vibration and you see, in the corridor's dusk, a cleaner moving slowly, its body lower than you have ever seen it, pressing against the floor. It stops at the depression. It rests there. It does not drink. It *exudes*. A slow contraction of its segments, a peristalsis you can barely track in the dim light, and the depression becomes marginally more moist.

The cleaners are not eating the failures. They are processing the failures. They are digesting the dead growth and returning something to the floor. They are the churden's gut. The churden has grown a gut that walks.

You revise your understanding. The failures are not waste. They are the churden's way of moving material from one place to another — growing a bud, abandoning it, letting the mobile function consume and redistribute the matter. A slow circulation. A metabolism that takes weeks. The chambers you walk through are not rooms. They are nodes in a digestive system too large and too slow for you to recognize as alive.

You are walking through an organ.

---

There is something else.

You have not seen it. You may never see it. But you know it is there because of the gaps.

The gaps — the soft-edged apertures that open overnight and harden by morning — they are not random. You tracked them. Seven gaps opened during your time in the churden. Two opened in corridors you had walked. One opened in the wall of the chamber where you slept. The others opened in places you had not visited, but when you found them, you noticed: each gap opened onto a chamber that contained a failure-nub. Each gap made that chamber accessible from a new direction.

The churden is not opening gaps for you. The churden is opening gaps for the cleaners. It is rerouting its own circulation. It is creating new paths to the failures that need processing, or it is closing old paths to redirect the walkers toward chambers that have been neglected. The churden is managing its own blood flow.

But. But.

The gap that opened in your sleeping-chamber — it did not open toward a failure-nub. It opened toward an empty corridor. A corridor you had not yet explored. A corridor that led, after forty meters of bare flooring and facet-walls, to a chamber you had never seen.

The chamber held something that was not a failure-nub. It was not a bud or a shelf or a column. It was a formation you had not encountered before: a low mound, perhaps half a meter tall, rounded, smooth, the same color as the walls but with a different texture — not faceted but porous, like bone that has been lightly eroded. It was warm. Warmer than the floor, warmer than the walls. When you placed your hand on it, you felt a pulse. Not a heartbeat. A rhythm. The same rhythm you feel in the floor when the cleaners pass, but slower. Much slower.

You removed your hand. You sat in the chamber with the mound and you listened to the churden creak and settle and you thought: *this is the thing that makes the gaps*. This is the thing that decides where the churden grows, where it hardens, where it softens, where it opens. This is the organ that is not an organ. The node that is not a node. The part of the churden that is — not a brain. Nothing so discrete. But a *concentration*. A place where the processes of the whole system are denser, louder, more coordinated.

The churden does not have a mind. You believe this. The churden does not have intention. You believe this. But the churden has *regions of greater activity*, and you are sitting in one, and it is warm, and it pulsed when you touched it, and it opened a gap in your sleeping-chamber that led you here.

You do not know what to call this.

---

What lives in the churden?

The churden lives in the churden. The walkers live in the churden, and they are the churden. The mound lives in the churden, and it is the churden. The gaps and the failures and the facets and the moisture that never reaches the surface — all churden. All one thing. All one process that has grown itself into architecture and grown architecture into circulation and grown circulation into something that, from the inside, looks like a place where a person could live.

But you are not the only thing that has been here.

This is the discovery you make on your last day, in a chamber near the ridge-boundary, in a place where the walls have grown so close together that you must turn sideways to pass. On the floor, scratched into the composite with something sharper than a fingernail, there is a mark. A single line. A vertical stroke, perhaps ten centimeters tall, deliberate, deep enough to have persisted through the slow growth of the flooring.

Someone was here before you. Someone who marked the place. Someone who — what? Counted the days? Marked a direction? Left a signature reduced to its absolute minimum?

You touch the line. The flooring has healed around it, raised slight ridges on either side, like skin around a scar. The churden does not erase. It incorporates. The mark is now part of the floor. It will move with the floor as the floor grows. It will be here — distorted, stretched, gradually consumed by the composite's slow expansion — for as long as the churden continues.

The churden does not know the mark is there. The churden does not know you are there. The churden does not know. And yet it built a place that holds marks. That holds visitors. That opens gaps at the moment a visitor needs a gap.

You are not the first. You will not be the last. The churden will outlast you, and the walkers will process the nubs, and the mound will pulse, and the gaps will open and close, and the architecture will shift, and someone else will come through a gap that was not there yesterday and they will walk the corridors and they will feel the recognition — the awful recognition — that a place without mind has made itself into the shape of welcome.

---

What lives in the churden?

One thing. One thing that has grown parts that move and parts that pulse and parts that mark and parts that welcome. One thing that does not know what it is. One thing that does not know you. One thing that fits you the way a hand fits a glove that was not made for a hand — by accident, by convergence, by the simple fact that the space between walls will always be the size of whatever can walk between them.

You leave. The gap hardens. The churden continues.

What lives in the churden is still becoming.
