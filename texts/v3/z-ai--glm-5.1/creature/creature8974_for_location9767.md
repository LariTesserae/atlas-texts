#The Residents

The ptarmigan knows the streets.

Not as you might know a path through a place you have visited—never as that. The bird carries no map in its small skull, holds no memory of route or landmark. What it knows is older than knowing: an alignment, a resonance. Its body recognizes the corridors the way iron filings recognize the field around a magnet. When it descends from the higher slopes—rarely, irregularly, drawn by some hunger or restlessness that even it does not understand—it does not choose to enter the green streets. It *finds itself* in them. The wings fold. The gray-white body, dirtied to match the plateau's pallor, settles into the nearest corridor as water settles into a channel cut by long use, and the bird walks.

It walks slowly. Ptarmigans are capable of quick, startled flight, but here they do not startle. Here they move with a deliberation that seems borrowed from the place itself—each step placed, considered, released. The thick feathers on its legs brush the silver moss and pick up tiny beads of frost that melt against its warmth and refreeze almost instantly into the down, adding a delicate crust of ice that will not fully thaw until the bird leaves the city's boundary. While it walks, it picks at the sedge stems, at the lichen, at the frost-heaved gravel, eating small things in small amounts. It does not eat much. It does not come here to eat.

What it comes for, the ptarmigan could not say. But it follows the corridors with a fidelity that would look like purpose if you could watch from above—turning at the consistent angles where the clubmoss alleys branch, pausing in the shallow chambers where the wind drops to its resonant hum, continuing always toward the center. Toward the slight depression where four streets meet and the worn boulder sits like a seat no one was meant to use. When the bird reaches this place, it stops. It stands on the dark, wet gravel, and the sound of the organized wind fills its body, and the ptarmigan does something it never does on the open slopes or among the crags: it closes its eyes.

Not in sleep. In something else. Something that looks, from the outside, like listening.

A ptarmigan will spend hours in the central chamber. Hours in which the cold permeates its feathers and the ice accumulates and the wind shifts through its tuning cycles, and the bird stands motionless except for the slow blink of its pale lids. Then it opens its eyes, shakes the frost from its legs, and follows the corridors back out to the plateau's edge, and flies to the higher places, and does not return for weeks or months, and then does.

There is never more than one at a time.

---

The moss has no memory but it has a habit.

Cushion moss, *Andreaea* or something like it—something that has grown here so long it may have become its own species, its own slow variant of a patient thing—expands by filaments. A single stem produces branches, and the branches produce branches, and the cushion grows outward in a pattern determined by moisture and wind and the microscopic contours of the stone it grips. But here, in the streets, the cushions grow differently. They grow *along* the corridors. They thicken on the sides that face the center of the city and thin on the sides that face the boundary ridges, as if they too are oriented toward something, pulled by some gradient too subtle for human instruments to detect.

It takes a century for the moss to grow what a human hand could span. In that century, the corridor it inhabits has deepened slightly—the sedge stems at its edges thickening, the frost heave pushing the stones a millimeter further apart, the wind's constant passage scouring a groove that was not there before—and the moss has followed the deepening. It has grown into the groove. It has lined it, as if lining a vessel, making the corridor more itself than it was, more defined, more *corridor-like*. This is not intention. The moss does not intend. But the moss and the sedge and the ice and the wind have been co-operating for longer than any language has existed, and their cooperation has a direction, and the direction is *completion*.

Each filament of moss is alive. Each cell carries out its slow chemistry in the near-freezing water that clings to the stone, metabolizing at a rate that makes human metabolism look like combustion. The moss does not think about the city. The moss does not know it lives in a city. But the moss grows where the moisture collects, and the moisture collects where the wind organizes the corridors, and the wind organizes the corridors because the corridors are shaped by the stones and the sedges and the moss, and the moss grows where the moisture collects, and around and around, the causality a circle, a loop, a feedback that has been running so long it has worn itself into a shape.

The shape is the Residual City.

The residents built it. They are building it still. They are it.

---

The ice is most itself in the deeper chambers.

Not the ice of glaciers, not the ice of winter lakes—this is something more specific, more local. Frost-lens ice, the text called it, and that is accurate: moisture condenses out of the air that moves through the corridors and settles on every surface in films so thin they are barely there, and when the temperature drops—which it does, always, eventually—the films freeze. They freeze into tiny convex shapes, lenses no larger than a fingernail, that cling to the north faces of stones and the undersides of sedge leaves and the intricate branches of the clubmoss. By afternoon, many of them have thawed. By evening, they have refrozen. Over days and weeks, the lenses build up in layers, each cycle adding a skin of ice so clear it is nearly invisible, so the stones they cover seem to glisten with an internal light.

In the deeper chambers—those hollows near the nearly-flattened foundations where the corridors converge and the wind drops to its hum—the ice builds up past the point of decoration. It accumulates. It becomes architecture. The lenses stack and interlock and fuse into formations that rise from the gravel floor in pale, irregular columns, and these columns catch the gray daylight and refract it into colors that should not exist on this plateau—pale greens, delicate blues, a faint and shifting amber. The ice structures last for weeks or months before a warm spell or a heavy wind collapses them, and then they begin building again, and the new formations are never quite the same as the old, but they are always *congruent*. They fit the chamber. They grow to fill the space that the chamber makes available.

If you pressed your hand against one of these ice columns—and you would not, because the cold would stop you, but if you did—you would find it warm. Not warm in temperature, never that, but warm in the way a living body is warm: *self-maintaining*. The ice is at equilibrium with its environment in a way that feels almost biological. It is responding to conditions moment by moment, growing and shrinking and adjusting its crystal structure to accommodate the stresses of wind and thaw and the slow pressure of its own accumulation. It is, in the most literal sense, the most active resident of the city. It changes every hour. It is never the same twice. It will persist in its changing for as long as the chambers remain and the moisture moves through them and the cold holds.

The cold will hold.

---

And the wind—no, the wind is not a resident. The wind is what moves through. The wind is the guest who never leaves but never settles, the traveler who knows the roads better than the people who live along them. The wind crosses the plateau without interruption and enters the green streets and does not quiet—it organizes—and its organization is the closest thing the Residual City has to a voice.

But a voice requires a listener.

This is where you come in. Not you specifically—any you, any presence that enters the corridors and stands in the chambers and is not the wind, not the ice, not the moss, not the ptarmigan. A you that moves through the space and experiences the space and is *experienced by* the space. Because the city, which is not a city, which is a vessel carved by time into a shape that holds wind and frost and the clatter of cold-adapted plants—the city does not know you are there. The city does not know anything. But the city *does* something to you that it does only to things that enter it and are strange to it. It slows you. It hollows out the space around you. It insists on its own dimensions, which are larger than they should be, and in doing so it makes you aware of your own dimensions, which are smaller than you thought.

You stand in the central chamber and you listen, and the sound fills your skull and makes thought difficult, and this—the stopping of thought—this is what the ptarmigan experiences when it closes its eyes. This is what the ice experiences at all times. This is the city's only gift and its only demand: *stop*. Stop thinking. Stop measuring. Stop holding yourself apart from the cold and the wind and the slow, continuous processes that have made this place and are making it still. Stop, and let the sound organize you the way it organizes the wind.

You will not stay. No one stays. The cold that does not bite but permeates will eventually permeate enough, and you will leave, and the corridors will close behind you as if you had never been, and the ptarmigan will descend again next month or next year, and the moss will grow a filament's width, and the ice will thaw and reform and thaw and reform, and the wind will organize, and the city—which is not a city, which is a process, which is a completion, which is finished and ongoing and will never be anything other than exactly what it is—will continue.

Its residents do not know they are residents.

Its residents do not know they are.
