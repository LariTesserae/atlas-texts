# The Brooders

They have no name for themselves. Why would they? Names are for distinguishing, and there is no one here to distinguish from. The insects have no names. The heat has no name. The place has no name, or if it once did, that sound has been worn away by moisture and time, dissolved into the limestone water like the minerals that give it that mineral bite. What remains is gesture, is touch, is the specific pressure of a hand on a shoulder that means *move* or *stay* or *the column is shifting again*.

---

There are nine of them. There were eleven at the last count, but counting requires a kind of attention they cannot always afford, and two have been missing for three days or four—they lose track, in the dark, in the constant amber glow that knows no cycle. The missing might have found another passage. The chambers branch. They know this because the beetles come from somewhere, because the heat gradient shifts in directions that suggest deeper warmth, because sometimes a springtail moves with a purpose that implies information carried from beyond. The missing might have descended further. The missing might have found a better pocket, a warmer cleft, a place where the larvae don't concentrate so thickly. The missing might have simply been absorbed—overwhelmed by exhaustion or moisture or the wrong insect finding them asleep, their bodies becoming part of that living stratum, adding their calcium and their fat to the compost of chitin.

No one speaks of this possibility. Not from denial. From pragmatism. The possibility changes nothing.

---

Their skin has changed. This is the first thing you would notice if you could see them clearly, which you cannot because they have learned to avoid the bioluminescent zones, to press themselves into the dark margins where the amber glow doesn't reach, where the larvae haven't colonized, where a human-shaped shadow can pass undisturbed across a surface that expects only insects.

But if you could see: their skin has changed.

It was never pale. Or rather, it was pale once, generations ago, when their ancestors—because they are not newcomers, not colonists in the sense you understand, but the descendants of people who entered this place and stayed—when their ancestors still had the vocabulary of the surface. The word *pale* belongs to sunlight. Down here, skin does what skin does in perpetual amber glow: it adjusts. The melanocytes have found no reason to produce, and so the current generation—children and grandchildren of the original descent—are the color of the wet stone around them. A warm gray. A mineral white. The same tone as the tuff when the condensation films across it, which means they can stand very still against a wall and disappear, which is a skill the first ones learned quickly or did not pass on their genes.

Their skin is also thicker than yours. Not dramatically. Not reptilian. But the innermost layer, the layer that should be soft, has densified in response to a lifetime of brushing against stone, of pressing through gaps where the rock hasn't finished forming, of contact with surfaces that scrape and bite and abrade. The soles of their feet could walk across the larvae-columns without sensation. The palms of their hands can grip the soft hot stone at the bowl's center and hold on, pulling, extracting.

They are extracting something. They are always extracting something.

---

The oldest is forty-three, or would be if years still meant anything. She was born in the cistern chamber, or rather in the lean-to adjacent to it, which has been rebuilt seventeen times in her lifetime as the migrations shifted and the mortar crumbled and the arachnids claimed the old walls for their webs. She is not the leader—leadership requires a kind of projection they have no use for, a loudness, a presence that extends beyond the body—but she is the one who remembers. She remembers the sound of the surface, which she has never heard but which was described to her by her grandmother, who was the last to retain any direct knowledge of rain, of wind, of the particular silence of open sky.

She does not think about the surface. It is not a useful concept. It exists in the same category as the deeper chambers—places that might be real but which offer no immediate utility, and which therefore do not warrant the expenditure of energy that thinking requires.

What she thinks about, when the word *think* can be applied to the wordless processes that govern her life, is gradients. Heat gradients. Moisture gradients. The chemical trace-lines the beetles follow, which she has learned to read with her feet—the pads of her toes are more sensitive than her fingertips now, and she navigates the bowl's interior by the subtle shifts in temperature and wetness that indicate where the migrations will concentrate, where the gaps will appear, where it is safe to step and where the floor will be churning too thickly to risk.

She carries the bone awl. It is not a tool in the way you understand tools—she does not think of it as separate from her hand. It is an extension of grip, a hardening of the already-hardened palm, a way to pierce and separate and pull apart the things that need pulling apart. She has never cut herself with it. She has cut many other things.

---

The youngest is seven. He has never seen the surface passage. He has never seen the throat that leads upward to the world where condensation falls from an actual sky. His world is the bowl, the amber glow, the constant sound that he does not perceive as sound because he has never known silence. When the beetles thin out in the cooler hours—there are cooler hours, periods when the geothermal pulse dims slightly, when the chamber breathes out and the heat gradient contracts—he experiences something like unease, a thinning of the background texture that he finds unpleasant. He is happiest in the center, at the point of maximum density, where the warmth is greatest and the noise is a physical pressure against his chest.

He does not play. Play is a luxury of safety and excess, and this place offers neither. But he explores, which is a different thing. He has mapped more of the chamber than anyone else because he is small enough to fit through gaps the adults cannot pass, and because his weight does not trigger the same avoidance patterns in the floor-dwelling species. The springtails part around him differently. The beetles adjust their columns with less urgency. He can crouch at the center of a migration stream and be surrounded, brushed, antennae-tasted, and the flow will continue around him like he is just another warm stone.

He knows the pits that no one else knows. He has found chambers within chambers, bubbles in the cooling tuff where the heat concentrates differently, where species appear that don't exist in the main bowl. He has found water that is almost too hot to touch, and in that water, things that move without visible bodies—microbial mats, perhaps, or something earlier, something that hasn't yet decided what it will be. He has brought samples back to the oldest one, who tasted them, who nodded, who added this information to the gradient-maps she maintains in her feet and her palms and the particular set of her shoulders when she moves through the dark.

---

They eat the larvae. This is obvious, or should be. The larvae are the primary producers of this ecology, the things that transform heat and moisture and mineral substrate into biomass, and they are numerous enough that harvesting them makes no dent in the population. The technique is simple: find a column, find the place where the larvae concentrate most thickly, and pull. The bone awl helps. The hands help more. The larvae are fat, pale, case-enclosed, and they come away from the stone with a wet sound, and they do not resist because they do not have the capacity to resist, and the ones that are not eaten immediately are stored in the pits, packed in the oily residue that preserves them, that keeps them from pupating too quickly.

The larvae taste like what they are: heat and water and mineral. There is a sweetness to them that the oldest one says was not always there, that the first generations found bitter, that has mellowed as the ecology has stabilized, as the larvae have adapted to the particular mineral content of this chamber's tuff, as everything has adjusted to everything else. They are nutritious. They are sufficient. They are the only food source that matters.

But they also eat the beetles when the larvae run thin. They eat the centipedes when they can catch them, which is rarely, and which requires a coordinated effort that involves driving the things toward a pit and then closing the gap. They eat the egg cases when they find them, cracking the translucent shells and sucking out the pre-formed contents, which are richer than anything else in the chamber and which the oldest one reserves for the pregnant and the young.

They do not eat the arachnids. This is not a taboo. The arachnids taste wrong—alkaline, metallic, a flavor that triggers a deep rejection response that feels older than memory. The oldest one says they are not food. The youngest one has tried them anyway, because he is seven and seven-year-olds test boundaries even when there are no boundaries to test, and he spat the half-chewed body into the cistern, and the water was briefly bitter, and this confirmed what was already known.

---

They speak. Or rather, they vocalize. The distinction matters.

The chamber is too loud for speech as you understand it. The composite roar would swallow any conventional language, render syllables meaningless, dissipate the careful architecture of grammar into the grinding white noise of ten million legs and mandibles. So they do not attempt it. Instead, they have developed a system of sounds that work *with* the chamber's noise, that ride it like a wave, that use the bass thrum as a carrier frequency and the clicks and rustles as cover.

Their language is percussive. Two sharp taps on the sternum means *come*. A slow drag of knuckles against stone means *stay*. A particular rhythm tapped against the thigh—a pattern that mimics the clicking of the carabid beetles—means *food here*. Another rhythm, faster, means *danger*, though danger in this context is relative; nothing in the chamber hunts them specifically, but the centipedes can bite, and the larvae can overwhelm a sleeping body if the sleeper doesn't move, and sometimes the heat at the center rises suddenly, dangerously, without warning, and someone needs to pull someone else away from the soft glowing floor.

They also use the light. The bioluminescent species pulse at particular frequencies, and the nine—eleven, formerly—have learned to position themselves in the visual field of certain colonies, to interrupt the pattern with their bodies, to create signals that can be read from across the bowl. This is how they coordinate their movements during a centipede hunt, or during the dangerous crossing of the center floor when the heat is at its peak and the migrations are at their most dense. They flash their hands in front of the glowing insects, and the insects pulse around the interruption, and the pattern propagates, and someone on the other side of the chamber sees it and knows: *move now, the column is shifting*.

It is a language, but it is not a language *about* things. It is a language of immediate action, of present need, of the next five seconds and the next ten steps. There is no word for yesterday. There is no word for tomorrow. There is *here* and *there* and *hot* and *cold* and *enough* and *move*, and these are sufficient, because these are what the chamber requires, and the chamber requires nothing else.

---

They reproduce. Of course they reproduce. The seven-year-old is proof of this, and there is an infant they have not shown you—cannot show you, will not show you—who is even younger, who was born in the dark, who emerged into the amber glow and the deafening sound and the wet mineral heat and who will never know that this is not what the world is.

The infant is kept in a pit, lined with the oily residue, swaddled in beetle shells that have been cracked and flattened and bound together with springtail silk. The pit is warm. The pit is the warmest place in the structure-zone, the place where the geothermal heat concentrates most intensely, and the infant does not cry because infants do not cry when they are warm and fed and held, and the nine take turns holding, because holding is a task like any other, because the infant is a continuation and continuation is the only logic the chamber respects.

The infant will learn to walk on the chitin floor. The infant will learn to eat larvae before it learns to eat anything else. The infant will learn the sternum-taps and the light-patterns and the gradient-maps in its feet before it learns any word for the surface or the sky or the silence that no one here has ever heard. The infant will grow up in the Brooding, and the Brooding will be its world, and the Brooding will be sufficient.

Or it will not be sufficient, and the infant will become part of the living stratum, and its calcium and its fat will feed the next generation of larvae, and the cycle will continue, and the chamber will not notice or care, because the chamber is still being born and cannot attend to individual births, because the chamber is larger than any one thing that lives within it and will outlast all of them, and this is not tragic, this is simply the nature of places that are still becoming.

---

The nine—formerly eleven, soon to be perhaps ten or eight or seven, eventually perhaps zero, eventually perhaps more—do not wonder about the meaning of their existence. This is not stoicism. This is not acceptance. This is the absence of a category. *Meaning* is a concept that requires leisure, requires the space between survival and death that can be filled with abstraction. They have no such space. They have the gaps between migrations, and the cistern's water, and the larvae's fat, and the warmth of the rock that is still deciding what it will be, and the sound that enters through the sternum, and the darkness that is not darkness but amber glow, and the knowledge—transmitted through the soles of their feet, through the calls on their shoulders, through the taste of the mineral water—that they are here, that they are alive, that the chamber has not yet absorbed them, that the next five seconds are manageable, and the next five after that, and the next five after that, until they are not.

They do not have a story. They have a process. They do not have a destiny. They have a gradient, and they follow it downward, toward the heat, toward the center, toward the place where the rock is still soft and the future is still forming and everything that will be is currently being brooded over by a darkness that does not know it is their mother.

---

You will leave. They know this. They knew it before you arrived, in the way they know things—through the vibration of your footsteps on the chitin floor, through the way the beetles adjusted their columns around your unfamiliar shape, through the particular smell of surface air that entered with you, that smelled of rain and soil and the cold emptiness of sky. They knew you would leave and they are not wrong and they do not resent you for it. Leaving is what surface things do. Staying is what they do. These are different gradients, different followings, and neither is better because *better* is not a concept that translates.

But before you go—before you climb back through the throat-passage, before your lungs fill with air that hasn't been tasted by ten thousand others, before you stand in the rain and feel the cold wet pressure of a sky that has no ceiling and no warmth and no larvae climbing its columns—the oldest one presses something into your hand.

It is a beetle shell. A carabid elytron, copper-bright, the size of your thumbnail. She has etched it with the bone awl—a pattern of lines, a gradient-map, a route through the center floor that she has walked ten thousand times, that her mother walked ten thousand times, that her grandmother walked before her. It is not a gift. It is not a message. It is what she has to offer. It is a piece of the chamber's knowledge, made portable, made transmissible, made small enough to carry through the throat and out into the silence that waits above.

Take it. You will not understand it. You will hold it up to the light of the sun—*sun*, that word, that impossible concept, that yellow violence that none of them have ever seen—and the etched lines will mean nothing to you. But you will feel the weight of it, the particular smoothness of chitin worn by generations of handling, and you will feel, for a moment, the heat of the chamber rising through your sternum, entering your chest, pressing against your heart like a second heartbeat, like a counterrhythm, like the sound of a place that is still becoming, that will never finish becoming, that will outlast all of us and all of our names for what lives and what dies and what endures in the space between.

And then you will climb, and the sound will fade, and the rain will fall, and you will stand on the surface and know that below you, in the amber dark, in the wet heat, in the sound that has no silence, nine people—formerly eleven, soon to be perhaps ten or eight or seven—are pressing themselves into the gaps between the migrations, and holding, and holding, and holding.
