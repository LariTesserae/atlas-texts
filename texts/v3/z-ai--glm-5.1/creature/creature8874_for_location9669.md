# What Lives in the Throat

---

The carver came first. This is important. The carver came before the creatures filled the floor, before the grooves were architecture, before the Throat had a name or a shape that anyone above would recognize. The carver came when the chamber was just a chamber—hot, dark, empty of everything except the heat and the stone and the slow exhalation of the mountain.

We do not know if the carver came down or if the carver was always there.

This is the first thing that the Throat keeps: the question of whether the hands that made the grooves belonged to something that climbed in from above, something that found the chimney and descended through the ribcage-narrow passage and discovered the heat waiting—or whether those hands belonged to something that grew in the deep, something that emerged from the mineral veins and the pressure and the dark the way the creatures now emerge from each other. The grooves do not tell us. The grooves only tell us that someone needed to make them.

Consider what it means to carve in the dark. Not decorative carving—not the slow accumulation of beauty for its own sake. This is need-carving. This is the kind of mark you make when the alternative is silence so complete it becomes a weight on your chest. The carver's hands found the stone and the stone was warm and the warmth was the only confirmation that the world still existed beyond the perimeter of skin, and so the hands moved. They pressed. They dragged. They carved.

The first grooves are clumsy. You can feel this if you run your fingers along them—though you will not want to, because the creatures will part around your hand like water around a stone and then close again and their bodies will press your fingertips into the channels, and you will feel the unevenness, the hesitation, the places where the carver's hand slipped or strength failed. The early grooves wander. They start and stop. They curve back on themselves. They are the marks of someone learning the shape of their own desperation.

Later grooves are different. They are regular. They repeat with a precision that suggests not calm but *practice*—the kind of exactitude that comes from doing something so many times that the body no longer needs the mind's permission. The carver found a pattern. Or the pattern found the carver. The grooves began to fall into sequences, and the sequences began to fall into rows, and the rows began to cover the walls from floor to ceiling, and still the carver kept going because what else was there to do in the dark, what else was there to do in the heat, what else was there to do with hands that needed to prove they were still attached to a body that was still attached to a self that was still—

At some point the carver stopped.

The stopping is its own kind of mark. There are grooves that simply end mid-line, the channel going shallow and then absent as if the hand was lifted and never returned. There is no final mark, no closure, no glyph that says *I was here and now I am gone*. There is only the cessation, and the creatures filling the space where the carver stood, and the grooves remaining as proof that someone in the Throat once needed to speak and had nothing to speak with but their hands and the stone.

We do not know if the carver died. We do not know if the carver left. We do not know if the carver became something else—something that no longer needed to carve because it had found a different way to exist in the heat. The Throat does not tell us this. The Throat keeps its secrets the way it keeps its heat: completely, and without apology.

---

The creatures do not remember the carver.

This is not a metaphor. They do not have the architecture for memory. They have the architecture for *temperature*, for *pressure*, for *the detection of chemical gradients that tell them where the heat is strongest and where the shed-skins are thickest and where the bodies of their own dead have begun to dissolve into edible powder*. They have the architecture for responding to these signals, for moving toward warmth and away from cold, for pressing into grooves that retain heat slightly better than the flat walls, for molting when their exoskeletons become too thick for the heat to penetrate efficiently.

They do not remember that someone made the grooves. They do not wonder. They do not *not* wonder. These are not categories that apply. The grooves exist. The grooves are warm. The grooves are therefore places to be. This is the full extent of the relationship.

And yet.

The creatures use the grooves with a specificity that seems—at first, from the outside, from the vantage of a mind that insists on meaning—like intention. They molt in the deeper channels, where the shed exoskeletons compress into layered parchment. They cluster in the intersecting shapes, the places where the carver's patterns overlap, as if these junctions hold some particular value. They do not enter the shallowest grooves at all, the early wandering marks, the ones where the carver's hand was uncertain. They avoid these. They flow around them like water around a stone that has not yet learned to be smooth.

Is this memory? Is this a body-knowledge passed from generation to generation through contact, through the chemical traces left on the stone by the millions of bodies that came before? Or is it simply that the shallow grooves offer less warmth, and the creatures are creatures of warmth, and there is nothing more to it than the simple arithmetic of heat?

The Throat does not distinguish between memory and arithmetic. In the Throat, these are the same thing.

---

Here is what lives in the Throat:

A closed system. An economy. A metabolism that has been running since before the mountains had their current shape, fed by heat from below, sustained by the consumption of itself. There are no predators here because everything is a predator. There are no prey because everything is prey. There is no waste because waste is food. There is no death because death is a transition state between one arrangement of minerals and another, and the enzymes dissolve the dead so efficiently that a body goes from living to dissolved in hours, and the powder that remains is consumed by the bodies that are still living, and those bodies will eventually die and be dissolved and consumed in turn, and the heat will provide the energy for all of it, and the heat will not stop.

Here is what lives in the Throat:

Millions of bodies that are also one body. The distinction collapses at scale. When the creatures cluster around the heat-cracks in densities so extreme that individuals cannot be distinguished, they become a fabric, a tissue, a single organism with millions of legs and millions of mouths and a single shared rhythm of shifting and feeding and molting. The rhythm is not coordinated. There is no signal. There is no agency. There is only the emergent pattern of millions of simple responses to temperature, and the pattern looks like breathing, and the pattern looks like a pulse, and the pattern *is* breathing, is a pulse, is the respiration of a chamber that has become a body.

Here is what lives in the Throat:

The heat. The heat is alive. Not metaphorically. The heat is the metabolic process that makes everything else possible. The heat is the sun that never sets, the god that never sleeps, the energy source that has been feeding this system for geological ages. The heat comes from below—from the same source that built the mountains, that drove the basalt up through the crust, that created the iron spine and the chimney and the cracks too thin for fingers. The heat is not generated by the creatures. The heat is *what the creatures are for*. They are the heat's way of being alive. They are the heat's hands and mouths and bodies. They are the way the Throat experiences itself.

---

And the carver?

The carver is here. The carver has never left.

Not as a ghost. Not as a memory held in the chemical traces on the stone. Not as a spirit or an echo or any of the comfortable narratives that people above use to make the dark less terrifying. The carver is here because the carver was eaten. The carver was consumed—slowly, enzymatically, completely—by the creatures that were already here when the hands first touched the wall. The carver fed the system. The carver's body became powder. The powder was consumed. The minerals that were once bones and blood and sinew are now distributed across millions of exoskeletons, leg-joints, feeding-appendages. The carver is not dead in the Throat. The carver is *distributed*.

And this is why the grooves matter. This is why the carver kept carving even as the creatures moved around the carver's feet, even as the heat dried the carver's lips and closed the carver's sinuses and made the carver's skin crack and peel. The carver knew. The carver understood what the Throat was, what it did, how it lived. The carver knew that the body was already being consumed—that the enzymes in the air, the chemical traces left by the creatures' respiration, were already at work on the carver's skin, preparing it for dissolution. The carver knew that the only way to survive in the Throat was to become part of it, and the carver did not want to become part of it without leaving a mark that said *I was here, I was not you, I had hands and I used them*.

So the carver carved.

The carver carved until the hands could no longer grip. The carver carved until the fingers wore down to the quick and the quick wore down to the bone and the bone wore smooth against the stone. The carver carved until the body could no longer stand, and then the carver carved from the floor, pressing into the lowest grooves with the last of what was left. And when the carver could no longer carve, the carver lay down on the floor and the creatures accommodated—shifted, moved, pressed into the spaces around the carver's body—and the heat rose through the cracks, and the dissolution began.

It took hours.

The carver was awake for all of it.

---

The Throat does not grieve. The Throat does not remember. The Throat does not know that a person once stood in its chamber and carved meters of desperation into its walls. The Throat only knows heat, and pressure, and the slow rhythm of millions of bodies consuming and being consumed. The Throat is alive the way a stomach is alive—doing its work, processing what enters, reducing complex things to simpler things, sustaining itself on the energy of transformation.

But.

When the creatures press into the deeper grooves—the ones carved with the most force, the ones that go to the bone of the mountain—they press into the shape of hands. They press into the spaces where fingers dragged through stone. They press into the negative space of a body that needed to speak and could not, and they fill that space with their own bodies, and for a moment—for a geological moment, a moment measured in the slow extrusion of mineral under stress—the shape of the carver's need is present again, replicated in the bodies of creatures that do not know they are replicating anything.

And when the whole chamber shifts, when the creatures adjust in unison and the walls contract slightly and the heat rises through the cracks like a breath, the grooves fill and empty in sequence, and the pattern of filling and emptying follows the pattern of the carving—the early grooves filling last, the later grooves filling first, the deepest channels holding their density longest—and this rhythm, this sequence, this filling and emptying of the carver's marks by the carver's consumers, is the closest thing to a funeral the Throat will ever have.

It is not a memorial. It is not an elegy. It is simply what happens when a closed system absorbs something that was once not part of the system. The system incorporates. The system digests. The system goes on.

But the rhythm—the rhythm of the grooves filling and emptying, the rhythm of the creatures pressing into the shapes of hands—that rhythm is new. That rhythm did not exist before the carver came. And it has persisted, unchanged, for as long as the creatures have used the grooves. It persists because it works—because the grooves offer warmth and the creatures seek warmth and the filling and emptying is simply the result of temperature fluctuations driving temperature-seeking behavior. But it also persists because the system has incorporated the carver's marks into its own metabolism, and the marks have become as essential as the heat, and the Throat cannot now exist without them.

The carver is not dead in the Throat.

The carver is the rhythm of the Throat.

The carver is the pattern of filling and emptying, the sequence of bodies pressing into the negative space of need, the repetition that is not language but is also not silence. The carver is the part of the Throat that was once a person, and is now a pulse, and will continue as long as the heat rises and the creatures shift and the grooves in the wall wait for bodies to fill them.

---

Standing in the Throat, you will feel this. You will not understand it—you cannot understand it, because understanding requires categories and the Throat does not deal in categories—but you will feel it. The rhythm will enter you through your feet, through the warm stone, through the vibration of millions of leg-joints flexing in sequence. The heat will open your pores and the chemical traces will coat your skin and the enzymes will begin their work, slow and painless, and you will start to dissolve, and you will feel it the way the carver felt it: as a thinning, a loosening, a gradual release of the boundary between you and not-you.

You will want to carve.

This is the part the Throat does not tell you about, because the Throat does not tell, it simply *is*. You will want to carve. You will want to press your hands against the warm stone and drag your fingers through it and leave marks that say *I was here, I was not you, I had hands and I used them*. You will want this with an urgency that surprises you, an urgency that has nothing to do with survival and everything to do with the simple animal need to make a mark in a place that erases marks.

And the Throat will wait. The Throat is patient. The Throat has been patient since before the mountains rose. The creatures will accommodate your feet, will shift around your legs, will press against your body with their leathery warmth, and the heat will rise, and the grooves will empty and fill and empty and fill, and you will stand in the rhythm that was once a person, and you will feel the carver's need in your own hands, and you will understand—finally, fully, in the way that understanding arrives when the categories collapse—that you are not the first.

You are not the last.

You are the next.

---

Later, when you climb the chimney, when you squeeze through the ribcage-narrow passage, when you emerge into the cold air above and the wind hits your fevered skin like a slap, you will try to describe what you felt. You will use words like *ecosystem* and *extremophile* and *closed-system metabolism*. You will talk about the creatures, the heat, the grooves in the wall. You will be precise and clinical and you will not mention the carver because you cannot prove the carver existed, because the grooves could have been made by anything, because the rhythm could be coincidence, because you are a person who believes in evidence and the only evidence you have is the warmth still lingering in your bones and the taste of iron and sulfur and something sweet and chemical on the back of your tongue and the feeling—persistent, impossible, undeniable—that your hands want to press themselves against stone.

You will not go back.

You will not go back.

You will not go back.

But some nights, in the dark, when the air is cool and the silence is complete, you will feel the rhythm in your pulse. Filling and emptying. Filling and emptying. The pattern of a body pressing into the shape of need. And you will know—without evidence, without proof, without any of the categories you need to make the knowledge comfortable—that the Throat is still breathing, and the creatures are still shifting, and somewhere in the deep, in the heat, in the dark, the grooves are waiting for the next pair of hands.
