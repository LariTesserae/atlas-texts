# The People of Mokthar

## Their Bodies

They are not beautiful in the way that statues are beautiful. They are beautiful in the way that weathered stone is beautiful — not despite what time has done to them, but because of it, because the thing time has done is finally the right thing.

They are not tall. Generations of the thin air have shortened them, compressed their frames into something denser, more compact, as though their bodies have learned that there is less oxygen here and have responded by becoming more efficient with what they get. Their chests are broad. Their lungs are large. They breathe slowly, even by the standards of the city's slow rhythm, and when they exert themselves — carrying stone, splitting timber, walking the switchback path — they do not quicken their breath so much as deepen it, drawing the air down into capacities that a lowlander would not know they possessed.

Their skin is dark — not the darkness of distant equatorial ancestry, though that may be in the deep history, but a darkness that has been thickened and enriched by the altitude, by the sharp light, by the constant damp. It is a brown that has blue in it, a brown that leans toward the color of the wet schist when the sun is on it. Their hands are rough, always rough, no matter how carefully they work, because the stone and the wood and the fiber they handle all have texture, all have opinions about the skin that touches them, and over a lifetime those opinions accumulate into callus and crease.

Their hair is dark and fine, worn long by the women and by some of the men, cut close by others. It tends to grow in ways that follow the grain of the wind — swept back from their faces, falling along the lines their heads turn most often, as though even their hair has learned the prevailing direction of the air and submits to it. Some of them braid it, simply, without ornament. Some let it hang. A few of the older women wind it into coils that sit at the backs of their necks like small dark nests.

Their eyes are the last thing you notice and the thing you remember. Light-colored — grey or pale amber or a green that matches the moss — and very still. Not unseeing. Not vacant. But settled, the way the surface of the pool is settled, reflecting what comes before them without seeming to reach for it. When a person from Mokthar looks at you, you have the uncanny sense of being seen exactly as you are, with no judgment and no particular curiosity, simply acknowledged and then released.

## Their Way of Moving

They do not hurry. This is not the same as being slow — a person from Mokthar can move quickly when the need arises, and some of them are genuinely fast in the way that people who climb rock faces from childhood must become fast. But they do not hurry. They do not move with the anxious momentum of someone trying to arrive before something ends, or before something begins, or before the opportunity passes. There is no passing opportunity in Mokthar. The opportunity is always here. It will be here tomorrow.

Watch one of them cross a plaza. The stride is even but not rigid. The body leans slightly forward, not from eagerness but from the simple physics of walking on stone that is never quite flat — you lean into the tilt, you accommodate the ground, and in doing so you develop a gait that looks, to a lowlander, like purposefulness. But it is not purposefulness exactly. It is *agreement*. The walker and the ground have agreed on the best way to cross each other.

They climb with their whole bodies. Hands on the stone, feet finding the holds that the schist offers, a kind of four-limbed conversation with the rock that looks like dance from a distance and feels like breathing up close. Children learn this before they learn to walk steadily on flat ground — which may be why there are no children under five or six visible in the city. They are on the walls, the outcroppings, the lower faces, learning the dialogue of hand and stone, and they do not come down into the plazas until they have mastered it.

When they sit, they sit completely. This is harder than it sounds. Most people sit with part of themselves still in motion — a foot tapping, a finger drumming, the mind already on the next thing and the body waiting to follow. The people of Mokthar sit the way the conifers stand — fully present, fully in position, with nothing in them that is trying to be elsewhere. You can see this most clearly in the plazas in the late afternoon, when the light goes amber and the stone begins to glow. A person will sit on a low wall beside the pool, or on a flat rock near a stream, and they will simply be there, and the being-there is not inactivity. It is something more active than that, more intentional. It is the activity of attending.

## Their Voices

They speak a language that is not spoken anywhere else.

This is not a claim of uniqueness for its own sake. It is a practical observation: the language of Mokthar could not be spoken anywhere else, because it is designed for the acoustics of the plateau, for the way sound behaves among the stone walls and the open spaces and the constant whisper of water. It is a language of soft consonants and breathy vowels, of tones that carry across a plaza without rising to a shout, of words that seem to dissolve into the damp air just as they reach the edge of hearing, so that a conversation at one end of a street is inaudible at the other.

They do not talk much. Not because they are unfriendly or taciturn by nature, but because they have discovered — over how many generations? — that most of what people say to each other in other places is said to fill silence, to maintain connection, to reassure both speakers that they are not alone. The people of Mokthar do not need this reassurance. They know they are not alone. They can hear the person in the next room breathing. They can hear the footsteps on the path outside. The city itself provides the constant low proof of presence, and so the people are freed from the obligation of proving it to each other.

When they do speak, it is often in very short utterances. A word, sometimes two. A phrase that seems incomplete until you realize that the context has already supplied the rest of the meaning — the gesture, the glance, the shared understanding of what is happening and what needs to be done. They have, over time, compressed their language the way they have compressed their architecture, stripping away everything that is not load-bearing, until only the essential structure remains.

They sing, though. This surprises visitors, when there are visitors. They sing in the evenings, sometimes, in groups of three or five, and the singing is not like the speaking — it is full and resonant and it fills the spaces between the buildings the way the fog fills the spaces between the trees. The songs have no words. They are melodies only, long and slow and modal, and they seem to be about nothing and about everything, and they end not with resolution but with a kind of settling, as though the song has found a place where it can rest and has simply stopped moving.

## Their Work

They are craftspeople. This is the most accurate word, though it is not quite right, because it implies a separation between the maker and the made that does not exist in Mokthar.

When one of them splits a block of schist from the outcrop, they are not producing a building material. They are participating in the stone's desire to become a wall. The stone, they would say if they said things like this — which they do not, because it is too obvious to need saying — has been waiting in the outcrop for the hands that will free it and place it where it belongs. The craftsperson is the instrument of the stone's intention.

This sounds like mysticism. It is not. Or rather, it is mysticism that has been so thoroughly tested by practice that it has become simply the way things are done. You work the stone by listening to it — tapping it with your harder stone, feeling the vibration travel through your fingers, finding the line where the rock wants to break. You cut the timber by following its grain, letting the saw find the path of least resistance, because the tree grew in response to the wind and the grain records that response, and if you work against it you are working against the tree's life, and the timber will warp and twist and betray you.

Their pots are extraordinary. This is not immediately apparent, because they are simple pots — small, rounded, undecorated, the color of the clay from which they are made, which is the color of the plateau's underlying earth, which is a deep warm red-brown. But hold one and you understand: the walls are exactly as thick as they need to be and no thicker. The curve follows the curve of the hand that will hold it. The rim turns outward at precisely the angle that lets liquid pour without dripping. The pot is not a container that was made. It is a container that emerged, the way a hollow emerges in rock where water has flowed.

They do not make more than they need. This is crucial. There are no stockpiles of pots, no spare timber stacked against future use, no larders filled with preserved food. When a pot cracks — which happens rarely, because the pots are so well made — someone makes another. When a tool wears out, someone replaces it. The work exists in the present tense, always. It does not anticipate. It does not prepare. It meets the need as the need arises, with the confidence that the need will be met, because it always has been.

## Their Relationships

They are not demonstrative. You will not see public displays of affection in Mokthar, no embraces in the plazas, no hand-holding along the lanes. But you will notice, if you watch for a while, that they are always aware of each other.

A woman sits on a wall near the pool, running her fingers over a piece of wood she is shaping. A man crosses the plaza on some errand. They do not greet each other. They do not nod or smile. But the woman's fingers pause for a fraction of a second as the man passes, and the man's stride adjusts almost imperceptibly to carry him closer to the wall, and in that brief coordination — the pause, the adjustment — there is a whole conversation, a whole recognition, a whole acknowledgment of the other's presence and warmth and reality.

They pair, and they stay paired. Not because of convention or obligation, but because in a place where nothing changes, love does not change either, and the person who was right for you at twenty-five is still right at fifty and seventy, because both of you have been becoming more yourselves in parallel, and the selves you have become still fit. They live together in the broad houses, and they give each other space within the shared rooms, and they know each other so thoroughly that privacy has become unnecessary, not because they have nothing to hide but because there is nothing left to discover that would diminish what they have.

Children are raised by everyone. Not in the modern lowland sense of communal childcare, with schedules and rotations and explicit agreements. In the Mokthar sense, which is simpler and more organic: the children belong to the plateau. They eat in whatever house is nearest when they are hungry. They sleep wherever they find warmth. They learn from whichever adult is doing the work they want to learn. The adults do not claim them, do not own them, do not direct them with any more authority than the goats direct their kids — which is to say, they model, they demonstrate, they correct when correction is needed, and they let the young ones find their own footing on the stone.

And the elders — the ones who are beginning the long turn toward death, who move more slowly, who sit more often — they are not revered and not ignored. They are simply present, the way the conifers are present: rooted, enduring, offering shade and the sound of wind through needles, asking nothing, providing what they can. When an elder dies, they are placed in the stream at the point where it leaves the plateau, and the water carries them down into the valley, and the people return to their houses, and the city continues. There is no grief in this, or there is grief but it is a small grief, a brief grief, the grief of watching a leaf fall from a tree — not because the tree is diminished but because the moment of separation is sharp and real and then it is over, and the tree is still there, and the leaf is becoming something else.

## Their Knowledge

They know things that the lowlands have forgotten, or never learned, or learned and discarded as useless.

They know the language of water — not its chemistry, not its mechanics, but its *mood*, its disposition, the way it wants to move on any given morning. A person from Mokthar can look at the surface of a stream and tell you whether the water is rising or falling, whether the spring that feeds it is strong or weak, whether the path it follows today is the same path it followed yesterday. This is not magic. It is attention, accumulated over lifetimes, passed down through example until it becomes instinct.

They know the stone the way a doctor knows a body — by listening to it, by touching it, by paying attention to the places where it is strong and the places where it aches. They know which outcrops are still settling, still finding their final shape, and they know not to build on those, to let the rock finish its conversation with the mountain before asking it to hold a wall. They know the timber the same way — which trees are healthy, which are beginning the slow turn toward death, which will stand for another century and which will fall in the next decade. They never take from a tree that is still growing. They wait until the tree tells them it is finished, and then they take what it offers.

And they know the pool.

Not its mechanism — they do not understand, any more than a lowlander would, why it reflects a sky that does not match the one above. But they know its *behavior*. They know that on some mornings the reflected sky shows clouds that will not arrive for three days. They know that on some evenings the reflected sun is lower than the real sun, as though the pool is looking at a time several hours ahead. They know that very rarely — once or twice in a lifetime — the reflection shows something that is not sky at all: a darkness that moves, a light that does not come from any sun, a shape that might be a mountain range that does not exist on any map of the world they know.

They have never tried to use this knowledge. They do not predict the weather from the pool, do not divine the future, do not attempt to communicate with whatever the pool is seeing. They accept it the way they accept the fog and the rain and the slow growth of moss — as a quality of the place, a feature of the world they inhabit, neither useful nor dangerous but simply *there*, and deserving of the same quiet respect they give to everything else.

When a child asks about the pool — and children do ask, once, when they are old enough to notice the discrepancy and young enough to think it should be explained — an adult will say something that translates roughly as: "The water sees further than we do." And the child will consider this, and find it sufficient, and go back to watching the reflected sky shift and settle, shift and settle, learning without instruction that some things are not meant to be understood but simply witnessed.

## Their Inner Lives

You cannot know this. They would not tell you if you asked, and you would not ask, because asking would be a violation of something too fundamental to name. But you can observe, and you can infer, and you can sit in a plaza at dusk and feel what they seem to feel, and maybe that is close enough.

They are not bored. This is the first thing to understand, because it is the first thing a lowlander would assume — that a life without change, without progress, without the stimulation of novelty must be a life of tedium. But the people of Mokthar are not bored for the same reason that a tree is not bored, or a river is not bored: because they are engaged in the full experience of being what they are. Every day is the same in the way that every breath is the same — identical in structure, unique in the living of it, and never once cause for complaint.

They are not unhappy. They are also not happy in the way that lowlanders chase happiness — the rush of excitement, the thrill of acquisition, the surge of emotion that accompanies getting what you want. They have what they want. They have had it for as long as anyone can remember. The wanting has burned down to something quieter, a low and steady warmth that does not flare and does not go out, and they live within that warmth the way they live within the cool damp air, as an element of their existence rather than a response to it.

They think. This might be the hardest thing for a visitor to accept — that these quiet, slow-moving people, who speak in half-sentences and sit for hours without visible occupation, are thinking constantly, deeply, with a rigor and patience that would exhaust a lowland philosopher in an afternoon. Their minds are not idle. Their minds are engaged in the sustained contemplation of what is — not what was, not what might be, but what *is*, right now, in this moment, and in every moment that has ever been and will ever be on this plateau.

They have, over generations, thought their way to the bottom of things. Not to the bottom of all things — they would be the first to acknowledge that there are depths they have not reached, questions they have not answered, mysteries that the pool reflects but does not resolve. But to the bottom of *their* things, the questions that arise naturally from living in this place, on this rock, beside this water, under this sky. They have thought about what it means to be a person who does not change in a city that does not change, and they have arrived at an answer that satisfies them, and they do not need to talk about it because the answer is not a proposition but a way of being.

The answer, if it could be expressed in lowland language, would be something like: *We are not separate from the stillness. We are the stillness, noticing itself.*

But they would never say this. They would look at you with those pale, settled eyes, and they would perhaps tilt their head slightly, and they would wait for you to realize that the answer is not something they can give you but something you must find by sitting still long enough to hear it in the water and the wind and the slow beating of your own patient heart.

## Their Relationship to the City

They did not build Mokthar.

This is the secret that the city keeps, that the stones would tell you if they could speak, that the people know and do not say because there is no need to say it. The city was here before them — not in its current form, not with the shaped stone and the fitted timber, but in its essence, in the arrangement of plateau and water and rock that makes the place what it is. They found it. They arrived, the way the city arrived, placed by a hand that knew exactly where it belonged.

And then they built, because building is what people do, but they built according to the plan that the plateau had already drawn. The streams showed them where the walls should go. The outcrops told them where the foundations should rest. The conifers indicated where the open spaces should be, because the trees were there first and their roots had already claimed the ground, and the people understood that the trees' claim was older and more legitimate than their own.

This is why nothing decays in Mokthar. This is why the city holds its equilibrium. The people are not maintaining the city. The city is maintaining itself, and the people are part of the system it maintains — along with the moss and the grasses and the goats and the birds and the water that threads through everything like a pulse. The city does not need the people. The city includes the people. There is a difference.

And the people understand this. Not as an intellectual proposition but as a lived reality, as the ground they walk on and the air they breathe and the water they drink from the stream that has been flowing since before they were born and will flow after they are carried away on it. They belong to Mokthar. Mokthar does not belong to them.

This is the source of their stillness. This is why they do not strive, do not accumulate, do not fear the future or mourn the past. They are held. Not by a god, not by a force, not by anything that could be named or worshipped or supplicated, but by the city itself, by the perfect fit between who they are and where they are, by the equilibrium that has found them and included them and will not let them fall.

## What They Are Becoming

And yet.

And yet there is the pool, and the pool reflects a sky that is not this sky, and the people who watch the pool — which is all of them, at one time or another — see something that the city alone cannot explain.

They are becoming something. Not something different — they are not evolving in the lowland sense, not progressing toward a goal or transforming into a new form. They are becoming more fully what they already are. More still. More settled. More deeply in contact with the stillness that underlies the stillness, the quiet that lies beneath the quiet, the place where even the sound of water ceases and there is only the holding.

Some of the elders — the ones who are nearest to death, who have begun to spend their days sitting by the pool with their eyes half-closed — have reported, without urgency, without emphasis, that they can feel the plateau thinking. Not metaphorically. Actually. The stone beneath them has a mind, and the mind is slow and vast and patient, and it has been thinking a single thought for as long as the mountain has been rising, and the thought is: *I am here.*

And the elders, who have spent their lives listening to water and wind and the small sounds of the city, can hear this thought now, can feel it resonating through the schist and into their bones, and they find that they agree with it. *I am here.* Yes. That is enough. That has always been enough.

When they die — when the body that has held them is placed in the stream and carried away — something of them remains. Not a ghost. Not a spirit. Something more like an imprint, a warmth left in the stone where they sat, a memory held in the water that flows past the place where they lived. The city remembers them the way it remembers everything — not with attachment, not with loss, but with the simple acknowledgment that they were here, and that their being-here was part of the city's being-here, and that both of these things are true and will continue to be true long after the names and the faces have been forgotten.

The people of Mokthar are becoming the city. Not dying into it, not dissolving into it, but growing into it, the way moss grows into stone, the way roots grow into cracks, the way water grows into the channel it has carved. They are becoming the stillness they have practiced. They are becoming the quiet they have kept. They are becoming the held.

And the pool reflects this too, sometimes, in the early morning when the fog is lifting: a figure at the edge of the water, not quite human, not quite stone, something in between that looks up at the sky with pale eyes and sees the reflection of a world that has found its balance and chosen to remain there, standing, breathing, listening to the slow and patient heartbeat of the mountain beneath.
