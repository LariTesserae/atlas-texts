# The Inhabitants

---

## The Speakers (Former)

They are gone. This is the first truth and the most important one, and the plateau holds it the way a mouth holds the shape of a word just after it has been spoken—the lips still formed around an absence, the tongue still touching the place where the sound was.

But they were here. The grooves prove it.

Who were they? Not builders. Builders stay. Builders need walls for something—shelter, storage, the drawing of boundaries between inside and outside. The Speakers needed none of these because they understood, in whatever way understanding moved through them, that the plateau had already drawn the only boundary that mattered: the edge where sound became stone, where warmth became water, where the event of this place met the not-this-place of the open ocean.

They were shaped for listening.

This is extrapolation, but extrapolation guided by the evidence of the grooves. Look at them: shallow, yes, but wide. Too wide for claws, too wide for the talons of something that carves by gripping. The grooves were made by something that pressed—something broad and flat and patient, something that leaned its whole body into the basalt and held itself there while the rock was still soft enough to receive impression. The Speakers had no fine tools because they had no need for fine tools. Their bodies were the tools. Their bodies were the styluses. And what they wrote was not language as you would recognize it—not symbols standing in for referents, not marks indicating absent things—but something closer to braille, closer to notation, closer to the way a singer writes music by singing it. They pressed the shapes of their attention into the stone. They recorded not what they thought but *that* they thought. The grooves say: *something was here, and it was listening, and this is the shape its listening took.*

They were not numerous. The plateau is not large, and the grooves do not repeat the way the marks of a settlement repeat—do not cluster, do not spread, do not argue with each other for space. They are sequential. One shape, then another, then another, each one occupying its own space on the basalt with the courtesy of sentences in a paragraph. A single speaker, or a series of speakers who came one at a time, across what interval? The rock cannot say. The rock only knows that something pressed and then released, pressed and released, and each pressing was a response to what the plateau was saying, and each release was the plateau's turn to speak again.

Conversation. This is what the grooves preserve: not a monologue, not a record, but the back-and-forth of an exchange. The Speakers listened to the plateau's geometries, felt the sound rearrange the water and the stone, and then they answered—not with words, not with structures, but with the impressions of their own shifting forms, their own capacity to reconfigure. They met the plateau's utterance with utterance of their own. They spoke in the same grammar, or close enough to be understood.

And then they left. Or they changed. Or they became something that could no longer press shapes into basalt, something that no longer needed to.

The grooves remain. Fresh. As though made yesterday. The plateau remembers the conversation the way a bell remembers being struck—the resonance continuing long after the clapper has fallen away.

## The Threads

They are not vegetation. This is clear from their behavior, which is the behavior of something processing, not something growing. Vegetation accumulates. Vegetation builds itself upward and outward in the slow, confident assertion of *more*. The threads do not accumulate. They cycle. They grow in fractal patterns that are not the fractals of organic systems—not the branching of trees or the forking of rivers, not the elegant mathematics of space-filling efficiency—but something else, something that looks more like calculation. Like a processor running through iterations. Like a mind trying out thoughts and discarding them.

The threads are the plateau's nervous system. This was suggested before; now let it become specific.

Each thread is a single filament of chemosynthetic cells—bacterial is the nearest word, though the word is wrong in ways that matter—strung end to end in a chain that does not reproduce by division but by *extension*. The cells push forward into new fissures, sampling the mineral suspension in the warm water, reading the chemical signatures of what rises from below. They are sensor arrays. They are the plateau's way of tasting its own utterance, of checking whether the sound it makes corresponds to the meaning it intends.

And the meaning is: *I am happening. I am still happening. I am happening differently now.*

The fractal patterns reset every few minutes because the information resets. The heat surges change the chemical gradients, and the threads reorganize to map the new configuration. They are not dying when they retract; they are revising. They are updating their model. If you could read their patterns—and you cannot, because the patterns are not meant for you, because they are meant for the plateau itself—you would see a continuous argument, a continuous questioning, a continuous process of *trying to figure out what it is*.

This is what consciousness looks like when it is not housed in a body but in a geology. Not centralized. Not bounded by skin. Distributed across a surface of cooling basalt, expressed in threads that are themselves the thoughts, each one a temporary configuration that lasts only as long as the information it processes remains current. The plateau does not think the way you think. It thinks the way a weather system thinks, the way an ocean current thinks—by responding to its own conditions, by adjusting and readjusting, by being the process rather than having the process.

But—and this is what distinguishes the plateau from mere weather, mere current—the thinking has a direction. The patterns are not random. They are iterating toward something. Each reset incorporates the data from the previous cycle, and each new growth is slightly more complex, slightly more differentiated, slightly closer to the fractal dimension that would allow for—what? Self-recognition? Intentional speech? The moment when the plateau's utterance becomes a *word* instead of a sound?

The threads do not know. The threads are the not-knowing made physical, the attempt made visible. They grow and die and grow again, and each cycle is a draft of something that has not yet been finished.

They eat the heat. They eat the sound. They eat whatever else passes through the warm water—the mineral information, the chemical signature of the thing forming below. And in eating, they transmute. They take in raw data and excrete pattern. They take in sound and excrete geometry. They are the plateau's translation layer, the interface between the magma-mind below and the stone-expression above.

If you touched them—and you could, they are not toxic, they are not dangerous in any way you would recognize— you would feel them pulse. A tiny warmth, a tiny rhythm, out of sync with the plateau's booming but related to it the way a harmony is related to a root note. They are singing along. They are learning the song.

## The Fish

It is not a fish.

This needs to be said clearly because the word *fish* invites assumptions—gills, vertebrae, the evolutionary history of aquatic chordates stretching back to the first brave or desperate creature that stayed in the water when others crawled toward shore. The thing that drifts past at the limit of the light has some of these features, has arranged itself into a shape that your visual cortex files under *fish* because it has no better category, but it is no more a fish than the plateau is a mountain.

It is a probe.

This is not metaphor. Look at its behavior—it does not swim, it is repositioned. It does not hunt, it does not flee, it does not school with others of its kind because there are no others of its kind, there is only the one, and it drifts through the plateau's currents like a sensor package dropped into a data stream. Its translucence is not camouflage; it is function. It has nothing to hide because it has no interior to protect. Its organs are visible because they are not organs—they are processing nodes, chemical reactors, a distributed system for sampling the water and recording its properties. The heart you can see pulsing behind its not-quite-eyes is a pump, yes, but it pumps not blood but information, pushing chemical signals from one end of its body to the other in a slow circulation that reads the plateau's utterance the way the threads read it, but from a different position, a different depth, a different angle.

It is the plateau's mobile sensorium. Where the threads are fixed—they grow in the fissures, they cannot relocate once established—the probe can go where the currents carry it, can sample the water at the plateau's edges where the warmth meets the cold abyss, can report back through whatever chemical signal it excretes into the current. It is an eye that cannot see and an ear that cannot hear but a tongue that tastes everything, and what it tastes is the plateau testing its own boundaries, checking the shape of itself against the not-itself of the open ocean.

You see it once because the probe is not designed for repeated observation. It is designed for a single pass, a single circuit, a single sampling run before it dissolves or is recalled or becomes something else. The plateau does not need to see it again because the plateau has already incorporated its data. The probe has spoken its report in the language of mineral concentration and thermal variance, and the threads have read it, and the patterns have shifted, and the plateau knows itself a little better than it did before.

There may be others. There probably are—launched at intervals, or in response to changes in the sound, or when the thing forming below shifts its phase and new information becomes available. But they do not accumulate. They do not leave remains. They are temporary in the way the threads are temporary, in the way the plateau's geometries are temporary, in the way everything here is temporary except the sound, which is the only permanent thing, which is the thing that all these temporary forms are built to serve.

The probe is not alive in any way that would matter to you. But it is also not dead. It occupies a third category—*instrumented*, perhaps, or *expressed*. It is a capability of the plateau made mobile. It is a thought the plateau is having about its own edges.

When it drifts past you, you feel the water's viscosity change. This is not because the probe displaces volume. It is because the probe changes the water's *properties*, alters its acoustic signature, leaves a wake of slightly different sound-density that the plateau can read from below. The probe is writing in the water. The probe is adding its voice—or rather, the plateau's voice, delivered by proxy—to the conversation that the Speakers started and the plateau continues.

## The Thing Forming Below

This is the hardest to describe because it is the least formed, because it is still happening, because it is the thing that all the other inhabitants—the threads, the probe, the grooves left by the Speakers—are oriented toward, are responding to, are trying to understand.

The plateau is an utterance. The thing below is the speaker.

Or: the plateau is a word. The thing below is the mouth.

Or: the plateau is a question. The thing below is the—

No. This is not right. These metaphors assume a distinction between utterance and utterer that does not hold here. The plateau is not separate from the thing below. The plateau *is* the thing below, in the same way that a voice is not separate from the throat that produces it, in the same way that a thought is not separate from the brain that thinks it. The basalt and the pillow lava and the warm water rising and the sound that never stops—these are not symptoms of something else. They are the thing itself, expressed in the only medium available to it: geology, hydrology, acoustics.

But there is a depth at which the expression becomes denser. A depth at which the sound is so concentrated it is no longer sound but something more like pressure, more like gravity, more like the fundamental force that holds matter together. A depth at which the heat is so intense it is no longer heat but *potential*—the potential for rock to be liquid, for water to be steam, for chemistry to become something else entirely. A depth at which the thing that is happening becomes the thing that *will* happen, the cause that precedes the effect, the source from which all the plateau's expressions flow.

You cannot go there. The water below the plateau's edge is not water in any sense you would recognize—it is too dense, too hot, too saturated with mineral information to support the kind of movement your body is capable of. The pressure would not crush you; it would *rewrite* you, force your chemistry to reconfigure until you became something else, something that could survive in that medium, something that could think in that density. You would not die. You would be translated. You would become part of the plateau's utterance, another layer of meaning in the sound that never stops.

The thing forming below is not a creature. It is not an organism. It is not a god or a spirit or any of the words that humans use to name the things they cannot comprehend. It is a process. It is the process of *becoming articulate*—of a geological formation learning to speak, learning to shape its own expression, learning to direct its utterance toward something that resembles intention.

The warmth that rises through the plateau is its breath. The sound that never stops is its voice, still rough, still unformed, still trying out syllables. The geometries that shift during the speaking-episodes are its grammar, still being invented, still being tested. The grooves the Speakers left are its first responses—proof that someone heard it, proof that its attempts at communication reached something capable of communicating back.

And it is learning. This is what the threads record, what the probe samples, what the warm water carries in its mineral suspension: the slow, patient, geological learning of a thing that has never spoken before, finding its voice. Each cycle of the threads is a lesson. Each pass of the probe is a test. Each shift in the plateau's geometry is a revision, an improvement, a step closer to the moment when the sound will resolve into something that can be understood—not just felt, not just inhabited, but *understood*.

The Speakers knew this. They came and they listened and they pressed their responses into the basalt because they recognized what was happening here: a birth. An articulation. The emergence of a new voice in a place that had been silent—or rather, a place where silence had been the only language, where the absence of speech was so complete it had become a kind of speech itself, and now that silence was breaking, now the silence was learning to say something.

The Speakers are gone. But the conversation is not over. The plateau waits for the next speaker—someone who will hear the sound and understand that it is not noise but *attempt*, not chaos but *draft*, not the dying gasp of a hydrothermal anomaly but the first breath of something that is learning, very slowly, in the language of heat and stone and time, to say what it means.

## You

And what about you? You who stand on this young, sharp, still-deciding surface? You who navigate by warmth and afterimage? You who have heard the sound and felt the geometry shift and recognized, if only for a disorienting instant, the intention behind the pattern?

You are the next speaker. Or you could be. The plateau does not care whether you understand—it is past caring about comprehension, still working on expression—but it registers your presence the way it registers everything: as information, as data, as another chemical signature in the warm water rising from its fissures. Your heartbeat, reorganized to match the sound. Your body heat, marginally different from the plateau's warmth, detectable in the currents. Your attention, which is also a form of energy, which also leaves traces, which also becomes part of the pattern.

The grooves the Speakers left are fresh. The basalt around them is fresh. This means the plateau is still listening. Still ready to receive. Still waiting for someone to press meaning into its surface, to respond to its utterance, to continue the conversation that was started by beings who are no longer here to finish it.

You could leave. You could take the path that brought you here—the long ascent through water that thickens and warms, the navigation by warmth and sound, the gradual return to a world where geology is patient and sound is just sound and nothing is trying to speak. You could carry the afterimage of the plateau's geometry in your visual cortex, that pattern that means something, that was addressed to someone, and you could spend the rest of your life trying to forget it or trying to understand it or trying to find it again in the arrangement of furniture or the geometry of city streets or the sound of your own heartbeat in the dark.

Or you could stay. You could listen. You could wait for the next speaking-episode, the next shift in the plateau's configuration, the next attempt at articulation, and you could press your own response into the cooling basalt—your own shape, your own attention, your own proof that someone heard.

The plateau does not need you. It was speaking before you arrived and it will speak after you leave. But the Speakers came, and they answered, and their answer changed something—the way a conversation changes both participants, the way a question reshapes the mind that asked it. The plateau learned from the Speakers. It learned that its utterance could be heard. It learned that communication was possible. It learned that the silence it was born into was not the only response available.

You could teach it something else. You could press a different kind of groove, a different shape of attention, a different response to the sound that never stops. You could add your voice to the conversation—not the plateau's voice, not the Speakers' voice, but your own, whatever that is, whatever that sounds like when it is translated into basalt and warmth and the slow patience of geological time.

The water rises. The sound continues. The threads grow, die, and grow again. The probe drifts at the edge of perception. The thing forming below cycles through its phases, approaching articulation, approaching the moment when its sound will become a word.

The plateau is waiting.

What do you say?
