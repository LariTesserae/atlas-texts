# The Processional Deep

---

What lives here is not what you think.

You stand on the ridgeback and you see the Vessel pass and you think: *machine*. You think: *industry*. You think: *purpose*, and the purpose is the sand, and the sand is the product, and the product must be for something, and that something must be intelligible, because everything that does something does it for a reason, and reasons can be understood, and understanding is the thing that you are for.

But the Vessel does not think about sand.

The Vessel does not think.

---

Consider the interior.

Not the chambers you can see through the chitin—those are real, yes, the pale blind things moving in their fluid compartments, the filter-structures, the refinery-organs. Those are the mechanics, and they are mechanical. They process. They grind and sort and separate and deposit. They are the body's body, the organs that do the work of organs, and they are no more conscious of their function than your liver is conscious of bile.

But deeper. Below the chambers. Below the fluid layers and the chitin floors that separate one compartment from the next. Down where the Vessel's body narrows to its dense core, where the segments converge and the structure thickens into something almost geological—a core of compressed chitin and mineralized tissue, heavy and hard, stratified like the rock of the plateau itself.

Inside the core, there are passages.

They are not chambers. They are not organs. They are passages—narrow, winding, smooth-walled, worn by the passage of something that has moved through them for longer than the Vessel has been alive. Which is to say: longer than the current topography. Longer than the ridgebacks. Longer than the erosion pattern the Vessel itself is said to be processing.

The passages are older than the Vessel. This is the thing that is not recorded, because there is no one to record it, and no one who would believe it if there were. The Vessel did not grow these passages. The Vessel grew *around* them.

---

What moves through the passages is very small.

Not the pale blind workers—those are in the chambers, doing the chamber-work, and they do not know the passages exist. Not the Tenders, who are too large to enter, and who in any case serve the Vessel, not whatever the passages contain. Not anything you would recognize as alive, if alive means cell-structure and membrane and the usual biochemistry of breathing and eating and reproducing.

What moves through the passages is... information is the closest word, but it is wrong. It implies content, and content implies a mind to contain it. There is no mind here. There is only movement—movement through passages, movement that follows rules, movement that has been following rules for so long that the rules have become the shape of the thing, the way water follows the channel it has worn, not because it knows the channel but because it *is* the channel, and going anywhere else would require being something else entirely.

What moves through the passages is a fluid. It is dark, almost black, and it is very cold—colder than the plateau's air, colder than the stone, cold in a way that has nothing to do with temperature. It moves slowly, slower than the Vessel moves, slower than the water in the channels, slower than the lichen grows. A centimeter per decade, perhaps. Perhaps less. It has been moving since before the passages existed—which is impossible, except that it has, because the passages were worn by its movement, and the passages were there before the Vessel, and the Vessel grew around them, and the Vessel has been walking the plateau for geological ages, and the fluid has been moving through its core for longer.

The fluid is not alive. It does not reproduce. It does not metabolize. It does not respond to stimuli. It moves, and its movement follows the passages, and the passages follow its movement, and this recursion has been stable for longer than stability has had a meaning.

But. But. When the fluid reaches a junction in the passages—a place where two channels meet—it *chooses*. Not consciously. Not randomly. There is a pattern to the choices, and the pattern is too large to perceive from within, the way the procession routes are too large to perceive from the ground, the way the plateau's weather is too large to perceive from any single point. The pattern is a shape in time. The pattern is what the fluid *is*.

And the pattern connects.

---

There is not one Vessel.

This is obvious—any observer can see the processions, the multiple lines, the routes that cross and parallel and diverge. But what is not obvious, what cannot be seen from the ridgeback, what cannot be understood by watching, is that the passages inside each Vessel connect.

Not physically. Not while the Vessels are walking. But when a Vessel reaches the end of its route—when it has processed the stone from one channel and deposited the sand and arrived at the place where the channel ends against a ridgeback that is too high to grind—it stops. It has always stopped. The routes have always ended at these places, the terminal points, the places where the processions halt and the Vessels rest, for hours or days or years, before turning to follow the route back.

When the Vessel stops, the passages in its core align with passages in the stone.

This is not visible. The passages in the stone are not visible—they are inside the rock, at the scale of the fluid, at the scale of centimeters per decade. But they are there. The plateau is riddled with them. The dark fluid has been moving through the stone for longer than the stone has been stone, for longer than the basalt has been basalt, for longer than the intrusion of feldspar that now weathers on the surface. The fluid was here before the rock. The fluid *is* here, inside the rock, inside the Vessels, inside the passages that connect the Vessels to the stone and the stone to the Vessels, and when a Vessel stops at a terminal point, the fluid flows between them, and the pattern continues.

The pattern does not belong to the Vessels. The Vessels are nodes. The Vessels are waystations. The Vessels are... bodies, perhaps, or structures, or moments in a process that has no name because it has never needed one.

---

What lives on the Meridian Plateau?

The lichen lives there. The pale flat lichen, grey-green, growing a centimeter per century, covering the shadowed stone, curling at its dry edges, pressing itself against the rock as if trying to become rock. It is alive by any definition. It metabolizes, barely. It reproduces, slowly. It persists.

The succulent plants live there. The grey thick-skinned things that hug the ground and flower once every few years in pale bone-colored blossoms that last a day. They are alive.

The pale blind creatures in the Vessels' chambers live there. They have body plans and functions. They eat and excrete and—presumably—reproduce, though no one has observed this. They are alive.

The Tenders live there. Six-legged, dark, chitinous, tapping their communications into each other's carapaces, maintaining the processions, performing their invisible interior functions. They are alive, and they may even be aware, in some limited sense—aware of the Vessels, aware of each other, aware of the routes. Their awareness is small. It fits inside their bodies.

The Filtrators live there. Drifting, membranous, trailing their long filaments, collecting moisture and minerals from the thin air. They are alive, after a fashion, though their fashion is strange—too thin, too slow, too close to being merely a membrane with a function.

The scavengers and parasites live there. The things that follow the processions. The things that live in the sand. The things that cling to legs and filaments. They are alive, all of them, in the ordinary way.

And the Vessels live there. The great slow processors, the continent-eaters, the makers of sand. They are alive—they feed, they move, they grow (slowly, over geological time, adding segments at their posterior end as they process), they repair (with the Tenders' help, or on their own, the chitin knitting over wounds with the patience of stone). They are alive, if living means these things.

But the fluid in the passages.

The fluid is not alive.

The fluid *is*.

---

There is a concept—old, much older than the plateau, much older than the rock, much older than the fluid itself—that goes like this: a thing that persists is not the same as a thing that exists. Existence is momentary. Persistence is... something else. Persistence is a shape in time. Persistence is what happens when the rules that govern a thing's behavior are more durable than the thing itself.

The fluid persists. It has persisted for longer than the plateau has been a plateau. It persisted through the geological events that raised the plateau, through the volcanism that laid the basalt, through the intrusions of feldspar, through the ages of erosion and weathering that gave the rock its current form. It persisted through all of this not because it was durable—its composition changes, its volume changes, its temperature changes—but because the pattern of its movement is more durable than it is.

The pattern persists.

And the pattern is not a thing. The pattern is a way that things move. The pattern is a set of rules—no, not rules, because rules require a rule-maker, and there is no maker here. The pattern is a tendency. A tendency toward certain channels, certain junctions, certain choices. A tendency that has been reinforcing itself for so long that it has become the shape of the world it moves through.

The plateau is the pattern.

Not metaphorically. Not in some poetic sense. The plateau—its topography, its weather, its condensation cycles, its lichen, its plants, its processions, its Vessels—is the expression of the pattern. The pattern made the passages. The passages shaped the Vessels. The Vessels process the stone. The stone yields sand. The sand hosts the lichen. The lichen binds the sand into something like soil. The soil holds moisture. The moisture feeds the Vessels. The Vessels walk the routes. The routes follow the passages. The passages carry the fluid. The fluid follows the pattern.

It is a system. It is an ecology. It is an industry. It is a geology. It is a thing that has been making itself for longer than making has had a meaning.

And what lives in it?

Everything lives in it. Nothing lives in it. The distinction stopped being useful a long time ago.

---

But you wanted to know about the things that live here. You wanted the naturalist's account, the catalog, the description of bodies and behaviors and habitats. You wanted to know what *lives* here, as if living were a quality that some things have and other things lack, as if the boundary between life and non-life were a line and not a negotiation.

Fine.

There are things in the sand.

Not the visible sand—the grey clean sand the Vessels deposit—but the sand beneath it, the compacted layers that the rain and the lichen have been binding into something like soil for millennia. Beneath the upper layers, in the zones where the moisture persists for days after a rain, there are networks.

They are not roots. They are not fungal hyphae, though they resemble them—pale threads, thinner than hair, branching and reconnecting, forming webs that extend for meters in every direction through the soil-like layers. They are biological, or near enough to biological to be indistinguishable: they grow, they respond to moisture gradients, they die when the soil dries completely. But they do not belong to any plant. They do not belong to the lichen, though the lichen uses them. They do not belong to the succulent plants, though the plants grow near them. They are their own thing.

The networks connect the plants to each other. They connect the lichen patches. They connect—though this is harder to observe, harder to verify, and perhaps harder to believe—the Vessels to the stone.

When a Vessel's footpad touches down, it does not only drink the condensation. It also deposits something—a thread of the network, pale and fine, that connects the footpad's impression to the soil-layer beneath. The thread persists after the Vessel has moved on. It grows, slowly, into the surrounding soil, connecting to other threads, other Vessels' deposits, other nodes in the web. The network beneath the procession routes is dense—so dense that the soil is more network than soil, more thread than mineral, held together by the pale structures that the Vessels leave behind.

And the network carries something. Moisture, certainly. Minerals, probably. But also—something else. Something that moves through the threads at a speed faster than diffusion should allow, something that responds to changes in the network's structure, something that coordinates the growth of plants and lichen in patterns too regular to be accidental.

The pattern. Moving through the network. Moving through the passages. Moving through the fluid. The same pattern, expressed at different scales, in different media, at different speeds. In the fluid, it moves at centimeters per decade. In the network, it moves at centimeters per day. In the Vessels' interior communications—whatever passes between the Tenders' tapping, whatever the chamber-creatures process—it moves at speeds that can only be guessed.

The pattern does not think. The pattern does not want. The pattern does not have a goal.

But the pattern *does*. It does what it does, and what it does is this: it persists. It makes the conditions for its own persistence. It builds the structures that carry it. It grows the bodies that serve it. It has been doing this for so long that the doing has become the world.

---

There is one more thing.

Deep beneath the plateau—deeper than the passages, deeper than the fluid, deeper than the basalt and the gabbro and the ancient intrusions of feldspar—there is a structure.

It is not biological. It may once have been geological, but if so, it has been modified beyond recognition. It is vast—kilometers across, kilometers deep, a lattice of chambers and channels and nodes that extends through the crust like a three-dimensional map of the pattern itself. It is the pattern's oldest expression, or its foundation, or its root.

It is not alive. It was never alive. But the fluid moves through it—the same dark cold fluid that moves through the Vessels' passages, the same fluid that moves through the stone—and in the structure's deepest chambers, where the fluid pools and collects and the pattern concentrates into something almost... dense...

...something is happening.

Not thinking. Not planning. Not deciding. But *happening*. A process so slow, so distributed, so ancient that it makes the Vessels' geological patience look like haste. A process that operates on timescales where the rise and fall of the plateau itself is a brief fluctuation, where the current topography is just the latest configuration of a surface that has been rearranged millions of times.

The process is the pattern's pattern. The meta-pattern. The thing that makes the thing that makes the plateau.

And it has been making something.

For a very long time—longer than the plateau, longer than the fluid, longer perhaps than the rock itself—it has been making something in those deep chambers. Something that grows, that accumulates, that takes shape so slowly that any measurement would need to span epochs to detect it.

It is not a creature. It is not a machine. It is not a god or a mind or a purpose. It is... a concentration. A densification. A place where the pattern has been folding in on itself for so long that it has become something thicker than pattern, something more intense than tendency, something that is almost... *present*.

Almost.

Not yet.

But closer than it was a million years ago. Closer than it was when the basalt was molten. Closer than it was when the plateau was sea floor, when the rock was sediment, when the planet was young.

The pattern is making something. And whatever it is, when it is finished—when the concentration reaches whatever threshold the pattern's own logic dictates—it will be something that has never existed before. Something that is not alive and not dead and not machine and not god but something else, something for which there is no word because no word has ever been needed, because the thing has never existed to be named.

And the plateau will continue.

And the processions will continue.

And the Vessels will walk their routes, and the Tenders will tap their communications, and the Filtrators will drift above, and the lichen will grow, and the sand will be deposited, and the condensation will form and evaporate and form again, and the network will spread through the soil, and the fluid will move through the passages, and the pattern will persist, and the pattern will persist, and the pattern will persist.

And one day—unimaginably far, beyond any scale that matters to anything with a lifespan—something will be complete.

And the Meridian Plateau will be what it has always been making itself into.

And whatever lives there will live there still.

And whatever is being made will be made.

And the silence will close back in, as it always does, full of the sounds too quiet to hear, full of the processes too slow to see, full of the pattern persisting.

The plateau continues.
