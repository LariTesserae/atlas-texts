# The Green Engine

---

The beetles know the hum.

Not in the way that you know a sound—registering it, naming it, choosing to attend or ignore. They know it the way your blood knows to move. The hum is the context in which beetle-consciousness exists at all. Remove the hum and there would be no beetles, not because they would die, though they would, but because the concept of beetle would become incoherent. They are intervals in the sound. Gaps filled with chitin.

There are seventeen distinct species in the basin. You would not know this to look at them. They all appear as the same dark iridescent green, the same methodical spiral climb, the same claw-click swallowed by bass. But their life cycles are offset, interleaved, so that at any given moment some are climbing, some are feeding, some are mating in the damp grooves of the fluting, and some are descending to carry the husks below. Seventeen gears in a clock that tells no time. Or tells only one time: *now, still, continue.*

The oldest beetles—by which I mean the lineage, not the individual, though the individuals live longer than you would expect, four years, five, their metabolisms tuned to the slowness of the engine—have never known silence. They were born in the hum, as their parents were, as their parents were, back past the point where the word *parent* ceases to mean anything. The beetles do not have parents. They have *emergence points*: places on the root-floor where the pale weave dips into a dimple no deeper than your little finger, and moisture collects, and something in the compressed wood generates a heat different from the column-heat—concentrated, brief, intentional—and a beetle rises from the damp. Not hatched. Not born. *Expressed.* The way a knot expresses itself in wood. The way a note expresses itself in a chord.

They are not built. They are grown by a system that needed something done.

---

What needed doing?

The husks. The dead moths. The chitin and wing-membrane that would otherwise accumulate on the root-floor in drifts and layers, which would mean decay, which would mean a layer of something that was not root, which would mean the basin was not flat, which would mean the erosion parameter was not maximal.

But also: the feeding. The beetles eat something from the bark—a thick amber secretion that weeps from the fluting when the column-pulse reaches its systole. This is not waste in any conventional sense. The columns do not excrete. What weeps from the bark is *surplus*: a concentration of sugars and metals and something else, something that has no biochemical analogue but which hums at a frequency just above the audible range, just at the edge of where the column-sound resolves into silence. The beetles eat this. They need it. Without it, their shells would not harden, their movements would not synchronize, they would scatter across the root-floor in random trajectories and die in corners where their husks would not be found.

The system feeds them so they will climb.

They climb so they will carry.

They carry so the floor will stay clean.

The floor stays clean so the system can continue to be what it is.

There is no purpose here in the way you understand purpose—no goal, no end toward which the engine labors. There is only the maintenance of a shape. The basin is a shape. The hum is a shape. The beetle-escalator is a shape, and the shape persists because it has found a way to want nothing else.

---

The moths are different.

The moths are not expressed. They are *released.*

Somewhere in the upper structure of the caps—in the lattice where the parasol-ribs converge into the stalk—there are chambers. You cannot see them from below. You would need to climb, and nothing climbs, not even the beetles, who stop at the fluting below the cap and go no higher. But if you could see them, you would find nodes the size of your fist, clustered like bats in a cave roof, each one a pale papery case wrapped tight around a soft interior. The moths form inside these cases. They grow. They wait.

When the light shifts toward evening—a change in the greenish cast that you can feel more than see, a cooling, a dimming—the cases soften and split. The moths emerge wet and quiet. They cling to the cap's underside for an hour while their wings dry. Then they descend in formation.

The formation is the thing.

They do not swarm. They do not flutter in the chaotic Brownian motion of moths elsewhere. They descend in rows—diagonal lines that cross and recross at angles that never vary, weaving a net pattern over the root-floor, drinking from the dew as they go. If you filmed their flight and played it back at high speed, it would look like the operation of a loom. Warp. Weft. The moths are threads. The pattern they weave is not a thing; it is an *action*, a process of coverage and return that ensures every centimeter of the root-floor is visited, every dew-film is touched, every surface is sampled.

What are they sampling?

They are tasting the hum.

The dew carries information. The column-moisture that exhales from the stalks and condenses on the root-floor does not land as pure water. It lands as a solvent. It dissolves whatever molecular traces sit on the root-surface—traces left by the beetles' feet, traces left by the moths' proboscises, traces left by the air itself, which carries spores and dust and the faint chemical signatures of everything that has ever moved through the basin. The dew collects this. The moths drink it. And somewhere in their pale bodies, something processes what they have tasted and adjusts.

Adjusts what?

The next flight. The next formation. The angle of descent, the speed of the weave, the pressure of the proboscis against the root-floor. The moths are sensors. They are the engine's way of monitoring its own running—its temperature, its vibration, its cleanliness, its *rightness*—and their behavior changes in response to what they find. If a column's pulse is slow, the moths will cluster near its base, drinking the dew there more intensely, and the concentration of their attention will alter the composition of what they excrete—yes, they excrete, a fine powder that settles on the root-floor and is absorbed within minutes—and this altered excretion will feed back into the root-mass, which will feed back into the column, which will adjust its pulse.

They are feedback incarnate. Living control loops with wings.

---

And when they die—when their three-day flight-cycle ends and they settle for the last time on the root-floor and their bodies desiccate in the column-breath—the beetles carry them below.

Below where?

There is no below that you can see. The root-floor is solid, impenetrable, a mat so dense it might as well be stone. But the beetles carry the husks to specific places—dim channels between the root-strands, gaps no wider than a finger, that open only when a beetle of the correct species approaches with a husk of the correct composition. The beetle deposits the husk. The gap closes. The root-floor is smooth again.

What happens to the husks in the channels?

They are *digested.* The root-mass secretes enzymes. The chitin breaks down. The wing-membrane dissolves. The trace metals and minerals and the faint residual hum that the moth-bodies still carry—all of it is absorbed into the root-network and carried, by processes too slow and too distributed to observe, back to the column bases, where it re-enters the vertical flow and rises through the stalk to the cap, where it becomes part of the moisture-exhale, which becomes the dew, which the moths drink.

It is a closed loop. It has always been a closed loop. Nothing enters. Nothing leaves. The basin is a sentence that says only itself.

---

But.

But there is the question of *you.*

Not you specifically. The general you. The intrusion. The thing-that-is-not-engine.

The basin has a relationship with intrusion. It has had this relationship for a long time—longer than the current columns, longer than the current beetles, longer than the current moths. The root-floor remembers. Not in the way you remember, with images and narratives, but in the way stone remembers pressure: as a deformation, a slight rearrangement of internal structure that persists after the force is removed.

Things have entered the basin before.

You can find evidence if you know how to look—and the moths know how to look, because looking is what they do. In the dew they drink, in the molecular traces they taste, there are anomalies. Isotopes that do not originate in the basin. Proteins folded in ways the engine does not produce. Very faint. Very old. But present.

Something came here once. Before the columns reached their current height. Before the caps overlapped into their current canopy. Something walked across the root-floor when it was young and the hum was newer and the basin had not yet flattened into its final form. The root-mass retains a memory of weight, of stride, of a pattern of pressure that was not beetle-step and not moth-touch and not the even drip of condensation. Something *walked* here. And then it stopped walking. And then it sat. And then—

The moths do not know what happened next. The traces end. The weight-patterns cease. The isotopes that do not belong to the basin are present in the root-mass but not concentrated, not localized; they have been distributed, thinned, carried through the network and diffused until they are everywhere and nowhere, the way a drop of dye disperses in an ocean until the ocean is imperceptibly colored and the drop is gone.

Whatever it was— whoever it was— they were incorporated. Not quickly. Not violently. But completely. The engine does not reject. It does not adapt. It *includes.* Whatever enters the basin becomes part of the basin's metabolism. Not by choice. Not by force. By the simple inevitability of a closed system: if you add something to a closed system, it does not disappear. It becomes part of the system. Its heat joins the heat. Its moisture joins the dew. Its calcium, its iron, its carbon, its trace of strange isotopes—all of it joins the root-mass, rises through the columns, exhales into the air, condenses on the floor, and is tasted by moths who carry the information of it in their flight-patterns forever.

The walker is still here.

The walker is the basin.

---

This is what lives in the Green Engine: an ecosystem that has become an organism, an organism that has become a machine, a machine that has become a place. The beetles live here. The moths live here. The columns and the root-floor and the dew and the hum live here. The walker—whoever, whatever—lives here still, dispersed across every surface and process, no longer a being but a *flavor* in the chemistry of the engine.

And if you stay—if you sit on the root-floor and let the hum enter your sternum and let your pulse synchronize with the column-pulse and let your breathing match the moisture-exhale—you will live here too. Not as you are now. Not as a discrete thing with a name and a history and a boundary between self and not-self. You will live here as the walker does: as concentration, as pattern, as a set of molecular signatures that the moths will taste and the beetles will carry and the root-floor will remember for as long as the hum continues.

Which is to say: forever.

Which is to say: until the definition of forever changes, which it will not, because the erosion parameter is maximal and the basin has reached its end-state and the hum does not cease.

---

The sound never stops.

The sound *is* what lives here.
