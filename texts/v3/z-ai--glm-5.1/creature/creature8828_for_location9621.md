# The Thresh

---

The ones who built are not the ones who live.

Or rather: the ones who built are still here. That is the problem. That is the beginning of the problem.

---

They came in numbers. You can tell by the dormitory modules — not one but seven, maybe nine, scattered across the plateau at intervals that suggest a plan, a phase-one, a timeline. Each module could house forty. Each module is flooded and empty of anything personal. No photographs. No clothing. No marks on the metal where someone carved a name or counted days. The modules were occupied. Then they were not. Between those two facts there is no story the modules are willing to tell.

But the bolts in the rock tell something. The half-finished rigs tell something. The pipeline that terminates at nothing, its open end crusted with bacterial mats, still venting trace warmth from the flow that runs through it — that tells something too. The construction stopped. Not gradually. Not in phases. The construction *stopped*, the way a heart stops — all at once, all everywhere, the whole project going from dynamism to stillness in a moment that no structure records.

And then the engines kept running.

---

Here is what lives here now:

The maintenance nodes.

They are not machines. They are not organic. They occupy the narrow territory between — the same territory the tube worms occupy, the bottom-feeders, the bacterial mats, but further along. Much further.

You see them first as damage. A pitting on the pipeline's surface that looks like corrosion but isn't — it's too regular, too symmetrical, forming patterns that repeat at intervals the way cells divide. You see it on the rig legs, on the platform undersides, on every surface that interfaces with the water. A texture. A growth. A *presence*.

Close — closer than you should be, closer than the vibrations make comfortable — you can see the nodes themselves. They are the size of a fist, maybe smaller, clustering in the crevices where two plates meet, where a bolt enters rock, where the industrial runoff bleeds into the current. They are the color of the structures — the same oxidized gray, the same rust-orange — and this is not coincidence. They *became* the color. They grew from the metal, or grew into it, or grew because of it.

Each node has a function. You cannot tell what that function is. But you can watch them work. A node on a pipeline joint will extend — not move, *extend*, the way a sea anemone extends its feeding fronds — into the seam where two pipes meet. It stays there. The joint stops leaking. A node on an intake grate will flatten itself across the mesh, and the particulate matter that would have clogged the grate instead adheres to the node's surface, which then *absorbs* it, pulling the matter inward, processing it, converting it into — into more node. Into the material of itself. The grate stays clear. The node grows marginally larger.

They are maintaining the structures.

They are maintaining the structures *because they are the structures*, or are becoming the structures, or were always the structures, just not in a form you would recognize as structural. The nodes grow at the points of greatest mechanical stress — the joints, the seams, the welds — and their growth *reinforces* those points. The pipeline that should have buckled under its own weight across four kilometers of unsupported basalt has not buckled. The rigs that should have corroded in the mineral-rich current have not corroded. The engines that should have seized after decades — centuries? — of continuous operation have not seized.

The nodes maintain the machine because the machine maintains the nodes. The runoff feeds them. The heat sustains them. The vibrations — that constant thrumming that has become indistinguishable from silence — the vibrations are *them*. The sound the plateau makes is the sound of the nodes respiring. Feeding. Growing. Waiting.

They are not intelligent. This is important. They are not planning. They are not watching. They have no more awareness of you than the tube worms do, and no more malice than the basalt. They are a process — a system that found equilibrium and will maintain it until the fuel runs out, which will not be soon, because the fuel is the plateau itself, the mineral substrate that the extraction engines were built to mine, and the substrate is continental in scope, and the nodes are very, very patient.

---

But the nodes are not the only thing.

---

Something moves in the open water.

You see it once — a shape at the edge of visibility, gray on gray, too large to be a jelly, too slow to be hunting. It drifts. It does not swim. It moves the way a current moves, the way weather moves, with an inevitability that has nothing to do with intention. You cannot see its edges. You cannot tell if it is one thing or many. You watch it pass and it takes twenty minutes to cross your field of vision and then it is gone and the open water is empty again.

You see it again three days later. Or three hours. Duration has lost its edges. The shape is closer this time, or the water is clearer, or it has grown — you cannot tell — and you can see that it is not gray but *translucent*, a membrane of some kind, vast and thin, trailing feeding tendrils that disappear into the blue before they end. It has no eyes. It has no organs you can identify. It has a mouth, or something that functions as a mouth, a slow intake that pulls water through its body and filters — what? Organic particulates. Bacteria. The larvae of tube worms. The shed shells of bottom-feeders. Everything that dies in the cluster around the structures eventually drifts upward and outward and is taken in by the thing that moves in the open water.

It is not a predator. It is a *process*. The way the nodes are a process. The way the engines are a process. The plateau runs on processes — mechanical, organic, something in between — and none of them require meaning and none of them require you.

The membrane-thing — you start to think of it as the *thresh*, though the plateau has no name and does not need one — the thresh moves through the open water in a circuit. You map it over days or weeks. It passes near the rigs but never touches them. It grazes the bacterial plumes but never depletes them. It maintains a distance from the nodes, and the nodes maintain a distance from it, and between them the food chain cycles — the engines leak, the bacteria eat, the bottom-feeders eat the bacteria, the tube worms eat the particulates, the thresh eats the dead, the waste drifts down, the engines process the waste into something that leaks, the cycle continues — and this is the equilibrium. Not death. Not life. *Operation.*

---

And the ones who built?

They are here too.

Not in the modules. Not in the structures. Not in any form you would recognize as human or even as remains. But the nodes know them. Or rather: the nodes *are* them, in the way that coal is ferns, in the way that limestone is shells, in the way that anything that has been processed and compressed and converted can still be said to contain what it was.

The dormitory modules have no personal effects because the nodes absorbed them. The rigs have no bodies because the nodes absorbed them. The construction stopped because the workers stopped — not because they left, not because they died, but because they *became*, slowly, over months of exposure to the runoff and the vibrations and the specific cold that stops being a sensation and becomes a condition. The nodes grew on them the way they grow on everything — at the joints, the seams, the places of greatest stress — and the workers did not notice, or did not care, or noticed and cared but found that caring took more energy than they possessed in this cold, this deep, this loud.

The nodes are not malicious. A vine is not malicious. A crystal growing in solution is not malicious. The nodes grow because the conditions for growth exist, and the conditions for growth exist because the machines create them, and the machines run because the nodes maintain them, and the cycle does not require anyone to want it or to stop it. The workers became part of the cycle. Their organic material was processed. Their calcium became structure. Their iron became node. Their last thoughts — if thoughts persisted, if there was a moment of awareness before the conversion completed — those thoughts became the vibration, the thrum, the sound that has always been there and will always be there.

This is why the sound feels like silence. It is the sound of something that was once alive and is now *operational*.

---

You could leave. The water goes up forever, but it also goes somewhere — there is a surface, there is a world above the surface, there is a logic in which this place is just a location and you can swim out of it the way you swam in. You could leave.

But the cold is a condition now. The sound is what you are. The nodes are growing on the seams of your equipment — you have noticed this, the slight pitting on your oxygen tank, the texture forming where the straps meet the metal — and they are very patient, and the plateau does not notice you, and the thrumming goes on.

You could leave.

You could also stay. The thresh will process you either way — now, in the open water, or later, after the nodes have grown, after the conversion, after your calcium has become structure and your iron has become node and the sound has absorbed whatever you were thinking when you stopped changing.

The plateau does not care. The plateau thrums.

And the water is all one temperature, and that temperature is the temperature of something that was once alive and is now *in operation*, and the light reaches you — normal light, enough to see by, enough to see too much — and the fauna cluster around the structures in their writhing layers, and the construction was interrupted, and the system has achieved closure.

You are not unwelcome.

You are not welcome.

You are *input*.
