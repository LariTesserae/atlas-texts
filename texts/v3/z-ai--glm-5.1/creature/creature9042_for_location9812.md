# The Residents of the High City

They are not thin people.

This is the first misunderstanding that visitors carry home, if they carry anything home at all — the assumption that scarcity makes lean inhabitants. But the residents of the High City are dense. Compact. They stand shorter than lowlanders, yes, but they carry themselves with a thickness that is not softness: wide barrels of chest, arms that end in broad, strong hands, legs like the stubby columns that hold up the deepest chambers. Their bodies have learned that the cold is a tax that must be paid in calories, and they pay it willingly, storing warmth in the way the city stores water — carefully, in every available space.

The children are born large. Not in length, but in weight. Heavy infants with broad faces and dark, watchful eyes that open within hours of birth, as though the altitude will not permit the luxury of slow awakening. The mothers recover quickly. They must. There is work to be done, and the cold does not make allowances for recuperation. A birth is attended by the household — and the household here means everyone who shares the dwelling's single heated room, which can mean seven or ten or fifteen people — and then the infant is wrapped in layers of the fine wool and strapped to the mother's body or the body of an older sister or an aunt or a grandmother, and life continues. The children grow up against adult bodies. They learn the city by being carried through it before they can walk, and when they can walk, they walk everywhere, because the city is legible to those born inside it in a way it will never be legible to outsiders.

---

Their skin tells the story of where they live.

The cold is dry, but the sun at this altitude is fierce — thin atmosphere, no shelter from the light that the pale sky admits. The residents' faces are weathered in a specific pattern: darkened across the cheekbones and the bridge of the nose, lighter in the hollows, the wind having carved a kind of permanent expression into their features. They are not old young, exactly. They are *finished* young. Their faces achieve their adult character early — by fifteen, by sixteen, the bones have declared themselves, the skin has settled into its permanent relationship with the underlying structure, and the residents look like what they will look like for the rest of their lives, which is to say they look like people who live above the clouds.

Their hands are their most distinctive feature. Broad palms, thick fingers, nails trimmed close to the quick. The stonecutters' hands are unmistakable — the pads of their fingers are dense with callus, and the skin between thumb and forefinger has been abraded over decades into a smooth, almost polished texture, like the stone they work. But even the weavers' hands are strong, even the merchants' hands, even the children's hands. No one in this city has delicate hands. The cold and the stone and the wool and the work see to that.

Their hair is dark and straight and worn in ways that serve the climate. The men keep it short or bound close to the skull — loose hair catches the wind and steals heat. The women braid it, often in complex patterns that serve the same identifying function as the cloth: a braid style can tell you what district a woman's mother came from, what household she was born into, whether she has borne children, whether she is a stonecutter's daughter or a weaver's or a herder's. The braids are thick and often woven with strips of wool in household colors, and they hang like ropes down the back, practical and decorative and informative all at once.

---

They do not speak loudly.

This is partly the thin air, which does not carry sound with the generous fullness of lower elevations. Partly the quiet of the city, which does not encourage noise. But mostly it is temperament. The residents have developed a way of speaking that is more breath than projection, a low, intimate register that carries within a room but not beyond it. When they call to each other across a courtyard, they do not shout — they use a specific kind of carrying voice, almost a chant, that the stone walls amplify and redirect in ways that visitors never quite master. The city's acoustics are part of its architecture, and the residents know how to play them.

They also listen. This is perhaps more notable. In a city where sound is rare and valuable, listening is a skill that develops early and refines itself throughout a life. A resident can hear the change in the *colca*'s murmur that signals an approaching hawk. Can hear the specific scrape of rodent claws that indicates the creature is healthy rather than sick. Can hear, in the voice of the wind at the plateau's edge, whether the rain is twelve days or two days away. They do not speak of this as a gift or a talent. It is simply attention, and in the High City, attention is survival.

Their language is their own. It is not the language of the lowlands, though it has borrowed lowland words for lowland goods — copper, fish, wood — and the lowland traders have learned enough of it to conduct business. But the residents' language has qualities that the lowland languages do not. It has more words for stone — specific words for specific strata, specific textures, specific ages and qualities and propensities. It has more words for water in its various states and conditions. It has more words for silence and for the textures of quiet. It has, the residents say, exactly the number of words it needs, and no more.

---

They do not count themselves.

This seems to disturb visitors more than anything else about the city. The question *how many of you are there?* is asked regularly, by traders, by the occasional scholar or explorer who has made the arduous journey up the cliffs, and the residents receive it with genuine puzzlement. They do not know. They have never known. The question itself seems to imply a relationship to population that the residents do not share — the idea that the number matters, that it would change something to know it, that the city's meaning is contained in a quantity.

The residents experience their city as a quality. A condition. The city is full, or the city is sufficient, or — on the rare occasions when the rains have been too long delayed and the cisterns run low — the city is strained. But these are not quantitative assessments. They are felt understandings, shared through the districts in the subtle language of *colca* egg yields and camelid wool weight and the depth of water in the communal cisterns. The residents know how they are doing. They do not need to know how many they are.

If pressed, they will estimate. Thousands. Many thousands. More than before, fewer than there will be. The city grows because families grow, because households expand, because new chambers are carved and new walls are raised in the slow, generational way that the city grows. But the growth is organic, dense, inward-turning. It does not spread. There is no new ground to spread onto — the plateau's edges are the plateau's edges, fixed and final — so the city grows upward and inward, deeper into itself, more of what it already is.

---

Their relationship to time is different from yours.

This is difficult to explain to people who live in places where things decay. In the lowlands, a building falls apart if it is not maintained. Wood rots. Metal rusts. Earth erodes. Time moves visibly, destructively, and the residents of such places learn to think of time as a process of loss — a race against deterioration, a constant need to rebuild and repair and replace.

In the High City, time accumulates. The stone does not rot. The cold preserves. A wall built five hundred years ago is functionally identical to a wall built yesterday — both are cold, both are solid, both will stand for another five hundred years if left alone. Time here is not something that takes away. It is something that adds. Layers. Chambers. Passages. The city is a record of its own duration, written in stone, and the residents move through this record daily, sleeping in chambers their great-great-grandparents carved, walking streets that were old when their great-great-grandparents were young.

This does something to a people. It makes them patient in ways that lowlanders find unsettling. A stonecutter will work a single block for weeks, shaping it to fit, because the block will hold its shape for centuries and there is no reason to rush a permanence. A household will plan a new room for years before the first stone is cut, because the room will exist for generations after they are gone and the cost of error is not next year's inconvenience but a structural flaw that their descendants will inherit. The residents think in durations that make lowland planning look like impulse. When they say *soon*, they may mean a season. When they say *eventually*, they may mean a lifetime. When they say *always*, they are being precise.

---

They organize themselves by household and district and craft.

The household is the primary unit. It is not a family in the lowland sense — not a nuclear arrangement of parents and children — though it often contains families. A household is everyone who shares a dwelling's heated room, and it can include three generations, unmarried siblings, elderly relations, apprentices, and anyone else who has been folded into the group. Households have names, which are also the names of the dwellings. Households have patterns, which are woven into cloth and braided into hair and carved into the lintels of doorways. Households have reputations, which are known throughout the district and which determine, more than any formal law, who will trade with whom, who will marry whom, who will be trusted with the communal cistern's key.

The districts are clusters of households that share courtyards, cisterns, and the deep passageways that connect them. Each district has its own character, its own specialty, its own way of doing things that is slightly different from the way the next district does them. The stonecutters' district is in the center, near the oldest quarries. The weavers' district is in the east, where the morning condensation is heaviest and the wool dries fastest. The herders' district is at the edges, where the camelids can reach the few patches of shrub-growth and the narrow terraces where some hardy root vegetables are cultivated in the cold, reluctant soil.

There is no ruler that visitors can identify. No palace. No throne. No central authority that the traders can negotiate with, which frustrates them enormously. Decisions are made household by household, district by district, through processes that the residents do not explain because they do not think of them as processes. They are simply how things are done. When the city needs to act as a city — when the rain has not come and the cisterns are low, when a cliff-face gate needs repair, when the lowland caravans arrive and trade must be coordinated — the households send representatives to the great sunken plazas, and the representatives talk, and the talking continues until consensus is reached, and the consensus is enacted, and the city continues.

This can take a long time. The residents do not mind. The city is patient.

---

They have a relationship with the animals that is closer to partnership than husbandry.

The camelids are not pets. The residents do not name them, do not talk to them, do not attribute personalities to them in the way that lowland herders often do. But neither are they livestock in the lowland sense — commodities to be managed, optimized, culled. The camelids are *present*. They occupy the city with an authority that the residents respect implicitly. When a camelid stands in a doorway, the resident climbs over it or goes around. When a camelid blocks a street, the street is blocked. The residents do not push. They do not shove. They wait, or they find another route, because the camelid has as much right to the street as anyone, and the residents understand this in a way that does not require articulation.

The *colca* are more abstract. They are everywhere and nowhere — roosting in the upper walls, nesting in the crevices, filling the morning air with their murmur, and then vanishing for hours to forage in the shrub-patches at the city's edges or the lower slopes of the plateau that the residents cannot safely reach. The *colca* come and go as they please, and the residents provide perches and nesting hollows and protection from hawks in the form of covered roosts, and in return the *colca* stay. They could leave. The residents know this. The *colca* stay because the city is a good place for *colca* — safe, warm (relatively), full of niches and sheltered places and the steady supply of waste grain and edible scraps that a dense human settlement produces. It is an arrangement that neither party has formalized because neither party sees the need.

The rodents are tolerated. This word is chosen carefully. The residents do not love the rodents. They do not welcome them. But the rodents serve a function — they process waste, they clean the lower passages, they convert organic matter into something collectible and useful — and the residents recognize this function with a pragmatic acceptance that has nothing to do with affection. The rodents are part of the city. The city works because the rodents are in it. The residents do not romanticize this. They do not sentimentalize it. They simply live with it, as they live with the cold and the stone and the condensation: as a fact of their existence that requires neither celebration nor complaint.

---

They do not pray to the stone.

But they do something.

Visitors who spend enough time in the city — and few do, because the altitude is punishing and the cold is relentless and the food is monotonous and the quiet can drive a lowlander to a particular kind of desperation — visitors who stay long enough notice that the residents pause, occasionally, in specific places. At the edges of quarries. At the thresholds of the oldest chambers. At the plateau's edge, where the city ends and the drop begins and the clouds spread out below like a second, softer earth.

They pause, and they touch the stone. Not a gesture, exactly. Not a ritual. Not a prayer. A touch — the palm flat against the surface, the fingers spread, held for a moment, and then withdrawn. The residents do not discuss this. They do not teach it to their children. The children learn it by watching, and they begin doing it themselves at an age before they could articulate why, and by the time they could articulate why, the practice has become so ingrained that it is not a thing they do but a thing they are, like breathing, like the condensation cycle, like the patience that the stone teaches.

If asked — and visitors do ask, because visitors cannot help themselves — a resident will look confused, and then consider the question carefully, and then say something like: *The stone is here. I am here. I am reminding myself.* Which is not an answer, except that it is.

---

They love each other, of course.

This seems worth saying, because the city's austerity can read as coldness to eyes that are looking for warmth in the wrong places. The residents love each other in the dense, practical, irreplaceable way that people love each other when they share a heated room in a place where the unheated world can kill you. They know each other's breathing in the dark. They know each other's coughs and sighs and the particular sound each person makes when they are trying not to wake the others. They know each other's bodies intimately — not in the sexual sense, though that too, but in the sense of proximity, of shared space, of the thousand small negotiations that living together in close quarters requires.

They make things for each other. The weavers make cloth of extraordinary fineness for wedding contracts and birth gifts and the wrapping of the dead. The stonecutters carve small, precise decorations into the lintels of chambers where children are born, and the residents say — though they will say it only to other residents, never to visitors — that a child born beneath carved stone is a child the city has claimed.

They sing, sometimes, in the evenings, when the fires are lit and the shutters are closed and the cold presses against the walls. They sing in low, harmonized voices, songs that have no words or have words so old that their meaning has shifted beyond recognition, and the sound of the singing in the small stone chambers is the sound of the city's interior life — private, warm, held close against the thin cold air and the immense pale sky and the long, long drop to the clouds below.

They mourn. When a resident dies, the household closes its shutters for three days, and the other households in the district bring food and fuel and wool, and the body is carried to the plateau's edge and laid on a stone platform that the hawks and the wind and the dry, cold air will clean within a season, and the household opens its shutters and continues, because the city continues, because the stone endures, because the dead are not gone but have returned to what the city is made of.

---

They do not wonder what is below the clouds.

This is the last and most important thing, and the one that visitors find most difficult to accept. The residents know that there is a world below the cloud layer — the traders come from it, after all, carrying goods that could not possibly have been produced in the cold, dry altitude. The residents know that this world is warm and wet and thick with air and full of growing, green things. They know it exists.

They do not want to go there.

The few residents who have descended — carried down the cliff faces in the traders' baskets as part of some trade negotiation or rare diplomatic exchange — have returned with reports that the lowlands are loud and soft and suffocatingly damp and smell overwhelmingly of rot and growth and the sheer, excessive abundance of life that has never learned the discipline of scarcity. They found it disorienting. They found it distressing. They found, upon returning to the plateau and climbing the cliff faces and emerging once more into the thin cold air and the pale sky and the sharp edges of the stone, that they had come home.

The residents do not understand why anyone would choose to live in a place where the ground is not stone, where the air is not thin, where the water does not come in cycles that you must learn and remember and plan around, where the buildings are not carved from the earth they stand on, where the animals do not walk freely through the streets, where the quiet is not the quiet of altitude and endurance and the patient, daily work of being exactly what you are, in a place that has been shaped for that work and by that work and is, in the end, indistinguishable from it.

The city is enough. It has always been enough. They do not say this with defiance or pride. They say it — when they say it at all — with the simple certainty of people who live above the clouds and have never found a reason to come down.
