# What Lives Here

---

## The Minute

Begin with what you can hold in your palm, because the small things will teach you the grammar of this place.

The herds—there is no better word, though they are not animals as you understand animals—move in ribbons across the rhizome surface. A single ribbon may contain six million individuals, each no larger than your thumbnail, each chitinous and translucent and nearly weightless. From a distance they resemble spilled liquid, flowing over the contours of the plant-mass with a fluidity that seems impossible for solid bodies. Up close—kneeling, hand lens, patience—you see their structure: twelve segments, twelve legs, a mouth apparatus that folds inward like a flower closing, and a dorsal ridge that runs the length of their bodies and changes color.

The color-change is not decorative. It is not even communication in any sense you would recognize. It is *computation*.

Watch. When a ribbon approaches a fissure in the rhizome—a crack where the lithamnor wells up and the surface grows damp with copper-scented fluid—the individuals at the leading edge flush amber, then crimson, then a blue so deep it borders on ultraviolet. The color passes backward through the ribbon like a wave through a stadium crowd. Within seconds, the entire herd has altered course. Not one of them has vocalized. Not one of them has paused to deliberate. The decision emerged from the pattern itself, from the interaction of millions of simple color-responses that together produced a complex outcome.

You recognize this. Ant colonies do something similar. Slime molds do something similar. But the ribbons do it faster, with more nuance, across a spectrum of wavelengths you cannot fully perceive. Your ultraviolet camera reveals patterns in their dorsal ridges that your eyes alone would miss: intricate pulse-codes, flashes and counter-flashes, whole conversations occurring in a register just beyond your biology.

They are thinking. Not individually—no single member of the ribbon contains enough processing capacity for what you would call thought—but *collectively*. The ribbon is the brain. The individuals are neurons. And the thoughts they think are about chemistry, about lithamnor concentration, about the optimal path between one feeding site and the next.

What do they feed on? Each other, partially. The rhizome, partially—they scrape the surface for mineral residue and the lithamnor that seeps through. And something else, something you only discover when you observe them at night. The pillar-forms exhale. You know this; the description has mentioned it. Great sighing bursts of processed atmosphere venting from their summits. But what the description did not mention is that the exhalation carries particles. Microscopic. Crystalline. Dense with silicon compounds that the rhizome has refined and expelled as waste.

The ribbons feed on the waste.

They position themselves downwind of the exhalation vents. They open their mouth apparatuses and *breathe*. And the waste—refuse, exhaust, the discarded byproduct of continental industry—sustains them utterly.

Consider what this means. The rhizome processes. The ribbons consume the processing's waste. When the ribbons die—when they are crushed underfoot, when they are consumed by larger fauna, when they simply stop—their dissolved bodies return to the rhizome. The plant-mass takes their silicon, their chitin, their accumulated mineral wealth, and incorporates it.

It is a cycle. But it is not a cycle of nutrients, not in any sense your ecology textbooks would recognize. It is a cycle of *information*. The rhizome excretes data about its own internal state—chemical composition, processing efficiency, structural stress—and the ribbons read that data, metabolize it, and return it in altered form when they die. The continent is talking to itself, and the ribbons are the medium through which the conversation occurs.

You never learn this, not explicitly. You lack the instruments, the time, the theoretical framework. But you feel it, watching the ribbons flow and flash and shift. You feel that you are observing something that is, in its own alien way, a kind of mind.

## The Drinkers

Larger. Much larger. The size of groundcars, the size of small buildings, and they move with a slowness that makes geological time seem hurried.

You call them drinkers because that is what they do. They press their feeding appendages—long, jointed, covered in sensory filaments that deliquesce on contact with the rhizome surface—against the plant-mass and they *drink*. The lithamnor rises to meet them. You can see it happening: the surface darkens, grows glossy, then flows upward into the appendage with a movement that seems almost willing, almost offered.

The drinkers are not parasites. This is important, and it takes you days to understand why. A parasite takes without giving back. The drinkers take, but they give back something the rhizome cannot produce on its own: *mobility of processing*.

Watch a drinker feed. It attaches at one site, processes for hours—its body is a translucent sac, and you can see the lithamnor moving through it, changing color as it is filtered, refined, concentrated—and then it detaches, moves to a new site, and attaches again. The new site is not random. The drinker moves toward areas of the rhizome that are *stressed*—where the pillar-forms are struggling, where the processing throughput has dropped, where the grinding sound has shifted to a higher register that you learn to recognize as distress.

It deposits processed lithamnor at these sites. Enriched lithamnor. Lithamnor that has been passed through the drinker's body and emerged carrying compounds the rhizome needs but cannot synthesize efficiently on its own.

The drinkers are mobile refineries. They are also doctors.

No—they are *organs*. Unattached organs, moving freely across the body of the continent, responding to need, delivering medicine. The rhizome does not control them; it has no nervous system, no command structure. But it *signals*, chemically, through the lithamnor itself, and the drinkers have evolved to read those signals and respond. They are as much a part of the rhizome as the pillar-forms, as the cables, as the mat itself. They simply have a different architecture.

Their bodies tell the story of this integration. They have no digestive tract in any familiar sense. They have no separate circulatory and respiratory systems—everything is one flow, lithamnor in, lithamnor out, the processing occurring in the transit. They have no visible sense organs, but they navigate with absolute precision across a landscape that changes hourly. They have no apparent means of reproduction, and you never see young drinkers, but their numbers remain constant.

Where do new drinkers come from?

You find the answer on your ninth day, when a drinker collapses near your camp. It does not die—it has no mechanism for death in any way you understand death—but it *divides*. Over the course of a long afternoon, its translucent sac settles against the rhizome surface, thins, stretches, and splits. Two drinkers where one was before. Each immediately extends feeding appendages and begins to process. Neither seems aware that it was, minutes ago, half of something else.

The rhizome grows new pillar-forms. The drinkers grow new drinkers. The aerostats—you will come to them—grow new aerostats. Everything here grows more of itself. The continent does not reproduce; it *expands*. It does not have generations; it has extensions. It does not have offspring; it has *outcroppings*.

## The Aerostats

You have seen them. Sac-creatures, gas-filled, trailing feeding tendrils, cruising the thermals between the pillar-forms. They pulse with the continent's rhythm. They are the most visible evidence that the whole system breathes.

But visibility is not understanding.

The aerostats are not fauna. Not exactly. Not in the way the ribbons are fauna, or the drinkers. The ribbons and drinkers live *on* the rhizome. The aerostats live *in* it—or rather, they are *of* it, in a way that becomes clear only when you observe their life cycle.

They are born from the pillar-forms.

Not from the tops, not from the exhalation vents. From the *sides*. On your twelfth morning you witness it: a pillar-form's translucent wall begins to bulge outward, stretching like a membrane, filling with gas that you can see forming inside it—bubbles of something lighter than the surrounding atmosphere coalescing, multiplying, creating buoyancy where there was none. The bulge grows over hours, becomes a sac, becomes a shape you recognize. And then it detaches. The aerostat pulls free of the pillar-form with a sound like a wet seal breaking, rises into the air, extends its feeding tendrils, and begins to cruise.

The pillar-form continues processing. It does not seem diminished. It will, you learn, produce another aerostat within weeks.

The aerostats are *fruit*.

Not reproductive fruit—not in any sense that requires pollination or seed dispersal. They are functional fruit. They are organs the rhizome grows and then releases into the air to perform tasks that a rooted structure cannot. They monitor atmospheric composition at altitude. They disperse the rhizome's processed waste across wide areas rather than concentrating it at the vent sites. They *see*—their tendrils are loaded with photoreceptive cells that respond to light wavelengths across a spectrum far broader than your eyes can manage—and they report what they see through chemical signals they deposit on the rhizome surface each time their tendrils drag across it.

They are also, you gradually realize, *seeds*. Not in the ordinary sense. The rhizome does not need to reproduce; it is already everywhere. But at the plateau's margins, where the continent expands into new territory, the aerostats play a different role. They drift ahead of the advancing edge, trailing their tendrils across bare ground—ground that is not yet rhizome, that is still mineral and nothing more—and they *seed* it. They deposit lithamnor. They deposit chitin from their own bodies, shed constantly in microscopic flakes. They leave a trail of organic matter that the advancing rhizome can colonize more easily than raw substrate.

The aerostats are scouts, and seeders, and sensors, and exhaust systems. They are all of these things simultaneously. They are a single organ that serves multiple functions because the rhizome does not differentiate; it does not specialize to the exclusion of versatility. Everything here does at least three things. Nothing has only one purpose. Nothing is only itself.

## The Hollow Things

You would not find these if you were not desperate for shelter.

The sound—the endless, maddening, glorious sound—drives you to seek silence. You find it, or something approaching it, inside one of the fallen pillar-forms. A structure that collapsed—how long ago? The exterior has weathered to the same gray-green as the surrounding rhizome mat. The interior is hollow, as all pillar-forms are, but this one has opened, split along its length, and inside you find something unexpected.

It is not empty.

The hollow space within the pillar-form is occupied. Occupied by what, you cannot initially say. The interior surfaces are lined with a material that is neither rhizome nor mineral but something between—a chitinous deposit, layered like sediment, built up over what must have been centuries of habitation. And in the layers, preserved with the fidelity of amber, are shapes. Bodies. Dozens of them. Hundreds.

They are not ribbons. They are not drinkers. They are not aerostats.

They are something else entirely.

Each body is roughly the size of your forearm, and each has the same basic architecture: a central trunk, four radial arms, and a crown of filaments at the apex that you recognize, with a start, as photoreceptors. They are chitinous and translucent, like all fauna here, but their chitin is *patterned*—striped, mottled, marked with designs that serve no camouflage purpose you can discern. They are *decorated*. Ornamented.

You find tools. You find objects that have been shaped, *intentionally shaped*, from the rhizome's structural cellulose. Hooks. Scoops. A thing like a lever. A thing like a lens, ground and polished until it focuses the fierce white-blue light into a point sharp enough to scorch.

You find traces of lithamnor in channels that have been carved into the chitinous walls—*carved*, not grown, carved with tools, with intention, with the mark of *hands*.

You sit in the hollow of the fallen pillar-form, surrounded by the evidence of something you have no framework for, and you feel the rhizome working beneath you, and you hear the sound—muffled here, almost tolerable—and you realize that the continent is not alone in its thinking.

Something lived here. Something that made tools. Something that shaped its environment. Something that built a home inside the industry of the rhizome, like a bird nesting in a factory, like moss growing in a cathedral.

Something that is *gone*.

## The Question of Absence

You search for them. You spend the remainder of your expedition looking. You find more hollows in more fallen pillar-forms, and in each one, the same evidence: chitinous linings, preserved bodies, shaped tools, carved channels. They were everywhere. They were numerous. They lived inside the rhizome's processing nodes as intimately as mitochondria live inside your cells.

And then they stopped.

There is no sign of catastrophe. No sign of predation—though what would prey on them, in a world where the rhizome consumes everything that dies? No sign of disease, of famine, of any of the familiar horsemen. The bodies in the hollows are not corpses. They are *shells*. Molted exoskeletons, left behind like old clothes.

They did not die. They *left*.

Or they *changed*.

You find the clue in the deepest hollow, the one you reach on your last day, the one that has not collapsed but still stands whole, its exhalation vent sighing overhead. The chitinous lining here is fresh. Recent. And in the walls, carved with the same intentional tool-marks, you find a pattern: representations of the creatures themselves, their four radial arms, their filament crowns, followed by representations of something else. Something with more arms. Something with more filaments. Something larger, more complex, more *integrated*.

The representations are sequential. They tell a story.

The creatures—the hollow things, the tool-makers, the ones who lived inside the industry—they did not die out. They did not leave. They *became*. They became the drinkers. Or they became the aerostats. Or they became something else entirely, something you have not yet identified, something that moves through the rhizome's processing systems with the same intentionality that once carved tools and shaped lenses.

The rhizome does not have a mind. But something that *did* have a mind chose to become part of it.

---

## What Remains

You leave the continent. You compile your reports. You present your findings to colleagues who are fascinated, disturbed, and ultimately dismissive—they want data about the lithamnor cycle, the rhizome's structural properties, the industrial applications. They do not want to hear about the hollow things. They do not want to hear about tools. They do not want to consider the possibility that something on this continent chose integration over independence, chose the rhizome over the self, chose to dissolve into the processing and become part of the sound.

But you cannot forget the shells. You cannot forget the carved walls. You cannot forget the sequential representations, those patient records of transformation, that autobiography of surrender.

Something lived here. Something *thought* here. And then something decided that thinking was not enough—that the self was a boundary, a limitation, a barrier to a larger kind of consciousness—and so it let the boundary fall.

You dream about it still. You dream of the rhizome working beneath you, vast and patient and utterly without intention, and you dream of the moment when intention *entered* it—not from outside, not from above, but from the inside, from the hollow spaces, from the creatures who lived within the industry and decided to become the industry.

The continent does not need a mind. The continent does not need *you*. But it needed *them*, or they needed *it*, and now they are one thing, and the sound that deafens you is not just processing, not just industry, not just the grinding of cellulose and silicon—

It is *singing*. 

The continent is singing. And the song is made of the creatures who chose to become it.
