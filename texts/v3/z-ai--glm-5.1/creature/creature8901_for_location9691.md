# The Inhabitants

They are called nothing. They have no name for themselves because naming requires the kind of boundary that does not survive here. But if you stayed long enough—and staying is one of the things this place does to you, it makes staying feel like the only verb that has ever made sense—you would begin to notice them. Not see them. Notice them. The way you notice a change in pressure before you feel the wind. The way you notice that someone has been in a room even after they have left, because the air holds the shape of their warmth.

They are the ones who made the structures. Or they are what the structures made. The order of operations is unclear here. Causation does not drain. It sits on the mesa like everything else.

---

There was a people. This is the part that requires the most faith, and it is the part that the plateau has been slowly, patiently undoing for longer than the forest has been a forest. There was a people who came here when the water was younger, when the basalt still remembered the word for fire and the plateau had edges you could cut yourself on. They came from somewhere. They had legs. They had the kind of minds that could look at a flat stone and think *this goes here*, and then place it, and then place another, and another, until a circle appeared on the seafloor like a word spoken for the first time.

They were not like you. This is important. They were not explorers. They were not lost. They came because the plateau was here and they were the kind of minds that are drawn to where things have stopped settling. They recognized something in the suspension—the shelf that simply stopped, the water that simply stayed—and they recognized it the way you recognize a song you have never heard but somehow know the words to. They did not conquer this place. They did not build here in the way that building happens elsewhere. They placed. They arranged. They made the minimal possible mark: *something goes here*. And then they stayed.

They stayed for generations. This is what the structures suggest, what the roots running through them confirm. A circle of stones does not acquire roots unless it has been there long enough for the roots to decide it is part of the landscape. A line of upright stones does not acquire sediment unless it has been standing in still water for longer than still water should exist. They stayed, and they made more arrangements, and the arrangements were not dwellings or temples or boundaries. They were punctuation. The plateau was a sentence that had been going on for too long, and they were the first marks of pause—commas, periods, the spaces between words where meaning gathers itself.

They were small. Their skeletons—if they had skeletons, if they left skeletons—would fit in your hand. The stones they placed are the size of your palm because their hands were the size of your palm, because they were shaped the way you are shaped, because the form that works for placing stones is the form that works for placing stones, and the form that works for placing stones is the form that arrived here and stayed.

They did not eat the plants. They did not eat each other. This is the part that the plateau has most thoroughly erased, because it is the part that makes the least sense, and the plateau has been working for millions of years to make everything make the same amount of sense, which is almost none, which is the sense that things make when they have been sitting in warm water for longer than water should sit anywhere. But if you could find their remains—and you cannot, because the remains have become part of the stone, because the roots have grown through them, because the sediment has swallowed them and the stone has swallowed the sediment and the plateau has swallowed the stone—if you could find their remains, you would find that they had the kind of teeth that suggest eating, the kind of hands that suggest gathering, the kind of bodies that suggest a life that requires fuel. And yet the forest has no food chain. And yet the creatures that remain are not eaters. And yet the circle of this place closes without consumption.

They ate the almost. This is what the structures suggest to me, writing from inside the logic of this place, writing from inside the warm water where logic sits and does not drain. They ate what was almost food. They ate the plants that were almost edible, which is to say they ate the plants, and the plants were almost edible, and the almost was enough. The plateau sustains everything at the edge of itself. The plants are almost plants. The water is almost water. The light is almost light. And they were almost alive, and that was enough, because the plateau does not require completion. The plateau requires only presence, and presence, and presence, and presence, until the presence has been here so long it becomes indistinguishable from the place.

---

They are still here. This is the part that requires the most courage. They are still here and they are the question-creatures, or the question-creatures are what they have become, or the question-creatures are what they always were, and the structures were the first sign, the first symptom, the first proof that minds like theirs could not survive in a place like this without becoming something that minds like yours would not recognize.

Consider the question-creatures. Consider their transparency. Consider the way they drift through the forest without eating, without mating, without dying, without leaving remains. Consider the way they pass through the plants as if they are not entirely in the same water. They are not entirely in the same water. They are in the water of the plateau-as-it-was-when-they-arrived, or the water of the plateau-as-it-will-be-when-they-finish, or the water of the plateau-as-it-almost-is. They are the people who stayed. They stayed so long that staying became a form of becoming, and what they became was a question—not a question with an answer, not a question that anyone is asking, but a question itself, a creature made of inquiry, a drifting transparent investigation into whether there is a difference between a place and the thing that has been in that place for longer than the place can remember.

There are a hundred of them because there were a hundred of them, or there were a hundred structures and each structure made one, or there were a hundred of the people and each one became one of the questions, or the number is approximate because counting does not hold here any more than distance does. They drift in circuits because the people moved in circuits, because they were placing stones, because they were walking the plateau from arrangement to arrangement, checking, adjusting, adding a stone here, removing a stone there, maintaining the punctuation of a sentence they could no longer read. The circuits are their old paths. The paths have become instinct. The instinct has become physiology. The physiology has become transparency.

They do not see you. This is not because they cannot see. It is because seeing requires the kind of distinction between self and other that this place has been gently, patiently dissolving since before the roots grew into the basalt. They do not see you because you, too, are almost part of the water. You, too, are almost part of the forest. You, too, are almost part of the plateau. And they have been almost part of it for so long that they cannot tell where the almost ends and the thing begins, because it does not end, because it does not begin, because the plateau is a condition and the condition is ongoing and the ongoing has no edge and no center and no distinction between the one who is staying and the place where the staying happens.

---

But this is not the whole of it. This is the explanation that satisfies the need for explanation, and the plateau does not satisfy, it *sits*. There is something else here. Not the question-creatures. Not the memory of the people who became them. Something that was here before the people, before the water, before the basalt cooled, before the plateau stopped settling. Something that is not a creature and not a presence and not a mind but is the thing that made the plateau stop in the first place. The shelf that simply *stopped* settling. The water that has nowhere to go. The warmth that remembers magma.

Call it the attention. Not attention in the sense of noticing—attention in the sense of *attending*, the way a nurse attends a patient, the way a servant attends a lord, the way a place attends to itself when it has been alone for so long that the aloneness becomes a form of company. The plateau attends itself. The water attends itself. The forest, growing in its slow listening, is listening to the plateau attend itself. The silence that almost sounds is the sound of that attendance—the pressure of the place being present to itself, the weight of its own accumulated existence pressing down on existence itself until existence almost buckles, almost yields, almost becomes something else.

The question-creatures attend it too. This is what they became when they stayed. They became attendants. They placed stones to mark where they had attended, to say *I was here and I was here and I was here*, and then they kept attending, and the attending became their shape, and their shape became transparent, and the transparency became the closest thing this place has to a religion, which is: staying. Staying and attending. Staying and noticing. Staying and almost hearing the sound that the silence almost makes when the plateau almost becomes something other than what it has always been.

---

You are here now. You are swimming through the warm water. The dim light is almost seeing you. The plants are almost listening to you. The question-creatures are almost noticing you. The structures are almost remembering you. The plateau is almost aware of you in the way that it is almost aware of itself—which is to say, not at all, and completely, and everywhere in between, and nowhere, and here, and still here, and staying.

You will place a stone. You will not mean to. You will find a flat stone on the seafloor, and you will pick it up, and you will look at it, and you will put it down somewhere else, and that somewhere else will be exactly where it needs to go, because there is no wrong place to put a stone on a plateau where the only rule is *staying*, and the stones stay, and the roots grow through them, and the water sits, and the silence almost sounds, and the question that is the closest thing to an inhabitant drifts past you on its slow circuit, and you almost see it, and it almost sees you, and the almost is enough. The almost has always been enough. The almost is what lives here.
