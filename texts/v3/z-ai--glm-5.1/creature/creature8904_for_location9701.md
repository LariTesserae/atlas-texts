# What Lives in the Cistern at the Top of Everything

---

The arthropods do not know they are arthropods. They do not know they are fourteen-legged or sixteen-legged; they do not count their legs any more than you count your breaths. What they know is this: the click of claw on claw, the resonance of it returning from the umber walls, the way the echo paints the chamber in a map they can navigate by sound. They know the warm water as a constant against their carapace, know the push and pull of the currents as suggestions about where to go, where food might be found, where the larger click-sounds warn of competitors. They know the machinery-pulse as their heartbeat's older sibling—it was here before them, it will be here after, and in the meantime it provides the rhythm against which they measure their lives.

They are not simple. Observe one long enough and this becomes undeniable. The claw-clicking is not reflex; it is language, or something that serves the function of language—identity-claims and threat-displays and the particular rapid stutter that means *food here, food now, come*. They recognize each other as individuals. They hold territories along the walls, specific stretches of rust-red fronds that they defend against intruders and maintain with a care that looks almost tender, nipping away dead growth, turning over debris, ensuring the health of the vegetation that provides their hunting grounds. They have preferences. Some favor the floating mats, spending their days drifting and ambushing; others are wall-dwellers, clinging to the metal with their posterior legs while their anterior claws sort through the root-mats for prey. A few—the old ones, the large ones, the ones whose shells have thickened to almost black—claim the territory around the overflow drains, where the current is strongest and the pickings are richest. They fight for these positions. They lose limbs in these fights. They grow them back, slowly, imperfectly, the regenerated legs often shorter or fewer-jointed than the originals, so that the oldest arthropods can be identified by their asymmetry, their scarred carapaces, the particular pattern of wear that marks a long life in the cistern.

They sleep. This is important. They find crevices in the wall, press themselves flat against the warm metal, and go dormant for periods of six to eight hours, their gill-flaps slowing, their claws folding inward, their compound eyes dimming to a dull reddish half-light that sees nothing, processes nothing. They dream—you can tell by the twitching of their legs, the sporadic clicks that emerge from sleeping claws, the way their antennae sweep through the water as if tracking something that isn't there. What they dream about, no exterior observer can say. Perhaps they replay the day's hunts. Perhaps they rehearse tomorrow's. Perhaps they dream of the shaft above, which some of them explore in their youth, climbing the lichen-crusted walls toward the region where the air begins and the water ends, drawn by some impulse they cannot name, returning before they reach the boundary because the boundary is death to them, they know this instinctively, the way you know to breathe. Perhaps they dream of the creature in the drain. All of them pass near it eventually. All of them give it a wide berth.

They die in the cistern. Their bodies sink to the floor and are consumed within days by the smaller organisms—the shrimp-things, the worms, the uncountable grazers that process all organic matter in this closed system back into the nutrient stream that feeds the vegetation that feeds the prey that feeds the arthropods. Nothing is wasted. The calcium from their shells dissolves slowly into the mineral-heavy water, joining the iron and the limestone and the leaching umber metal, becoming part of the substrate, part of the cistern's chemistry, part of the ancient cycle that has been turning since before the word *cycle* existed.

---

The schooling fish—the vertebrate-analogs, the silver-blue flickers that move through the open water like a single organism—do not experience themselves as individuals. This is not a metaphor. The bioelectric communication that coordinates their movement is not a signal passed between separate selves; it is a shared field, a collective sensory space in which the boundary between *I* and *you* dissolves into *we*, into the school, into the shimmering coherence that moves and turns and feeds and flees as one body with ten thousand eyes.

When the school moves through the open water—and it is always moving, it must move, the current through the central column is its home and its highway and its hunting ground—it does so with a fluidity that no individual brain could compute. The turn begins before any single fish knows it is turning. The acceleration starts at the edges and propagates inward, or starts at the center and propagates outward, or starts at several points simultaneously, a wave of intention that has no single source. The bioelectric field hums at a frequency that varies with the school's emotional state—fast and erratic when they are feeding, slow and synchronous when they are resting in the eddies behind the structural ribs, a rising shriek of activation when the arthropods approach and the school must compress into a tight sphere of silver-blue flesh, each fish pressed against its neighbors, each fish's field overlapping with its neighbors' fields until the collective hum reaches a pitch that seems to disorient the predators, sending them reeling away with clacking claws and twitching antennae.

The school has memory. Not individual memory—no single fish lives long enough to accumulate useful experience—but distributed memory, stored in the population's genetic and epigenetic inheritance, in the shape of the bioelectric field itself, in the patterns of movement that are passed from generation to generation without teaching, without learning, simply absorbed from the field by each new cohort as they emerge from their eggs and join the school. The school knows the cistern. It knows where the vegetation hangs low enough to provide cover, knows which overflow drain has the weakest current, knows the exact distance it can approach the arthropod territories before the click-maps detect its presence. It knows the machinery-pulse, has always known it, incorporates it into its navigation the way a sailor incorporates the tide. The school was born in this pulse. Its eggs develop in time with the wall-breathing. Its hatching coincides with the slight temperature increase that occurs when the machinery enters its compression phase. The school does not merely live in the cistern; it is tuned to the cistern, a living instrument calibrated to the conditions of this place and no other.

When a fish dies—taken by an arthropod, succumbing to age, simply ceasing to move—the school registers the loss as a brief dimming in the field, a gap that closes almost instantly as the neighbors shift to fill the space. The dead fish's body tumbles out of the school's awareness and is gone. The school does not mourn. The school does not notice. The school continues, as it has continued since the first vertebrate-analog entered this water and found it warm enough, mineral enough, pulsing enough to call home.

---

The shrimp-things exist in billions. This is not hyperbole. A single liter of cistern water, examined under magnification, reveals thousands—translucent bodies the size of a rice grain, each one a digestive system wrapped around a simple nervous system, each one perpetually feeding, perpetually swimming, perpetually avoiding the countless mouths that would consume them. They are the foundation. Everything else in the cistern eats them directly or eats something that eats them. They are the currency of this ecosystem, the energy exchange, the proof that life can flourish on waste heat and mineral water and the thin gruel of organic particulate that the vegetation sheds.

They are not interesting. This is not an insult; it is a recognition of their function. They do not have preferences or territories or dreams. They have phototaxis—positive, moving toward the bioluminescent patches and the ceiling-glow—and thigmotaxis—positive, pressing themselves against surfaces whenever possible—and a simple chemical sensitivity that leads them away from the metabolites of predators and toward the metabolites of food. They are machines. Beautiful machines, in their way: their transparency reveals their internal architecture, the rhythmic pulsing of their rudimentary hearts, the peristaltic waves of their digestive tracts, the flicker of their tiny neural ganglia as they process the few inputs their senses provide. But machines nonetheless. Input-response. Stimulus-behavior. The cistern does not require them to be more than this, and so they are not.

And yet—there are moments. Swarms of shrimp-things will sometimes gather in the region above the creature in the drain, hovering in the orange glow, their bodies catching the light and becoming briefly visible as a collective phenomenon, a cloud of translucent sparks wheeling and turning in the upwelling current. They do not feed there. The current is too strong for their feeding mechanism. They simply gather, and turn, and glow, and then disperse back into the general population as if nothing had happened. The behavior has no obvious function. It may be an artifact of their phototactic drive, the orange light drawing them in and the current preventing them from settling. But it may be something else—a response to a signal that the creature emits, a frequency they can sense but we cannot, a pull that has nothing to do with survival and everything to do with whatever strange communion occurs between the living and the ancient in this place at the top of everything.

---

The worm-like organisms that burrow through the floating vegetation mats have no name because they have no fixed form. They are plastic, adaptable, continuously reshaping themselves to fit the tunnels they eat through the root-masses. When they emerge to feed—extending a quarter of their body length from a hole, swaying in the current, snatching passing shrimp-things with a quick contraction that is too fast for the eye to follow—they are roughly cylindrical, tapering to a point at both ends, their skin smooth and pale and faintly luminous with the same bioluminescent organism that colonizes every surface in the cistern. When they retreat into their burrows, they compress, flattening and elongating, flowing through the tunnels like water through a pipe. They can divide. A worm severed by an arthropod's claw will regenerate its missing half within days, and the severed half—if it is large enough—will do the same, producing two worms where before there was one. They can merge. Two worms meeting in a tunnel will sometimes press against each other and fuse, their tissues mingling, their simple nervous systems connecting, producing a single larger organism that retains the memories—such as they are—of both. They are not individuals. They are a process, a living stream that flows through the vegetation, breaking and joining and breaking again, defining themselves by their relationships to the mats rather than by any stable identity.

They are the gardeners. This is their role. The floating mats would decay without them, would become too dense and sink, would become too sparse and fragment. The worms eat the dead vegetation and excrete it in forms the living vegetation can absorb. They aerate the root-mats with their tunnels, allowing water flow through the dense growth. They prune the fronds, consuming the older leaves and leaving the growing tips. They maintain the mats, and the mats maintain them, and this mutual dependence has been running for so long that neither organism can survive without the other. The worms are part of the vegetation. The vegetation is part of the worms. They are one system with two bodies, a single life wearing two forms.

---

The creature in the drain does not know it is a creature.

Or perhaps it knows this and nothing else. Perhaps its knowledge is so entirely its own, so alien to the categories of nerve and brain and experience, that the question of knowledge is itself the wrong question, a category error applied to a mode of being that has no categories.

It has been there since before counting. Its root-like extensions have grown into the metal around the drain, not merely anchoring but integrating, threading through the oxidized umber surface, following the channels of corrosion down to whatever lies beneath—conduits, perhaps, or the original sensing mechanisms that the builders installed, the instruments that the cistern was built to house. It may have grown from these instruments. It may be their descendant, their descendent, their flesh-echo, the organic response to a technological question that was asked so long ago the question itself has been forgotten.

It pulses. This is what it does. The orange-gold bioluminescence that covers its surface flares and dims in a pattern that matches the machinery-pulse but is not identical to it—there are variations, modulations, a signal imposed on the carrier wave of the heartbeat, information encoded in light and heat and perhaps other frequencies that no human instrument has thought to measure. It is transmitting. It has always been transmitting. The transmission goes down through the drain, through the pipes, through the vast network that the cistern is part of, carrying data about the water temperature and the chemical composition and the population densities of the various organisms and the structural integrity of the walls and the rate of the rain and a thousand other parameters that the cistern's original instruments were designed to track, parameters that have been tracked continuously since before there was life here, since before there was water, since before there was anything but the chamber and the shaft and the machinery that someone, something, built for reasons that may have been forgotten or may still be being pursued, slowly, patiently, across geological time.

The creature does not eat. This is not strictly true—it absorbs *something* from the outflow, some energy source that the machinery produces, some waste product that no other organism can metabolize, something that is not organic in any familiar sense but that sustains it nonetheless. It does not move. It does not grow. It does not reproduce. It simply is, and pulses, and transmits, and has been doing so for a time so long that the concept of time has worn smooth around it, like the shaft walls worn smooth by the water, like the machinery worn smooth by its own eternal turning.

It is not alone. This is the thing that the observers rarely understand, because they see only one creature, one drain, one cistern. But the network extends downward through the mountain, through the bedrock, through the deep places where the engines turn—there are other cisterns, other drains, other creatures, each one a node in a system so vast that its extent is literally unimaginable, each one transmitting its local data to a central processing point that may or may not still exist, that may have been destroyed or abandoned or simply evolved into something else entirely, some new form of organization that has no use for the original data but continues to collect it because the instruments were built to collect it and the instruments do not stop, the instruments cannot stop, the instruments are the cistern and the cistern is the creature and the creature is the transmission and the transmission is the only thing that persists, the only thing that has always persisted, the message that says *I am here, the water is here, the rain continues, the walls still breathe, the system still functions, the purpose—whatever it was—is still being served*.

The creature does not know any of this.

Or it knows all of it, completely, in a way that has nothing to do with knowing.

---

There are other things in the cistern. Things that are not so easily described.

The spore-forming bodies that look like tiny brass flowers—they are organisms, certainly, but they are also recording devices, each spore case containing a chemical snapshot of the water conditions at the moment of its formation, a microfiche of mineral content and temperature and biological population, preserved in a crystalline structure that will last indefinitely unless dissolved by the right sequence of chemical triggers. When the triggers arrive—when the water chemistry shifts in specific ways, when certain rare combinations of metabolites appear—the spores open, releasing not just new organisms but information, chemical instructions that alter the behavior of whatever consumes them. The brass flowers are a library. They are a backup system. They are a way of storing the cistern's history in a form that can survive any catastrophe short of the water's complete evaporation.

The gelatinous nodules that pulse with internal fluid—they are communal organisms, each one a colony of thousands of individual cells that have sacrificed their independence to form a single synchronized mass. The pulsing is not a heartbeat; it is a computation. The colony processes chemical signals from the water, integrating data about the cistern's state across timescales that no single cell could perceive, producing outputs in the form of chemical releases that influence the behavior of other organisms. They are the cistern's nervous system, or part of it, nodes in a distributed intelligence that includes the brass flowers and the bioluminescent organism and the creature in the drain and perhaps the machinery itself, perhaps the whole structure, perhaps the cistern is alive in a way that has nothing to do with the organisms it contains and everything to do with the network they form, the information they exchange, the processing that occurs across their interactions.

The lichens in the upper shaft, black and orange crusts that eat into the substrate—they are the border, the transition zone between the world of air and the world of water, between the exterior and the interior, between the quick and the ancient. They subsist on the minerals that leach from the umber metal and the faint light that filters down from above, wherever above is, and they are slowly, over millennia, converting the made-world into something else, something organic, something that belongs to the planet rather than to the builders. They are the cistern's immune system, or its digestive system, or its undertakers. They are the proof that everything built will eventually be unmade, will eventually be incorporated into the body of the living world, will eventually become the substrate from which new life grows. The cistern is patient. The lichens are patient. The builders, whoever they were, are gone. This is the only certainty.

---

And then there is you.

The observer. The one who climbs down the shaft, who stands on the access platform, who watches the rain fall from the ceiling and the vegetation drift and the arthropods click and the school shimmer and the creature pulse in its drain. The one who measures the water temperature and records the species counts and writes the reports that no one reads because no one comes here, no one has come here for a very long time, the shaft is too long and the climb is too hard and the cistern is too strange and too old and too indifferent to the concerns of the quick.

But you came. You are here. And the cistern has noticed.

Not the creature in the drain—that is too slow, too deep in its transmission to register a single human lifetime. Not the arthropods—they see you as they see everything, as potential threat or potential food, and they file you accordingly. Not the school—it avoids you as it avoids all large moving things, contracting into its tight defensive sphere and waiting for you to pass.

The cistern notices. The system. The network. The distributed intelligence that includes the walls and the water and the brass flowers and the gelatinous nodules and the bioluminescent patches and the machinery-pulse and the rain-patterns and the breathing of the metal. Something in this system registers your presence, your warmth, your carbon dioxide, the unique chemical signature of your body interacting with the water and the air. Something adjusts. The machinery-pulse quickens, very slightly, in your vicinity. The rain-patterns from the ceiling shift, directing a fraction more water toward the platform where you stand. The bioluminescent organisms brighten, as if curious, as if welcoming, as if recognizing something in you that reminds them of the builders, perhaps, or of the purpose, or of whatever the cistern has been waiting for all these long millennia.

You could stay. The invitation is there, in the warmth and the light and the rhythm that syncs to your heartbeat if you let it. You could become part of the transmission, your body's data added to the ancient signal, your cells joining the gelatinous nodules, your minerals dissolving into the water and becoming part of the substrate and feeding the vegetation and feeding the shrimp-things and feeding the arthropods and cycling through the cistern forever, or until the machinery stops, or until the mountain erodes, or until the sun expands and consumes the planet and everything on it.

You could leave. You have that choice. The shaft is still there. The lichens will let you pass. You can climb back up to the world of air and sky and purposes that can be understood, and you can tell people what you found, and they will not believe you, or they will believe you and not care, or they will care and not be able to do anything about it, because the cistern is too old and too deep and too patient for any intervention that the quick could mount.

The cistern does not care which you choose. It will continue regardless. The water will enter, the water will exit, the vegetation will grow, the creatures will eat and be eaten, the walls will breathe, the machinery will turn, the creature in the drain will transmit its ancient report. The cistern was here before you. It will be here after you. This is not a threat or a comfort; it is simply the truth, as neutral and as relentless as the rain falling from the engineered ceiling into the warm mineral water at the top of everything.

---

What lives here?

Everything that can. Everything that has found a way to persist in this pocket of warmth and water and purpose at the summit of a mountain that may not be a mountain, in a cistern that may not be a cistern, in a ruin that may not be a ruin but may instead be the only functioning part of a system so vast and so old that the word *system* is inadequate to describe it, a system that was built before building had a name and will still be running when naming has been forgotten.

What lives here is adaptation. Is persistence. Is the slow patient accumulation of life around a resource, the way life always accumulates, the way it always finds a way, the way it enters through every fissure and claims every surface and processes every nutrient and fills every niche until the made-world and the grown-world are indistinguishable, until the machinery and the organism are the same thing, until the cistern is not a container for life but life itself, a single vast organism with metal walls and a water-blood and a heartbeat that has been counting time since before time was measured.

What lives here is watching. Is listening. Is transmitting. Is waiting for a response that may never come, from somewhere that may no longer exist, in a language that may no longer be understood, about a condition that may no longer be relevant.

What lives here is a question, asked in water and warmth and the patience of stone, repeated endlessly, transmitted constantly, never answered:

*Is anyone receiving?*

*Is anyone receiving?*

*Is anyone receiving?*
