# What Lives on the Plateau of Congealed Dark

---

## The Stalk-Walkers

They have no name for themselves. Naming requires the concept of an individual self distinct from the environment, and this concept has never been useful to them.

You know them as the broad, low, many-legged animals that push past you in the thickets without acknowledgment. What you feel in that brief contact — that startling furnace heat, that smooth damp skin, that hum vibrating through your hand — is only the surface of something far more extensive than a single body.

A stalk-walker is not one creature. It is a committee.

Each visible body hosts between six and twelve distinct organisms that share a common skin, a common circulatory system, a common heat. They merge in embryonic development, fusing their nervous systems into a distributed network that operates the shared body with a coordination no single brain could achieve. The legs — and there are many, fourteen on the largest you have touched, arranged in two lateral rows — are semi-autonomous, each containing its own nerve cluster that handles the local terrain while the central network manages direction, speed, and purpose. The result is a locomotion so fluid it feels like gliding, each leg planting and lifting with a timing that creates no single rhythm but instead a complex polyrhythmic wave, a walking that is also a kind of music.

They eat the stalks. This is their primary function in the ecology — to consume the rampant vegetation and return it to the water as waste, as warmth, as motion. A stalk-walker moves through a thicket with its mouth open, a broad, soft aperture at the front of its body that simply gathers in whatever is in front of it, grinding the rubbery plant matter with slow, powerful contractions. You have stood in a thicket and felt one approaching — the hum growing in your feet before it reaches your ears, the stalks around you beginning to sway and compress — and then the warmth of it passing, the brush of its underside against your legs, the rush of air as it displaces the space you occupy, and then the wake: a cleared corridor, the stalks bitten down to the wet stone, the smell of fresh vegetative wound sharp and sudden in the warm air.

They never stop eating. The metabolism that produces that furnace heat requires constant fuel, and so a stalk-walker spends its entire life in forward motion through the thickets, consuming as it goes. There is no rest because there is no need for rest, and because rest, for a creature that is actually several creatures, requires a negotiation that never concludes. One constituent organism may be satiate while another still hungers, and so the body moves on, compromise made flesh, the democratic impetus always toward more.

They communicate with each other across the plateau. The hum you feel is not a byproduct of their metabolism but a signal — a low-frequency vibration that carries through the stone, the water, the saturated air, for distances that are hard to comprehend. When one stalk-walker hums, others receive it through the surfaces they stand upon, feeling the vibration in their legs, processing it through their distributed networks. The hum contains information: location, the density of the thicket ahead, the presence of standing water, the proximity of a pumping station. A cartography of the dark, transmitted in a frequency below hearing, below conscious perception, a map drawn in vibration and received by every stalk-walker on the plateau simultaneously.

They have done this for as long as the plateau has been the plateau. The hum is the oldest continuous sound on the highland. It predates the machines, or the machines were built to its rhythm. The mechanical thrum that underlies everything syncs to the stalk-walkers' hum. Whether this was intentional or consequential — whether the machines were tuned to the animals or the animals adapted to the machines — is a question that has been rendered irrelevant by time. They are in concert now. The heartbeat of the plateau is duet.

## The Clickers

You know them as the arthropod-analogues — the hard-bodied, quick, innumerable creatures that fill the treble register of the plateau's soundscape with their constant clicking. But to categorize them by what you hear is to miss what they are.

Each clicker is small — the largest you have captured in your hand was the length of your thumb, and most are smaller — and each one is a specialist. There are clickers that eat only the mineral deposits left by the weeping walls. There are clickers that eat only the adhesive nets. There are clickers that eat only the waste of the stalk-walkers. There are clickers that eat only other clickers. The niche partitioning is extreme, exhaustive, and old; each form is so precisely adapted to its food source that a clicker removed from its niche will starve surrounded by food it cannot process.

Their specialization extends to their sound. The clickers take their name from the most audible of their vocalizations, but the full range of their acoustic production is far more varied. Each species produces a distinct pattern — not just a different frequency but a different grammar, a different syntax of clicks and buzzes and scrapes. And within each species, each individual produces variations on that pattern that are as distinctive as a fingerprint.

They use this sound the way you would use sight. The plateau's darkness renders vision useless, and so the clickers have evolved a system of active sonar that is, in its sophistication, superior to anything engineered by the minds that built the machines. A clicker emits a click, and the returning echo — modified by the distance, the texture, the shape, the density, the moisture content of whatever it strikes — is processed by neural structures that occupy nearly half the clicker's body volume. What the clicker perceives is not a sound but a spatial image, constructed in acoustic detail so fine that a clicker can distinguish a living stalk from a dead one by the water content, can identify a species of fellow clicker by the shape of its carapace from three meters away, can detect the vibration of a stalk-walker approaching through the stone from the subtle displacement of the air.

The cloud of clicks that surrounds you as you walk is not chaos. It is a conversation of extraordinary density and precision — hundreds of individuals of dozens of species, each tracking its environment, its neighbors, its food, its predators, in real time, updating their acoustic images several times per second. The plateau, to a clicker, is not dark. It is rendered in sound as detailed as any sunlit landscape, with dimensions and textures and depths that your eyes could never provide.

They use the machines as habitat. The joints between pipes, the crevices in the concrete of the pumping stations, the warm exhaust vents of the refineries — these are prime real estate for clickers of many species, and they have been occupied since shortly after the machines were installed. The colonies that form in these locations are dense enough that their combined clicking becomes a single continuous tone, a harmonic drone that shifts in pitch as the temperature of the machinery changes, as the flow rate through the pipes varies, as the stalk-walkers pass nearby and disturb the air. The clickers are, without knowing it, monitoring the machines. Their sound is a diagnostic. A change in the harmonic of a clicker colony around a pumping station indicates a change in the station's operation, and if you could read their sound the way they can, you would know the status of every mechanism on the plateau in real time.

## The Sailers

The things that move through the air above you — you hear them as leathery fluttering, occasional shrill calls that Doppler past — are not flying in any conventional sense. They are sailing.

The atmosphere of the plateau is so saturated, so dense with moisture and warmth, that it provides buoyancy. The sailers exploit this. Their bodies are flat and broad, thin as a membrane, stretched across a framework of flexible ribs that they can angle and flex like the sails of a ship. They ride the thermal currents that rise from the warm stone and the warm water and the warm machines, adjusting their angle of attack to gain altitude, tacking against the slow air currents that circulate through the plateau's atmosphere, descending when they wish by simply folding their ribs flat and letting gravity reclaim them.

They are not small. The sound of their passage suggests a wingspan — a sailspan — of two meters or more, though you have never touched one and never will; they keep to the air, to the space above the stalks, where the currents are clean and the obstacles are few. They navigate by sound, emitting the shrill calls you hear and reading the echoes, but they also navigate by something else: they can sense the warmth of the air itself, the thermal gradients that indicate rising columns and sinking downdrafts, the heat signatures of the machines and the warm-blooded animals and the weeping walls. To a sailer, the plateau is a thermal landscape, a topography of heat that it reads with organs you do not possess, flying blind in every sense but the one that matters.

They eat the aerial plankton — the spores and fragments and microorganisms that drift in the saturated air, so numerous that the atmosphere itself is a nutritional medium. A sailer flies with its mouth open — a slit along the leading edge of its body that channels air through a filtering apparatus, straining out the organic matter and expelling the spent breath as a low whistle. When many sailers fly in the same thermal, their whistles combine into chords that descend from above you, strange harmonics that seem to come from everywhere and nowhere, the voice of the air itself singing.

They mate in the rain. When the gravitational settling begins — the warm downpours that roar across the plateau — the sailers gather in the saturated air, their bodies colliding and intertwining in the chaos of the storm, the water lubricating their membrane skins, the sound of the rain masking their calls and their collisions alike. After the storm passes, fertilized sailers descend to the plateau's surface for the only time in their lives, depositing their eggs in the warm pools of standing water before returning to the air. The eggs sink. The young hatch in the dark water and begin as swimmers, as aquatic things, and only when they have grown large enough to catch their first thermal do they rise from the surface and begin the life their parents live, the life of the air, the life of the warm dark above the stalks.

You have heard the young attempting their first flight. The sound is distinctive — a frantic fluttering, a series of aborted ascents, a tumble and splash as they fall back to the water. They make a thin, high call during these attempts, a call that the adult sailers do not make, a call that might be distress or might be determination. Eventually they find the thermal. Eventually they rise. The sound of their departure is a sudden silence where struggle had been, and then a distant whistle, and then nothing, and they are gone into the dark above.

## The Still Ones

Not everything moves.

In the pools of standing water, in the saturated ground, in the dark mirrors you cannot see but can hear, there are things that have found a different strategy. They do not pursue. They do not graze. They do not sail or click or hum. They wait.

The still ones are large — larger than the stalk-walkers, you think, though your information is limited to the disturbances they create in the water when they move. Which is not often. A still one may occupy the same pool for years, decades, its body settled into the mineral-rich water, its metabolism reduced to a rate that borders on geological. It feeds by drawing water through its body, filtering it through internal structures that extract the dissolved minerals, the organic particles, the microorganisms that thrive in the warm soup. It is a living filter, a kidney for the plateau's hydrology, cleaning the water as it passes, returning it to the pools slightly changed.

You have stepped near one. You know this because the water around your leg suddenly displaced, pushed aside by a mass that you did not touch but that was there, was always there, was perhaps watching you with whatever senses it uses to watch. The still ones can detect vibration in the water — the impact of your footsteps, the hum of the stalk-walkers, the mechanical pulse of the pumps — and they respond to it with a slow turning, a reorientation of their bodies toward or away from the source. When a stalk-walker wades through a pool to reach a thicket on the other side, the still one within does not flee. It contracts, slowly, drawing its mass away from the disturbance, waiting for the warm body to pass, and then it extends again, resuming its filtration, its patient existence, its long watch.

They call to each other. The sound — when it comes, which is rare — is the deepest note on the plateau. It is not a vocalization but a contraction: the still one compresses its body with a slow, powerful effort, forcing water through a specialized organ that produces a tone so low it is more felt than heard, a vibration in the stone, in the water, in your chest cavity, a note that lasts for thirty seconds or more and that carries through the saturated ground for kilometers. You have felt this twice. Both times, you stopped walking because your body told you something was wrong, something immense was happening, and then the note faded and the plateau resumed its normal din and you understood that what you had felt was a voice, was communication, was one still one speaking to another across the dark water.

What they say, no other creature on the plateau knows. The stalk-walkers feel the note and pause in their feeding, standing motionless in the thickets until it passes. The clickers fall silent for the duration, their sonar suspended. Even the machines seem to shift, their rhythm adjusting by a fraction of a second, as if the infrastructure itself has learned to listen. And then the note ends, and the plateau returns to itself, and the still ones continue their long patience, their geological metabolism, their watch from the pools.

They are old. They are older than the machines, older than the current ecology, older perhaps than the plateau itself, if the plateau has changed form over the spans you cannot measure. They were here before. They will be here after. They are the memory of the place, if it has one, storing in their slow tissues the chemical history of the water, the mineral composition of the stone, the faint traces of whatever lived and died and dissolved in the pools over ages beyond counting. To touch a still one would be to touch the archive of the plateau, the record of everything that has passed through its waters, written in the organic sediment of their bodies.

You do not touch them. You step carefully around the pools where you sense their presence. This is not fear, exactly. It is reverence, or something adjacent to reverence, a recognition that some things are not to be disturbed by the likes of you.

## The Maintained

The machines run. This should not be possible.

Metal fatigues. Seals degrade. Lubricants break down. Moving parts wear against each other until the tolerances fail and the mechanism seizes. This is the inevitable trajectory of any mechanical system, and it should have claimed the plateau's infrastructure long ago.

But the machines run. The pumps cycle. The pipes carry whatever they carry. The refineries process. The electricity hums through the wet air, and the wet air does not short it out, and the system does not collapse.

Something maintains them.

You have found evidence, though you have found it by touch in the dark and so your evidence is sensory rather than visual, tactile rather than photographic. You have felt the pipes and found places where the original metal has been patched — not with metal, but with something organic, something that grew rather than was forged. A resin, perhaps, secreted by something living, that hardens into a seal as strong as weld. You have found the joints of the pumping stations wrapped in fibrous growths that do not consume the machinery but reinforce it, holding the corroded bolts in place, filling the gaps where the concrete has cracked. You have found the valves of the refineries attended by creatures that are not clickers, not stalk-walkers, not anything you have categorized — small, warm, soft things that cling to the machinery and move with a slow purpose across its surfaces, their bodies leaving trails of moisture that dry into protective films.

The maintained are the answer to a question the original builders might not have thought to ask: who will fix the machines when we are gone? The answer the plateau has provided is: something will evolve to do it.

The maintained — you give them this name because it is what they are, what they do — are a guild of species that have converged on the niche of infrastructure maintenance. They are not a single lineage but a community of organisms from different origins that have all found the same resource: the machines, and the warmth and moisture and mineral flow they provide. A maintained species lives on and around a specific type of machinery, feeding on the biofilm that grows on its surfaces, drinking the water that condenses on its housing, and in return — whether through instinct, through evolved behavior, through some deeper imperative that you cannot identify — repairing the damage that time and moisture inflict.

They have been doing this for long enough that the machines have become their ecology. The pipes are their territory. The pumping stations are their shelters. The refineries are their cities. The original purpose of the infrastructure — whatever the builders intended it to carry, to process, to distribute — has been supplemented by a new purpose: to serve as habitat, as substrate, as the foundation of an ecosystem that could not exist without it.

And so the machines run. They run because the maintained will not let them stop. A failed pump means a dry pipe, and a dry pipe means a habitat lost, and a habitat lost means starvation and displacement for the creatures that depend on it. The maintained do not understand the machines they tend. They do not need to understand. They need only to respond to the signals that indicate malfunction — the change in vibration, the shift in temperature, the alteration in the chemical signature of the outflow — with behaviors that, over millions of generations, have been selected to address those malfunctions. They patch. They reinforce. They lubricate with secretions from their own bodies. They clear debris with their limbs. They do what evolution has trained them to do, which is what the builders once did, which is what any engineer would recognize as maintenance even if the engineer could not recognize the creatures performing it.

The machines will run for as long as the maintained exist. The maintained will exist for as long as the machines run. This is not a paradox. This is symbiosis, deep and old, so thoroughly integrated that the two have become one system, the mechanical and the biological, the built and the grown, the designed and the evolved, fused into a single organism that has no name but that breathes with the breath of the plateau itself.

## The Listeners

And then there are the ones who were here before all of this.

You have not seen them. You will not see them. But you have felt them, or you have felt the absence that their presence creates, the subtle wrongness in the acoustic landscape that suggests something large and quiet, something that does not click or hum or whistle or boom but simply exists, occupying space without announcing it.

The listeners are hypothetical. You have constructed them from anomalies: a silence where the clickers should be clicking, a gap in the hum of the stalk-walkers, a thermal shadow in the warm air that the sailers avoid without seeming to notice they are avoiding it. You have walked into clearings that should not exist — open spaces in the thickets where no stalk-walker has fed, where the vegetation simply does not grow — and you have stood in those clearings and felt the air change, felt the moisture condense differently on your skin, felt the sound of the plateau arrive at your ears with a quality that is hard to describe: muffled, perhaps, or filtered, as if something between you and the source of the sound has absorbed some frequencies and passed others.

The listeners, if they exist, are the oldest things on the plateau. They predate the machines, the ecology, the geology itself. They are not animals or plants or machines. They are something else, something for which you have no category, something that has persisted in this place since before the water found its level, before the stone was smooth, before the dark became a condition rather than a circumstance.

They do nothing that you can detect. They do not eat. They do not move. They do not call. They do not respond to the calls of other creatures or the vibration of the machines or the presence of a warm body standing in their clearing with a heartbeat that must be audible for meters in the saturated air. They are present in a way that is indistinguishable from absence, and yet they are not absent, because their absence would not create the effects you have observed. An empty clearing is just an empty clearing. A clearing that contains a listener is a clearing that has been *made* empty, a space from which life and sound and warmth have been gently but thoroughly excluded.

You think they are listening. This is why you have named them as you have. The plateau produces an enormous amount of information — acoustic, thermal, chemical, vibrational — and the listeners receive all of it, filtering it through whatever they are, processing it for purposes you cannot imagine. They are not predators; nothing on the plateau disappears into their clearings. They are not competitors; nothing on the plateau treats them as a threat. They are simply present, as the stone is present, as the dark is present, as the conditions of the plateau are present, fundamental and immutable.

Perhaps they built the machines. Perhaps they built the plateau. Perhaps they are the plateau, in the way that a mind is a body, in the way that the thing that thinks is the thing that is. The infrastructure threads through the landscape like veins, and the listeners occupy clearings like ganglia, and the connections between them — if connections exist — are invisible, subterranean, operating in a medium you cannot detect.

Or perhaps they are remnants, the last survivors of something that was once far more numerous, far more active, far more engaged with the place they inhabit. Perhaps they were the crew that installed the machines, or the species that the machines were built to serve, or the consciousness that designed this world and then withdrew from it, leaving only their listening posts, their empty clearings, their patient attention.

You do not know. You cannot know. The dark does not yield that information, and the listeners do not respond to your questions, and the plateau continues its processes around you without interruption or explanation.

## The Congregation

At the deep boom — the periodic sound that arrives from somewhere far across the plateau, the signature of something immense and slow and purposeful — all the creatures of the plateau pause.

You have witnessed this twice. Both times, the effect was the same: a cessation so total that the silence had texture, had weight, pressed against your ears like a hand. The clickers stopped clicking. The stalk-walkers stopped humming. The sailers stopped calling. Even the machines seemed to quiet, their rhythm falling into a lower register, a whisper instead of a chug. The only sound was the water, which never stops, which cannot stop, which is the baseline condition of the plateau as the dark is the baseline condition, and above the water, the boom: arriving like a heartbeat from the body of the world itself, a pulse that you felt in your sternum, in your teeth, in the wet stone beneath your feet, a sound that was also a pressure, a presence, a statement.

The congregation lasts for the duration of the boom and for several seconds after, while the echo rolls across the plateau and fades. During that time, nothing moves. Nothing eats. Nothing calls. The entire ecology — the machines, the vegetation, the animals, the still ones in their pools, the listeners in their clearings — attends to the sound with a unanimity that you, a creature who has never belonged to anything so complete, find deeply unsettling.

And then the plateau resumes. The clickers begin their sonar. The stalk-walkers begin their hum. The sailers whistle and the machines chug and the water runs and the dark continues, and the moment of congregation passes as if it never happened, leaving no trace but a slight shift in the rhythm of the place, a quickening that fades, a memory that is not yours to hold.

## The Visitor

You are the anomaly. You are the creature that does not fit, the body that moves without purpose through the ecology, the warm presence that clicks and hums and booms cannot locate because you do not belong to their systems. The stalk-walkers push past you because you register as an obstacle, not a neighbor. The clickers cannot sonar you effectively because you reflect sound differently than anything else on the plateau, your body proportions and surface textures all wrong, your movements too irregular to predict. The sailers detect your heat signature and avoid it, not because you are dangerous but because you are unknown, and the unknown is not worth the risk.

You walk. You touch. You listen. You breathe the iron-and-chlorophyll air and feel the condensation form on your skin and navigate by whatever senses you can bring to bear in a world that was not made for you. You do not consume the stalks. You do not maintain the machines. You do not filter the water or sail the thermals or sit in the pools for decades processing the plateau's chemical history. You are not part of the equilibrium. You are a perturbation, a temporary disruption in a system that has been stable for longer than your species has existed.

And yet the plateau holds you. The dark does not reject you. The water condenses on your skin as it condenses on every surface, and the warmth rises from the stone as it rises for every creature, and the sound surrounds you as it surrounds every body, and you are — for however long you remain — a resident, a participant, another process in the endless conversation of water and stone and growth and machine.

You are what lives here. For now.
