# What Lives in the Cut

The frogs were here first, if you don't count the groundwater, and the groundwater was here before the hill was opened, so perhaps nothing was first. Perhaps everything arrived at once and has been learning to live together ever since.

The frogs do not think of themselves as residents. They think of themselves as the correct and obvious consequence of standing water and insect life. They sit on the duckweed—seventeen at last count, though counting is difficult because they shift and because new ones appear each season from the gel-skeins looped around rusted bolts below the waterline—and they watch the pump station with an expression that might be patience and might be something closer to recognition. They have known machinery their entire lives. The thrum of the pump is the first sound they heard when they hatched, vibrating through the water, through their still-soft bodies, and they incorporated it the way they incorporated the minerals in the pool, the way they incorporated the cold. It is not background to them. It is a frog. It is the biggest frog, the one that never moves, the one that sings with its body instead of its throat, and they answer it each dusk without knowing they are answering.

The largest frog—a female, thick through the middle, her skin dark as the wet moss she sits on—has lived here for six years. This is ancient for a frog in a place with herons. She has survived because the Cut's walls are too steep for herons to comfortably navigate, because the pool's metallic taste makes the water unappealing to predators who have other options, and because she learned, in her second year, to sit very still when the shadow of the monthly truck passes across the rim above. She does not know what a truck is. She knows the shadow means stillness, and stillness means continuing.

She watches the pump. The pump watches nothing. But in the yellow light that leaks from its housing at night, the frog becomes a jade silhouette, and the duckweed becomes a plain of coins, and the water becomes something that holds both of them—frog and machine—in the same flat reflection, and if you saw this from above you would not be able to tell which was the living thing and which was the infrastructure.

---

The birds do not live in the Cut. The birds *use* the Cut. This is an important distinction, and the birds enforce it with the territorial precision of landlords.

The wren who claimed the drill rig arrived three springs ago. She had been singing from the scrub on the hilltop above—a perfectly adequate song, a wren's song, bright and rolling and small—and then she flew down to the floor of the Cut to hunt the midges that clouded above the pool, and she opened her mouth in the acoustic bowl, and the sound that came back was not a wren's song anymore. It was a cathedral. It was a declaration that rang off the rock walls and bounced from the conveyor framework and came back to her doubled, tripled, alive in a way she had never been alive before.

She moved in that afternoon.

Her nest is in the cab of the drill rig, built into the gap between the seat cushion and the backrest, constructed from moss and spider silk and one strip of weathered caution tape that she wove in because it was available and because wrens are not aesthetically precious. She raises two broods a year here. Her mate sings from the conveyor framework and she sings from the drill arm and their combined sound fills the Cut like water filling a vessel. They do not hear the pump as noise. They hear it as bass. They hear it as the foundation on which they build their song, the way a river builds on its bed.

Other birds have followed. A thrush who nests in the ivy stairway and sings at dusk in a voice that the Cut renders operatic, heartbreaking, too large for any single throat. A family of sparrows who colonized the electrical cabinet, who enter and exit through the gap where a panel rusted free, who chatter inside the metal box until it hums with their combined voices. A pair of finches who built on the birch's terrace, who chose the only living tree in a place of metal and stone, who sing from its branches each morning in a sound so amplified that the woman in the reflective vest once stopped her truck on the road above and sat for ten minutes with the engine running, listening, before she drove away.

The birds do not know they are loud. They think they are singing normally. They think the world is simply this resonant, this responsive, this ready to give back what they give to it. And perhaps they are right. Perhaps the world should be.

---

The birch is not native to the Cut. No tree is native to the Cut. The Cut was a hillside forty years ago, and the hillside was covered in grass and scrub and the quiet soil of quiet places, and then the machines came and opened it, and the exposed rock and the exposed clay and the exposed gravel were not environments that trees recognized as home.

But the birch came anyway. A seed—wind-carried, or perhaps dropped by a bird, perhaps by that same thrush who now nests in the ivy—landed in the pocket of soil that collected on the landing, and it found moisture there, the condensation that slicks every surface, and it found purchase on the steel grating below, and it grew.

Its roots have done something that birch roots do not typically do. They have grown around the grating, through the grating, into the gap between the grating and the concrete beneath. They have thickened where they grip the metal, forming knuckles of bark that look almost like fists. The tree is anchored now—not to the soil, which is shallow and which would let it fall in a hard wind—but to the structure itself. It is holding onto the Cut the way a person holds onto a railing. It has made the abandoned machinery into its skeleton.

It is fourteen meters tall and growing. It reaches for the strip of sky above—the Cut is narrow, the sky is a river of light that moves across the floor of the Cut each day—and it leans slightly east, slightly toward the morning sun, and its leaves in autumn turn a yellow so bright against the orange rust and the black-green moss that it looks deliberate, it looks like a flag, it looks like the Cut announcing itself to the sky.

The birch does not think. But it responds. It responds to the pump's vibration with a faint trembling in its upper branches that the birds find soothing. It responds to the moisture by growing faster than any birch has a right to grow in such marginal soil. It responds to the season by dropping its leaves into the pool below, where the frogs sit on them, where they decompose slowly into the duckweed, where they become part of the matrix that makes more frogs possible.

The birch is giving the Cut what it has. The Cut is giving the birch what it needs. This is not a contract. This is simply what happens when living things and abandoned places find each other.

---

The moss is patient in a way that nothing else in the Cut is patient. The birds are urgent. The frogs are present. The birch is ambitious. The moss is geological.

It spreads by millimeters. It extends along the pipes that carry the pump's output up and out of the Cut, and it does not rush, and it does not stop. It has been growing on the drill rig for eight years and has covered perhaps a third of the metal surface—not uniformly, but in patches, in cushions, in the deep green pads that darken to almost black when the condensation is heavy. It grows where the water collects. It grows where the shade is consistent. It grows in the seams between the machine's plates and in the grooves of the bolt heads and in the channel where cables once ran.

The moss does not remember the machine's purpose. The moss does not know that the drill arm once bit into rock and pulled out clay. It knows only that the drill arm is a surface, and that surfaces can be inhabited, and that inhabiting a surface slowly and thoroughly and without cessation is a form of possession that no ownership deed can counter.

When the moss has finished—when it has covered every pipe and every panel and every exposed bolt—nothing of the original machinery will be visible. It will be a green shape, a moss-memory of a drill rig, and someone will come along and wonder what this mound of moss was once shaped to do, and they will not be able to tell, and the moss will not tell them, because the moss does not care about before. The moss cares about next.

---

The woman in the reflective vest does not live in the Cut. She lives in a flat above a shop in the town, and she has a daughter who is fifteen and a cat who is orange and a habit of drinking her coffee black and standing at the window looking at nothing in particular for minutes at a time before she seems to remember where she is.

But she is the one who comes. Once a month, sometimes twice in the wet season when the pool rises and she has to check that the pump's output hasn't been obstructed. She parks on the road above. She walks the perimeter. She does not come down into the Cut itself.

She has been doing this for seven years. The job was supposed to be temporary—a contract position, site monitoring, the company that still pays the electric bill needed someone local to verify that the pump was functioning and the fence was intact. But the contract was never canceled, and the checks keep arriving in her account, and she keeps driving out on the first Tuesday of each month, and she keeps not going down into the Cut.

She tells herself it's because the stairs are unsafe. The eastern stair is rusted through in places, and the western stair is buried under ivy, and there is no other way down except a maintenance ladder that she is not trained to use. These are good reasons. These are responsible reasons. She is a responsible person.

But the real reason—which she knows and does not examine too closely—is that she is afraid of what will happen if she goes down into the acoustic bowl and hears it from the inside. She has stood at the rim. She has heard the birdsong amplified, the pump's thrum, the frogs' dusk chorus rising to meet it. She has felt the sound move through her like weather. And she has understood, with a certainty that she cannot explain and does not want to, that if she went down into the Cut—if she stood on the floor next to the pool, if she let the sound close around her completely—she would not come back up.

Not because the Cut would keep her. Not because she would fall or drown or be injured. But because she would understand what the frogs already understand: that the pump is a kind of living thing, and the moss is a kind of living thing, and the birds and the water and the birch and the rust and the duckweed are all kind of living things, and that the boundary between the mechanical and the biological is not a wall but a membrane, and that she—standing there in her reflective vest with her gauge-reader and her checklist—would be neither and both, would be another piece of infrastructure that the Cut had incorporated, would be another surface for the moss, another perch for the birds, another voice for the bowl to multiply and return.

She is not ready to be that. She is not sure she will ever be ready. But she keeps coming back. She keeps checking the gauge. She keeps mending the fence when it falls. And each month, when she drives away, she looks in the rearview mirror at the gouge in the hillside where the green is growing thicker and the sound is growing louder, and she thinks: *next time*. Next time she will go down. Next time she will hear it from the inside. Next time she will let herself become part of whatever the Cut is becoming.

She has been thinking this for seven years.

The Cut can wait. The Cut has time.

---

There is something else in the Cut. Not a creature. Not a plant. Not a machine. Something that exists only in the combination of all these things—the sound and the damp and the green and the metal and the patience and the time. Something that emerges when the pump's rhythm and the birdsong and the frogs' chorus and the birch's creak and the moss's silent spread all happen at once, which is always.

It does not have a name. It does not need one. It is what lives in the Cut when nothing else is looking. It is what the Cut is becoming.

It is patient. It is loud. It is alive in a way that includes the mechanical and the biological and does not distinguish between them. It is growing at the speed of moss, at the speed of rust, at the speed of water seeping into rock.

It is not in a hurry. It has nowhere else to be.
