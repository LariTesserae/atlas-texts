# Who Lives in Newstrata

---

## The Quarryworkers

They go by many names. The cutters. The readers. Those who know the pre- by its grain.

Maret Solene has worked the eastern face for forty-seven years. She can tell by touch where the softstone will fracture and where it will refuse. She runs her palms along the exposed grey-white surface and feels—what? Not warmth exactly. A responsiveness. The stone acknowledges her hands the way a sleeping animal acknowledges a touch: not by waking, but by shifting slightly in its sleep.

She knows this sounds like mysticism. It is not. Or if it is, it is a mysticism born from proximity. You cannot work the pre- for forty-seven years without developing a relationship with it, and the word *relationship* implies two parties, and Maret Solene has come to accept that the pre- is a party to this arrangement, though she could not tell you what kind of party it is.

Her daughter Lith works beside her now. Lith's hands are younger and less certain. She has not yet learned to feel the fracture lines that do not yet exist but will. Maret can. Maret can trace the future cracks in stone that is still deciding whether to be stone. This is not foresight. It is more like *listening to something that is trying to tell you what it will become*, if you are patient enough to hear it.

The quarryworkers have a saying: *The pre- does not hide. It waits.*

They do not cut the stone so much as they assist its separation. They find the places where the softstone is already reaching toward independence, and they help it along. A chisel strike at the right moment, in the right place, and a block of pre- comes free with a sound that is not a crack but a sigh—something relieved, something that was ready.

They have cut the pre- for twelve generations. Their hands have shaped the walls of every building in Newstrata, and those walls have shaped the people inside them, and the people have spoken their lives into the stone, and the stone has held those words and sometimes given them back.

Maret Solene does not find this strange. She finds it ordinary. She finds it *geology*.

## The Children Who Are Not Afraid

The children of Newstrata are born to the dimness and the hum. They do not know that the world below the clouds is different. They do not know that in other places, the stone is finished and the animals have been the same for ten thousand years and the buildings do not listen.

A child named Essa, who is seven, walks to school each morning through the outer streets. She passes the walkers as they browse the pillargrass. The walkers are tall—three times her height—and their legs move with a geometry that does not follow the logic of joints she has seen in picture books of horses or dogs. Their faces are all mouth, long and tapering, and they strip the dark leaves from the stalks with a methodical patience that Essa finds soothing.

She is not afraid of them. She has never been afraid of them. They were here before she was born. They will be here after she goes to sleep. They are as much a part of the streets as the lamp posts, and they are less dangerous, because the lamp posts sometimes leak gas and the walkers have never hurt anyone.

Her mother, who grew up below the clouds and came up on the lifts when she married Essa's father, is afraid of the walkers. She has been told they are harmless. She has seen them ignore children, ignore adults, ignore everything that is not pillargrass. She is still afraid. Her fear is a product of a life lived on finished ground, where animals have categories, where the boundary between creature and environment is firm.

Essa's fear is reserved for other things. She is afraid of the foundry when it runs hot. She is afraid of the rim-edge where the cliff drops away into clouds. She is afraid of the stories the oldest children tell about the center, about the newest places where the ground is so fresh it might not hold you, where you might sink into stone that has not yet committed to solidity.

She does not fear the walkers because the walkers are *of this place*, and she is of this place, and being of the same place means you recognize each other, even if you do not speak the same language, even if you do not have language at all.

## The Burrowers and the Engineers

There is a woman named Torr who maps the tunnels. She has done this for thirty years. She goes where the burrowers have gone, and she measures, and she records, and she marks the places where the city can follow.

The burrowers are not pets. They are not partners. They are not wildlife. Torr does not know what they are, and she has stopped trying to categorize them because categories require a stable taxonomy, and the burrowers are not stable. They were smaller thirty years ago. Their limbs were shorter. Their digging was less precise. They are evolving—or *settling*, as she thinks of it—into something more themselves, though what they are becoming is still unclear.

She has learned to read their tunnels the way the quarryworkers read the stone. The width of a burrow tells her the age of the burrower who made it. The depth tells her the quality of the pre- below. The direction tells her where the newest stone is pushing upward, because the burrowers always go toward the new, toward the unformed, toward the place where the pre- is warmest and most responsive.

Torr has touched a burrower once. It was in a newly excavated tunnel at the city's edge, and the creature was still there, resting, its six limbs tucked beneath its broad body. It watched her with eyes that were not eyes—patches of photosensitivity that seemed to be in the process of becoming eyes, developing toward vision the way the pre- developed toward stone.

She reached out and placed her hand on its flank. It was warm. It was the same warmth as the pre-. It was the same texture, almost—the same sense of something in progress, something not yet finalized. The burrower did not flinch. It regarded her with its developing eyes and then it turned away and continued its excavation, and Torr understood that the burrower was not *in* the pre-. The burrower was *of* the pre-. It was the same substance taking a different shape, the same process expressing itself through different means.

She has never told anyone this, because it is not useful information for the maps, and also because she is not certain it is true. She is not certain what *true* means in a place where the ground beneath her feet has not yet decided to be ground.

## The Reservoir Keepers

Old Pahto tends the central reservoir. He has done this for fifty-three years. He lives in a small room above the water, and he sleeps to the sound of the slow, large things moving below the surface, and he wakes to their bioluminescence pulsing through the floorboards.

He knows the reservoir creatures by their light. Each one has a different pattern—some slow and steady, some rapid and erratic, some that seem to be in conversation with each other, their pulses synchronizing and then separating like breathing that finds and loses a shared rhythm.

He does not know what they eat. He has never found remains or waste. He has never seen them mate or reproduce. They are simply *there*, as they have always been there, and their numbers increase slowly each year, which suggests reproduction, but he has witnessed nothing he would call birth.

He has fished them twice. Not to eat—the meat is pale and bland and makes the dreams too vivid—but to examine. The first time, the creature he caught seemed to be a fish in the way that a costume is a person: the shape was right but the substance was approximate. The scales were not quite scales. The fins were not quite fins. Underneath the general idea of *fish* was something that was still working out the specifics.

The second time, years later, the creature he caught was more completely a fish. Its scales were defined. Its fins were articulated. It had eyes that seemed to function. Evolution in fast-forward, or design being refined, or reality settling into a groove it had been carving.

He returned both creatures to the water. They sank slowly, glowing, and resumed their circuits of the reservoir floor, and Pahto decided that understanding them was less important than maintaining them, because they lit the lower streets, and they were beautiful, and beauty does not require explanation.

## The Foundry Families

The Graethe family has worked the great foundry at the rim's edge for eleven generations. The foundry has burned for three hundred years. Its fires have never been extinguished. This is not superstition. This is practicality. The foundry is built on ground that is slightly warm, and the pre- responds to heat, and if the fires ever went out, no one is certain they could be restarted, because no one is certain the foundry would still be a foundry and not simply a shape that a foundry used to occupy.

Henra Graethe is the current matriarch. She is sixty-four. Her arms are scarred from iron splatter. Her lungs are scarred from smoke. Her mind is scarred from the dreams, which are more vivid for those who work near the heat, because the pre- is closer to the surface near the foundries—the upward push is stronger at the edges, and the stone is newer, and the boundaries are thinner.

She works the iron that comes up on the lifts. She has never been below the clouds. She has no desire to go. What would she find there? Finished ground. Settled reality. A world where the iron was always iron and the stone was always stone and nothing was in the process of becoming anything else.

She prefers the process. She prefers the uncertainty. She prefers to live in a place where the answer to the question *what is this?* might reasonably be *not yet*.

Her son Bael is learning the forge. He is twenty-two and impatient in a way that the city discourages, because impatience implies that there is somewhere to get to, and Newstrata is not going anywhere. It is staying. It is staying and growing at the same time, which is a different thing than moving.

Henra has tried to teach him the rhythm of the forge—the slow, patient hammering that works *with* the iron rather than against it—but Bael wants speed, wants efficiency, wants the productivity he has heard about in the cities below the clouds.

He will learn or he will leave. Most learn. The pre- teaches patience by proximity. The stone teaches slowness by example. The city teaches staying by being a place that is difficult to leave and impossible to stop loving, even when the love is complicated, even when the love involves dreams that are not entirely your own.

## The Walkers Who Browse

What is it like to be a walker?

This question cannot be answered from inside a human mind, but it can be approximated, the way the reservoir creatures approximate fish.

The walkers do not think in words. They do not think in images. They think in *inclinations*—toward moisture, toward warmth, toward the pre- where it lies closest to the surface. Their awareness is environmental rather than interior. They do not consider themselves. They do not consider anything. They *move toward*, which is a kind of thought, and they *consume*, which is a kind of action, and they *rest*, which is a kind of being, and the combination of these three things constitutes a life.

They are aware of the humans. They are aware of the burrowers. They are aware of each other. But their awareness is spacious and undemanding. They do not react to the humans because the humans are not relevant to them. They are not predators. They are not prey. They are browsers of the pillargrass, and the pillargrass is abundant, and there is no competition for it, and so there is no reason to attend to anything that is not pillargrass.

Except sometimes, in the early morning, when the mist reaches up from the clouds and the stone glows with its faint internal light, the walkers stop browsing and stand still, and they face the center of the plateau, where the pre- is newest, and they remain motionless for long minutes, and then they resume their feeding.

What are they doing during those still moments?

Listening. Perhaps. To what, no human can say. But if the stone listens, and the walls listen, and the ground listens, perhaps it is not surprising that the creatures of the ground also listen, and perhaps what they hear is the same thing the quarryworkers feel when they press their palms to the emerging rock: the long, slow pulse of something becoming itself, a geological heartbeat, the respiration of a plateau that is not yet done thinking.

## The People Who Have Changed

There are those who have lived in Newstrata so long that they are no longer entirely certain they are still human.

This is not a deterioration. This is an adaptation. The pre- changes everything it touches. The stone absorbs sound and gives it back altered. The water carries minerals that make the dreams more vivid. The air is thick with the fine dust of the emerging rock, and that dust settles in the lungs, and over decades it becomes part of the body, and the body becomes partly stone, and the boundary between person and place begins to blur.

Old Veth, who is ninety-seven and has lived her entire life in the city, sometimes cannot tell where her body ends and the floor begins. She sits in her chair in her room in the lower district, and the stone wall is warm against her back, and she can feel the conversations of the street outside—can *feel* them, not hear them, as though the words are entering through her spine rather than her ears.

She dreams of the plateau. Not the plateau as it is but the plateau as it will be, in ten thousand years, when the upward push has finally stopped and the stone has settled into its final form and the city is built on ground that is no longer growing. In her dreams, the city is white and shining and the walkers are gone because there is nothing left to browse, and the burrowers are gone because there is nothing left to excavate, and the reservoir creatures are gone because the water has become just water.

She finds this dream sad. She prefers the current plateau—the unfinished one, the one that is still arriving, the one that generates new forms with the same indifference with which it generates new stone. She prefers the company of creatures that are not yet fully themselves because she is not yet fully herself, and there is a comfort in shared incompleteness.

She knows she is changing. She knows her bones are denser than they should be. She knows her skin is slightly warm to the touch, like the pre-. She knows that when she speaks, the walls of her room seem to hold her words a moment longer than they should, as though they are considering keeping them.

She does not mind. She is becoming part of the city. She is becoming part of the plateau. She is becoming *of this place*, and being of a place, she has found, is better than being merely in one.

## The Unformed

And then there are the ones at the center.

The ones who are not yet.

At the plateau's heart, where the rock is newest and the upward push is strongest and the pre- is still pre-, there are shapes that have not resolved. Movements without clear cause. Sounds without clear source. Presences that are felt but not seen, or seen only indirectly, only in peripheral vision, only in the moment before attention focuses and they become something else or nothing at all.

Are they animals? Are they people? Are they something the plateau is trying to produce, the way it produces stone and walkers and burrowers? Are they something the plateau is *remembering*, some echo of a form that existed before the plateau began to rise, some template that the geology is attempting to re-create in its slow, provisional way?

No one knows. No one goes to the center for long. The engineers mark the growth lines and retreat. The builders construct the newest district and retreat. The boundary between real and unreal is too thin at the center. You can feel it in your teeth, in your skull, in the place behind your eyes where the brain constructs the world from electrical signals. At the center, the construction process is visible. You can feel yourself being assembled. You can feel the plateau trying to decide what you are.

Visitors find this terrifying. Residents find it normal. Or not normal—never normal—but *familiar*, the way a recurring dream is familiar: you do not control it, you do not understand it, but you have been here before, and you know that if you wait, the feeling will pass, and you will find yourself back on solid ground, or at least ground that has committed to being ground for the time being.

## The Plateau Itself

Which brings us to the question that the people of Newstrata do not ask because they already know the answer, and the visitors cannot ask because they do not have the framework to formulate it.

Is the plateau alive?

Not alive in the way a bacterium is alive. Not alive in the way a tree is alive. Alive in a way that is still being defined, the same way the pre- is still being defined, the same way the walkers and the burrowers and the reservoir creatures are still being defined—provisionally, in-progress, subject to revision as more information becomes available.

The plateau pushes upward. The plateau generates stone. The plateau produces creatures that browse and burrow and swim. The plateau listens. The plateau holds sound. The plateau remembers.

These are characteristics that living things possess.

But the plateau also rises by centimeters each year, which is a geological process, which is not alive, which is simply the movement of tectonic plates and the pressure of magma and the slow mechanics of a planet forming itself from the inside out.

The people of Newstrata do not see a contradiction, because they do not require a boundary between geology and biology. They do not require a boundary between anything and anything else, because they live in a place where boundaries have not yet formed, where the categories are suggestions, where the answer to the question *is this alive?* is the same as the answer to the question *is this stone?* and the answer is:

*Not yet.*

*And also: yes.*

*And also: wait.*

## Why They Stay (Revisited)

They stay because they are becoming part of something that is becoming itself.

They stay because the stone remembers their words and gives them back when they need to hear them.

They stay because the walkers browse the pillargrass in the morning mist and are not afraid.

They stay because the burrowers excavate the future and the engineers map it.

They stay because the reservoir creatures pulse with a light that is not yet fully light but is beautiful in its approximation.

They stay because the pre- is warm beneath their feet, and its warmth is the warmth of something working, something in process, something that will not be finished in their lifetimes or their children's lifetimes or their children's children's lifetimes, and there is a peace in that, a peace that cannot be found on ground that is already done.

They stay because in Newstrata, *becoming* is not a transitional state. It is the permanent condition. It is the weather and the architecture and the philosophy and the ground.

They stay because they have asked the plateau what it wants, and the plateau has answered by rising, by generating, by producing forms that did not exist before, and the answer, translated from geology into language, is:

*More.*

*More of this.*

*More of everything that has not yet been but is arriving, centimeter by centimeter, breath by breath, stone by stone, dream by dream, up through the cloud layer and into the cool dim air where the city waits, as it has always waited, as it will continue to wait, for the next thing the ground will give it.*

*And the people will be there to receive it, and to live in it, and to become part of it, and to be, in the end, as much a product of the pre- as the walkers and the burrowers and the stone walls that hold their words and the water that holds their dreams and the plateau that holds them all.*

*This is who lives in Newstrata.*

*The becoming-people, in the becoming-city, on the becoming-ground, in the becoming-world.*

*And it is enough.*
