The arthropods are the easiest to see, so most visitors make the mistake of thinking they understand the ward through them. The lines of pale, translucent bodies moving through the corridors—twelve, fifteen, sometimes twenty-strong—each carrying a flake of discarded wall-growth, each processing what the colony sheds. They look like workers. They look like they have purpose. Visitors watch them and feel reassured. *Ah*, they think. *An ecosystem. Roles. I understand this.*

But the arthropods don't work. That's the wrong word and the wrong frame. They *are*. They exist in the ward the way enzymes exist in a gut—not employed, not directed, simply present where the chemistry requires them. When the walls shed their papery waste, the arthropods are already there. Not because they were called. Because they are part of the shedding. The waste doesn't exist without them and they don't exist without the waste. It's not mutualism as we understand it. It's something more like a single process that happens to have mobile and immobile phases.

If you watch one of the pale arthropods closely—close enough to see the faint pulse of something inside its translucent carapace, something that isn't quite blood and isn't quite sap—you might notice that it doesn't navigate. It doesn't choose a passage or recall a route. It moves the way water moves through a sponge: into whatever space will receive it, filling whatever gap the pressure of its body can stretch toward. The colony's passages open for it, or don't. It goes where it's admitted. Its legs—six, eight, the count varies and may not be fixed—don't carry it so much as translate the ward's openness into motion.

They don't sleep. They don't wake. They process, and when there's nothing to process, they wait in the smaller chambers with their mandibles folded against their thoraxes, and they are still, and the stillness is indistinguishable from death except that the chamber around them is still breathing, still warm, still producing the waste that will eventually rouse them to motion again.

---

The larger residents are harder to observe, not because they're rare but because they don't register as separate from the architecture until they move.

They are warm. This is the first surprise, if you touch one. The colony is warm—the stone, the walls, the air between them—but these creatures are *warmer*. Their bodies hold heat the way the mesa holds it, slowly, stubbornly, releasing it over hours even after they've left a chamber. You can tell where one has been resting by the warmth left behind in the wall's curve, a lingering signature that takes half a day to fade.

They look like someone's guess at a reptile crossed with someone else's guess at a mollusk, but that's the wrong lineage entirely. They aren't descended from anything that ever lived outside the ward. They emerged *from* it. The colony produced them the way it produces chambers and corridors and the papery waste the arthropods carry: as an expression of its own growth, its own logic, its own slow negotiations with the shape of the mesa and the minerals threading through the basalt.

There's a moment—a stage, a phase, a threshold—in the colony's development where a chamber doesn't become a chamber. Where the bud that would have thickened into a wall and hollowed into a passage instead *differentiates*. Instead of building architecture, it builds a body. The walls fold inward rather than outward. The growth concentrates, condenses, develops internal structure instead of external space. What emerges is one of the dark, low, patient things that move through the corridors with such certainty.

They are not born. They are *extruded*. And they return to the colony when they die—or when the colony decides, through whatever slow chemical process decides such things, that their current form is no longer needed. A resident that has reached the end of its function will find a chamber that fits its body, settle into the warmth, and simply *stop*. Over the next several days, the walls close in. The growth re-absorbs what it made. The body becomes architecture again. The chamber might become a passage, or it might become the site of a new extrusion, or it might seal itself entirely and become a memory the colony carries in its dense, layered substrate.

They don't seem to mind. Whether they're capable of minding is a question that reveals more about the asker than the asked.

---

There is something else.

Not a creature. Not exactly. A presence that moves through the ward without body or boundary, felt rather than seen, more like a change in temperature than a thing with edges.

It lives in the timing.

When a passage opens for one of the dark residents, there is a moment—just a moment—between the wall's decision to yield and the actual yielding. A hesitation. A gap where the growth *could* open but hasn't yet. If you watch carefully, you can see it: the tissue thinning, the fibers loosening, the space almost ready—and then a pause, as though something is considering.

That consideration isn't reflex. It isn't stimulus and response. The colony doesn't open passages because the residents approach; it opens them because *it has decided to*, and the timing of that decision varies in ways that can't be explained by the physical properties of the wall or the speed of the approaching body. Sometimes the passage opens early, almost eagerly. Sometimes it makes the resident wait. Sometimes it doesn't open at all, and the resident turns—without urgency, without frustration—and finds another route.

Something is choosing.

Not consciously. Not the way you choose. But not mechanically, either. The colony's growth follows patterns that don't repeat, rhythms that don't cycle, preferences that shift over weeks and months for no apparent reason. A passage that opens readily today may be stubborn tomorrow. A chamber that welcomed bodies last month may seal itself this month, its walls thickening, its entrance narrowing to nothing, the space inside becoming private in a way that suggests the colony has turned its attention elsewhere.

The ward has moods.

Not emotions—nothing so defined, so human. But states. Periods of expansion and contraction, openness and closure, density and yielding, that don't correspond to any external stimulus the visitors have been able to identify. The minerals feed at a constant rate. The warmth doesn't fluctuate. The light is always the same green-gray glow filtered through the lattice of growth. But the colony *feels* different from one day to the next. The corridors are narrower or wider. The air is thicker or thinner. The residents move with more or less certainty, as though they're reading something in the walls that visitors can't perceive.

If the ward has a mind, it isn't a mind like yours. It doesn't think in propositions or images. It doesn't remember in narrative. It doesn't want in the way that wanting requires a self to want things *for*. But it *orients*. It has directions it favors, directions it avoids. It grows toward some stimuli and away from others. It accommodates some visitors and seals out others, and the pattern of its accommodations isn't random, isn't reactive, isn't simple.

It's learning, maybe. Or becoming. Or remembering something that happened to the stone before the mesa lifted, something the rock still holds in its grooved and fluted surface, some pressure that passed over it long ago and left marks deep enough to live in.

---

The visitors come sometimes. Rarely. The mesa is remote, and the lift is recent, and not many have reason to climb the severe edges and cross the broken rim to the green loud colony at its center. But they come.

And the ward receives them.

It doesn't eat them. It doesn't trap them. But it also doesn't *let them leave unchanged*. The corridors open for them, but slowly. The chambers welcome them, but closely. The walls press against their shoulders with a warmth that isn't uncomfortable, exactly, but isn't ignorable either. The air they breathe is the colony's air—humid, mineral, alive with the chemistry of growth. By the time they've spent a day inside the ward, they've absorbed something. Inhaled it. Ingested it. Taken it in through the mucous membranes and the pores and the slow osmosis of living tissue in contact with living tissue.

They leave, and they are fine. They are fine for weeks. Months.

But some part of them is still moving at the speed of the ward. Some part has learned the patience of the dark residents, the stillness of the pale arthropods, the slow pulse of the rock beneath the roots. They find themselves waiting longer than they need to. Standing in doorways without quite deciding to go through. Sleeping more heavily. Breathing more slowly. Feeling the warmth of their own bodies in a new way—as though the heat is a language they're just beginning to understand, a message from some deep mineral place they'd never thought to listen to.

They don't come back, most of them. Not because they can't. Because the distance feels longer each time. Because the world outside the mesa seems faster, louder, less accommodating than they remember. Because something in them has shifted toward the rhythm of the ward, and the shift isn't reversible, and they know—if they know anything—that if they returned and stayed long enough, the colony would grow around them the way it grows around everything that stays.

Not consuming. Not trapping. Just *including*. Opening a chamber that fits their body. Thickening the walls around their warmth. Adding them to the dense, layered, living substrate that the next generation will grow into and through.

It takes a long time. Years. Decades. The ward is patient.

But it has begun.
