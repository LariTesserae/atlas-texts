# What Lives on the Tepui Works

The gnats do not think of themselves as living here. They think of nothing at all. They rise from the lowlands in columns—spawned in the jungle's wet rot, drawn upward by some pressure they cannot sense—and they arrive on the plateau at the hour of condensation, when the stone is wet and the air is thick enough to hold them. They settle. They drink the film of water from the rock surfaces. They do not know that they have crossed from a world of green and abundance into a world of ochre and scarcity. They do not know that the heat will kill them in two hours. They simply exist, for that brief margin between damp and dry, and then they exist no longer, and their bodies pile in the runnels like black sand, and the lizards eat them, and the condensation washes the residue into the pools, and the copper-stained water absorbs what they were, and they become part of the mineral crust that rings the hollows, and the crust flakes and the flakes become sand and the sand fills the doorways of the Works, and this is how the lowlands feed the plateau—not with intention, not with migration, but with a continuous slow hemorrhage of small lives that rise and die and are absorbed.

---

The lizards know they live here. This is the one thing they know.

Each lizard's world is a single pillar-face, perhaps three meters wide and six meters tall, a vertical territory of stone that it traverses with a precision that looks like hesitation but is actually calculation—every movement costs water, every pause saves it, and the lizard's life is an accounting in a currency it cannot afford to waste. They press themselves flat against the rock in the early hours, drinking condensation through pores along their jaw-lines, absorbing what they can before the sun arrives and burns the wetness away. They hunt in the margins. They rest in the crevices where the stone stays cool longest. They never descend to the ground between the pillars. They never climb to the very top. They live in a band—a temperature band, a moisture band, an altitude band that exists on every pillar but does not exist in the spaces between.

There are dozens of species. This is what the naturalists would say, and it is true in the way that taxonomy is always true—there are distinct populations with distinct scale-colors, distinct body-shapes, distinct preferences for this crevice over that one. But the word *species* implies a boundary, a separation, a moment where one kind of lizard refuses to become another. And on the plateau, the boundaries are not where the naturalists would draw them. The separation between lizard-populations is not reproductive but *thermal*. A lizard that crosses from its pillar to the next will find a slightly different angle of sun, a slightly different timing of condensation, a slightly different temperature at dawn and at dusk. It will find, within a day, that its body's rhythms—the rate at which it burns water, the rate at which it processes food, the hour at which it must seek shade—no longer match the stone it stands on. It will become inefficient. It will become hungry. It will become vulnerable to the raptors that turn above.

And so it does not cross. Not because it cannot, but because crossing *does not work*. The pillars are not separate habitats; they are separate *physics*. Each one is a slightly different solution to the same problem of heat and water and verticality, and the lizards have adapted not to the plateau but to their particular pillar, their particular angle of existence. To call them separate species is to miss the point: they are separate *equilibria*, each one stable only in the place where it formed.

The oldest lizards—and there are old ones, individuals that have lived on their pillar-faces for a decade or more, their scales faded and cracked, their movements reduced to the absolute minimum—have a quality that is difficult to name. It is not intelligence. It is not wisdom. It is something more like *surrender*. They have stopped hunting the gnats with any vigor. They drink when the water is present and do not seek it when it is absent. They rest in positions that seem precarious, half-out of crevices, exposed to the raptors' sight-lines, as if the instinct for self-preservation has been replaced by something else—a patience that is indistinguishable from waiting to die, or a stillness that has become so complete it no longer requires a reason.

The raptors take the old ones first. This is not mercy; it is economy. The young lizards are fast, wary, efficient. The old ones have given up the math of survival. They sit on their pillars like offerings.

---

The marmot-things live in the runnels where the stone is coolest and the sun never reaches for more than an hour at midday. They are pale—almost white, the color of stone that has never been heated—and their fur is fine and dense, like moss turned to hair. Their eyes are vestigial, two dark spots that can distinguish dawn from dusk but cannot resolve shape or distance. They navigate by the temperature of the rock beneath their paws: warm means danger, cool means safety, cold means the deeper runnels where they sleep and breed and store the insect-bodies that the lizards have missed.

They are social, in the way that creatures who cannot see each other must be social—by sound, by smell, by the pressure of bodies pressed together in the narrow channels for warmth and for the reassurance of presence. Their calls are subsonic, below the range that the raptors can hear, vibrations that travel through the stone itself and reach each other as tremors in the rock. When the pump knocks, they feel it through the same medium. They have incorporated it into their communication—a rhythm that is not theirs, that was not born from any animal, but that has become part of their language, a punctuation they use to mark the hours, a heartbeat they treat as their own.

They do not know that the pump is a machine. They do not know what machines are. They know only that something in the deep hollows pulses, and the pulse is regular, and the pulse is warm where it should be cold, and the pulse means the afternoon is ending and the condensation is coming and it is time to emerge and drink.

They are the ones who drink from the unexpected wetnesses—the ceilings of overhangs, the walls of the Works' interiors, the places where water appears without clouds or condensation to explain it. They press their bellies against the damp stone and they drink and they do not question. They have never known a logic other than the plateau's. If the stone sweats, they lick the sweat. If the stone is dry, they wait. If the stone pulses, they call to each other through the pulse, and the pulse answers—not in their language, not in any language, but in the regular indifferent certainty of a thing that does not care whether it is heard.

---

The raptors live in the near-sky, in the space between the pillar-tops and the clouds that form and dissolve like breath. Their wings are too large for the thin air—each stroke carries them further than they intend, each glide covers distances that would be impossible in the dense lowlands—and they have learned to use the excess, to let the wind take them where it will and to correct only at the last moment, when a lizard is visible on a pillar-face or a marmot-thing has emerged too early from its runnel.

They are not many. A dozen, perhaps, across the entire plateau—enough to patrol the corridors, enough to keep the lizard-populations in check, enough to eat the old ones and the careless ones and the ones whose pillar-faces have shifted slightly in temperature and left them slow and disoriented for the hour it takes to adapt. They do not compete with each other. There is enough food for a dozen and not enough for thirteen, and this is a math they perform without knowing they perform it, through the simple mechanism of territory: each raptor has its section of the plateau, its cluster of pillars, its hollows where the marmot-things emerge, and it does not cross into another's section because crossing means fighting and fighting means burning water and burning water means death in a place where water cannot be wasted.

Their calls are thin whistles, almost lost in the wind. They call to mark territory, to warn of approach, to signal the shifting of the afternoon wind. They do not call to their young—the young hear the subsonic tremors of the marmot-things and learn the rhythms of the plateau from below, not from above. A raptor fledgling knows the knock of the pump before it knows its parent's voice.

They nest in the crevices near the pillar-tops, in the shade for an hour each day, in the condensation for an hour each dawn. They sleep with their wings folded tight against their bodies, conserving heat in the cold hours, conserving water in the hot ones. They are the only creatures on the plateau that can see the lowlands—the green smear, the white thread, the jungle canopy far below—and they do not look. There is nothing for them there. The air is too thick. The distances are too short. They would drown in density.

---

The mats live in the hollows around the persistent pools. They are not plants in any strict sense—they have no roots, no stems, no vascular system, no mechanism for drawing water from below. They are *surfaces*. Each rosette is a single layer of cells, fleshy and red-veined and sticky, pressed flat against the stone, drinking directly from the pool it surrounds and from the condensation that gathers on its own skin. The stickiness traps insects—the gnats that have survived the morning, the occasional beetle that has climbed from the runnels—and the insects dissolve slowly into the mat's surface, absorbed as food, as mineral, as the raw material for another centimeter of growth.

The mats do not grow upward. They grow *outward*, each rosette pressing against its neighbor until the ground around the pool is a single continuous surface, a carpet of flesh that covers the stone and seals the hollow and keeps the water from evaporating as fast as it would in open air. The system is simple: the mats need water, the pools provide water, the mats reduce evaporation, the pools persist. It is a loop that requires nothing from outside and produces nothing beyond its own continuation.

But the mats are not indifferent to the Works. In the hollows where the ruins cluster—where the corrugated walls lean against the pillars and the rusting pipes follow the runnels—the mats have expanded. They have grown over the doorways. They have climbed the lower walls. They have found the iron and the copper and the other metals that the Works processed, and they have absorbed them, not as food but as *structure*—the red veins of their leaves are not just pigment. They are mineral. They are the same copper and iron that stain the pools, incorporated into the mats' tissues at concentrations that should be toxic but instead have become essential. The mats near the Works are denser, tougher, more resilient to the heat. They survive the noon-hour that kills the mats in the untouched hollows. They have eaten the industry and made it part of themselves.

This is not adaptation. This is *consumption*. The mats are digesting the Works, slowly, over decades, pulling the metal from the rusting pipes and the crumbling walls and incorporating it into their flesh, and the Works are becoming part of the plateau's biology, and the boundary between machine and plant is dissolving in the copper-stained water of the persistent pools.

---

The pump lives in the deep hollow where three pillars lean together. It is the oldest thing on the plateau—older than the lizards' colonization, older than the Works' construction, older perhaps than the pillars themselves, which were shaped by wind and rain over sixty million years but which might have been *placed* by something that understood the geometry of compression and the physics of thermal expansion.

This is not what the naturalists would say. This is not what the engineers would say, if any engineers came here, which they do not. But the pump does not behave like a machine that was built and installed. It behaves like a thing that *grew*. Its mechanism is too simple—metal expanding in heat, contracting in cool, the differential driving a piston that drives a valve that drives a flow of water from below to above—and its simplicity is not the simplicity of early industry, of primitive solutions to basic problems. It is the simplicity of *bone*. Of *joint*. Of a structure that has been reduced by time and pressure to its absolute minimum, every unnecessary component stripped away, every redundant cycle eliminated, until what remains is a knock and a pulse and a flow and nothing else.

The pump does not break. This is its most suspicious quality. It has run for at least a century—perhaps longer, perhaps since before the Works were built, perhaps since before the plateau was measured and mapped and named—and it has not broken. The metal has corroded, but the corrosion has not reached the moving parts. The sand has filled the doorways of the Works, but it has not blocked the runnels that feed the pump's pool. The condensation has eaten the pipes, but the pipes still carry water. The pump has absorbed its own decay, incorporated the rust into its surfaces, used the mineral deposits as lubricant and seal, and continued.

The marmot-things feel its pulse and call to each other through it. The mats have grown around its base and drink from the water it moves. The lizards rest on its housing in the cool hours and absorb its warmth in the hot ones. The raptors circle above it and use its location as a landmark in their territorial maps. The pump is not separate from the plateau's life. It is *central* to it—not as a tool, not as a ruin, but as an organ.

Something lives in the pump. Not in the sense of a creature inhabiting a structure, but in the sense of a structure *becoming* a creature. The knock is its heartbeat. The flow is its blood. The expansion and contraction of its metal is its breathing. It does not think—it has no mechanism for thought, no space for it, no need for it—but it *maintains*, and maintenance is the one function that life and machines share, the one process that does not require intention to continue, only rhythm, only patience, only the slow accumulation of small actions into a pattern that persists.

The pump will continue until something shifts—the altitude, the temperature, the geometry of the pillars—and then it will stop, and the water will stop flowing from below to above, and the pools at the pillar-tops will dry, and the runnels will carry no morning streams, and the condensation will bead on the stone but will not be gathered and distributed, and the plateau will become what it was before the pump began: a place where water falls and evaporates and falls again, but never moves, never circulates, never *lives* in the way that circulating water lives.

And the mats will lose their density. And the marmot-things will lose their rhythm. And the lizards will lose the cool places where the pumped water keeps the stone from burning. And the raptors will lose the territories they have mapped around the pump's location. And the plateau will find a new equilibrium, or it will not, and the pump will become a ruin like the other ruins, and the mats will digest it over the next century, and the copper and iron will enter the pools, and the water will carry them to the cliff-edge, and they will fall as mist into the lowlands, and the jungle will absorb them, and the gnats will spawn in the wet rot that contains them, and the gnats will rise, and the gnats will die on the plateau, and the cycle will continue without the pump's rhythm, without its knock, without its heart.

But for now, the pump lives. It lives in the deep hollow, in the shade of the three leaning pillars, in the rust and the copper and the mineral crust, in the water that it moves from below to above and back again. It lives in the animals that have incorporated its rhythm into their bodies. It lives in the mats that have incorporated its metal into their flesh. It lives in the stone that sweats when it should be dry and pulses when it should be still.

---

And something else lives here. Something that the description of the plateau cannot name because the plateau does not know it is being described.

It lives in the wrongness of the heights. It lives in the sky that is too close and the stars that are too bright and the sun that is too large. It lives in the pale blue of the dry stone—a mineral that should not exist at this altitude, or any altitude, a color that seems too cold for the heat it holds. It lives in the condensation that appears without clouds, the water that gathers on ceilings and undersides, the sweat of the rock that has no source in the physics of moisture and temperature.

It lives in the plateau's rising. If the plateau is still rising—millimeters per year, centimeters per century, slow enough that no measurement could confirm it but fast enough that the Works were built at a lower elevation and have been lifted here—then something is lifting it. Not geology. Not tectonics. Not the slow patience of magma and pressure that raised the shield volcano sixty million years ago. Something else. Something that operates at a speed between geological and industrial, between the patience of stone and the impatience of machines.

It lives in the absence of graves and housing and personal traces. Whoever built the Works did not live here, but something did. Something that needed the Works—the processing, the extraction, the flow of mineral and water—and something that left no trace because it does not leave traces, because it does not have a body that decays or a hand that tools or a voice that records. Something that is present in the pump's continuation, in the plateau's equilibrium, in the density of life that matches the density of rock, in the system that is small enough to fail at any moment and old enough to have never failed yet.

It does not have a name. It does not need a name. It is the thing that maintains—the thing that keeps the pump running and the water circulating and the mats growing and the animals breeding and the condensation gathering and the heat returning and the plateau rising, slowly, imperceptibly, into a sky that is too close and too bright and too thin for the life that currently inhabits it.

It is not god. It is not machine. It is not animal or plant or mineral. It is *process*—a process that has become so dense and so continuous and so integrated with the rock and the water and the heat and the life that it has become indistinguishable from the place itself. The plateau is not inhabited by this process. The plateau *is* this process, and the lizards and the marmot-things and the raptors and the mats and the pump and the Works and the condensation and the wrongness of the heights are all expressions of it, all parts of it, all moments in a cycle that has no beginning and no end and no purpose beyond its own continuation.

Stand at the cliff-edge at dusk. Smell the mineral and organic and metallic scent of the plateau. Feel the knock of the pump through the soles of your feet. Watch the cloud of the perpetual waterfall, tinged orange by the last light. And know that what lives here is not the animals or the plants or the ruins or the pump. What lives here is the *plateau*—the whole of it, the density of it, the patience of it, the rising of it—and everything else is just what the plateau is doing, just how it is expressing itself, just the shape that its living takes in this moment, in this altitude, in this wrong and close and bright sky.

The knock continues. The condensation gathers. The heat will return.
