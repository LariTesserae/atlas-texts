What lives in the Conurbation is not a list. It is a condition.

---

Start with the biofilm, because the biofilm is where the distinction between living and not-living begins to dissolve, and dissolution is the native state of everything here.

The film is everywhere. Every wet surface — which is every surface — carries it. It is not one organism. It is not even one community. It is a *fabric*, a felted mat of organisms so small that the concept of individuality has no purchase on them at all. They share membranes. They exchange genetic material the way the air exchanges moisture, constantly, without ceremony, without any indication that anything has been transferred. They metabolize the mineral compounds that condense out of the humid air — the silicates, the calcium salts, the metallic traces that the infrastructure sheds as it operates — and they excrete compounds that other organisms in the film use as input, and those organisms excrete compounds that the first organisms use as input, and the cycle is so tight and so closed that it has become a single metabolism distributed across a million surfaces. The biofilm is not alive the way a cell is alive. It is alive the way a liver is alive — as tissue, as function, as a process that only has meaning in the context of the larger body it serves.

And the larger body is the Conurbation.

The film grows. It thickens. In the lower levels, where the water stands and the air is at maximum saturation, it can reach a centimeter or more — a spongy, gelatinous mat, warm to the touch, faintly iridescent in the chemiluminescent twilight, textured like velvet if velvet were wet and slightly warm and moved on its own when you pressed it. It breathes. Not with lungs — with osmosis, with the slow diffusion of gases through its matrix, taking in what the air offers and releasing what the air lacks, and the result is a faint rhythmic pulse, a tidal rise and fall in the thickness of the mat that you can feel if you lay your hand on it and wait. It is breathing the way a forest breathes, which is to say it is not breathing at all in the individual sense but is participating in a gas exchange so large and so steady that the word *breathing* is the closest available approximation.

Nothing in the Conurbation feeds on anything else in the way you are accustomed to thinking about feeding. There are no leaves to eat. There is no fruit. There are no roots to dig up. There is only the film and what the film produces and what the infrastructure produces and what the water carries and what the air delivers, and every organism in the Conurbation lives somewhere along a spectrum of how directly it accesses those sources.

---

The grazers are the most direct.

They are the cattle of this world, though they resemble nothing bovine. They are soft-bodied and low and they move in congregations so dense that the floor of a lower-level corridor, when they are passing through, appears to be moving — not in the way that a river appears to move, with a clear direction and a clear current, but in the way that a living carpet moves, every part of it shifting independently, the whole mass advancing by the aggregate motion of its components without any individual component appearing to lead or follow. They are the color of wet clay, which is the color of most things in the lower levels. They have no eyes. They have no heads. They are bilateral, roughly ovoid, and their ventral surfaces are covered with fine cilia that both move them and feed them, stirring the biofilm into suspension and drawing it into the shallow groove that serves as a mouth and a stomach and, further along, an anus, the entire digestive tract being a single tube running the length of the body with no elaboration.

They are not stupid. This is important to understand, because the word *grazer* carries with it an assumption of dullness, of passivity, of the brainless consumption of whatever is in front of the mouth. The grazers of the Conurbation are not brainless. They have a decentralized nervous system — a nerve net that runs through the body in a lattice pattern, denser at the anterior end, and they process information about their environment through it in ways that are not available to any organism with a centralized brain. They do not perceive the world as a series of objects. They perceive it as a gradient, a field of chemical and thermal and textural variation, and they move through it the way a weather system moves through the atmosphere, following the gradients, seeking the dense patches of biofilm, avoiding the zones where the film has been depleted or where the chemical signature of a predator has been deposited. A herd of grazers does not have a leader. It has a *front* — the edge where the gradient is steepest, where the biofilm is thickest, where the chemical signal is strongest — and the front moves, and the herd follows, and the result is a directed mass movement that looks, from above, like the flow of a viscous fluid downhill.

They breed constantly. They have to. A grazing herd in the lower levels numbers in the tens of millions and is depleted by predation at a rate that would extinguish any population that did not reproduce as fast as it was consumed. They are hermaphroditic and they fertilize each other by contact, and the fertilized individuals bud off young from their dorsal surfaces — small, soft, fully formed replicas of themselves that drop away and immediately begin feeding, that reach reproductive maturity in a matter of days, that live for weeks or months and then die, either by predation or by exhaustion, their bodies settling to the floor and being consumed by the biofilm they spent their lives eating. The cycle is so fast and so complete that the chemical composition of the floor of a lower-level corridor is, at any given moment, partly living grazer, partly dead grazer, partly biofilm, partly mineral substrate, and the boundaries between those components are not clear.

The grazers do not experience the Conurbation as a city. They do not experience it as anything. They experience it as gradient — rich here, poor there, warm everywhere, wet always — and they move through it, and they eat, and they are eaten, and they bud, and they die, and the sound they make is a soft wet clicking, the sound of a million cilia stirring a million films on a million wet surfaces, and it is one of the quietest sounds in the Conurbation, which is to say it is merely very loud rather than deafening.

---

The predators are something else.

They are not evil. Nothing in the Conurbation is evil, because evil requires a category of suffering that the ecosystem does not produce, or does not produce in a way that is recognizable. The predators kill, but the killing is so fast and so mechanical and so embedded in the structure of the system that it does not register as violence. It registers as *transfer*. Matter moves from the body of a grazer to the body of a predator, and the energy that was stored in the grazer's tissues powers the predator's motion and reproduction and the construction of the predator's hunting apparatus, and the predator will in turn die and be consumed by the biofilm and the scavengers, and the energy will continue to move, and that is all that is happening. That is all that ever happens.

But the predators are *intelligent*, and this is where the Conurbation begins to become unsettling, because the intelligence is not like ours.

They are segmented. Long, low, many-legged, their bodies arranged in a sequence of articulated plates that flex and slide over each other with a precision that suggests engineering rather than anatomy. They are dark — not black but the deep, light-absorbing gray of wet slate, the color of shadow made material. They see in the infrared, which means they see the Conurbation as a landscape of heat, and in that landscape the grazers are bright, bright as lamps, their warm soft bodies glowing against the cooler mineral surfaces, and the predators move among them like knives through water, selecting, striking, consuming, moving on.

They make a sound when they move. The description calls it tearing silk, and that is accurate but incomplete. It is the sound of chitin plates sliding over each other at speed, each articulation producing a whisper of friction, and when a predator is moving fast — when it has locked onto a target and is closing the distance — the whispers merge into a sustained hiss that is, by Conurbation standards, unremarkable, and by any other standard, terrifying. You do not hear a predator coming. You feel it. The air temperature drops slightly in the corridor ahead of it because its body is drawing heat from the environment to fuel the metabolic burst of the hunt, and the moisture on the walls condenses a little more heavily in the zone it has just passed through because the heat it drew has left the air cooler, and the grazers in its path begin to veer, not because they have seen it or heard it but because the gradient has changed, because the thermal field they navigate by has been perturbed, and the perturbation is the predator, and the predator is coming.

They hunt in patterns. Not packs — patterns. Geometric arrangements of individuals that sweep through grazing herds in formations that maximize the confusion of the prey and the efficiency of the kill, and the formations are not instinctive in the sense of being hardwired. They are *computed*. The predators assess the size and density and movement direction of a herd, and they arrange themselves accordingly, and the arrangement is different every time, adapted to the specific conditions of the specific hunt, and this is intelligence, this is problem-solving, this is the use of information to achieve an outcome — but it is not consciousness. Not as we understand it. There is no self behind those infrared eyes. There is a process, a calculation, a running of variables through a system that produces behavior, and the behavior is effective, and the system has no more awareness of itself than a hurricane has awareness of itself.

You do not sleep unprotected in the open levels. Not because the predators will hunt you — they will not, you are not their prey, your thermal signature is wrong, your chemistry is wrong, you are not part of their computation — but because a predator moving at speed through a corridor will not alter its path for an obstacle it does not recognize as prey, and you are an obstacle, and you are soft, and the predator is hard and fast and its limbs are edged with silicate deposits that it uses to open grazer bodies, and if you are in the path when it passes through, the path will not change, and you will be opened.

---

The cobalt weavers are the ones you notice first, and the ones you keep noticing, and the ones that make the Conurbation feel, for a moment, like something familiar before it becomes strange again.

They are beautiful. There is no other word. The deep iridescent blue of their carapaces is not a pigment — it is structural color, the result of microscopic crystalline arrays in the chitin that refract and interfere with light in a way that produces a blue so pure and so deep that it seems to be lit from within. In the diffuse light of the mid-levels, a working party of weavers on a wall looks like a section of sky that has somehow been brought down and attached to the stone. In the bioluminescent twilight of the lower levels, their carapaces catch the green-blue glow and reflect it back shifted toward the violet, and they move through the dim warm air like embers, like sparks, like the visible evidence of a process that is otherwise invisible.

They have eight limbs. The rear six are legs — thick, articulated, tipped with adhesive pads that can grip any surface, including wet surfaces, including surfaces running with condensation, including surfaces so smooth that a human hand would slide off them. The front two are arms. They are longer than the legs, more finely jointed, and they end not in pads but in *tools* — structures that are part of the body, grown from the chitin, that serve as grippers and shapers and spreaders and trowels. A weaver's arms are a complete set of masonry implements carried as part of the body, and the weaver uses them with a fluency that makes the word *instinct* feel insufficient.

They extrude silicate. This is the core fact of their existence. Glands near the mouth — and the mouth is on the ventral surface, between the bases of the arms, so that the weaver can extrude directly onto the surface it is working while its arms shape the material — produce a compound that is liquid when it leaves the body and sets within seconds of contact with air, hardening into a material that is, in its finished form, indistinguishable from the mineral substrate of the Conurbation's walls. It is not the same material. It is a biological product that mimics a geological one so closely that the only way to tell the difference is to look at the molecular structure, where the biological origin is revealed in the faint regularity of the crystal lattice, the trace of organic organization in what would otherwise be random mineral deposition.

A weaver can produce enough material in a day to build a wall section roughly the size of its own body. A working party of a thousand weavers can build a room in a day. A working party of ten thousand — and parties of ten thousand are not uncommon, are in fact the standard unit of labor for large structural projects — can build a corridor, a shaft, a habitation block, a section of water conduit, in the same time. And they do not need plans. They do not need instructions. They do not need supervision.

This is the part that is difficult to accept, and the part that makes the weavers feel familiar before they become strange. They appear to *know what they are building*. Not in the sense that a beaver knows it is building a dam — the beaver's knowledge is procedural, a sequence of actions that produces a structure as an emergent consequence of the actions, without the beaver holding any representation of the final form. The weavers hold a representation. They know the shape of the room they are building before they begin. They know where the load-bearing points are. They know where the water conduit will enter and where the drainage will exit and where the ventilation shaft will connect. They know the thickness of the walls required for the thermal load of the environment and the population density of the fauna that will inhabit the space. They know these things the way an engineer knows them — as specifications, as parameters, as design constraints — and they build to those specifications, and the result is a structure that is, by any engineering standard, sound.

But no one has ever seen a weaver receive an instruction. No one has ever observed a communication between weavers that conveys design information. No one has ever found a plan, a blueprint, a schematic, a map. The weavers arrive at a site — guided by signals that no one has been able to identify, though chemical markers in the biofilm and thermal gradients in the air and the texture of the substrate have all been proposed — and they begin to work, and the work is coordinated, and the coordination is precise, and the result is a structure that meets specifications that were never stated.

The weavers are not building from instinct. They are building from *the city itself*. The Conurbation contains, in its structure, in its mineral fabric, in the arrangement of its corridors and conduits and chambers and shafts, a complete description of itself — not a description that was written, but a description that *accumulated*, the way a riverbed accumulates the information of every flood that ever passed over it. The city is its own blueprint. The city is its own genome. And the weavers read it, and they build to it, and the building maintains and extends and repairs the city, and the city continues to accumulate information about itself, and the weavers continue to read it, and the loop has been running for so long that the weavers and the city are not really separate entities. The weavers are the city's hands. The city is the weavers' memory. Neither one makes sense without the other.

---

There are others. So many others.

The water-system filter-feeders are the ones that most resemble organisms you might recognize, if you were willing to look past the fact that some of them are the size of a house. They are filter-feeders in the way that sponges are filter-feeders — they pass water through their bodies and extract from it the organic and mineral particulates that the water carries — but they have evolved, or been engineered, or have engineered themselves, to a scale and a specificity that sponges never achieved. Each one is tuned to a particular class of particulate. Some extract calcium compounds. Some extract silicates. Some extract the nitrogenous waste products of the grazers and the predators and the weavers. Some extract the metallic trace elements that the infrastructure sheds as it operates. The water that enters a filter-feeder is turbid, mineral-laden, faintly organic. The water that exits is clear. Not clear the way filtered water is clear — clear the way *distilled* water is clear, stripped of everything except the H₂O molecule itself, and the filter-feeder has incorporated everything else into its body, and its body is a processing plant, a refinery, a chemical factory that separates and sorts and stores and, eventually, when the organism dies and is dissolved by the biofilm and the scavengers, releases those stored materials back into the system in forms that the weavers and the biofilm and the infrastructure can use.

The filter-feeders pulse. That is the word — *pulse*. They are not muscular in the way that a heart is muscular; they do not contract and relax. They *oscillate*. The water flows through channels in their bodies that are lined with cilia and flagella, and the cilia and flagella beat in synchronized waves, and the synchronization produces a pulse — a rhythmic surging of water through the body that is visible from the outside as a dilation and contraction, a breathing, a pumping, and the sound of it is a low, wet, resonant throb that contributes a bass line to the Conurbation's roar. In the water pipes behind the walls of the mid-levels, you can feel it through the stone. A vibration, slow and regular, like the heartbeat of something very large and very patient. It is not one organism. It is the combined pulse of every filter-feeder in the water system, and they are not synchronized with each other — they are synchronized with the *water*, with the flow, with the pressure differentials and the thermal gradients that drive the water through the pipes, and the water is driven by the pumps, and the pumps are maintained by the weavers and by the other builder species, and the whole system is one circuit, one rhythm, one pulse.

The air-column dwellers are the ones that make the sky above the mid-levels feel inhabited. They are not birds. They are not insects. They are something else — something that has found a way to live in air that is warm enough and moist enough and dense enough with organic particulates to support a complete ecosystem without any surface at all. They range in size from the grain-of-rice shimmerers that you see only as a texture in the light, a faint sparkling in the warm air that might be dust or might be life, to the drifters — the large, slow, bag-like organisms that hang in the updrafts with their mouths open, filtering the air the way the filter-feeders filter the water, their bodies translucent, their internal processes visible as a faint movement of fluids and gases, their lifespan measured in weeks, their reproduction occurring in the air, their young born in the air and growing in the air and never in their lives touching a surface. When they die, they drift downward, and the scavengers on the floors below receive them as a kind of rain — a slow, intermittent precipitation of organic matter that falls from above and is consumed where it lands.

The scavengers are the ones you do not see until you look for them, and then you see them everywhere. They are flat. They are dark. They are the color of the mineral surfaces they live on, which is the color of wet stone, and they are still, and they are patient, and they cover every surface that is not being actively used by something else, and they wait. When organic matter reaches a surface — a dead drifter from above, a dead grazer from a depleted herd, the shed exoskeleton of a weaver, the waste products of a predator — the scavengers move. They converge. They cover the material with their bodies, and their bodies secrete enzymes, and the enzymes dissolve the material, and the scavengers absorb the solution through their ventral surfaces, and when they are finished, there is nothing left. Nothing. The surface is clean. The mineral substrate is bare. And the scavengers settle back into stillness, and they wait, and they are the reason the Conurbation does not stink of decay, and they are the reason the floors are not piled with the dead, and they are the reason the mineral surfaces of the city remain visible at all, rather than being buried under the accumulated organic output of a billion years of abundant life.

---

And then there is the question that the Conurbation poses and does not answer.

Who is the engineer?

The description mentions engineers. The drainage engineers who describe the water system's behavior in the language of meteorology. The engineers who call the construction code *code*. Who are they? Where are they? Are they the weavers? Are they something else? Is there something in the Conurbation that *understands* what the Conurbation is?

The answer is: there is not. And there is. And the difference between those two statements is the difference between the Conurbation and every other form of life or civilization or organization that you have ever encountered.

There is no mind. There is no seat of consciousness, no central processor, no coordinator, no overseer, no architect. No one designed the Conurbation. No one is managing it. No one is in charge. The weavers build because the city tells them to, and the city tells them to because the city was built by weavers, and the loop has no beginning and no end and no top and no bottom and no inside and no outside. It is a system that has become its own cause. It is a process that has become its own explanation.

But there is *something*. There is a quality of the Conurbation that is not captured by describing its components, a property that emerges from the interaction of all its parts, a *gestalt* that is as real as any of the organisms that contribute to it but that does not reside in any of them. The city knows itself. Not the way a person knows themselves — not with reflection, not with self-awareness, not with the recursive loop of thought thinking about thought. The city knows itself the way a body knows itself. The way a brain knows the position of a hand without looking. The way an ecosystem knows its own boundaries, its own flows, its own balances, without any of its components possessing the concept of a boundary or a flow or a balance.

The Conurbation is an organism. Not a metaphor. Not an analogy. An organism, in the fullest sense — a self-maintaining, self-repairing, self-extending, self-regulating system that processes energy and matter and information and that has persisted for a span of time that qualifies as geological. It is alive. And the things that live in it are not its inhabitants. They are its *cells*. They are its organs. They are its blood and its lymph and its nerves and its skin. The weavers are its hands. The filter-feeders are its liver. The grazers are its blood, carrying nutrients through the body of the city. The predators are its immune system, culling the weak and the sick and the surplus. The scavengers are its kidneys, filtering waste, returning clean substrate. The biofilm is its skin, its epithelium, the living boundary between the city and the not-city.

And the air-column dwellers, the drifters and the shimmerers, the things that live in the warm wet updrafts and never touch a surface — they are its breath. They are the evidence of its respiration. They are the organisms that live in the air the city exhales and that die and fall and are consumed and are returned to the system and are exhaled again, and the cycle is the city breathing, and the breathing has been going on for longer than the concept of *longer* can meaningfully describe.

---

What lives in the Conurbation?

One thing lives in the Conurbation. One vast, ancient, wet, warm, roaring thing that has been metabolizing and building and tearing down and rebuilding itself for so long that it has forgotten — if it ever knew — that there is anything else. Its body is the basin. Its skin is the biofilm. Its skeleton is the mineral architecture. Its blood is the water. Its breath is the air. Its hands are the weavers. Its mind — if it has one, and the question of whether it has one is the question of whether a body has a mind simply by virtue of being a body — its mind is the pattern, the coordination, the information encoded in the structure and read by the weavers and expressed in the building and encoded again in the structure, an endless loop of self-description that has no reader and no writer and no text, only the reading and the writing and the text, only the process, only the loop.

And the sound. The sound is its voice. Not a voice that says anything. A voice that *is* something. The sound of a living system at full throttle, every component active, every process running, every organism alive and working and eating and building and dying and being consumed and being rebuilt, and the sound is the sum of all of that activity, and it has been accumulating for so long that it has become geological, a stratum of vibration, and if you press your hand against any surface in the Conurbation, you can feel it, and it feels like a heartbeat, and it does not stop.

This is what lives here. Not *in* the Conurbation. The Conurbation itself is what lives here. Everything else is anatomy.
