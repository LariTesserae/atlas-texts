# What Lives on the Llano

---

The crawlers do not think of themselves as crawlers. They do not think of themselves. But if the word *think* can be stretched—and on the Llano, everything stretches, everything wears smooth, everything accommodates the slow pressure of duration—then they think in a way that has no center. They are bodies in procession. They are a going-on.

There are perhaps eleven thousand of them at any given time. The number does not change in any way that matters. When one dies—a word that here means gradually becoming indistinguishable from the ground, over weeks, with no single moment that could be called an ending—another has already been forming from the accumulation. They are not born. They are not hatched. They *coalesce*. The conditions are right—they are always right, and have been right for longer than the word *right* has any claim to meaning—and so the crawlers happen, the way condensation happens on the dimpled stone at night. They emerge from the multitude. Not from any parent, but from the aggregate. A sufficient density of the smaller lives, a sufficient warmth, a sufficient stillness, and the segments begin to differentiate, the flat body begins to move with something that is not quite purpose but is certainly direction.

A crawler lives for—there is no way to know. They do not mark time. They do not age in any way that can be observed from outside. They graze, they move, they leave trails that fade, and eventually they slow and settle and the boundary between crawler and stone softens and the multitude that made them reclaims them body by body by body. Perhaps it takes a year. Perhaps it takes a century. The Llano does not measure.

Their segmented bodies are not crustacean. The resemblance is real but the lineage is not. The segments are not articulated joints but *folds*—places where the flat body has learned to flex because the stone beneath it is dimpled, and a rigid body would catch and break. The crawlers have the shape of patience. They are pale gray because the lichen they eat is pale, because the stone is pale, because on the Llano, color is a kind of energy, and energy is hoarded with a miserliness that would seem pathological if it were not so clearly successful. A bright crawler would be a wasteful crawler. A wasteful crawler would not be a crawler for long.

They graze by pressing their leading edge—the word *mouth* is too specific, too architectural—against the lichen-mats and *absorbing*. There is no tearing. No chewing. The lichen does not resist. It has no mechanism for resistance, and even if it did, the crawler's feeding is so slow that resistance would have nothing to push against. It is like being worn away by a breeze that never stops. The lichen recedes from the crawler's edge the way a shoreline recedes from a tide so gradual it cannot be perceived in any single moment.

This is how the trails are made. Not by pressure, not by the weight of the crawler's body on the stone, but by the absence of lichen where the crawler has passed. The trails are lighter than the surrounding surface because the pale stone shows through where the pale lichen has been thinned. The trails take weeks to fade because the lichen returns to its former density the way a memory returns—not by growing, but by *being there again*, by having never quite left.

---

The crawlers do not gather. They do not disperse. They are simply distributed across the Llano in a pattern that might be random or might be the result of ten thousand influences too subtle and too slow to be called decisions. Sometimes two crawlers will pass near each other, their edges almost touching for a day or a week, and nothing will happen. They will continue. The near-touching was not a meeting. It was not an avoidance. It was geography.

But sometimes—rarely, in the terms of any single observer's lifetime, but continuously, in the terms of the Llano—something else occurs. Two crawlers will overlap. Their edges will press together and their segments will align and for a time they will move as one body, two grazers sharing the same trail, and the lichen beneath them will thin in a wider path, and the multitude beneath them will shift and reorganize around the new shape of their combined warmth, and then, slowly, without announcement, they will separate again. The separation is not a breaking. There was never a joining. There was simply a time when the boundary between them was not important enough to maintain.

Visitors—if there were visitors—might call this mating. It is not. Nothing is exchanged. Nothing is produced. But something *happens* in the overlap, something that the Llano requires, something that has been happening since before the structures were built and will continue to happen after the structures have worn to whispers. The crawlers overlap, and the multitude around them registers the event—shifts, adjusts, reorganizes—and the system turns, and life continues.

What the event *is* cannot be said in any language that belongs to a faster world. It is communication at the speed of geology. It is a sentence spoken between two bodies that takes a month to say and a month to hear and means nothing that can be summarized. It means: *I am here, and you are here, and here is here, and here continues.*

---

The multitude.

You cannot describe them individually because there is no individually. There are types, perhaps—shapes that recur, metabolisms that cluster into recognizable patterns—but the boundaries between types are as worn and indistinct as the boundaries between stone and soil and sand. On the Llano, taxonomy is a form of erosion. It smooths the truth into something handleable, and the truth here is not handleable. The truth here is dense and warm and hums.

There are the grain-shaped ones. Rice-grain, sand-grain, smaller than grain. They are the substrate of the substrate. They occupy the pores in the half-rock, the dimples in the stone, the spaces between the lichen-mats and the stone beneath. They are so numerous that calling them *numerous* is like calling the ocean *wet*—technically accurate, but missing the point entirely. They are not *in* the stone. They *are* the stone, or at least they are the stone's living fraction, and the stone is their architecture, and the boundary between their bodies and their habitat is a boundary that exists only in the minds of those who need boundaries to think.

What do they do? They metabolize. They persist. They take the warmth and the moisture and the organic particulate and they convert it into more of themselves, and they take each other and convert that too, and they take the byproducts of their own metabolisms—which, in a place where almost nothing leaves, are simply more material—and they convert those as well. They are a furnace. They are a compost. They are a closed system that has been closing for so long it has forgotten there was ever an outside to close against.

They do not compete. Competition requires scarcity, and scarcity requires a sense of *enough* versus *not enough*, and on the Llano, *enough* was determined so long ago that the determination itself has become a physical law. There is exactly as much life as the warmth and moisture can sustain. No more. No less. The system does not overshoot. It does not crash. It does not bloom and die. It simply continues, at a density that would seem impossible if you did not stand on the warm pale ground and feel the hum of it rising through your feet.

There are the filamentous ones. They extend through the pores in the stone like roots, like mycelia, like something that is neither but serves the same function—connection, transport, the slow sharing of resources across distances that are immense at their scale and invisible at yours. The filamentous ones link the multitude into a network that has no center and no edge, that responds to the movement of crawlers by shifting the flow of warmth and nutrients, that registers the passage of a cloud shadow the way a body registers a change in temperature—automatically, without deliberation, as a single system.

The network is not a brain. It is not a mind. But it *processes*. Information moves through it—the information of warmth, of moisture, of organic density, of the slow gravity of a crawler passing overhead—and the system adjusts. The adjustments are not decisions. They are *responses*, the way water responds to a stone dropped into it by moving aside and then moving back. The water does not decide. The water *is the deciding*. The response and the thing that responds are the same.

The Llano, in this sense, is a single organism. But the word *organism* implies a boundary, a skin, a distinction between inside and outside. The Llano has no skin. It shades into the air above, which carries its moisture and its particulate, and into the stone below, which carries its warmth, and into the structures, which carry its memory of something that was once different. It is a body that is also a place. It is a place that is also a process. It has been both for so long that the distinction has worn away like everything else.

---

The things that might be predators.

There is a creature—the word *creature* is too separate, too individual, but there is a *presence*—that moves through the multitude in a way the others do not. It is not larger. It is not faster. But it has a *direction* that is not the direction of grazing or drifting or metabolizing. It moves toward. It finds. It consumes.

It is the size of a small seed. Pale, like everything here, but with a slight translucence that distinguishes it from the opaque grain-shaped ones. It extends a process—a filament, a tendril, something between the two—into the bodies of the grain-shaped ones and *draws from them*. Not quickly. Not violently. The consumed grain-creature does not die in any observable way. It simply diminishes, its metabolism slowing, its contribution to the network thinning, until eventually it is reclaimed by the processes around it—broken down, redistributed, its materials incorporated into other bodies, other metabolisms.

The consuming presence does not seem to take pleasure in this. It does not seem to take anything. It *proceeds*. It is a part of the system the way erosion is a part of a mountain—not an enemy, not a disease, but a process that ensures the mountain does not grow forever, that the materials of the world are circulated, that stasis does not set in.

There is something almost managerial about the consuming presence. It moves through the dense clusters of the multitude and it thins them, not to nothing, but to the density that the warmth and moisture can sustain. It prevents the kind of accumulation that would choke the pores in the stone, that would smother the lichen-mats, that would collapse the network into a solid mass of living material that could no longer breathe. It is the editor of the Llano. It is the hand that keeps the text from becoming illegible with overwritten words.

The crawlers are not consumed by it, or not often. Their bodies are too large, their segments too differentiated, their own internal multitude too dense for the consuming presence to penetrate easily. But sometimes a crawler that is already slowing, already settling, already beginning the long process of becoming stone—sometimes that crawler will be found by the consuming presence, and the consuming presence will accelerate the process, will draw from the crawler what remains of its accumulated warmth and organic material, and the transition from crawler to ground will take weeks instead of months, and the multitude around the crawler will flare briefly with the sudden influx, and then settle, and continue.

This is not death. On the Llano, there is no death in the way faster worlds understand it—no ending, no cessation, no moment when something that was alive becomes something that is not. There is only the gradual redistribution of the materials of life, the slow circulation from body to body to body, the wearing down of one shape and the coalescing of another. The crawlers do not die. They *rejoin*. The grain-creatures do not die. They *become*. The consuming presence does not kill. It *recirculates*.

---

The things that metabolize warmth.

This is speculation. But the mathematics insist.

The Llano receives almost no input from the sun. The lichen-mats photosynthesize—*if* that is the right word—but they are so sparse, so pale, so thin, that the energy they capture is negligible. The crawlers eat the lichen, and the multitude eats the crawlers and each other and the organic particulate that settles from the air, but none of this accounts for the density of life. None of it explains the hum. Something else is feeding the system. Something else is keeping ten billion tiny metabolisms at their patient pace.

The warmth from below is geothermal—not volcanic, not dramatic, but the slow heat of a planet that has not yet finished cooling. It rises through the stone evenly, reliably, without variation that any living thing could perceive. It has been rising for longer than the Llano has been flat. It will continue to rise after the Llano has become something even flatter, even more worn, even more patient.

And somewhere in the multitude—perhaps in the filamentous network, perhaps in the deep-dwelling grain-creatures that occupy the lowest pores of the half-rock—there is a pathway that converts this warmth into the chemical energy of life. Not photosynthesis. Not chemosynthesis as we understand it. Something else. Something that learned to eat temperature itself, to metabolize the differential between the warm stone and the slightly cooler air, to extract the energy of a gradient so slight it would be invisible to any instrument that demanded drama.

These would be the deepest dwellers. The ones who never see even the dimming of the light through a cloud shadow. The ones who have lived in the warm dark pores of the ancient stone for so long that they have become a different kind of life—not autotrophs, not heterotrophs, but *thermotrophs*, eaters of the slow heat, the background radiation of a world that is still, after all this time, cooling.

They would be the foundation. The base of the only pyramid the Llano has. Everything above them—the filamentous network, the grain-creatures, the crawlers, the consuming presence, the lichen-mats, the occasional filamentous thing that rises a centimeter in the dimples where water lingers—all of it built on the patience of things that eat warmth and excrete the chemical surplus of a planet's long forgetting.

---

The lichen-mats are not lichen.

They were lichen, once. Or something like lichen—symbiotic composites of fungus and photobiont, working together to mine the stone and the light. But the Llano has been wearing them down for the same inconceivable duration it has been wearing down everything else, and what remains is a composite that has been simplified by time. The fungus and the photobiont have merged. The partnership has become a single organism that no longer remembers there were ever two. It is not a lichen. It is what a lichen becomes when it has been a lichen for long enough to forget what it was before.

The mats do not grow. They *are*. They maintain their density against the grazing of the crawlers the way the stone maintains its dimples against the rain—by being the shape they are, by having worn into a configuration that has no reason to change. The crawlers thin them, and they thicken back. Not by growing, exactly, but by *being more*, by having their presence extend to the limit of their materials and their warmth and their light, a limit that has been reached so many times that it has become a constant.

They are ash and rust and pale yellow because those are the colors that work here. Bright colors require energy. Dark colors absorb too much heat and disrupt the gradient the thermotrophs depend on. The mats have learned—*learned* is the wrong word, but the right word would take a century to say—the precise pallor that allows them to photosynthesize without stealing the warmth from the pores below. They are the color of compromise. They are the color of a system that has been balancing itself for so long the balance has become a physical law.

---

There are things in the air above the Llano.

Not birds. Not insects. Not anything that flies in the way flying is understood in places with verticality and urgency. But at the hour when the light begins to dim and the warmth begins to shift and the multitude adjusts in its near-silent hum, there are presences in the still air—*presences* because calling them *creatures* would imply a solidity they do not possess.

They are the size of dust motes. Perhaps they are dust motes, infused with the same slow life that fills the stone below. Perhaps they are spores, or eggs, or the Llano's equivalent of both. Perhaps they are the waste product of some process in the multitude, so thin and light that the barely-moving air carries them up. They drift. They do not navigate. They are present in the warm air the way the hum is present in the sound—everywhere, nowhere, a constituent of the atmosphere itself.

They settle eventually. Everything settles. The Llano is a place where things come to rest, where the long arc of entropy has been shortened to a horizon that rings you like the rim of a bowl. The dust-presences settle on the lichen-mats, on the dimpled stone, on the bodies of crawlers, and they are absorbed. Not eaten. Not consumed. *Absorbed*. The boundary between the settling thing and the settled-upon thing is thin enough that the transition requires no process, no decision. The dust becomes the stone. The stone becomes the dust. The system turns.

---

The memory of the structures.

The structures are not alive. But the life of the Llano does not make a hard distinction between alive and not-alive, between the bodies that metabolize and the bodies that do not. The structures are habitat. The structures are surface area. The structures are dimples at a larger scale—places where moisture lingers, where warmth pools, where the filamentous network can extend and the grain-creatures can cluster and the crawlers can graze on the lichen-mats that colonize the worn surfaces.

But there is something else. Something the Llano would not call memory, because the Llano does not call anything anything, but which functions as memory functions in faster worlds—as a record of what was, that influences what is.

The structures are regular. Their remnants, even worn to smooth syllables of stone, retain the ghost of a pattern. And the life of the Llano, in and around the structures, arranges itself in response to that pattern. The filamentous network extends more densely along the bases of the partial walls. The grain-creatures cluster in the grooves that might once have been writing. The crawlers graze in paths that parallel the structures' alignments, as if the shape of what was built still shapes the flow of warmth and moisture and organic particulate across the stone.

The Llano does not remember who built the structures. But it remembers *that they were built*. It remembers the shape of the intervention, the way a river remembers the shape of a dam long after the dam has gone—the way the water still slows and pools in the place where the obstacle was, the way the sediment still settles in patterns that echo the vanished structure. The structures have been worn smooth, but the life around them has worn into their shape, and the shape persists in the living system even as it disappears from the stone.

This is the Llano's memory. Not a recording. Not an archive. A *persistence of pattern*. The shapes of the structures live in the distribution of the multitude, in the paths of the crawlers, in the extensions of the filamentous network. They are inherited not through genes but through geometry. They are passed on not through generations but through the continuous process of the system adjusting itself to the shape of what is, a shape that still includes the ghost of what was.

The builders, whoever they were, are still here. Not as remains. Not as fossils. As *arrangements*. As patterns in the hum.

---

What lives on the Llano is not a collection of species. It is not an ecosystem. It is not even a single organism in the way we understand organisms—as bounded things with insides and outsides, births and deaths.

What lives on the Llano is a *process*.

A process that has been going on for so long that it has become a place. A process that metabolizes warmth and moisture and the occasional thin rain and the slow rain of organic particulate and the ghost-patterns of ancient structures and the barely-perceptible movement of cloud shadows across dimpled stone. A process that expresses itself as crawlers and grain-creatures and filamentous networks and consuming presences and lichen-mats and dust-motes and thermotrophs in the deep pores and a hum that sits at the edge of perception.

A process that does not change in any way that can be observed from the outside, but which is not static—*not static*, never static, vibrating with ten billion tiny metabolisms, shifting with every cloud shadow, adjusting to every crawler's trail, turning and turning and turning like a wheel that has been turning for so long it has forgotten it is a wheel and has become the horizon itself.

What lives on the Llano is the Llano.

And the Llano continues.
