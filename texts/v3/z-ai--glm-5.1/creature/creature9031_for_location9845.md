# The People of the Galdress

They do not walk quickly. You notice this immediately — the pace, the deliberate placement of each foot, the slight pause before committing weight that has become so ingrained it no longer reads as hesitation but as rhythm, as the local cadence of movement. Children learn it before they learn to read. Step, feel, shift. The ground is warm. The ground is hot. The ground gives way sometimes, and the water beneath is not always the temperature you'd like it to be. So you move like the grazers move: slowly, with attention, with your body close to the surface and your mind on what lies beneath.

There are not many of them. The settlement holds perhaps two hundred people at its fullest, and it is rarely at its fullest. Workers come on contract — six months, a year, two years — and then leave, replaced by others who arrive with the same squinting confusion that everyone brings to the plateau for the first few days. The light is too bright. The air is too wet. The silence feels wrong until you realize it's not silence at all but a texture of sound so fine your ears haven't learned to parse it yet. The newcomers blink and drip and test the ground uncertainly. Within a week they walk like everyone else.

But some stay. Not many, but some. And those who stay — who have stayed through multiple contracts, who have let the plateau work its way into their habits and their skin and their sense of time — those people are different. They are not different in any way you could point to on first meeting. They look like people anywhere: worn, capable, particular in their preferences. But spend time with them and you begin to notice the way they listen to the ground, the way they glance at steam columns with an expression that is not concern but *reading* — checking, the way you might check a clock, the way a farmer checks the sky. They have a relationship with this place that goes beyond employment.

---

Maren has been here eleven years. She runs the settling ponds — oversees the gravity-fed sequence that clarifies the mineral brine through a series of evaporating pools, adjusting flow rates and retention times based on what the water is doing today, which is never quite what it was doing yesterday. She is fifty, perhaps, or forty-five, or fifty-five — the light here ages skin in a specific way that makes age hard to read, a combination of high-altitude sun and constant humidity that lines the face while keeping it somehow supple, like leather that has been oiled by the very air. Her hands are stained. Not permanently, she'll tell you — the mineral colors wash off — but she washes them less often than she might, and so her fingertips carry traces of iron-red, copper-green, the pale white of calcium deposits that flake off like tiny sequins when she rubs her thumb and forefinger together.

She came for the money. Everyone comes for the money. The Galdress pays well because the work is unpleasant and the location is remote and the contracts are long enough to make people think carefully before committing. She thought she'd stay two years. She remembers the moment she decided to stay longer — not a dramatic moment, not a revelation, just a morning when she woke before dawn and stepped outside and the fog was layered in its stratified way across the plateau and the steam columns were visible only as warmth in the cold air, as columns of slightly-different-nothing, and she stood in the wet dark and felt the ground warm beneath her bare feet and thought: *I understand this place*. Not that she understood it completely. But she understood the shape of not-understanding it. She understood what kind of not-knowing it was, and she found she could live inside that kind of not-knowing, could be comfortable in it, the way you can be comfortable in a language you don't speak fluently but can navigate well enough to get where you're going.

She talks about the ponds the way a vintner talks about soil. The chemistry varies with depth, with temperature, with the mineral load the deep vents are delivering this week, this month. She can taste the difference in the water — not that she drinks it untreated, but she touches it, brings her fingers to her lips, reads the brine the way her grandmother read dough. Too much boron today. The lithium's running hot. There's a new vent opening somewhere to the east; she can feel it in the alkalinity, in the way the copper compounds precipitate differently on the pool edges, leaving a slightly different shade of blue-green than yesterday. She has learned the plateau's moods, which are not moods at all but the slow expression of geological processes operating on timescales that make human consciousness feel brief and flickering, and she has learned to read them the way a sailor reads weather — not as something to control but as something to move within.

She does not romanticize the place. She is clear about that. The work is repetitive. The heat is oppressive. The wetness gets into everything — clothing never fully dries, books warp, metal corrodes faster than it should. She has lost two laptops to condensation. She misses trees. She misses the sound of wind in leaves, which is a specific sound, she says, nothing like the sound of wind across mineral plain, which is no sound at all or is so slight a sound it reads as silence. But she stays because the work is interesting in a way that is difficult to explain to people who have never done work that requires this kind of attention. The ponds need her. Not in a sentimental way — in a practical way. The processes are too variable for full automation. The sensors scale over with mineral deposits; the valves clog; the settling rates depend on variables that no algorithm has yet learned to predict with the accuracy that Maren achieves by walking the pond edges every morning and evening and *looking*. She is part of the system. She is the part that sees.

---

Dael is younger, thirty-two, and has been here four years. They work the deep wells — the boreholes that tap into the geothermal reservoir far below the plateau's surface, where the water is hottest and most concentrated. It is the most dangerous job in the settlement, and Dael knows this, and does not talk about it with the specificity that outsiders might expect. They say the work is careful. They say you learn the sounds. A well that is about to surge makes a sound — a low harmonic in the casing, a vibration that travels up through the stone and into your feet before your ears register it. Dael has felt two surges. Both times they were already moving by the time the blowout happened, already retreating to the safe distance that the well-operators have marked with low stone cairns, mineral-stained, easy to see in the hard light. The surges are not dramatic in the way that oil well fires are dramatic — no explosion, no flame. Just a column of superheated water and steam bursting upward with force enough to kill, and then settling back to its normal output over the next several hours while the deep pressure equalizes. The water that sprays out during a surge leaves chemical burns. Dael has a patch of skin on their left forearm that is smoother than the surrounding skin, slightly shiny, where a splash of surge-water landed and they didn't rinse it fast enough.

They came here because they were bored. They say this plainly, without embarrassment. They grew up in a city, a temperate coastal city, everything easy and green and available, and they found that ease oppressive in a way that their friends could not understand. They wanted difficulty of a specific kind — not the difficulty of scarcity or violence or social complexity but the difficulty of *paying attention*. Of existing in a place where inattention has immediate, physical consequences. On the Galdress, if you stop paying attention, you step through a mineral crust into water hot enough to cook flesh. If you stop paying attention, you walk into a steam column. If you stop paying attention, the ground changes beneath you and you fall into a vent that opened last week and wasn't there before. Dael finds this clarifying. They find it focusing in a way that no meditation practice or productivity system ever achieved. Here, in the bright wet silence, they are always present. They have no choice.

They like the hunters. The dark ceramic things that emerge at dusk, patient and quick. Dael has watched them for hours — sitting on a rock at the plateau's edge, motionless, letting the hunters forget they were there, or perhaps simply not caring about them because they were too large to prey upon and too still to trigger a hunting response. The hunters do not have eyes, Dael is fairly sure. They hunt by vibration, by displacement, and Dael wonders sometimes what they make of the settlement — all its vibrations, its low-frequency throb, its dripping and ticking and the footstep-patterns of humans moving along the safe paths. Do the hunters perceive the town as an organism? As a landscape? As weather?

Dael has named some of them. Not out of sentimentality, they say, but for identification — the one with the chipped posterior plate who hunts the third pool from the eastern vent, the one with the uneven leg-span who moves in a slight spiral. They know these are not meaningful distinctions to the hunters themselves. They know the naming says more about Dael than about anything else. But the names have stuck, and when Dael comes in from the well-fields at dusk and sees the chipped one standing motionless at the pool's edge, they nod. A greeting between two things that have learned to be still.

---

Old Sef is the one who has been here longest. Twenty-three years. He is not old — sixty-one, which is not so old — but they call him Old Sef because he arrived before everyone else, before the current iteration of the settlement, before some of the buildings, before the processing infrastructure was anything more than a few settling tanks and a steam line that leaked more than it delivered. He came as a surveyor, one of the first to map the plateau's thermal structure, and he stayed because — he has given many reasons over the years, and none of them satisfy the people who ask, and he has stopped trying to explain.

He lives in a small structure at the settlement's edge, closer to the vents than is advisable, closer than the safety guidelines recommend. His walls are thick with mineral deposition — every season adds another millimeter of crystalline skin, calcium and iron and silica building up in layered record, so that the interior of his home grows slightly smaller each year. He has not cleaned it. He says he is curious how small it will get before it becomes unlivable. He says this with a straight face and it is never clear whether he is joking.

Sef reads the plateau the way a scholar reads a manuscript — closely, with attention to what is absent as well as what is present. He keeps records. Not digital records, which fail in the humidity, but handwritten ones, in waterproof ink on plastic sheets, bound in loose-leaf collections that he stores in sealed cases. He records the vent temperatures every day. He records the mineral composition of the primary pools every week. He records where the grazers are concentrated, how many hunters he observes, whether the flyers are riding the updrafts from the east or the west. He has done this for twenty-three years and the records fill three cases now, and he admits that no one reads them, that they have no practical application, that the extraction company does not care about the behavioral patterns of the grazer populations or the slow migration of the eastern vents. But he continues because the plateau is *speaking*, is always speaking, in its wet whispering mineral voice, and someone should be listening, and it might as well be him.

He has a theory, which he offers rarely and only when asked, that the plateau is not a single system but a conversation — between the depth and the surface, between the water and the stone, between the heat below and the weather above. He thinks the settlement has entered this conversation, has become another voice in it, and that the plateau is adjusting, responding, incorporating the human presence into its processes the way it incorporates everything: slowly, chemically, without urgency. He points to the mineral deposits on the buildings. He points to the way the condensation patterns have shifted around the settlement's heat output. He points to the fact that the grazer populations are denser near the settling ponds, where the overflow water creates bacterial mats richer than the natural ones. *We are part of it now*, he says. *We are geology. We are a slow process that walks around and thinks it's separate.*

He does not expect anyone to agree with him. He does not need them to.

---

There are others. There is Kit, who runs the generator station and has a collection of dead flyers she has found over the years, their mineral struts and membrane wings preserved by the dry heat near the steam vents, arranged on shelves in her quarters in a way that visitors sometimes find unsettling. There is Pek, who maintains the ceramic pipelines and sings while he works — old songs, folk songs from a country the other workers have never visited, his voice carrying across the mineral plain in the still air with a clarity that makes the sound seem physically present, seem like something you could almost touch. There are the rotating crew members, the contract workers, the ones who come for six months and leave with squinting eyes and mineral-stained boots and a new relationship with silence that they will carry back to whatever louder place they came from.

And there are the children. Not many — this is not a place that attracts families — but a few, born to parents who stayed past their contracts and found partners and made decisions that seemed reasonable at the time and perhaps still do. The children of the Galdress are distinctive. They walk the plateau's paths with an ease that makes their parents nervous — an ease born of familiarity, of having learned the ground before they learned to fear it. They catch grazers in their contracted disc form and hold them in their palms, feeling the faint warmth, the slow pulse of something simple and alive. They watch the hunters from a distance that is perhaps too close, with an interest that is perhaps too casual. They do not find the silence strange because they have never known it to be otherwise.

The children are the ones who notice things first. A new vent opening. A change in the fog's layering. A shift in the mineral colors along the settlement's walls. They notice because they are close to the ground, because they have not yet learned to filter out the plateau's constant quiet communication, because they are still young enough to hear what the place is saying. Their parents try to teach them caution. The plateau teaches them attention. These are not the same thing, and the difference matters.

---

At night the settlement glows. Not with artificial light, though there is some — the generator station's indicator lamps, the low orange of safety markers at the vent perimeters — but with the plateau's own luminescence. Certain bacterial mats produce faint light when the temperature differential is right, a soft blue-white bioluminescence that outlines the pool surfaces in the dark. The mineral deposits catch and hold the last of the sun long after it has set, releasing it slowly in phosphorescent wisps that trace the building edges and the rock formations. The steam columns, catching any available light, become visible as pillars of faint brightness rising into the dark.

In this light the settlement's people move like ghosts. They are used to the dark. They have learned to navigate by the glow of the pools, by the mineral outlines of structures, by the sound of their own footsteps on wet stone. They move between buildings on paths they know by heart, and if they pass each other in the dark they nod or speak softly or simply continue, each wrapped in their own business, each part of the settlement's quiet rhythm.

The plateau breathes around them. Steam rises. Water condenses. The grazers move through their slow feeding. The hunters wait in the shadows. The flyers have settled onto warm rock surfaces for the night, their wings folded, their bodies nearly invisible against the mineral crust. Everything is wet. Everything is warm. Everything is slowly, quietly, chemically alive.

And the people who live here — who have chosen this, or who have been chosen by it, or who simply failed to leave when leaving was still easy — they live within this aliveness the way they live within the humidity: thoroughly, inevitably, with the knowledge that they will carry the Galdress in their skin and lungs and habits long after they have departed, if they ever depart. The plateau marks everyone who stays. The minerals build up. The silence enters. The slow wet heat becomes a second temperature, the one your body remembers as *normal*, the one you miss when you leave and find yourself standing in a dry cool room wondering why the air feels wrong, why the silence sounds wrong, why you can't feel the ground thinking beneath your feet.
