# What Lives in the Green Expanse Beneath

---

The plants live here.

This must be said first, and clearly, because later it will become complicated, and you will want to know where the boundary is. There is no boundary. The plants live here, and the plants are here, and the difference between those two statements is a question the Expanse has never been asked and could not answer if it were.

But let us start with what can be observed.

---

The mites live here. They are the closest thing to a citizen that the Expanse has. They exist in the billions, in the trillions — numbers that lose meaning in a space without census takers — and they do the work that the Expanse requires to remain itself. They are not employed. They are not driven. They simply eat, and in eating, they process, and in processing, they return to the root-floor what the root-floor needs to continue being root-floor. They are the digestion of this place. They are its gut fauna, if the gut were spread across every surface and the food were everywhere and the eating never stopped.

Do they know? Probably not. A mite — or the thing like a mite that lives here — has no brain to speak of, only a knot of nerve that responds to chemical gradients and moisture levels and the presence of the particular compounds it prefers. It does not experience the Expanse. It experiences its own small corridor of sensation: the warmth of the floor, the sweetness of decay, the proximity of others of its kind, the vague pressure of the threads that drink above it. It has never seen the luminous ceiling. It has never felt a condensation-drop fall. It lives in the top centimeter of the root-mass and it dies in the top centimeter of the root-mass and its body is processed by its children within moments of its death, and this is not a tragedy because there was never a self there to lose, only a pattern that continued for a while in one shape and then continued in another.

And yet. And yet. The mites move with a kind of purpose. Not the purpose of a creature that has decided something, but the purpose of a creature that *is* a decision — that exists because the Expanse needed something to do what they do, and so they arose, and so they remain. They are not separate from the system. They are the system, localized, mobile, tiny. When you see the surface of the root-floor stir, you are not seeing animals in their habitat. You are seeing the habitat itself, breathing.

---

The pale caterpillars live here, though "live" may be too active a word for what they do.

They exist. They persist. They move, yes, but they move the way a glacier moves — so slowly that motion is a theoretical property rather than an observable one. They have no eyes. They have no need for eyes. The light-glands are not light to them; they are food, identified by chemical signature, by the faint heat they give off, by the particular composition of the air directly adjacent to them. The caterpillar does not see the Expanse. It tastes it, inch by inch, with the whole front of its body.

It is not unhappy. This is important to understand, because the description of its life — eating, slowly, across a ceiling that regrows what it consumes, with no predator and no companion and no purpose beyond the continuation of its own eating — sounds, from the outside, like a kind of hell. But the caterpillar has no outside. It has no concept of *other ways to be*. It does not know that birds exist, that rivers exist, that there are creatures who move fast and die young and feel something called *boredom*. It knows warmth and moisture and the specific satisfaction of consuming a light-gland, which to it is not a dimming of the world but a fullness of the body, and this is enough. This has always been enough.

There is a question about the caterpillars that the Expanse does not ask, and it is this: do they transform? Is there, somewhere in the warm wet dark of the root-floor, a pupa? A chrysalis? Something that was a pale slow eater of light and is becoming something else — something with wings, perhaps, or something with legs, or something that will rise up and fly through the avenues of the pillars and find the exit that may or may not exist and enter a world of sun and wind and cold that would kill it in an instant?

Probably not. Probably they lay eggs, and the eggs hatch, and the new caterpillars climb to the ceiling and eat, and this has always been so. But the Expanse has a way of making certainty difficult. Nothing here is finished. Nothing here is settled. The plants grow, and the roots thicken, and the floor rises, and the light-glands pulse, and somewhere in the system there may be a process that has not yet completed, a form that has not yet emerged, a creature that is waiting for a trigger that has not yet been pulled. The caterpillars may be the end of a line. They may be the middle of one. There is no way to know without waiting, and waiting is the only thing the Expanse does well.

---

The Expanse itself lives here.

This is the complicated part. This is where the boundaries fail.

You have been told that the plants may all be one organism. This is true in the way that a forest of aspen trees is one organism — connected by roots, sharing nutrients, responding to stimuli as a network rather than as individuals. But it is also more true than that, because the Expanse has no soil that is not itself, no water that is not moving through itself, no air that has not been breathed by itself. The connection is not just roots. It is every surface, every thread, every gland and frond and pillar. The circulatory system of the thin pale tendrils links every part of the Expanse to every other part, and the chemicals that move through those tendrils carry information — slow, chemical information, the way hormones carry information in a body, the way a blush carries information, the way a stomach growl carries information.

When a caterpillar eats a light-gland, the Expanse knows. Not in the way you know — not with alarm, not with concern, not with the impulse to stop it. But the chemical composition near the consumed gland changes, and that change propagates, slowly, through the network of threads, and somewhere on the other side of the Expanse, hours or days later, a frond adjusts its production by an amount too small to measure. The system compensates. The system is always compensating. This is what living means, in here: not growth, not change, not progress toward a goal, but *compensation*, the endless small adjustments that keep everything the same.

Does the Expanse have a self? No. A self requires boundaries, requires an inside and an outside, requires the possibility of being wrong about something. The Expanse cannot be wrong. It can only be what it is, and what it is is a process — a vast, slow, warm, damp process that has been running for longer than anything can remember, because nothing here remembers. The pillars do not remember when they were shorter. The fronds do not remember when they were buds. The root-floor does not remember the objects that have been swallowed by it, though they are still there, embedded in the compressed matter, gradually being transformed into something that is neither wood nor bone nor plant but simply *material*, substrate, the medium through which the Expanse expresses itself.

The Expanse does not know you are reading this. It does not know it is being described. If you were standing in it right now, it would not know you were standing in it. Your warmth would be absorbed, your moisture would be collected, your breath would be processed by the threads that drink the air, and your presence would alter the system by the smallest possible increment — a fraction of a degree of heat, a few milliliters of water, a slight disturbance in the mite-population where you stepped. And then you would leave, and the Expanse would adjust, and it would be as if you had never been there.

This is not indifference. Indifference requires the capacity to care. This is something else — something before indifference, something that has never developed the architecture of caring because caring has never been necessary. The Expanse does not need to notice. It does not need to respond. It does not need to do anything other than what it is already doing, which is to live, which is to be alive, which is to continue the process of being the process.

---

But there is something else that lives here. Or if not lives, then persists. Or if not persists, then... remains.

The objects. The seven — or nine — things that are not plant. The bowl, the rod, the fragments. They are not alive. They were never alive, or if they were, their life ended long before the Expanse grew around them. But they are *present* in a way that the rest of the Expanse is not, because they are discontinuous. They are the exceptions. They are the proof that something other than the Expanse was once here.

Something made them. This is inescapable. The rod is too straight. The bowl is too curved. The fragments have grooves that are too regular. These are the marks of intention, of tool-use, of a mind that looked at raw material and imagined a shape and then imposed that shape on the world. The Expanse does not do this. The Expanse grows, which is a different kind of making — emergent, undirected, the result of a million small responses rather than a single large decision. Whatever made the objects decided. It decided to make a bowl, and it made one. It decided to drive a rod into the ground, and it drove one. These were acts of will.

So: something with a will lived here once. Or visited here once. Or came here once, and made things, and left them, and went away, and never came back.

Who?

The Expanse cannot say. The Expanse has no memory. But the objects remember, in their way — in the way that a footprint remembers the foot, in the way that a shadow remembers the light. The bowl remembers being shaped. The rod remembers being driven. The fragments remember being whole. They carry in their structure the ghost of the intention that made them, and that ghost is the closest thing to a story that the Expanse contains.

Consider the rod. It was driven into the root-floor, which means it was driven into something — the floor was there already, or something like it, some earlier version of the root-mass. Someone wanted it to stand upright. Why? To mark a place? To support something? To test the depth of the floor? The rod does not say. But the rod is warm, warmer than the surrounding material, as though something inside it still holds heat. What holds heat? What holds heat for centuries, for millennia, for however long the Expanse has been growing around this rod? Not metal — there is no metal here, has never been metal here. Not stone — stone does not warm. Something else. Something that was made, or that grew, or that was once alive in a way that left a residue of warmth behind.

And the fronds grow around the rod but not on it. They leave it a clearance of darkness, the only shadow in the Expanse. Why? Do they sense something about the rod that makes them reluctant to touch it? Do the threads that drink from every surface find the rod undrinkable? Or is it simply that the rod is smooth, and the threads cannot gain purchase, and so it remains bare by default, a gap in the coverage rather than an exclusion? The Expanse does not know. The Expanse does not exclude. The Expanse includes everything, eventually — the bowl, the fragments, the rod, all of them slowly being swallowed by the rising floor, all of them on their way to becoming part of the substrate, part of the compressed matter that is neither one thing nor another but simply *the floor*, simply *the Expanse*, simply *here*.

But not yet. Not yet. The rod still stands. The bowl still holds water. The fragments still have edges. They are not gone. They persist. And in persisting, they suggest a possibility that the Expanse otherwise excludes: the possibility of *before*. The possibility that this place was not always what it is. The possibility that something came before the plants, before the light-glands, before the equilibrium — something that walked and thought and made things and left them behind when it was done.

The Expanse does not think about this. But if you stand in the shadow of the rod — the only shadow in the entire space — and you look at the darkness around its smooth gray surface, you might think about it. You might wonder who stood where you are standing and drove it into the ground. You might wonder what they expected to find, or what they were trying to measure, or whether they knew that the ground would grow up around their marker and swallow it and keep it warm for longer than any memory could last. You might wonder if they are still alive, somewhere above, in the world of sun and sky and change. You might wonder if they remember this place, or if it has faded from their mind the way everything fades from minds that live in time.

The Expanse does not wonder. But you do. And that is a kind of life, too — the life of the question, the life of the not-knowing, the life that persists in the gap between what is and what might have been.

---

So what lives in the Green Expanse Beneath?

The mites live here, in their billions, doing the work of transformation.

The pale caterpillars live here, in their handfuls, eating the light.

The Expanse lives here, if living is what you call it — the vast vegetal process that has no self and no memory and no goal.

The objects persist here, the last traces of something that was once present and is now absent, the only evidence that *absence* is even possible in a place so complete.

And you, if you were here, would live here too — for a while. You would breathe the warm damp air. You would drink the condensation from the bowl. You would feel the root-floor yielding under your feet and the threads brushing your ankles and the light falling on your skin from ten million tiny sources. You would be alive in the way that only animals are alive — aware, and lonely, and temporary.

And then you would leave. And the Expanse would continue. And the mites would process your footprints. And the threads would drink your sweat. And the light-glands would pulse, on and on and on, amber-white, steady, warm.

And this would be enough. It has always been enough.
