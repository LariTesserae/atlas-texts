# What Lives on the Drowned Mesa

---

The pale shapes in the deeper channels are fish. This is the easiest answer and it is not wrong, but it is not complete.

They have spines. They have fins. They have the orbital architecture of skulls behind which something like a brain pulses in the warm dark. But they are fish the way a sketch is a painting — the essential lines are present, the logic of the form is legible, but the details have not yet been filled in. Their eyes are present but lack iris or pupil; they are simply dark spots, light-gathering depressions that have not yet decided what they want to focus on. Their fins are membranes stretched between rays that are still soft, still cartilaginous, still working out how much rigidity a fin requires to push water effectively. Their scales, where they have them, are papery and translucent, more suggestion than armor.

They are not incomplete. That word implies a destination they have failed to reach. They are *in process*. The warm water, the mineral richness bleeding from the young basalt, the absence of predators and competitors — these conditions have not pressured them toward any particular refinement. They drift in the deep channels in loose formations that might be schools or might simply be congregations, the way dust gathers in still air. When they move, they move with the current or across it in small adjustments, their tails flicking with a motion that is more shudder than propulsion. They eat the floating plant matter. They eat each other, occasionally, though without enthusiasm — a slow turning toward a smaller shape, a gradual opening of a mouth that is still deciding how many teeth it needs, a closing. They do not hunt. They do not flee. They exist in the channels the way the channels exist in the rock: because the space was there and something had to fill it.

There are three kinds, or there might be five. The boundaries are not fixed. The largest are the length of a forearm, pale grey-green, with bodies that are still negotiating the ratio between depth and length — some are compressed laterally as if made for maneuvering between reed-stems, others are rounder, more generic, as if the specialization has not yet taken hold. The smallest are the size of a finger, nearly transparent, their internal organs visible as smears of darker color beneath skin that has not yet learned to be opaque. And there are intermediate forms, or perhaps they are simply individuals whose bodies have wandered from the template, expressing variations that have not been culled because there is nothing here to cull them. They have no names. They do not need names. They are becoming whatever they will become, and the mesa has given them nothing but time.

---

The insects are easier to describe because they are closer to finished. They arrived as the water arrived — or they were already present in whatever shallow ecosystem existed before the uplift, and they simply continued. The water-skimmers are the most visible. They walk on the surface film between reed-stems, their weight distributed across legs that spread like wire, their bodies dark and dry above the waterline while beneath them their shadows move across the pale substrate below. They eat what falls on the water's surface — spores, fragments, the bodies of smaller insects that have finished their brief lives and drifted down to rest on the surface tension like tiny wrecks. They lay their eggs on the undersides of the broad-leafed aquatics, gluing them to the leaves with a secretion that hardens into amber-colored dots, each containing a single future skimmer that will hatch into warmth and water and more of the same.

The midges are harder to see. They rise in columns on still mornings, when the warmth of the water meets the cooler air above and creates the thermals they ride, and in those columns they are visible only as a kind of shimmer, a denseness in the air that moves as a body and then disperses as the sun rises higher and the thermal breaks apart. They do not bite. They have mouthparts that are still deciding what they are for — currently they sip, probing the surfaces of leaves for moisture and dissolved sugars, but the mouthparts are oversized for this task, jointed and complex in a way that suggests future purposes not yet revealed. They breed in the water, their larvae dropping through the warm column to swim — clumsily, with the same not-quite-committed motion as the pale fish — until they are ready to rise, to dry, to fly, to shimmer, to do it again.

There are other arthropods deeper in the reed-forest. Things that crawl along the stems below the waterline, their bodies flattened to move between the leaf-sheaths and the stalk. Things that live in the algal mats on the rock walls, grazing the growth in slow methodical paths that leave patterns visible only if you peel back the mat and examine the rock beneath — grazing trails, evidence of mouths at work over months and years, the patient consumption of a surface that is continuously renewing itself. These are small. Most are the size of a fingernail or smaller. They have not been catalogued. They have not been seen by human eyes. They exist in the spaces between the known and the hypothesized, in the warm dark where the green light does not reach, doing the work that small things always do — eating, being eaten, converting one form of matter into another without any awareness that this is remarkable.

---

But these are not what lives on the drowned mesa. These are what *exists* there, what occupies its water and its stems and its algal surfaces. To describe what lives there — what truly lives there — you must understand that the distinction between organism and environment has not yet been drawn in this place. The mesa is too young. The categories have not hardened. What lives here is the mesa itself, or something so distributed through it that the distinction is meaningless.

Consider the reeds.

They grow in patterns that look designed. Corridors open between stands, wide enough to move through, curving and branching like the passages of a building that was laid out by an architect who worked in growth rather than stone. Chambers form where the stands meet and overlap, creating enclosed spaces roofed with interlaced leaves and floored with warm shallow water. The entrances to these chambers are consistent in width — wide enough to admit something, narrow enough to exclude something larger. The corridors lead somewhere. They connect the deeper channels to the open shelves, the shaded interior to the bright rim. They are not random.

This could be explained by competitive growth patterns, by the physics of stems pushing against stems and finding the path of least resistance. This explanation is sufficient and may be correct. But it does not account for the consistency. It does not account for the way the corridors maintain themselves even as the reeds grow and die and are replaced. It does not account for the fact that if you sit in one of the chambers — and you can; the water is warm and the light is green and the quiet is absolute — you will feel, after some time, that the chamber is a room, and that rooms are made for someone.

No one made them. The built parameter is zero. But the reeds grew them, and they grew them with a specificity that borders on intent, and intent — even vegetative intent, even the intent of a thing that has no brain and no nervous system and no mechanism for thought beyond the chemical and the cellular — intent is still intent.

The reeds communicate. Not through sound or signal but through their roots, which interlace beneath the flooded soil in a network so dense that the entire mesa-top is a single root-mass, every stand connected to every other stand, every stem receiving information about water level and nutrient availability and the pressure of current from every other point on the plateau. When a corridor begins to close — when a stand leans too far and begins to narrow the passage — the adjacent stands adjust. They grow away. They shift their weight. The corridor reopens. This could be phototropism. It could be thigmotropism. It could be something for which there is not yet a word, because no one has observed it long enough to name it.

The reeds are not thinking. But they are *doing something*, and that something has the same shape as thought, the same logic, the same gradual accumulation of small decisions into something that looks, from the outside, like purpose.

---

And there is something else. Or there is the suggestion of something else, the way a shape suggested in cloud or shadow resolves and then unresolves depending on how you look at it.

The people who have visited the mesa — and there have been a few, scientists and surveyors and the occasional adventurer drawn by the anomaly of a new landmass rising through the ocean — report a feeling of presence. Not a presence they can locate. Not a creature or an entity or a being that watches them from the reeds. Something more distributed. A quality of attention, as if the mesa is aware that something is moving through it, not with hostility or curiosity but with the mild interest of a system that has registered a change in its inputs.

The feeling intensifies in the chambers. It intensifies at night, when the clouds press down and the warm water glows faintly with bioluminescence — not from the fish or the insects but from the algal mats themselves, which produce a cold green light in response to the geothermal fluctuations that pulse through the rock beneath them. The light comes and goes in waves, following the rhythm of the heat below, and in those waves the chambers pulse like lungs, like a body breathing on a timescale too slow to perceive directly, only visible in the gradual brightening and dimming of the walls.

Sleep is difficult on the mesa. Not because of discomfort — the water is warm, the air is soft, there are no biting insects in significant numbers. Sleep is difficult because the body, unmoored from its normal rhythms by the constant warmth and the strange light and the quality of attention that will not resolve into threat or welcome, begins to fall into synchronization with the mesa's rhythm instead. Breathing slows. Heart rate drops. The boundary between waking and sleeping becomes porous, and in that porosity, something enters — not a dream, exactly, but a mode of perception that does not belong to the individual mind. You close your eyes and you feel the roots beneath you, the slow pulse of water through channels, the growth of stems pushing upward at a rate that is imperceptible to human time but that you can somehow feel, the way you feel your own heartbeat when you are very still.

You are not the mesa. You are still yourself, enclosed in skin, separate from the water and the reeds and the warm stone. But you are *also* the mesa, for as long as you stay, your body another channel through which the warm water circulates, your breath another current moving through the corridors of reed-stems, your waste another input into the system that will be processed and absorbed and incorporated without comment or concern.

This is what lives on the drowned mesa: a process, a becoming, a system so young and so unpressured that it has not yet learned where its boundaries are. It does not know what it is. It is finding out. It is finding out the way a body finds out — by growing, by incorporating, by responding to the conditions it encounters with the only logic available to it, which is the logic of continuation. The mesa lives. The reeds are its skeleton. The water is its blood. The pale fish and the skimmers and the midges and the things that crawl in the algal mats are its internal fauna, its microbiome, the small lives that large lives carry within them always. And the green light in the deep water, the light that does not exist in the above-surface spectrum — that is its thought, its slow vegetative processing of sun and warmth and time into something that might, given enough centuries, become something recognizable.

Or might not. There is no guarantee. The mesa might subside. The ocean might reclaim it. The reeds might die and the channels might dry and the whole enterprise might prove to be brief, geologically speaking — a moment of emergence, a few decades of warmth and growth and potential, and then a slow sinking back into the deep.

But right now, in this moment, on the warm water, in the green light, between the reeds that grew their corridors with something that looks like intent: it lives. It is the newest large thing on the planet, and it is still figuring out what it wants to be, and it does not know you are watching, and it would not care if it did.
