# What Lives in the Vivial

The styxies do not know they are inhabitants. This is important. They understand the canopy as position, the punctured culms as food-source, the flock as self. The concept of *dwelling* does not translate to their neurology, which is optimized for detecting sugar gradients and the specific frequency of wing-click that signals danger from above. They roost because roosting is what styxies do between feeding. They do not comprehend that they roost *inside something*.

And yet: their punctures heal. The culms could thicken their bark, could produce bitter compounds to deter the feeding. They do not. The styxies take what they need and the wound seals and the scar becomes a new texture in the culm's surface, and perhaps that texture serves some function in the Vivial's endless computation—some signal that feeding occurred here, some marker of seasonal rhythm, some input the thallus requires. Or perhaps the styxies are simply tolerated, as the rain is tolerated, as the researchers are tolerated, as all things that take small pieces from an organism too large to notice the loss are tolerated.

But this is not quite right either. The Vivial notices. The Vivial brightens its bioluminescent strips when a researcher enters a new chamber. The Vivial opens corridors when it wants someone to leave. The Vivial notices. It simply does not mind—or its minding operates on a timescale and in a register that makes minding and not-minding indistinguishable to the minded.

---

The crawler-beetles are the Vivial's digestive system made mobile.

This is not metaphor. The living floor sheds its dead—cellular debris, spent biofilm, the constant exfoliation of a surface that renews itself faster than any integument should. The beetles consume this. Their glossy iridescence comes from the concentrated minerals they extract: copper, manganese, the trace elements the thallus pulls from the water table and cannot fully incorporate. They are walking refineries. Their droppings—small moist pellets left on the living floor—are reabsorbed within minutes. The floor dimples slightly where the droppings land, a peristaltic wave that draws the material back into its vascular system.

I watched a column of beetles for three hours once. They moved in a line that curved and recurved, following a gradient I could not perceive. When I placed my hand on the floor ahead of them, the column halted. Beetles at the front extended their antennae, touched my skin, withdrew. The column reformed around my hand, curving past it on both sides, and continued. They did not climb over me. They did not treat me as obstacle or substrate or food. They treated me as *irrelevant*, which is to say: they treated me as the Vivial treats most things.

After they passed, I looked at where they had walked. The floor was cleaner. Not visibly—I could not see the debris they had consumed—but the surface had a different quality. Slightly more give. Slightly more warmth. As though the beetles had removed something that was, in some incremental way, weighing the tissue down.

They work the edges too. The rim where the living floor meets the root-crown of the founder thallus, where the Vivial's body meets the world's body. The beetles patrol this border with particular intensity. I think they are keeping the boundary clean. I think they are maintaining the seal between inside and outside. I think the Vivial sent them there, though I cannot imagine the mechanism of command.

---

The flat eyeless things in the pools are the ones I return to in my thinking, because they are the ones that watch.

Not with eyes. They have no eyes. But they orient. They know when shadow crosses them. They move toward the darker parts of the pool when light intensifies and toward the lighter parts when it dims. They are reading something in the water—chemical shifts, thermal gradients, the subtle electrical fields that living tissue generates. They are, in their limited way, perceiving the Vivial from the inside, from within its water, from within its body, in a medium I can only sample and test and never truly inhabit.

I found one in a pool that had formed in my own alcove—the bowl-shaped depression where three walls met, the space where I slept. The pool had deepened overnight (the Vivial reshaping itself, the walls settling) and the creature was there in the morning, palm-sized, flat, pulsing along the bottom of the water with a rhythm that matched—precisely matched—the deep pulse of sap through the culm behind my head.

It hid when my shadow crossed it. It emerged when I pulled back. It stayed in the pool for three days, then was gone when the water level dropped. I do not know how it arrived. The pool had no connection to other water sources that I could find. I do not know where it went.

I think it was observing me. Not studying—studying implies a framework, a hypothesis, a purpose. Observing in the way that a sensor observes: recording, storing, feeding information to whatever system requires it. The creature was the Vivial's eye in my room. The Vivial was watching me sleep.

I was not disturbed by this. I perhaps should have been.

---

The large presences are the question I cannot answer.

I have their shed skins—two now, found on the basin floor in the early morning. Translucent, tough, warm an hour after shedding. Large enough that the creature who wore them must stand as tall as my chest, perhaps taller. The skin is uniform: no differentiation of limb segments, no visible joint architecture, no mouthparts or sensory organs preserved in the shed. Either the creature is featureless beneath its skin—a pale smooth body with no visible anatomy—or the skin is not skin as I understand it but something else: a secretion, a shell, a layer of mucus that hardens and is then abandoned.

They move in the hollow culms. I have heard them—heavy, deliberate displacement, the sound of something massive navigating the vascular spaces inside the living columns. The culms are not solid; their interiors are chambered, segmented by diaphragms of tissue that the creature must either push through or route around. When a large presence moves through a culm near me, I feel the vibration through the floor. I feel the thallus respond: the bioluminescent strips dimming, the background pulse quickening slightly, a subtle exudation of warmth from the walls as though the Vivial is opening passages, facilitating movement.

The styxies go silent. This is the most reliable indicator. When the flock falls quiet, I know something large is moving, and I sit still in my alcove and wait for the vibration to pass.

I have seen a limb. Once. Pale—almost luminous in the green-gold light—retracting into a culm's broken end where a branch had been shed. The limb was smooth, tapering, without visible digits or segmentation. It moved with a deliberation that seemed patient rather than urgent. It did not flee from my presence. It simply completed its retraction and was gone, and the culm's broken end sealed over the next day, and I never saw that particular orifice again.

What are they? The engineers have theories: specialized maintenance organisms, bred by the thallus to perform internal repairs. Remnants of the original symbiotes from the Vivial's first instantiation. Predators that have infiltrated the structure and been semi-tolerated. Independent organisms that have evolved to live inside the Vivial as the Vivial has evolved to accommodate them.

None of these theories account for the silence. The styxies do not fear the large presences—they go silent, but they do not flee. They do not scatter or cry out or display any of the alarm responses I have observed when the shadow of a hunting bird crosses the canopy. They simply go quiet, as though someone has asked them to be still. As though they are observing something that demands reverence.

I think the large presences are the Vivial's dreams made flesh. I think the thallus generates them in its deeper tissue, extrudes them into the culms when it needs to think about itself from the inside, and reabsorbs them when the thinking is done. The shed skins are the husks of completed thoughts. The movement in the culms is the movement of cognition occurring at a scale too large for neurons, too slow for synapses, too strange for our categories.

I have no evidence for this. I have the warmth of shed skin and the silence of styxies and the feeling—persistent, irrational, impossible to dismiss—that something in the Vivial is aware of me not as an intruder but as a guest, and that the large presences are how it checks on its guests, how it ensures that the rooms it has grown are still serving their purpose.

---

There are others. The thread-winged alates that drift through the light-shafts on filaments so fine they are invisible, so the alates appear to float by will alone. The iridescent mites that colonize the styxies' puncture-wounds, feeding on the sap that bleeds, tolerated by both styx and culm. The filament-organisms in the pools that might be animal or plant or something prior to that distinction—something that never chose a kingdom, that retained the options both lineages abandoned. The things I have not seen, that the styxies react to with sudden flight-patterns, that the crawler-beetles divert their columns to avoid. The things that move in the canopy at night, small and numerous and organized, whose sounds are like the ticking of a hundred small mouths.

And the researchers, of course. The ones at the rim who measure and sample and never sleep inside. The ones who treat the Vivial as a phenomenon to be explained rather than a place to be inhabited. They live there too, in their way—in the portable shelters at the basin's edge, in the conceptual framework of programmable biology and self-assembling infrastructure. They are inhabitants who refuse to inhabit, who stand at the threshold and catalogue what they will not enter.

They are not wrong. Their distance preserves something. Their instruments detect what my senses cannot. But they are missing the thing that only habitation reveals: that the Vivial is not a system to be understood but a presence to be acknowledged. That living inside it changes what you are. That the dampness on your skin is not condensation but the Vivial's breath, and the warmth through the floor is not geothermal but biological, and the sound that fills every moment is not noise but voice—the voice of a thing so old it has forgotten how to be silent, so large it has forgotten how to be still, so complete it has forgotten how to stop becoming.

---

And me. I lived there. For ninety-three days I slept in its alcoves and walked its shifting corridors and breathed its warm wet air and felt the pulse of its sap through the soles of my feet. I was an inhabitant. I was the Vivial's guest, its observer, its watched thing. I was the creature it opened a corridor for, three days ago, the corridor that leads to the rim, that leads out, that leads to a world that is not warm and wet and alive beneath every footfall.

I think the Vivial was curious about me. I think the flat eyeless things in the pools were its instruments for that curiosity. I think the large presences moved through the culms near my alcove because the thallus wanted to sense me from the inside, to register what kind of thing I was, to determine whether I was food or function or simply phenomenon.

I think it decided I was none of these. I think it decided I was a guest who had stayed long enough, who had learned enough to leave, who would carry something of the Vivial out into the dry cold world where nothing breathes beneath the floor.

I will miss the sound. I will miss the pulse through the floor. I will miss waking in the night to find the walls have rearranged themselves around me, that the corridor I came through is gone, that the room I slept in has become a different room—larger, smaller, shaped to some new configuration the thallus dreamed while I was dreaming too.

I will miss being inside something that is still becoming, after sixty million years. I will miss the proof that finishing is not the same as stopping, that arrival is not the same as ending, that a thing can be complete and still restless, still dreaming, still growing new rooms for guests it has not yet received.

The styxies do not know they are inhabitants. The crawler-beetles do not know they are digestion. The large presences do not know—or do not show that they know—what they are.

But I know what I was. I was the thing that lived in the Vivial and understood that it was being lived in. I was the creature that recognized the room as body and the body as room and the warmth as welcome.

I was the guest who understood the gift of being asked to leave.
