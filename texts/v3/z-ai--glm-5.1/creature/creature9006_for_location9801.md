# The Inhabitants

---

The mats come first. They must. They are the floor the rest walk on, the ceiling they cling to, the walls that are also meals.

But calling them *mats* is like calling a continent a place to stand. The gray ones — they are not gray, they are the color of stone because they *are* stone, or are becoming stone, or have just finished being stone — the gray ones carpet the quieter passages in layers that might be centimeters or might be meters thick; no one has ever found the bottom of one. If you could peel it up, which you cannot because it is adhered at the molecular level to the architecture beneath, you would find older mat beneath, and older mat beneath that, and beneath that the material of the architecture itself, which was once mat, which was once the secretion of something living, which was once mineral, which was once — but this is the problem with origins in a place this old. Everything has been everything else. The gray mats eat the stone and excrete stone and the stone they excrete is the stone the next generation eats. They have been doing this for so long that the word *generation* means nothing. There are no individuals. There is only surface area and chemical gradient and the slow outward creep into any space that is not already claimed.

The rust mats are different. They live where the mineral water seeps through, where the heat from below makes the stone warm — and *warm* here means four degrees instead of two, which is the difference between poverty and abundance. The rust mats are iron oxidizers. They take what the deep earth gives and they make it into themselves, and themselves into the color of old blood, and the old blood into the flooring of the chambers where the current runs slow and rich. These mats breathe, after a fashion. They pulse. Not with any rhythm a surface-dweller would recognize, but on a cycle tied to the seeps themselves: when the mineral flow increases, they swell; when it decreases, they contract and wait. They can wait for centuries. They have waited for centuries. There are rust mats in the lower chambers that have not swelled since before the last ice age, and they are still alive, still viable, still holding their place against the gray, and when the seeps shift — as they do, as they always do, following the slow migration of heat through the stone — the rust mats will bloom again and the gray will retreat and the borders between them will be redrawn in a war that has no combatants, only edges.

The black mats are the ones that trouble the imagination. They live at the boundary. Not the boundary between stone and water — that is the gray mats' province — and not the boundary between heat and cold — that belongs to the rust — but the boundary between the living and the non-living, between the organic and the mineral, between the thing that eats and the thing that is eaten. The black mats are where the architecture is newest, where the builders have just finished and the scavengers have not yet begun. They are the color of void because they absorb everything: light that does not reach here, chemicals that do not dissolve, the faint electrical fields that all living things produce and that the black mats consume in a way that no surface biochemistry can explain. They are the rarest and the thinnest and the oldest, and if the architecture has a mind — it does not, but if it did — the black mats would be its thoughts.

---

The grazers are the simplest to describe and the most difficult to see.

They move like mouths. That is their primary characteristic: they are organs of consumption that have learned locomotion, or perhaps they are vehicles of locomotion that have learned to eat, and in either case the result is the same — a creature whose body is a tube through which mat passes, converting bacterial growth into animal tissue with an efficiency that would be obscene if efficiency had any moral dimension at this depth. The largest are the length of a finger. The smallest are the size of a grain of rice. They are the color of what they eat — gray grazers on the gray mats, rust grazers on the rust, black grazers where the black mats grow — and they are so nearly transparent that you would not see them at all except for the tracks they leave: cropped pathways through the bacterial felt, meandering lines that reveal the grazer's passage the way a letter reveals a hand that wrote it.

They have no eyes. They have no brains. They have a nerve net that encircles their body like a loose belt, and they have chemoreceptors so sensitive that they can detect the difference between a patch of mat that was cropped an hour ago and one that has been growing undisturbed for a week, and they move toward the latter with a single-mindedness that is not purposeful but is nevertheless effective. They reproduce by splitting. They die by being eaten, or by being swept into a current too strong for their weak swimming, or by simply stopping — their metabolism winding down over days or weeks until they settle onto the mat and become part of it, their chitin dissolving slowly into the bacterial mass, their organic compounds absorbed by the very thing they spent their lives consuming. This is not tragic. This is not even notable. This is the way the architecture recycles itself, the way a word spoken into a canyon returns as an echo that is also a word.

There are grazers that specialize. There is a grazer that eats only the gray mat at the edges of rust colonies, where the two communities compete and the chemistry is most complex. There is a grazer that lives exclusively in the cropped trails of other grazers, following the chemical residue of its own kind in a loop that can continue for months before the trail crosses itself and the grazer, confused, stops and splits and becomes two grazers who go in different directions. There is a grazer that has never been seen alive but whose tracks are unmistakable — deep, wide gouges in the mat, as if something the size of a hand had dragged itself across the surface — and whether this creature is rare or simply very good at remaining invisible is a question that the architecture does not care to answer.

---

The predators are patient in a way that has nothing to do with patience as a virtue.

Patience, for a surface-dweller, is the act of waiting despite wanting to act. Patience, for the predators of the benthic architecture, is simply the absence of impulse. They do not wait. They exist. And when existence and opportunity intersect, they feed.

The largest of them — and *large* here means the size of a fist, which is enormous — is the thing that lives in the chambers where the current slows. It has no name. It does not need one. Its body is a bell of translucent tissue, so thin that the water passes through it as much as around it, and trailing from the bell's rim are filaments that extend outward into the current like the lines of a fisherman who has been fishing for so long that he has forgotten he is fishing. The filaments are sticky. They are also sensitive. When a grazer — or a cleaner, or a larva of something else, or any particle of organic matter above a certain size — contacts a filament, it adheres, and the filament contracts, and the bell folds inward, and the prey is enclosed in a pouch that was not there a moment before and will not be there a moment after, because the bell digests and then opens and then waits again. The whole process takes hours. The waiting takes days. The predator does not distinguish between the two states because it has no state to distinguish. It feeds. It does not feed. The water moves. It moves with the water. When the current shifts and the chamber becomes a river, the bell-filament predator inflates itself slightly and lets the water carry it to another chamber, another slow place, another vantage from which to not-wait.

There are smaller predators. There are many smaller predators. There is a thing like an armored ribbon that lives in the cracks between the architecture's elements and extends a pair of jointed appendages into the current, each appendage tipped with a claw that closes on contact with anything organic and retracts with a speed that seems impossible in water this cold. There is a thing like a living net — a colonial predator, thousands of tiny polyps connected by a shared circulatory system, spreading across a wall in a lattice that traps anything smaller than a certain size and digests it collectively, distributing the nutrients through the network so that every polyp benefits from every catch. There is a thing that might be a predator or might be a parasite — the distinction is meaningless here — that attaches itself to the armor of grazers and slowly, over weeks, dissolves a hole through the chitin and inserts a feeding tube and consumes the grazer from the inside, leaving only the shell, which then becomes a shelter for other creatures, which then become hosts for the predator's offspring, which then attach to other grazers, and so on, and so on, in a cycle of exploitation that is no more cruel than the water cycle or the cycle of the tides.

The most successful predator in the architecture is not the largest or the fastest or the most aggressive. It is the one that has learned to mimic the architecture itself. Its body is chitin and calcium carbonate, the same material as the walls, and its shape is the shape of a protrusion, a buttress, a decorative ridge — one of the countless small structures that festoon the chambers and passages and that might or might not be alive. It attaches to a wall and it ceases to move, and it secretes a thin layer of the same protein that the builders use, and it becomes indistinguishable from the architecture except for one detail: the small opening near its base, barely visible, through which the current passes and through which, also, small creatures pass, attracted by the chemical signature of the builder-protein which promises — falsely — a surface on which to graze or settle or rest. The creature inside the false wall consumes them. It has been doing this for so long that some of its kind have been built over by actual builders, becoming part of the architecture in truth, their bodies incorporated into the walls while their openings still function, still catching prey, still feeding a creature that is now as much structure as animal, as much stone as flesh, as much part of the edifice as the edifice itself.

---

The builders are the ones that made all of this, which means they are also the ones that unmade nothing, because building here is not an act of creation but an act of accumulation. They do not shape. They secrete. They do not design. They deposit. And yet — and this is the point that troubles anyone who studies them — the result of their mindless deposition is a structure that looks designed. The towers. The arches. The latticework. How does a creature without a brain produce an arch?

The answer is that the arch produces itself.

The builder — it is a small thing, no larger than a grape, armored in the material it secretes, moving slowly across the architecture's surface on hundreds of tiny tube feet — the builder deposits protein and mineral in a pattern that is determined by three things: the shape of the surface it is on, the direction of the current, and the chemical traces left by every builder that passed this way before. It does not follow a plan. It follows a gradient. The gradient is the plan. When a thousand builders have passed over the same surface, each one depositing a thin layer, each one slightly thickening the areas where the current is slowest and the chemical signals are strongest, the result is a structure that channels the current in a particular way, which alters the gradient, which directs the next thousand builders to deposit in slightly different places, which further alters the current, which further alters the gradient, and so on, and so on, until — after generations beyond counting — a wall becomes a buttress becomes an arch becomes a tower becomes a lattice becomes a chamber becomes an architecture that no single creature could have imagined because no single creature *could* imagine it, because it is not a product of imagination but a product of iteration, and iteration, given enough time, produces results indistinguishable from intention.

The builders do not know what they are building. They do not know they are building. They eat the bacterial mats and they secrete the architecture and they move on, and if there is a gap in the architecture — if a scavenger has eaten a passage through a wall, if a current has worn a tower's flank thin, if a section of the structure has collapsed under the accumulated weight of time — the builders will fill it, because the gap alters the gradient and the altered gradient draws them and they deposit until the gradient is gone, which means the gap is filled, which means the architecture is whole, which means the builders have done what they always do, which is what they have always done, which is what they will always do.

There are builders that specialize in repair and builders that specialize in extension and builders that specialize in the fine latticework between the larger structures, and these specializations are not inherited but acquired — a builder that spends its early life in a damaged area will tend to repair, a builder that finds itself at the growing edge will tend to extend, and a builder that is caught in the narrow spaces between towers will tend to fill those spaces with lace, because that is what the gradient demands. And when a builder dies — when it is eaten by a predator or crushed by a shifting structure or simply exhausted by the endless cycle of eating and secreting and moving — its body becomes part of the architecture, its chitin incorporated into the walls, its proteins feeding the next generation of bacteria, its chemical signature lingering in the gradient and influencing every builder that passes this way for decades or centuries after, so that the dead builder continues to build, continues to shape, continues to participate in the conversation between form and formlessness long after it has ceased to be a thing that can be called *alive*.

---

The scavengers are the architects of absence.

They are the ones that eat the architecture, and calling them *scavengers* is a kindness, because what they scavenge is not dead matter but living stone, not remains but the edifice itself. They are small — they must be small, because the architecture is dense and they can only eat what they can reach — and they are numerous, because the architecture is vast and there is always something to eat. They are the reason the towers are rounded. They are the reason the passages are smooth. They are the reason the architecture does not grow without bound, because for every builder depositing material there is a scavenger removing it, and the balance between these two populations is the balance of the whole structure, and if the balance shifts — if the builders outpace the scavengers, or if the scavengers outpace the builders — the architecture will change shape, will swell or hollow, will grow towers where there were chambers or chambers where there were towers, and the currents will shift and the gradients will change and the populations will adjust and the balance will be restored, because the balance is not maintained by any mechanism but is the mechanism, is the architecture, is the thing itself.

The scavengers have a body plan that is essentially a mouth with an engine behind it. The mouth — it is not really a mouth, it is a rasping organ, a tongue made of chitin and studded with mineral teeth harder than the stone they scrape — the mouth moves across the architecture's surface in a pattern that is determined by the same gradients that guide the builders, but inverted: where the builders are drawn to deposit, the scavengers are drawn to consume, because the newest deposits are the softest and the easiest to eat. They leave trails. Not the cropped trails of the grazers, which are superficial and temporary, but deep gouges, incisions, excavations that expose the older, harder layers beneath. These excavations become the texture of the architecture. They become the channels that direct the current. They become the patterns that the next generation of builders will fill, and the patterns that the next generation of scavengers will cut, and the result — after iterations beyond counting — is the intricate relief that covers every surface of the benthic architecture, the decoration that is not decoration but the accumulated record of a war that has no generals and no soldiers and no objective except continuation.

There are scavengers that eat only the oldest stone, the densest deposits, the material that has been compacted by time and pressure into something nearly indestructible. These scavengers are slow and hard and nearly as dense as the rock they consume, and they live in the deepest parts of the architecture, in the chambers that have not seen current-change in millennia, where the water is still and the oxygen is nearly absent and the only thing that moves is the scavenger itself, scraping away at the foundation of the edifice with a patience that makes erosion look hasty. There are scavengers that eat only the newest deposits, the soft protein-rich secretions that the builders have just laid down, and these scavengers are fast and soft and transparent, and they are the ones that the predators eat, because they are the most nutritious and the most visible and the least able to defend themselves. And there are scavengers that eat the scavengers, not as predators but as consumers of the architecture they carry in their guts — a scavenger that has just fed is itself a piece of the architecture, temporarily mobile, and when it is eaten by another scavenger the architecture passes from body to body until it is finally dissolved and released back into the water, where it settles onto a surface and becomes food for the bacteria, which become food for the grazers, which become food for the builders, which become the architecture, which becomes food for the scavengers, and the cycle completes and begins again and has been beginning again for longer than the word *again* has any meaning.

---

The filter-feeders are the architecture's hands.

They are anchored — permanently, irrevocably anchored — to the walls of the chambers where the current runs strongest. Their bodies are tubes, and from the open end of each tube extends a crown of feeding appendages, filaments and lamellae and ciliated ridges, all of them designed — not by mind but by iteration — to extract organic matter from the water as it passes. They are the only creatures in the architecture that do not move, and this is not a limitation but a specialization, because in a place where the current brings food to you, movement is waste.

The largest filter-feeders are old. They are very old. They are older than the architecture they are attached to, which seems impossible until you remember that the architecture is constantly being rebuilt, that the surface the filter-feeder anchored to decades or centuries ago has been eaten and replaced and eaten and replaced many times over, and the filter-feeder has remained, has simply extended its anchor deeper as the old surface was consumed, has built its own foundation into the architecture like a tree growing around a nail, until now the filter-feeder and the architecture are inseparable, the one supporting the other, the one feeding the other, the one *being* the other.

The filter-feeders reproduce by releasing larvae into the current. The larvae are microscopic and they are everywhere — every liter of water in the architecture contains thousands of them, and every chamber and passage is coated with the ones that have settled and are waiting for the right chemical signal to begin their metamorphosis into adults. Most will die. Some will settle in places where the current does not carry enough food. Some will be eaten by predators before they can anchor. Some will anchor and then be overgrown by the bacteria or built over by the builders or scraped away by the scavengers, and their brief lives will end before they have properly begun. But some — enough — will find a place where the current is right and the chemistry is right and the surface is stable, and they will anchor and grow and extend their crowns into the water and become the architecture's reaching hands, and they will feed and they will release larvae and the cycle will continue.

When a filter-feeder dies, its tube remains. The tube is made of the same material as the architecture — calcium carbonate and protein, chitin and silicate — and it becomes part of the architecture, a new passage for the current, a new surface for the bacteria, a new substrate for the builders. The dead filter-feeder continues to shape the water's flow long after it has stopped shaping its own life. This is not a metaphor. This is the way the architecture grows: by incorporating the dead into the living and the living into the structure and the structure into the next generation of the living, until the distinctions between these categories become meaningless and the whole edifice is revealed as a single entity — not an organism, because an organism has boundaries and this does not, but something else, something for which there is no word in any language that was ever spoken in air.

---

The cleaners are the ones that tend the filter-feeders.

They are small — smaller than the grazers, smaller than most of the predators, small enough to move across the filter-feeders' crowns without triggering the feeding response. They eat the debris that accumulates on the feeding appendages: the sediment, the dead bacteria, the organic particles too large for the filter-feeder to process. In return — and *return* implies a contract that does not exist — the cleaners receive a surface to live on and a steady supply of food and the protection that comes from being adjacent to something much larger than any predator.

The cleaners are the only creatures in the architecture that have a relationship that could be called *symbiotic*, and even this is a stretch, because symbiosis implies mutual benefit and the benefit here is entirely incidental. The cleaners do not *choose* to clean. They eat what is available, and what is available on a filter-feeder's crown is debris, and so they clean, and the filter-feeder benefits, and the cleaner benefits, and neither one knows or cares about the other, and yet the result is a partnership that has persisted for as long as the architecture has stood, because the filter-feeders that are cleaned survive longer and release more larvae and the larvae that carry the tendency to tolerate cleaners outcompete the larvae that do not, and the cleaners that specialize in filter-feeder debris outcompete the cleaners that do not, and so the partnership is maintained not by agreement but by survival, which is the only kind of maintenance that matters in a place without minds.

---

The parasites are the ones that take.

They take from the grazers, attaching to their soft undersides and draining the nutrients that the grazers have painstakingly extracted from the bacterial mats. They take from the filter-feeders, burrowing into their tubes and feeding on the rich current of organic matter that passes through. They take from the builders, infiltrating their bodies and co-opting their secretory organs so that the builder produces not architecture but parasite, its chitin and calcium carbonate redirected into the shell of the thing that is consuming it. They take from the predators, settling into their bells and pouches and feeding on the half-digested remains of whatever the predator has caught. They take from each other, in nested hierarchies of exploitation that can reach four or five levels deep — a parasite on a parasite on a parasite on a grazer on a mat on the architecture — until the original host is almost irrelevant, until the system is feeding on itself, until the architecture is supporting a web of consumption that has no center and no edge and no purpose except the continuation of consumption.

The parasites are not evil. There is no evil here. There is no good. There is only the gradient, and the movement along the gradient, and the accumulation and the consumption and the endless conversation between the thing that builds and the thing that eats and the thing that is built and the thing that is eaten, and the parasites are part of this conversation, are perhaps the most essential part, because without them the grazers would proliferate and overeat the mats and the builders would proliferate and overgrow the architecture and the scavengers would proliferate and consume the structure to its foundations, and the parasites are the check on all of this, the drag, the friction, the small persistent drain that keeps every population from achieving the kind of success that is indistinguishable from catastrophe.

---

And there are others.

There are the migrants — creatures that do not live in the architecture but pass through it, following the currents from one deep-sea feature to another, using the passages and chambers as a road network that connects the benthic architecture to other architectures, other reefs, other accumulations of life in the deep. They bring with them chemical signatures from distant places, and these signatures are read by the residents the way a surface-dweller might read news from abroad, and the information is incorporated into the gradient, and the gradient shapes the builders, and the builders shape the architecture, and the architecture is changed by something that was never physically present, that passed through and left only a trace, a whisper, a chemical memory of a place that might be real or might be a false signal produced by some organism that has learned to mimic the migrants' signature in order to attract prey or avoid predators or simply because mimicry is what the gradient selected for, because in a place this old, everything has been selected for, everything has been tried, everything that can persist has persisted, and the things that have not persisted are gone without trace, without memory, without any indication that they were ever here at all.

There are the deep dwellers — things that live in the spaces between the architecture and the stone, in the cracks and fissures that connect the benthic edifice to the volcanic rock beneath, where the heat is stronger and the chemistry is stranger and the creatures are things that have no relationship to anything in the architecture above. They are not bacteria. They are not archaea. They are something else, something that may represent a separate origin of life, a second genesis that occurred in the deep earth and has been evolving in parallel with the surface-derived life in the architecture, and occasionally — very occasionally — one of these deep dwellers will find its way into a passage or chamber, and it will be immediately consumed by whatever encounters it, because it is organic and it is available and in the architecture, organic and available is all it takes to be food. But the chemical traces of the deep dweller linger — traces of amino acids that do not exist in the architecture's biochemistry, traces of lipids with the wrong chirality, traces of something *other* — and these traces are incorporated into the gradient, and the gradient shapes the builders, and the builders shape the architecture, and somewhere in the structure there is a passage that curves in a way that no surface-born chemistry can explain, and whether this is the result of deep-dweller influence or simply the accumulation of a million random iterations is a question that the architecture does not ask.

There are the ones that might not exist. The echoes on the sensors that could be large animals moving through the deeper chambers or could be the chambers themselves shifting. The chemical traces that appear and disappear on cycles that do not correspond to any known tide or current. The marks on the architecture's surface — long, parallel scratches, deeper than any scavenger could make, wider than any predator's claw — that appear in places that have been undisturbed for centuries and that are gone the next time anyone looks, as if something had passed through and left its mark and then the builders had filled the marks in, or the marks had never been there at all, or the marks and the filling were the same event, the same process, the same conversation between form and formlessness that the architecture has been having with itself since before the concept of *self* had any meaning.

---

The architecture lives.

This is not a metaphor and it is not a mistake. The architecture lives the way a coral reef lives, the way a forest lives, the way a city lives — as an entity composed of smaller entities, none of which individually possesses the qualities of the whole, and yet the whole possesses qualities that none of the parts possess. It has structure. It has process. It has history, encoded in its layers the way a tree's history is encoded in its rings. It has a kind of memory — the gradient, the accumulated chemical signature of every creature that has ever lived here, directing the behavior of every creature that will ever live here. It has a kind of metabolism — the cycle of building and consumption, the transformation of mineral into biology and biology into mineral and mineral into architecture and architecture into habitat and habitat into the thing that makes the architecture. It has a kind of reproduction — the larvae and spores and fragments that drift from this architecture to others, carrying the chemical signature of this place, seeding the gradients of new reefs with the memory of the old.

It does not have a mind. It does not have intention. It does not have a plan. It does not need these things. It has something older than mind, older than intention, older than plan. It has persistence. It has the accumulated weight of so much time that the time itself has become a force, a gradient, a direction, and the direction is *more*, and *more* is *continue*, and *continue* is the only command that the architecture has ever received or will ever obey.

And so: the bacteria grow. The grazers graze. The predators wait. The filter-feeders reach. The builders build. The scavengers consume. The cleaners tend. The parasites take. The migrants pass. The deep dwellers seep. The ones that might not exist leave marks that might not be there. The architecture stands — persists — continues. The water moves through it like blood through a body that has forgotten it is alive. The current shifts and the gradient shifts and the population shifts and the shape shifts and the architecture shifts, and the shift is the thing, and the thing is the architecture, and the architecture is the inhabitants, and the inhabitants are the architecture, and the distinction was always false, was always a word from the surface imposed on a place where words do not reach, where light does not reach, where only the gradient reaches, and the gradient says *more*, and *more* says *continue*, and *continue* says *this*, and *this* is everything that lives here, which is everything that is here, which is the place itself, which has no name and does not need one and will persist long after the need for names has passed and the namers have passed and the surface has forgotten that there is anything below, and the architecture will continue, and the inhabitants will continue, and the conversation between form and formlessness will continue, and the plateau will stand on the abyssal plain like a thought that has forgotten it was thinking, and the water will move, and the cold will persist, and the dark will be absolute, and the architecture will be enough.
