# The Inhabitants

---

They do not know they are inhabitants. This is important. This is perhaps the most important thing. They do not live *in* the mechanism the way a person lives in a house, with the understanding that the house was built before them and will stand after they leave. They do not occupy this place. They *are* this place, in the same way that the grooves are this place, in the same way that the water is this place. They emerged from the same process. They are made of the same patience.

But they are not the same. This is what you must understand, what takes time to understand, because the understanding itself has to erode its way past your assumptions the way water erodes stone—slowly, repeatedly, without violence, until suddenly the shape has changed and you cannot remember when it was different. The inhabitants are not the mechanism. They are what the mechanism *produced*. They are what happens when a world machines itself to such precision that the precision begins to breathe.

---

Start with the seal-things, because they are what you will see first, and because what you see first will mislead you.

You will see them feed at the vents and you will think: consumer. You will see them slide along the grooves and you will think: passenger. You will see them press flat against the stone and vanish and you will think: hidden. All of these are wrong, or not right enough, or right in the way that a single frame of a film is right—true for that instant, but missing the motion that gives the truth its meaning.

The seal-things are not feeding when they press their heads into the venting flow. Or they are feeding, but *feeding* here does not mean what you think. Watch one for long enough—not the dramatic moments, not the gathering at the vent, but the long stretches between, when it lies still in a groove with its eyes (if they are eyes) closed and its body flush against the stone and nothing seems to be happening at all—and you will begin to notice that the groove *changes* around it. The channel walls become slightly more defined. The polish becomes slightly more precise. The water that flows past the seal-thing flows slightly faster, slightly cleaner, with slightly less turbulent waste. The seal-thing is not riding in the groove. The seal-thing is *maintaining* the groove. It is re-machining, with its body, with the pressure of its skin and the chemistry of its secretions and the patient friction of its resting, the surface that the erosion is slowly degrading.

This is what they are. This is what all of them are. They are the mechanism's response to its own decay. They are the auto-correction that emerged, over millions of years, from the same process that created the mechanism itself—because the mechanism, once it reached sufficient precision, created a niche for anything that could maintain that precision, and the seal-things are what filled that niche. They eat the vent water not for calories, not for organic energy, but for *information*—the mineral content tells them where the stone is degrading, where the seals are failing, where the tolerance has drifted. And they go there. And they lie down. And they press themselves against the failing surface and they *correct* it, slowly, over hours or days, with the same patience that made the mechanism in the first place.

They are repair. They are living repair. They are what a machine grows when it has been running long enough to need one.

But they are not perfect. They are themselves products of the mechanism, and they are subject to the same erosion, the same drift. Their skin-grooves do not always match the stone-grooves anymore—not because they were made wrong, but because the stone has moved since they were born, since their mothers pressed them into the channels and their skin hardened into the machined pattern, and the pattern has shifted, and now there is a gap between what they are and what the stone needs them to be. The hesitation you see when they slide along the grooves—that is not uncertainty. That is *miscalibration*. That is a living repair system that no longer fits the thing it was designed—no, not designed—*grown* to repair.

And they know it. This is the part that is hardest to watch. They know they do not fit. They do not know it the way you know a mistake, with language and regret and the narrative of failure. They know it the way your body knows a misstep—the flinch before the fall, the lurch before the stumble, the deep animal certainty that something is wrong and there is nothing to be done about it except to continue and hope that the next placement is better. When a seal-thing hesitates in a groove, when it makes that small correction, there is a tightening in its body—a visible contraction, a ripple of something that passes through its hydraulics like a shudder—and you realize that what you are seeing is not mechanical adjustment. What you are seeing is *discomfort*. The mechanism's repair system is in pain because the mechanism is failing, and the failure is beyond the repair system's capacity to correct, and the repair system can feel this even though it has no brain you would recognize, no nervous system, no way to understand what it feels except to feel it.

They are in pain. All of them. All the time. The pain is slight—slight as the drift, slight as the widening of a groove by a fraction of a millimeter per century—but it is constant, and it is accumulating, and there is no healer for the healers. There is no mechanism-maintainer-maintainer. There is only the world, and the water, and the creatures pressing themselves against the failing stone and doing what they can, which is no longer enough.

---

The worms—the groove-liners, the filter-things, the translucent tubes that pack the channels—they are in better shape. Or they are in different shape. Their niche is more stable, because their niche is the water itself, and the water is the one thing in this world that has not changed. The water still flows. The water still carries the same minerals, the same dissolved chemistry, the same ancient signature of deep heat and patient stone. The water does not erode. The water *is* erosion—it is the agent, not the substrate—and agents do not wear themselves out in the same way.

So the worms fit. They fit exactly. They lie in their grooves and the water flows through them and they extract what they extract and they excrete what they excrete and the cycle continues without visible distortion. They are the healthiest part of the mechanism, the part that still works as it was grown to work.

But this means they are also the most *mechanical* part. The seal-things, despite their structural camouflage, are individuals—you can see it if you watch long enough, the slight variations in size and groove-pattern and feeding preference that distinguish one from another. The worms are not individuals. The worms are *tissue*. They are the lining of the mechanism's vessels, and they function as a lining—they grow, they die, they are replaced, and the replacement is identical to what came before because the groove has not changed and the water has not changed and there is no pressure on them to be anything other than what the channel requires.

Except. Except that in the places where the grooves are widening—and they are widening, everywhere, slowly, but faster in some places than others—the worms are *struggling*. Not individually. They cannot struggle individually; they have no individuality to struggle with. But the tissue itself is struggling. In the widened grooves, the water flow is turbulent, and the worms are shaped for laminar flow, and they flex and writhe and their whistling changes pitch and they cannot filter efficiently and they cannot maintain the smooth channel lining that the water requires, and the turbulent water erodes the groove faster, and the groove widens more, and the worms—

The worms are *thinning*. In the widened sections, they are not packing as densely. There are gaps between them. The water escapes through the gaps, bypassing the filter entirely, and the minerals that the worms would extract go past them, and the worms at the edges of the gap receive less, and they thin further, and the gap widens, and this is not a crisis—this is a process, this is the same kind of slow patient failure that the seal-things are experiencing—but it is a process with a visible direction, and the direction is *gone*. The worms in the widened grooves are going away. Not dying—dying implies an event, a moment, a transition. They are simply becoming less. Becoming sparser. Becoming unable to fill the space they were grown to fill. And the space they were grown to fill is becoming unable to hold them.

You kneel by a widened groove and you look into it and you see the worms—the translucent tubes, the filter-lining, the living tissue of the mechanism—and between them you see the bare stone underneath, and the bare stone is rough. Not machined. Not polished. Rough the way stone is rough when it has been eroded by turbulent water without the smoothing action of a living lining, and the roughness creates more turbulence, and the turbulence erodes more, and the worms thin more, and you are watching a feedback loop that has no brake, that has no correction, that has nothing in the mechanism's vast and auto-correcting system that can intervene because the mechanism's auto-correction depends on the worms and the worms are what is failing.

---

The air-things. The vapor-creatures. The streaks in the fog that might be living and might be weather and might be something for which there is no word.

They are the hardest to understand because they are the least bound. The seal-things are bound to the stone. The worms are bound to the water. The air-things are bound to the *phase boundary*—to the place where water becomes vapor, where liquid becomes gas, where the mechanism's exhaust becomes the mechanism's atmosphere. They live in the transition. They are made of the transition. They are what happens when condensation becomes so dense and so continuous that it starts to have opinions.

You cannot touch them. You have tried—or you will try; the impulse is unavoidable, the way you might reach for a soap bubble, for a snowflake, for anything that exists at the edge of not-existing. When your hand approaches an air-thing, it disperses. Not violently. Not with fear. It simply *spreads*, the way fog spreads when you breathe into it, and the particles of vapor that constituted the shape you saw become particles of vapor that constitute nothing, and they re-condense somewhere else, and the shape reforms, and it is the same shape—or a shape like the same shape—and it is watching you. You cannot tell how you know it is watching you. It has no eyes. It has no face. But it has *attention*, and the attention is directed at you, and it is not afraid, and it is not curious, and it is not any emotion you have a word for. It is simply *aware that you are there*, the way the water is aware of the groove—because you fit into its world, because you displace something, because you make a shape in the fog that was not there before.

The air-things are communicators. This is the speculation; this is the guess that forms after hours or days of watching; this is what the shape of their movement suggests but never proves. They trace patterns in the vapor—complex, repeating, varying—and the patterns seem to correspond to events in the mechanism below. When a vent sticks open, the air-things in the vicinity trace tight, agitated spirals. When the seal-things gather and feed, the air-things above them form broad, slow circles, like the condensation above a thermal. When the worms in a widened groove thin past some critical threshold, the air-things in the fog overhead *still*, becoming almost invisible, as if holding a breath they do not have.

They may be signaling. They may be a distributed sensory system—the mechanism's way of monitoring its own atmosphere, its own exhaust, its own slow decay. They may be nothing more than organized weather, self-sustaining patterns in the vapor that have no more agency than a standing wave. But you have stood in the fog and you have felt them pass close, and you have felt the cold wetness and the electrical tingle and the whisper of something that might be sound or might be meaning, and you cannot make yourself believe they are nothing. They are too specific. They are too responsive. They attend too carefully to a world that should not have anything in it worth attending to.

Or perhaps they are the opposite. Perhaps they are the mechanism's *memory*. Perhaps they are what happens when the same pattern repeats for long enough that the medium it repeats in begins to retain the impression, the way water retains the shape of a canyon it has flowed through for a million years. The air-things are the pattern of the mechanism's running, inscribed in the vapor that the running produces, repeated so many times that the repetition has become a thing in itself—a ghost made of humidity, a habit so ingrained it has grown a body.

If so, then the drift is killing them too. The pattern they are made of is changing. The rhythm is becoming less precise. The cycles are becoming irregular. The air-things trace their spirals and their circles and their stillnesses, but the spirals are slightly wrong, the circles are slightly irregular, the stillnesses come at the wrong times or last the wrong duration. They are memories of a mechanism that no longer exists—memories of precision, inscribed in a medium that cannot hold them accurately, repeating patterns that the world has drifted away from. They are nostalgic in a way that has nothing to do with emotion and everything to do with fidelity. They are copies of copies. They are what happens when a signal degrades.

---

And then there are the deep things.

You do not want to talk about the deep things. You do not want to think about the deep things. But they are there, in the pools, in the collected water that sinks into the flat stone and finds the low places and accumulates in silence, and they are moving, and they are large, and they do not come up.

The pools are not part of the mechanism. This is what makes them strange—stranger than the machined stone, stranger than the venting fissures, stranger than anything on the surface. The mechanism is running above them; the seal-things are sliding and the worms are filtering and the air-things are tracing and the whole packed system is performing its ancient, nearly-perfect function—but underneath, in the water that collects below the level of the grooves, below the level of the vents, below the level of the machined stone entirely, there is something else. Something that the erosion did not reach. Something that the water has not yet worn away. Something *un-machined*.

The deep things move in this un-machined space. They are slow—you can see that from the waves they push, the slow bulges that cross the pool surfaces like the breathing of something enormous and patient. They do not follow the grooves because there are no grooves. They do not feed at the vents because the vents do not reach them. They do not maintain anything because there is nothing to maintain—the un-machined stone is rough and irregular and has not been shaped into any functional form, and the deep things move through it without touching it, without changing it, without participating in the mechanism at all.

They are not part of the system. They are the *substrate*. They are what the mechanism is running on. They are the stone that has not yet been worn down, the rock that has not yet been machined, the raw material from which the precision was carved—and they are alive, or something like alive, something that moves and responds and *is* without being part of the function that everything above them serves.

What do they eat? What do they do? Why are they there? The pools give no answers. The water is dark and warm and mineral-heavy and it does not reflect light well enough to see below the first few centimeters, and the waves continue their slow crossing, and the seal-things avoid the pools, and the worms do not line them, and the air-things do not circle above them, and the mechanism simply *goes around* them, as if they are features of a landscape that has not yet been finished, as if they are the raw material waiting for the erosion to reach them, to wear them down, to machine them into grooves and channels and functional surfaces that the seal-things can maintain and the worms can line and the air-things can remember.

They are the future. This is the thought that comes to you, unbidden, unwanted, as you stand by the pool's edge and watch the slow waves and feel the hum of the mechanism in your teeth and your feet and your disoriented thoughts. They are the future. The mechanism is running now—running on the stone that has been machined, running on the precision that the erosion created—but the erosion has not stopped. The water is still flowing. The stone is still wearing. And underneath the machined surface, underneath the grooves and the vents and the living repair systems and the memories in the fog, there is more stone. Rough, unshaped, un-machined stone. And it is being worn. And it will be machined. And when it is machined, the mechanism will extend, and the precision will deepen, and the seal-things will have new surfaces to maintain, and the worms will have new channels to line, and the air-things will have new patterns to remember.

And the deep things—the things moving slowly in the pools, the un-machined things, the things that are not yet part of the system—they are what will be machined. They are the rough stone that the mechanism has not yet reached. They are the next phase. They are the erosion's next meal.

Or they are what is left when the meal is over. They are what the mechanism cannot wear down. They are the substrate that resists, the material that the water and the time and the patience cannot shape, and they move because they are alive in a way that the mechanism is not and can never be—not functional, not precise, not maintained, but *persistent*, stubbornly present, refusing to be ground into the shape that the world wants them to take.

You do not know which is true. The pools do not tell you. The slow waves continue, and the deep things move underneath them, and the mechanism hums and grinds and vents and slides and filters and remembers and slowly, slowly comes apart, and below it all there is something that may be raw material or may be resistance, and the difference between the two may be the difference between a world that is slowly dying and a world that is slowly becoming something else.

---

There is one more thing. You have been putting it off. You have been describing the inhabitants—seal-things, worms, air-things, deep things—because they are visible, because they are nameable, because they give you something to hold onto in a place that resists being held. But there is another inhabitant, and it is the one you do not want to name, because naming it makes it real, and making it real means accepting what it implies.

*You are an inhabitant now.*

Not because you chose to be. Not because you belong. But because you are here, in the mechanism, and the mechanism does not distinguish between the things that emerged from its process and the things that arrived from outside. The water flows over your skin. The vibration enters your bones. The humidity condenses on you and re-evaporates and re-condenses and you are part of the cycle now, part of the unending movement of water through the machined stone. You breathe the wet air and the air-things trace patterns around your head, curious or simply responsive, and your breath joins the vapor, and your heat joins the water, and your weight presses against the stone, and the stone feels you the way it feels a seal-thing—the way it feels pressure and warmth and the slight displacement of a body in a system that was not designed for bodies but has room for them anyway.

You are being *read* by the mechanism. The minerals in the vent water carry information about the stone's degradation, and the seal-things read it and go where they are needed. But your skin touches the stone, too, and your oils and your salts and your shed cells and your breath and your warmth are entering the water, and the water is carrying them into the grooves, and the worms are filtering them, and the air-things are tasting them, and the mechanism is processing you. It is incorporating you. It is wearing you down the way it wears everything down—not with violence, not with intent, but with the patient, ongoing, relentless pressure of a world that has been running for longer than you can imagine and will continue running after every atom of you has been dissolved and filtered and vented and condensed and worn into the grooves and pressed into the stone and made part of the precision or the imprecision or the slow, loud, nearly-perfect mechanism that will continue, that will always continue, that will continue until the erosion reaches the deep things, and the pools, and whatever is moving underneath them, and the world finds its next shape, and the next, and the next—

You press your hand to the weeping stone. You feel the hum. You feel the water pulsing around your fingers, warm then cold then warm. Somewhere, a vent sticks open. The steam screams. The seal-things scatter in a ripple of discomfort and miscalibration. The worms in the widened grooves whistle their thin, failing whistle. The air-things spiral and disperse and reform. The deep things move in their pools, slow, patient, waiting for the mechanism to reach them or to fail before it can.

And you are here. And the mechanism is running. And the erosion is continuing. And you are not the observer you thought you were. You are the observed. You are the worn. You are the water and the stone and the slow precision and the slower drift, and the sound is enormous and continuous and it is the sound of a world that does not know you are listening and does not care, because you are already part of it, already being processed, already on your way to becoming a groove, a channel, a living filter in a system that was never designed and never had a designer and never needed one because the patience was enough, the time was enough, the water was enough, and you are enough, and you are here, and the mechanism is running.

For now.
