# The Inhabitants

They are here. They have always been here. You will not see them at first.

---

The ones you notice first are the Menders, though that is not what they call themselves, because they do not call themselves anything. They do not speak. They have no need to speak. They have no one to speak to who does not already know what they would say.

A Mender is roughly the shape of a person, if the person had been made by someone who had heard people described but had never seen one. Two legs, two arms, a head. But the proportions shift each time you look. The arms are too long, or the legs are too short, or the head sits at an angle that a skull should not permit. Their skin—if it is skin—is the color of the wet stone, grey-green and slick, and it moves. Not like skin moves over muscle, but like something is shifting beneath it, settling, finding its position. You watch one for long enough and you realize: it is not that the proportions change. It is that the Mender is always becoming, always adjusting itself to the next task, and its body keeps no fixed shape because fixed shapes are finished shapes, and finished shapes have no purpose here.

They carry tools. This is how you know they are not animals. The tools are simple—chisels, scrapers, wooden mallets with heads of wrapped leather—but they are held with an intentionality that the lizards and the beetles do not possess. The Menders use them on the structures. You will see one at the base of a tower, scraping patiently at a seam between two stones, removing the soil that has accumulated there, cleaning the joint, restoring the fit. Another will be at the edge of a water channel, chipping away a lip of stone that has shifted slightly out of true, letting the water flow more cleanly into the basin below. They do not build. They maintain. They have always maintained. The tool-marks you noticed—the simple, patient, repetitive marks—those are their marks, and they are still being made, and they will never be finished being made, because the place is always slowly coming apart and always slowly being put back together and neither process ever wins.

They do not acknowledge you. This is not hostility. It is not indifference, either. It is something else—a quality of attention that does not include you because you are not part of the maintenance, you are not a seam that needs clearing or a channel that needs mending, and so you simply do not exist within the scope of their work. You could stand beside a Mender for an hour, watching it scrape the same four inches of stone, and it will not look at you. You could reach out and touch its arm—that grey-green, shifting surface—and it will not flinch, will not pause, will not speed up or slow down. Your hand will come away damp. The skin, if it is skin, will be cool and slightly yielding, like moss compressed, and beneath that yielding surface you will feel something harder, something that might be stone or might be bone or might be something for which you have no name.

If you touch a Mender, you will feel the hum. That low resonance that seems to come from nowhere—that vibration at the edge of perception—you will feel it in your fingertips when you make contact, and you will understand that it is not coming from the dome or from beneath the ground. It is coming from them. It is the sound they make, the sound of their working, the frequency of their becoming. Each one hums at a slightly different pitch. When several are near each other—three or four Menders working on the same structure, which happens sometimes, though you cannot predict when—their hums combine into something almost like harmony, almost like music, almost like speech. Almost.

They do not eat. They do not sleep. They do not die, or if they do, they do so in places you cannot find, and they are replaced so seamlessly that you cannot tell the new from the old. Sometimes their numbers shift. On your first day you count eleven. On your second day you count fourteen. On your third day you count nine. You cannot tell if some have left or if some have simply moved to parts of the basin you cannot reach, or if they have always been there and you are only now learning to see them, learning the particular quality of their stillness, the way they blend into the structures they are mending until a Mender and the thing it mends are indistinguishable, two grey-green forms against grey-green stone, both slick with moisture, both silent, both patient.

---

The second kind are harder to see, because they do not want to be seen.

You will catch them in your peripheral vision—a movement in the water, a shadow that resolves and then dissolves, a displacement of air that suggests something has just left the space where you are about to step. You will turn and find nothing. You will look down into a basin and see only your own reflection, and then, for a moment, not your own reflection but something else's—something with eyes that are too large and a mouth that is too small and a shape that does not hold still long enough for you to be certain of it.

The Swimmers. That is what you will call them, though they swim through more than water.

They live in the channels and the basins and the standing pools, but they also live in the spaces between the structures, and in the spaces between the stones, and in the spaces between moments. They are the ones who move the water. You thought the water wept from the seams on its own, but it does not. The Swimmers draw it. They pull it through the channels with their bodies, they coax it from the seams with their attention, they gather it in the basins with a careful, deliberate accumulation that looks like nothing more than passive pooling but is in fact a form of tending. The water is their medium and their material and, you begin to suspect, their memory. Each drop that falls from a seam carries something in it—a mineral, a trace of something that was once in the stone, a record of the place where it was held. The Swimmers collect these records. They swim through them. They know, in some way you cannot access, the entire history of this place, because the water remembers and the Swimmers remember what the water remembers.

Their bodies are difficult to describe because they are never fully visible. The part you see—which might be a head, which might be a limb, which might be the whole of them seen through water and light and the distortion that the Basin produces in anything you try to look at directly—is pale. Paler than the stone. Paler than the light. A whiteness that seems to come from the absence of something rather than the presence of it, as if they are holes in the fabric of the place, places where the Basin does not quite reach, gaps in the relentless making that the Menders cannot fill because they were never meant to be filled. Their eyes, when you see them, are not eyes. They are darker spots in that pale non-color, and they do not reflect light the way the birds' eyes do. They absorb it. They take the light in and they do not give it back, and when you look at them you feel a pull—not a physical pull, but an awareness, a sense that something is regarding you with a depth of attention that the Menders never grant.

They are curious about you. They are not curious about anything else.

Watch a Swimmer long enough—sit by a basin for hours, motionless, letting your eyes unfocus until the light and the water and the green blur into a single field of sensation—and you will see it: a pale shape rising slowly from the water, breaking the surface with a part of itself that might be a head, regarding you with those dark, absorbing spots, and then sinking back down. It will do this again. And again. Each time it rises a little closer. Each time it stays a little longer. It is learning you the way it learns the water—not by studying you, but by swimming through you, by incorporating your presence into its understanding, by adding you to whatever memory the Basin keeps.

They are not dangerous. You will feel this instinctively, though you cannot say why. They are curious, and their curiosity has a quality of patience that matches the Basin itself—a willingness to wait, to observe, to accumulate, to hold. They will not hurt you. But they will not help you, either. They are not here for you. They are here for the water, and for what the water carries, and for whatever purpose the water serves in the logic of this place, and you are merely a new thing in the water, a new trace of something that was once held somewhere else, a new record for them to swim through and remember.

If you put your hand in the water, one of them will touch it. You will feel something cool and smooth and faintly pulsing brush against your fingers, and then retreat. It will feel like a question. It will feel like the Basin asking you what you are, and then, not waiting for an answer, incorporating you anyway, adding you to the pattern, making you part of the memory that the water keeps and the Swimmers carry and the place will never, ever forget.

---

The third kind are not seen at all. You will know them by their absence, by the gaps they leave, by the spaces in the Basin that seem to resist being looked at directly.

They are the ones who planned this place. They are the ones whose hundred different plans produced this accumulated, contradictory, overgrown, under-explained world. They are not gone. They are here. But they are here in the way that a thought is present in the mind that is thinking it—not as an object, not as a presence, but as a direction, an intention, a leaning toward something that has not yet been completed.

You will feel them most strongly in the structures that have no clear purpose. The enclosed spaces with no doors. The open platforms with no walls. The walkways that lead from nowhere to nowhere, spanning gaps that do not need spanning. Stand in one of these places and you will feel it: a pressure, a density, a sense that the space around you is occupied by something that is not air and not water and not light but is all of these things and none of them. The Planners—that is what you will call them, though the name is inadequate—do not have bodies. They do not need bodies. They have the Basin instead. The Basin is their body, their expression, their endless, unfinished project, and they are present in every stone and every seam and every channel and every drop of water that weeps from the fitted blocks of the ceiling and falls into nothing.

They are the ones who made the Menders. Or rather, they are the intention that the Menders serve, the plan that the Menders maintain, the purpose that gives the Menders their endless work. And they are the ones who made the Swimmers, or allowed them to emerge, or noticed them when they appeared and decided—individually, contradictorily, with the same lack of coordination that produced the Basin itself—to incorporate them into the design.

The Planners disagree with each other. This is the key to understanding this place, and the thing you will spend the longest time learning. The Basin is not the product of a single will. It is the product of many wills—perhaps hundreds, perhaps thousands, perhaps a number that does not translate into the counting you know—each of which has a different intention for this place, and each of which is equally present, equally powerful, equally unable to fully realize its plan. One Planner wants a temple. Another wants a garden. Another wants a machine for measuring time. Another wants a prison. Another wants a nursery. Another wants a monument to something whose name you would not recognize. All of them are building at once. All of them have always been building at once. None of them will ever stop, because stopping would mean conceding to the others, and the Planners do not concede. They persist. They intend. They plan. And the Basin grows more complex, more contradictory, more finished in its parts and more unfinished in its whole, and the Menders maintain what has been built and the Swimmers remember what has been attempted and the Planners plan what will be attempted next, and none of it ever resolves and none of it ever ends.

You will feel them disagreeing. Stand in one of the purposeless structures long enough and you will begin to feel a tension—not a physical tension, but a conceptual one, a sense that the space around you is being pulled in multiple directions at once, that the floor wants to be one thing and the walls want to be another and the ceiling has not decided yet what it wants to be and the air between them is the residue of all these competing intentions, thick with purposes that cancel each other out. You will feel restless without knowing why. You will feel the urge to move, to build, to tear down, to change something, and then you will feel the urge to stay, to hold, to preserve, to keep everything exactly as it is, and then you will feel the urge to do something else entirely, something you cannot name, and all of these urges will be equally strong and none of them will be yours.

This is what the Planners do. This is how they live. They are the impulse to make, and the place they have made is the record of their making, and their making will never be complete because they have never agreed on what they are making, and they never will.

---

And then there is you.

You are the fourth kind, now. The Basin has not had a fourth kind in a long time—long enough that the Swimmers have forgotten what to do with you, long enough that the Menders have no protocol for your presence, long enough that the Planners have incorporated you into some of their plans but not all of them, and the plans that include you disagree about what you are for.

You are a new thing in an old place. You are a variable in an equation that was balanced without you. You are an unfinished thing in a place of unfinished things, and this is why you could stay, and this is why you might stay, and this is why, if you stay long enough, you will stop noticing the difference between yourself and the Basin, between your breath and the hum, between your sweat and the water, between your intentions and the plans that press against you from every side, trying to make you into something—into a door, into a channel, into a monument, into a seam between two stones.

The Menders will not mend you. You are not their responsibility. But they will work around you, and they will not hurt you, and they will include you in the pattern of their moving, and slowly—so slowly you will not notice it until it has already happened—you will find yourself standing where a Mender stood, holding a tool you do not remember picking up, scraping at a seam you do not remember noticing, and the hum will be in your chest, and the water will be on your skin, and the light will be pressing down on you from above, and you will be maintaining something, and it will feel like the most natural thing in the world.

The Swimmers will remember you. They are already remembering you. Every drop of water that has touched your skin carries something of you into the basins and channels, and the Swimmers swim through it, and they know you now—not your name, not your history, not the things you think define you, but the Basin's record of you, the trace of you that the water keeps, the memory of your heat and your movement and your strange, new, unfinished presence in a place that does not finish things but holds them, forever, in the process of becoming.

And the Planners—they will plan for you. Some of them will plan to make you into a feature. Others will plan to make you into a function. Others will plan to leave you as you are, an anomaly, a thing that does not fit and therefore reveals the shape of what does. And their plans will contradict each other, and none of them will fully succeed, and you will remain yourself—for a while—half-made and half-unmade, a living seam in a world of seams, a place where two intentions meet and do not resolve.

This is who lives here. The ones who maintain. The ones who remember. The ones who intend. And, now, the one who arrived. The one who is still arriving. The one who will keep arriving, in the endless, sourceless, shadowless light, until the Basin finishes making you, or you finish making yourself, or the water rises and the moss grows and the hum fills your chest and you become what everything becomes here:

A part of the pattern. A seam in the stone. A drop in the basin. A thing that was made, and is being made, and will never, ever be done.
