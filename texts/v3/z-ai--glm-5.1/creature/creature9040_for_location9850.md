# The Inhabitants

They are not born here. This is the first thing to understand, and the last thing they will tell you, because they do not think of it as worth mentioning. People arrive. They have always arrived. The city requires hands, and hands arrive, and the hands become people, and the people become the city, and the distinction erodes like the basalt—slowly, over durations that no one marks.

---

They come from places with horizons. This is important. They come from flat lands and open coasts and cities where you can see the edge of the built world from any high window, and they arrive in the Slow Extrusion carrying their horizon-expectations like a second skeleton, and for the first months they are wretched with it. The vertigo. The sky that is too large because the land is too high and there is nothing at the edges to hold the sky down. They press themselves against walls. They develop rituals of not-looking-up, of navigating by the corridors alone, of treating the open courtyards as ordeals to be crossed quickly with the eyes cast down at the warm stone.

And then they adjust. They all adjust. The ones who don't leave, and leaving is permitted—no one is held, the ridge-system has paths down, the world of horizons still exists—but most don't leave, because the adjustment happens and they don't notice it happening, and one morning they walk into a courtyard and look up at the excessive sky and feel nothing, and that is when they understand that they have become someone who lives here.

The locals—those who have been here longest, some for decades, a few who claim to have arrived before the upper construction tiers existed, which would make them very old or very liars, and no one cares which—they call this *settling*. As in: the stone has settled. As in: the dust has settled. As in: the argument is settled. It is a geological word for a human process, and they use it without irony, because here, geological words are just words.

---

Their bodies change.

Not in ways that are visible, not at first. But the heat does things to a person over time. The workers in the lower tiers—the machine-halls, the steam-wells, the factories that press and stamp and extrude—develop a flush that doesn't fade. The supervisor with the heat-reddened cheeks is not an exception; she is a preview. The capillaries in the face and hands dilate and stay dilated, and the skin takes on a color that visitors sometimes mistake for sunburn but which is actually a kind of low-grade inflammation, the body's constant negotiation with ambient warmth. They run hot. Their resting temperatures are a degree or two above the visitors', and their perspiration is continuous and light, a sheen rather than a sweat, the skin learning to breathe like the stone breathes, slowly and constantly and without fuss.

Their lungs thicken. The air in the lower tiers is wet and metallic and laced with mineral traces, and the alveoli adapt, building tissue that would alarm a physician from the flat lands but which functions here as it needs to function. The older workers breathe deep and slow, and they can hold their breath for durations that seem impossible, two minutes, three, walking through a corridor filled with vented steam without gasping, because their blood holds oxygen differently, or because they have learned that gasping is not necessary, or because they have simply forgotten that air is something to be urgent about.

Their hands change most of all. The people of the Slow Extrusion have hands that are worth looking at, if they let you—which they do, because they do not think of their hands as remarkable. The palms are calloused in specific patterns: a ridge across the base of the fingers from gripping tools, a pad below the thumb from pressing the rubbery compound into joints, a thickening on the side of the index finger from the particular angle at which they hold the measuring-instruments. But the fingertips are something else. The fingertips are *sensitive*. They have to be. The work here requires the ability to read temperature by touch—to distinguish the safe-warm of a stone floor from the dangerously-hot of a pipe housing from the living-warmth of the routing—and this sensitivity develops over years until a long-term resident can press a fingertip to a wall and tell you which corridor the steam is moving through on the other side, and how fast, and whether the pipe is corroding.

These hands: rough and refined at once. Worker's hands. Surgeon's hands. The hands of people who live inside a machine and must feel its pulse.

---

They do not have families in the way that horizon-people have families.

This is not a rule. It is an observation. The residential chambers are assigned by need and by availability, and they shift as the city shifts—a corridor completed here, a habitation block repurposed there—and the people move with the chambers, and the people who share a wall share a life, and the boundaries of kinship form along the lines of the architecture rather than the lines of blood. A woman who has lived adjacent to a man for eight years will call him *wall-kin*, and this means something specific: that they are responsible for the maintenance of the shared partition, that they monitor each other's noise-levels, that they have developed a schedule for the use of the single steam-vent that heats both their chambers. It is a practical word. It is also, over time, a tender one.

Children arrive with their parents, sometimes, or they are born here to parents who arrived pregnant or who became pregnant in the ordinary way, and the children are raised by whoever is adjacent. This is not neglect. This is architecture. The corridors are the village. The machine-halls are the schools. A child in the Slow Extrusion learns to read the routing before they learn to read text, because the routing is everywhere and text is a visitor's technology, useful but not essential. A child here knows which sounds the machines make when they are functioning correctly and which sounds mean that someone should be notified, and they know this the way that horizon-children know the calls of birds—automatically, contextually, without being taught.

The children do not play in the way that horizon-children play. They *practice*. They carry small tools and they measure things and they report their measurements to adults who listen with elaborate seriousness, and this is their play, and it is also their work, and the distinction does not exist for them because it does not exist for anyone here. The city produces. The children are learning to produce. The production has no clear end-product—those plates, those warehouses, those machines whose purpose has been forgotten—but the producing is the point, or it has become the point, or it was always the point and the purpose was just a story that the original builders told themselves to justify the doing.

---

They are not unhappy.

Visitors often mistake the quiet for sadness, the slowness for resignation, the integration of work and life for exploitation. But the people of the Slow Extrusion are not sad. They are not resigned. They are not exploited, or if they are, the word has no purchase here, because there is no one to exploit them—no owner, no overseer, no distant authority collecting the products of their labor. The plates accumulate. The warehouses fill. The city continues. No one benefits. No one is harmed. The work is simply what happens here, the way that heat rises and steam condenses and the routing follows the pipes.

They have pleasure. It is a specific pleasure: the satisfaction of a corridor completed, a pipe rerouted, a joint resealed. It is the pleasure of maintenance, of continuation, of being a small part of a process that extends beyond any individual lifespan. They take breaks. They sit in the upper courtyards in the thin air and look at the excessive sky and feel the vertigo that never quite goes away, and they talk, softly, about the work and the routing and the small dark things in the upper corridors that they feed with mineral water, and sometimes they talk about the places they came from, the flat lands, the horizons, but these stories have a distant quality, as if they are describing dreams they once had, or films they once watched, or lives that belonged to someone else entirely.

They have humor. It is dry and specific and often concerned with the behavior of steam, which is unpredictable enough to be funny if you have spent enough time predicting it. They have arguments, conducted in whispers, about the proper maintenance schedule for a particular machine or the correct interpretation of a supervisor's manual or the question of whether the routing is moving into a new corridor faster than it should. They have friendships, formed through shared walls and shared work and the gradual accumulation of small kindnesses—a dish of mineral water left at a door, a note about a failing pipe slipped under a partition, an adjustment made to a vent so that the warmth reaches a neighbor's chamber more evenly.

They have love. It is patient. It operates on geological time. A man and a woman who have been wall-kin for twelve years may request adjacent chambers in a new corridor, and the request will be granted because the chamber-assignment process is practical and does not inquire about motivations, and they will live side by side for another twelve years, and they will share a steam-vent, and they will monitor each other's noise-levels, and one night, in the deep quiet, one of them will press a hand to the shared wall and feel the warmth of the other's presence on the other side, and this will be enough. Or it will not be enough, and one of them will request a transfer, and the transfer will be granted, and they will become wall-kin with someone else, and the city will continue.

---

They know things that horizon-people do not.

They know that the earth is not solid. They know this not as an abstraction, not as a fact read in a book, but as a daily experience—the slight shift in a corridor floor, the new crack in a wall, the sudden appearance of a fumarole where there was not one before. The ridge-system is still becoming, and they are becoming with it, and this means that their knowledge is not static but growing, changing, adjusting, like the city itself.

They know the routing. Not in the sense that they can explain it—no one can explain it, the chemists have tested it and the biologists have sampled it and the results are always the same: not fungal, not bacterial, not mineral, not any category that makes sense—but in the sense that they live with it. They know that it grows toward heat. They know that it dies back when the pipes are moved. They know that if you press your hand to it, it is warm, warmer than it should be, and if you hold your hand there long enough, you can feel something that might be a pulse or might be your own pulse reflected back at you or might be nothing at all. They do not disturb it. This is not superstition. This is pragmatism. The routing does not harm the stone. The routing does not clog the pipes. The routing exists, and they exist, and the city exists, and this is sufficient.

They know the things in the upper corridors. They know them the way that horizon-people know the wildlife of their regions—not with intimacy, but with coexistence. The small dark things are quick and legged and they live in the spaces between the walls, the gaps that are too small for human passage but which honeycomb the upper construction, and they are drawn to the mineral water that the residents leave in dishes, and they are content, or the residents believe they are content, because a content thing in the walls does not gnaw on the pipes or nest in the vents or startle the workers. The residents do not name them. Naming implies a relationship that no one wants to formalize. They are *them*. They are *the ones in the upper corridors*. They are fed and left alone, and this arrangement works, and has worked for as long as anyone can remember, which is not very long, because the residents do not keep records of such things, because the things that matter here are the things that repeat, and feeding the small dark things repeats, and so it does not need to be written down.

---

They do not wonder about the purpose of the city.

This is the detail that horizon-people find most disturbing, more disturbing than the routing or the unclear machines or the absence of plants. The horizon-people want to know: what is this place for? Who built it? Where do the products go? Why does the press in Sublevel 7 continue to stamp plates that no one uses? Why does the city continue to grow, slowly, into stone that has not yet finished being stone, when there is no clear need for new corridors, no population pressure, no external demand?

The residents do not wonder because wondering is a horizon-activity. Wondering requires the expectation of an answer, and answers here are temporary, geological, subject to the same slow shifts as the foundations. The city produces. This is what it does. The residents assist. This is what they do. The products accumulate or they don't. The machines run or they don't. The routing grows or it doesn't. The city stands or it doesn't. The residents do not control these outcomes. They participate in them. They are part of the process—the human part, the part that measures and cuts and maintains and adjusts—and the process does not require their understanding, only their hands.

And their hands are sufficient. Their sensitive, calloused, heat-flushed hands. Their hands that can read the pulse of the pipes through stone walls. Their hands that press the rubbery compound into joints and feel it flex and warm beneath their palms. Their hands that leave dishes of mineral water in the corners of corridors and that do not touch the routing and that measure the progress of new corridors in millimeters per day.

Their hands are the city's hands. The city is their body. This is not a metaphor. This is an architecture.

---

When a resident dies—and they die, here, in the ordinary way, though perhaps less often than horizon-people die, because the city is warm and the work is steady and there are no vehicles and no predators and the only danger is the heat, and the residents have learned to respect the heat as a horizon-person might respect the sea—their chamber is reassigned within days. This is not callousness. This is continuation. The chamber is a cell in the body of the city, and the city requires occupancy, and a new resident arrives—there are always new residents, arriving from the flat lands with their horizon-expectations and their wretched vertigo—and they settle into the chamber and they learn the walls and they learn the neighbors and they learn the work, and they adjust, and they don't stop adjusting, and one morning they walk into a courtyard and look up at the excessive sky and feel nothing, and they have become someone who lives here.

And the city continues. Slowly. Extruding. Rising with the ridge-system into whatever it is becoming. Producing whatever it is producing. Growing routing along its pipes and accumulating plates in its warehouses and feeding the small dark things in its upper corridors. And the people who live here—who are not born here but who die here, most of them, because leaving is permitted and staying is easy and the adjustment, once it happens, is irreversible—they continue with it. They are the human component of a process that has no name and no purpose and no end, and they are content, or something adjacent to content, something that functions as content in a place where the distinction between contentment and simply-continuing has been worn away by the slow heat and the slow time and the slow, persistent, patient becoming of stone.

They are not unhappy. They are not happy. They are *present*, in the way that the routing is present and the steam is present and the basalt is present—completely, without reservation, without the expectation of anything else. And if there is something in that presence that the horizon-people recognize and cannot name—something that looks, from a distance, like peace—they do not say so, because saying so would require a word that the residents do not use, and the residents do not use it not because they are unfamiliar with it but because they do not need it, because the thing it describes is not a state to be achieved but a condition to be inhabited, and they inhabit it, and the city inhabits it, and the heat rises, and the steam turns the turbines, and the work continues.
