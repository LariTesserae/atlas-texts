What lives here now is what has always lived in places where people have left: the small, the patient, the overlooked.

Lichen, first. It was here before the walls went up and it remained when the walls came down. The grey-green crusts on the lower courses are *Lecanora*, probably, or something close — those round fruiting bodies like tiny volcanoes, erupting in slow motion over decades. The rust-orange patches are *Xanthoria*, nourished by the nitrogen in bird droppings, which means the birds came first, or the lichen waited for the birds, or they arrived together in some ancient arrangement that neither remembers. The lichen does not grow quickly. A centimeter a year, perhaps less. Some of these colonies have been spreading since before the last person walked out, and they will still be spreading when the last wall falls.

The grasses are harder to name. They are not the soft grasses of lowland meadows but the tough, clumping species that hold ground against wind — *Festuca*, maybe, or *Nardus*, the kind that livestock reject because the blades fold inward and become sharp. They grow in the crevices between rocks where moisture lingers and soil has accumulated in the cracks, and they grow in the depressions where the paths were, because the packed earth retains water differently than the surrounding ground. This means the people's passage still affects how things grow here. The dead continue to shape the living, just a little, just at the margins.

The creeper — the small-leaved thing that holds the path edges — is probably a kind of *Thymus*. Wild thyme, adapted to thin soil and exposure. If you knelt and pressed your face to it, you might still catch its scent on a warm afternoon, though no one does. The insects know it. Small bees, solitary species that do not form hives, visit the flowers in the brief weeks when they bloom. There are other insects too: beetles that live in the soil beneath the walls, flies that ride the wind and land on the grass stems, midges that rise in still air in dense clouds that the birds sometimes take advantage of. These are the settlement's largest population, and the least noticed.

The birds — the small dark ones that pass through in twos and threes — are pipits, most likely. Meadow pipits, which nest on the ground in the moorland below and come up to the ridge to feed on the insects that the wind carries. They do not nest here. The ridge offers no concealment, no cover from the wind, and their ground nests would be exposed to anything that walked the open ground. But they know the walls. They use the stones as perches, as vantage points, and sometimes they shelter in the corners where two walls meet, the same corners where the vegetation thickens. In this they are like the people who built the walls: seeking refuge where they can find it.

There are other birds too, though they are less visible. Raptors cross the ridge at altitude — buzzards or hen harriers, using the updraft from the steep side to gain height with minimal effort. They do not stop. Sometimes a raven passes, solitary and deliberate, and its call carries in the wind. Ravens remember places. This ridge may be a landmark in some raven's mental map, a way of navigating the moorland, though no raven nests here either.

At night, when the wind drops, the small mammals emerge. Field voles, most likely, working their runs along the base of the walls where the soil has accumulated and the grass grows thickest. They are the reason the birds of prey pass overhead — the ridge supports a population of voles that in turn supports a population of hunters that never touch the ground. The voles are the connective tissue between the settlement and the wider landscape. They come up from the moorland, following the walls because the walls offer cover, and they eat the grasses and the roots of the creeper, and they are eaten in turn. The walls that were built to shelter people now shelter voles. The paths that were worn by human feet are now maintained by the passage of small bodies moving in the dark.

There may be a fox. Not a resident — a fox would not live on the ridge full time, there is not enough prey — but a visitor, traveling its territory at night, checking the vole runs along the walls, using the paths that the people made because they are still the easiest way to move across the uneven ground. The fox would know the ridge as part of a larger circuit: the stream below for water, the moorland for rabbits, the ridge for voles and for the view it provides. A fox on the ridge at dawn would be visible for a long way. A fox would know this and would be gone before the light was full.

The most important thing that lives here, though, is the wind. Not as metaphor. The wind is a physical presence that shapes everything — the growth of the plants, the movement of the animals, the erosion of the walls, the presence or absence of moisture, the temperature, the sound. The ridge exists as it does because the wind exists as it does. If the wind stopped, everything would change. The grasses would grow differently. The insects would behave differently. The fog would linger rather than lifting. The walls would weather more slowly but would also become home to different plants, different creatures, different processes. The wind is the dominant organism here, if organism is the word — the force that determines what else can live, and how, and where.

And the people? They do not live here anymore. They left, or they died, or they gradually moved away over generations until the last one closed the gap in the wall behind them and walked down the path to the stream and kept walking. But they are present in the way that absence can be present. The walls they built create microclimates where specific things grow. The paths they walked are still the easiest routes across the ridge. The stones they piled at the corners provide shelter for insects and basking spots for whatever small cold-blooded creatures might venture up here on rare warm days. The grinding stone, broken and abandoned, collects rainwater in its concavity, and that water supports a different community of tiny organisms than the surrounding ground. The people shaped this place, and the shape persists, and the things that live here now live in and around and through that shape.

They were practical, the people who built here. They used what the ridge offered — stone that split along its bedding planes, soil for chinking, the slope of the ground for drainage. They spaced their structures far enough apart that a fire in one would not spread to the next. They built windbreaks and animal pens, which means they had animals — sheep, probably, or goats, something that could graze the moorland and provide milk and wool and meat. They walked twenty minutes to get water and twenty minutes back, which means they were not infirm, or they had children who could make the journey, or they collected rainwater in ways that have left no trace. They ground grain or seeds on the grinding stone, which means they had access to something to grind, whether cultivated or gathered. They needed to see, or to be seen, or to not be surprised. They needed each other, but not too close. They chose air and light over convenience, and they stayed long enough for their paths to wear into the ground, which is longer than a season, longer than a year.

What they did not leave was a story. No grave markers, no shrines, no inscriptions. No metal, no pottery, nothing that would survive the way stone survives. This does not mean they had no stories — it means their stories were the kind that are told, not the kind that are written. Their dead were buried or burned or left somewhere that is not here. Their important objects were taken when they left, or they were made of materials that the ridge consumed. What remains is the infrastructure of daily life: walls, paths, the arrangement of space. The skeleton of habit, stripped of its flesh.

So what lives at Ladhàin? Grasses and lichen and creeping thyme. Insects and voles and passing birds. The occasional fox, the frequent wind. Microbes in the soil, in the water that pools in the grinding stone, in the damp corners of the walls. Fungi beneath the surface, threading through the thin soil, breaking down what was once alive into what will nourish what comes next. All of these, together, in a configuration that was set by people who are gone and that shifts now only in the slow ways that abandoned places shift: growth, erosion, settling, the patient work of time on stone.

It is not empty. It has never been empty. It is full of the kind of life that does not require notice, the kind that was always here and will be here long after the walls are gone. The people were visitors. The ridge remembers them, in its way — in the shape of the walls and the depth of the paths and the accumulated soil at the bases of the stones. But the ridge does not mourn them. The ridge is not that kind of place.
