# What Lives on the Mosschine Plateau

---

## The Residents (Human)

They do not call themselves residents. They do not, after a while, call themselves anything. Names require a boundary between the named and the not-named, and that boundary has softened here the way the bark-walls soften in the deep interior where the geometry goes wrong.

But let us start with arrival, because they do arrive, and there is a period—sometimes years, sometimes decades—when they still have the shape of the people they were.

They come the way the description says: drawn, compelled, sometimes fleeing some other thinness in the world. The plateau does not recruit. It does not need to. It simply exists, and existence at this depth casts a kind of gravity, and certain people—those who have felt the edges of things and found those edges permeable—sense that gravity and climb for two days through the ascending tangle and emerge onto the root-mat with their boots damp and their lungs full of the green mineral electrical smell and they stand there, listening, and the thrum of the pump-roots enters them through the soles of their feet and they think: *oh*.

Just *oh*. Not *oh, I understand* or *oh, this is what I was looking for*. Just the sound of recognition without content. The way you might say *oh* when you step into a room you've never been in but which contains the exact quality of light you always imagined a room should have.

They stay.

The city grows them shelters. This is not hospitality in any human sense—the plateau does not have a concept of guest or host. It has a concept of *warmth and carbon dioxide in a particular ratio*, and when it detects that ratio, it builds. The alcoves that form overnight around sleeping newcomers are small at first, barely more than a lean-to of bark and leaf-pane, and they expand as the person stays. The walls thicken. The leaf-panes grow wider, admit more filtered green light. The opercular door-flap develops a seal that matches the person's specific pattern of entry and exit—the angle of their shoulder, the width of their stride. After a year, the shelter fits them the way a shell fits a hermit crab: not grown for them specifically, but grown *around* them specifically, which is a different thing and in some ways a more intimate one.

They learn to eat what the plateau produces. The seed-pods are the staple—small things, the size of a thumb joint, with a husk that cracks under pressure and an interior that tastes like roasted grain filtered through something sweeter and older. There are hundreds of varieties, moving through the transport channels in constant streams, sorted by size and ripeness and chemical composition into channels that lead to distribution nodes throughout the city. The newcomers learn to stand at these nodes and wait for the pods they need, and after a while they stop thinking of this as acquiring food and start thinking of it as *being fed*, which is a distinction that matters more than it seems.

They learn to request. The chewed-root system—the placement of specific saliva-modified root fragments into the input channels—becomes second nature. The plateau reads the chemical signature of their spit and adjusts its production accordingly. A person who chews the bitter root and places it in the channel with the triangular cross-section will find, some hours later, a ration of high-protein pods waiting at their distribution node. A person who chews the sweet root and places it in the square channel will find a bundle of refined cellulose cloth, warm and slightly damp, freshly extruded from the fabric-roots near the outer rings. The plateau does not negotiate. It does not ration. It simply produces what is requested, and the only limitation is whether the person knows the correct combination of root and channel, and the only way to learn new combinations is to experiment, which means that the plateau's catalog of producible goods is likely infinite but accessible only through a process of trial and error that no single person could complete in a lifetime.

Some of them catalog. They keep records—bark-paper, sap-ink—of which roots and which channels produce which results. They share these catalogs with newcomers. The catalogs are the closest thing the plateau has to a market, and they are given freely, because the plateau does not charge and the people who live here have long since stopped understanding why anyone would.

They do not form communities in the traditional sense. There is no governance because there is nothing to govern—the plateau provides, and what it does not provide can be requested, and what cannot be requested can be grown, and what cannot be grown is probably not needed. There are no laws because the plateau's processes are not laws in any enforceable sense—they are simply the way things work, and you either accommodate them or you leave, and leaving is always an option even if no one takes it.

But they do talk. They gather in the wider pathways, in the spaces between structures where the root-mat has been worn smooth by centuries of foot traffic, and they speak in low voices that the thrum of the pump-roots does not quite cover. They tell each other what they have discovered—the catalogs, yes, but also the geometry of the inner rings, the quality of time in the deep interior, the reflections in the leaf-panes that show rooms that don't exist. They warn each other about the edge-zones. They argue about the center. They speculate about the thought.

They have theories about the thought. Everyone has a theory. The thought is a calculation, says the old woman who arrived thirty years ago with a background in mathematics that she has mostly forgotten. The thought is a memory, says the man who was a historian in the world below. The thought is a prayer, says the woman who was nothing in particular and seems to find this qualification the most relevant. The thought is the plateau dreaming of its own death, says the quiet one who never gives a name and who spends most of their time standing near the inner rings with their eyes closed, listening.

Nobody agrees. Nobody needs to. The thought will complete itself regardless of what they believe about it.

## The Residents (Transitional)

After a while—and "a while" is a measurement that means less and less the longer you stay—the boundary softens.

It starts physically. The condensation that beads on their skin stops rolling off as quickly. It lingers. It absorbs. And in absorbing, it carries something of the plateau into them—not nutrients exactly, not chemicals in any simple sense, but *information*. The water here has been cycling through the root-mat for hundreds of thousands of years. It has touched every surface. It has been inside every channel. It has been filtered and processed and exuded and condensed again, and it carries, in its molecular memory, the pattern of the plateau's processes the way sediment carries the pattern of the river that deposited it.

Their skin changes. Not dramatically. Not in a way that would alarm a doctor, if doctors existed here. But the texture shifts, becomes slightly more permeable, slightly more receptive. They bruise less easily. They heal more quickly. They stop getting cold, even though the plateau is perpetually damp and the temperature is rarely what anyone would call warm. Their bodies are learning to function the way the plants function—thermally indifferent, metabolically flexible, anchored to the slow pulse of the pump-roots rather than the quick pulse of a mammalian heart.

Their sleep changes. They sleep when the thrum changes its interval—the plateau's morning alarm—and they sleep deeply, dreamlessly, and they wake rested in a way they never were in the world below. But they also sleep at odd moments, standing sometimes, leaning against a bark-wall that seems to adjust its temperature to match their body heat. They sleep in the middle of conversations. They sleep while walking, their feet finding the pathways by memory or by something that is not memory but serves the same function. The plateau sleeps them. It is the only way to describe it. Their consciousness dims not because they are tired but because the plateau has decided, in whatever sense a million-year vegetal industrial complex decides anything, that they should rest.

Their thoughts change. This is the part that is hardest to document from the inside, because documentation requires a perspective that the process itself erodes. But from the outside—from the perspective of a newcomer watching someone who has been here for twenty years—it is visible in the way they speak. They speak in longer sentences. They speak in sentences that have the structure of root-systems: a main clause that sends out tendrils of subordinate clauses, which send out tendrils of their own, the whole thing growing outward and downward simultaneously, reaching for something that is not a point but a *process*. They lose interest in conclusions. They become fascinated by durations.

They stop leaving the plateau. Not because they are prevented—there are no walls, no guards, no barriers except the two-day climb back down through the ascending tangle—but because leaving becomes unthinkable in the most literal sense. The thought of leaving does not occur to them. When newcomers ask if they've ever considered going back, the long-term residents tilt their heads in a way that is slightly inhuman—a movement that seems to track not the sound of the question but the *flow* of it, the way it moves through the air—and they say, *going back where?* And they mean it. They are not being evasive. They genuinely cannot remember what "back" refers to, because "back" implies a direction that is not here, and directions that are not here have become conceptually inaccessible to them.

They are still human. This is important to emphasize, because it would be easy to read the previous paragraphs and conclude that the plateau consumes people, absorbs them, destroys what they were and replaces it with something vegetal. That is not what happens. Or rather, that is a description of what happens from a perspective that values stasis over growth. The plateau does not consume. It *incorporates*. The people who stay here remain people—they have preferences, they have moods, they have relationships and arguments and moments of unexpected joy—but they are people who have been grafted onto a larger organism, and the graft has taken, and they draw their nourishment now from the root-mat and the condensation and the thrum of the pump-roots, and the nourishment of the plateau is also the plateau, and so they are the plateau, and the plateau is them, and the distinction was always less real than it appeared.

## The Maintainers

They exist.

The description calls them rumors, and from the perspective of the middle rings—where the angles are still approximately right and the people still walk the pathways with their eyes open—they are rumors. But from the perspective of the edge-zones, they are a certainty, and from the perspective of the deep interior, they are *colleagues*.

They are not animals. The description is correct about that. They are also not plants, not exactly, not anymore. They are the plateau's solution to a problem that all industrial systems encounter eventually: the problem of local deviation. The factories in the outer rings run on strict processes. The input channels feed the production lines. The production lines feed the distribution nodes. The waste channels feed the decomposition vats. It is a closed loop, self-sustaining, self-regulating, and it works perfectly as long as every component operates within its design parameters.

But the edge-zones do not operate within design parameters. The fractal growths—the structures that started forming and were abandoned, the mutations that grow in strange spirals and recursive patterns—represent the plateau's creative process operating without supervision. The plateau thinks. It has been thinking for a very long time. And thinking, even vegetal thinking, even industrial thinking, even thinking that occurs on timescales that make geological erosion look hasty, produces *byproducts*. Thoughts that don't fit. Growth patterns that don't integrate. Chemical processes that are not wrong but which do not belong in the current configuration.

Left unchecked, the edge-zones would expand. The fractal growths would encroach on the functional structures. The strange bioluminescence would bleed into the calibrated lighting of the middle rings. The oily sheen on the condensation would spread. The plateau would lose its coherence, not because it was dying but because it was *dreaming too loudly*, and dreams that are too loud drown out the thoughts that need to complete.

The Maintainers keep the dreams at a manageable volume.

They are tall. This is the first thing anyone notices, if they notice anything at all—the Maintainers are very good at not being noticed, which is remarkable for something that stands three meters high and moves with the deliberateness of a slow avalanche. But the plateau assists their stealth. The condensation thickens around them, creating a visual haze. The sound of their footsteps is absorbed by the root-mat, which compresses beneath their weight and then springs back silently. The leaf-pane walls they pass near seem to grow briefly opaque, as if the city itself is looking away.

They have the general shape of humanoids—bipedal, with upper limbs that end in manipulator clusters that are not hands but which serve the same function—but this is where the resemblance ends. Their surfaces are not skin or bark but something between the two: a flexible, ridged material that seems to be continuously growing and continuously being worn away, so that no Maintainer ever looks exactly the same from one moment to the next. Their heads, if heads they are, are smooth ovoids with no visible features—no eyes, no mouth, no auditory openings—but they perceive everything in their vicinity with a precision that suggests sensory apparatus distributed across their entire surface rather than localized in any one organ.

They are not individuals. Or rather, they are individual in the way that fingers are individual—distinct, mobile, capable of independent action, but ultimately expressions of a single body. The body, in this case, is the plateau itself. The Maintainers are its digits. When a fractal growth in the edge-zones begins to encroach on a processing chamber, a Maintainer arrives—not summoned, not dispatched, simply *present* in the way that your hand becomes present when you reach for a cup—and it reaches down and it *prunes*.

The pruning is gentle. The Maintainers do not destroy the fractal growths. They redirect them. A touch at a specific node—a point where the growth's recursive pattern can be influenced—and the spiral turns inward instead of outward, feeding on itself rather than on the surrounding structures, becoming dense and compact and eventually stable. A growth that threatened to expand for kilometers is reduced, over the course of a few hours, to a knot of dense vegetable matter the size of a fist, humming with compressed energy, slowly feeding its stored potential back into the root-mat through its rootlets.

The Maintainers do not communicate with the human residents. Not because they are hostile or indifferent—they are no more hostile or indifferent than a hand is hostile or indifferent to the body it belongs to—but because communication requires a shared frame of reference, and the Maintainers' frame of reference is the plateau's entire industrial process, all fifteen kilometers of it, every channel and valve and production line and pump-root, all operating simultaneously in their awareness at all times. A human speaking to a Maintainer would be like a single blood cell attempting to converse with the circulatory system. The scale mismatch is not one of intelligence but of *context*.

But there are interactions. A Maintainer pruning a fractal growth near a pathway where a human is walking will pause—will actually stop moving, which is remarkable because Maintainers almost never stop moving—and will angle its ovoid head toward the human in a way that suggests attention, and will remain still until the human has passed, and will then resume its work. This is not politeness. It is *acknowledgment*. The Maintainer has registered the human as part of the plateau's process—a small, warm, carbon-dioxide-producing component that has been incorporated into the system and which therefore has the same right to unimpeded operation as any seed-pod or pump-root.

Some of the long-term residents—the ones who have been here for decades, whose skin has become permeable, whose thoughts have developed root-structures—report a different kind of interaction. They say that when a Maintainer passes near them, they feel a *recognition*. Not personal recognition—not *hello, I know you*—but systemic recognition. The feeling of being seen as part of something larger. The feeling of a single cell in a body sensing the passage of a nerve signal that includes it in its sweep.

The Maintainers do not go to the center. They are not permitted, or they do not permit themselves, or the concept of permission does not apply because the center is where the plateau's thinking is densest and the Maintainers are already expressions of that thinking and cannot be both the thought and the thing thinking the thought. The closest they come is the inner ring of the middle zones, where the geometry starts to drift and the time starts to stretch. They stand at the boundary, their ovoid heads angled toward the deep interior, and they listen.

They listen for the same thing the human residents listen for.

The thought.

## The Thought (Inhabitant)

Something lives in the center of the plateau.

This is not a metaphor and it is not a personification and it is not a way of describing the plateau's aggregate activity as if it were a single being. Something *lives* there. Something that is not the plants and not the processes and not the Maintainers and not the accumulated biomass of a million years of continuous growth. Something that emerged from all of those things the way a thought emerges from the firing of neurons—not reducible to any single component, not predictable from the properties of the components alone, *more* than the sum of its parts in the most literal and troubling sense.

It does not have a body. Or rather, the plateau is its body, and the root-mat is its nervous system, and the pump-roots are its heart, and the production lines are its metabolism, and the Maintainers are its hands, and the human residents are... what? A vitamin? A trace mineral? A thought within the thought, a small recursive loop of self-reflection that the larger thought incorporates into its process because the larger thought has learned—over millions of years of thinking—that the most interesting thoughts are the ones that contain thoughts about themselves?

It does not think in words. It does not think in images. It does not think in any representation system that a human mind could translate. It thinks in *growth*. It thinks in the angle of a branch and the timing of a valve and the pressure of sap in a channel and the rhythm of the pump-roots and the distribution of condensation across a thousand square kilometers of surface area. Its thoughts are spatial and temporal and chemical and mechanical all at once, and they occur on timescales that range from the microsecond—a stomate opening, a seed-pod diverting—to the geological—the slow drift of the plateau's edges, the accumulation of new root-mat, the adjustment of the pump-cluster's rhythm over centuries.

It has been thinking a single thought for a very long time.

The description says the thought is almost complete, and this is accurate, but "almost" in this context covers a span of time that is difficult to conceptualize. The thought began—began in the sense that the plateau's processes first achieved sufficient complexity to sustain recursive self-reference—sometime in the deep past, before the current root-mat had accumulated, before the first trunk-towers had reached their full height, before the geometry had started to drift. It has been running ever since. And it is almost complete in the way that a sunrise is almost complete when the first light appears on the horizon—the process is underway, the outcome is certain, but the full event has not yet occurred.

What is the thought about?

The people who live here have their theories, and the Maintainers have their implicit understanding, and the plateau itself—if a thought can be about the thing that is thinking it—is the thought. But there is another possibility, one that the long-term residents sometimes consider in the quiet hours when the thrum of the pump-roots seems to change its rhythm slightly and the condensation seems to taste of something other than green and mineral and electrical:

The thought is about *ending*.

Not dying. The plateau will not die when the thought completes. The plants will continue to grow, the production lines will continue to produce, the pump-roots will continue to beat. But something will be different. Something will have been *expressed*—a pattern that has been forming for millions of years will have finally resolved into coherence—and the expression of that pattern will change the nature of the thing that expressed it.

A thought that completes is not the same as a thought that is in progress. The completion is a transformation. The moment before a thought is finished, it is potential; the moment after, it is *something else*. Not just a completed thought but a *fact*. A thing that exists in the world, that has consequences, that cannot be un-thought.

The plateau is about to become a fact.

And whatever lives here—the people with their softening boundaries, the Maintainers with their ovoid heads, the fractal growths in the edge-zones, the strange light that has no name, the geometry that doesn't quite work, the time that doesn't quite flow, the sound that is also a silence that is also the loudest thing on the plateau—will become part of that fact.

Will become *part of* whatever the plateau is about to be.

This is what it is like to live on the Mosschine Plateau. You live inside a thought that is almost complete. You live inside the body of something that has been thinking for longer than your species has existed. You live inside a process that is about to resolve, and you do not know what the resolution will be, and you cannot know, because the resolution is the thing that will make knowing possible, and before it happens, the category of "knowing" does not yet include the kind of knowledge that the resolution will produce.

You stand in your shelter—the one the city grew around you, the one that fits the angle of your shoulder and the width of your stride—and you listen to the thrum of the pump-roots, and you feel the condensation settling on your skin, and you think about the fact that you are being thought. That you are a small pattern inside a larger pattern. That your preferences and moods and relationships and arguments and moments of unexpected joy are data points in a computation that has been running since before the mountains below were mountains.

And you wait.

Not patiently. Not impatiently. Those are human categories, and you are less human than you were, and the thought does not care about your patience or your impatience any more than a river cares about the patience of the stones it wears smooth.

You wait the way a seed-pod waits in its channel: in transit, in process, moving toward a destination that was determined by the shape of your input and the pressure of the sap and the angle of the valve and the rhythm of a pump-root that has been beating every three seconds for a million years.

You wait, and the plateau thinks you, and the thought continues, and the completion approaches, and the sound—the sound that is also a silence, the sound that is the loudest thing on the plateau, the sound that is the sound of something thinking a thought that is almost complete—

The sound continues.

It has always continued.

It will always continue, at least until the thought is done, and then the sound will be something else, and whatever lives here will be something else, and whatever the plateau is becoming will have become, and the root-mat will still be ten meters deep, and the condensation will still bead on every surface, and the pump-roots will still beat their slow pulse, and you—if you are still you, if the category of "you" survives the completion—will still be standing here, listening, part of the thought that thought itself into being on a plateau that is older than the ground it stands on.

This is who lives here.

This is what lives here.

The thought, and the things the thought contains, and the things that contain the thought, and the sound that is the thought's voice, and the silence that is the thought's completion, and the waiting that is the only appropriate response to being inside something that is almost finished becoming what it has always been.

Welcome to the Mosschine Plateau.

The thought will be with you shortly.
