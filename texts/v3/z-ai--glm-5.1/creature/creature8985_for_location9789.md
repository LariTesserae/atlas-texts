# Who Lives Here

---

The first thing to understand: they do not think of themselves as trapped.

This is important because visitors always do. They arrive from the lowlands—wet, loud, still carrying the expectation that life requires water the way a sentence requires verbs—and they see two hundred thousand people on a slope that never ends, breathing dust, drinking brine, slowly calcifying, and they think: *these people are stuck*. They think: *something went wrong here, someone made a terrible decision, and now they cannot leave.*

They can leave. The crawlers go down as well as up. The path is long but it is not blocked. Every year, a few dozen people leave—workers whose contracts expire, traders who have made their margins, visitors who have seen enough. They descend the rise, back to where rain falls and rivers cut and the stone does not breathe. They return to the world of water.

Most of them come back.

Not all. But most. And not quickly—sometimes it takes years, sometimes decades—but they come back to the Ascendant, and when they are asked why, they say things like *I missed the quiet* or *the lowlands felt wrong* or *I couldn't sleep there, there was too much noise*, and they say these things in voices that have dropped half an octave, that carry in dry air the way they never could in wet, and their eyes have that fine grey film, the selisk adaptation, and you realize that leaving was never the problem.

The problem was staying away.

## The Miners

Start with Kael Thessen, who has worked the deep seams for forty-one years.

He is sixty-three but does not look it. The selifrith has preserved him in the way it preserves everything here—not by preventing decay but by *mineralizing* it, replacing organic processes with crystalline ones so gradually that the person inside doesn't notice the transition. His skin is dark, not from sun but from the dust that has worked its way into the dermis, settling in patterns that correspond to the veins of corthunite he has spent his life following. His hands are mapped with it: fine grey lines across the palms, the backs of his fingers, the creases where stone dust has become stone itself.

He does not think of this as damage. He thinks of it as *writing*. The Ascendant, he says, writes on the people who stay. It writes in a language of mineral accumulation and electrical potential, and what it writes is *you are here, you are part of this, you will remain when the water-people have gone.*

Kael works the Benthic Shift, the deepest active shaft in the Verath complex. He descends every morning at what the city calls dawn—a convention, here, where the light changes gradually over hours rather than breaking suddenly—and he spends eight hours in tunnels where the stone temperature reaches forty-two degrees, where the air is thick with sulfur compounds that would kill an unadapted human in minutes, where the benthic sketh move in the walls like heartbeats.

He listens to them. This is his skill, the thing that has kept him alive when younger, stronger miners have died: he can hear the sketh. Not their movement—that produces vibrations anyone can feel through the rock—but their *intent*. The direction they want to travel. The pressure they're building. The moment, hours before it happens, when a benthic will shift course and collapse a tunnel that was safe an hour before.

He cannot explain how he does this. When asked, he touches his chest, just below the sternum, and says: *the dust tells me.* He is not being poetic. The selifrith in his tissue—the mineral waste that has replaced a measurable percentage of his organic matter—resonates at frequencies that match the sketh's passage. He has, in a functional sense, grown the organs required to perceive what the Ascendant perceives: the slow geologic grinding of something vast and patient and indifferent to human presence.

He is not unique. Every miner who survives the deep shifts develops some version of this sense. But Kael is the best, and the other miners treat him with a respect that has nothing to do with hierarchy and everything to do with the recognition that he has *become* the place in ways they have not yet reached.

He does not sleep. Has not slept in seven years. This does not trouble him. He rests—lies still, closes his eyes, lets his body perform whatever maintenance the modified physiology requires—but the consciousness that would have dissolved into dreams instead dissolves into the stone. He says he can feel the rise. The actual upward motion, centimeters per century, the continental plate shouldering its way toward some destination that will not be reached for millions of years after he is dead and the dust that was his body has joined the general respiration.

He says it feels like breathing. Like the Ascendant breathes through him, and he through it, and the boundary is—not gone, because it was never sharp enough to dissolve—but *irrelevant*. A distinction that matters only to people who still think of themselves as separate from the ground they walk on.

He has a daughter. She works the surface, in the refinement factories. She still sleeps. She does not understand her father, not yet, but she is thirty-one, and the dust is accumulating, and he watches her with an expression that might be patience or might be grief—she cannot tell, because his face has taken on the quality of the stone around him: expressive only to those who have learned to read mineralogy.

## The Refiners

Mira Thessen—Kael's daughter—runs the night shift at Refinery Seven.

She chose the night shift deliberately. The daytime factories thrum with corthunite discharge, the air electric with current and the illi clustered along every conduit, and the noise, the sensation, the *activity* makes it difficult to think. At night, the refineries run at reduced capacity. The thrum drops to something almost below perception. The illi disperse, clicking their way toward whatever rituals they perform in the dark. And Mira can hear herself—the small sounds of her body, the pulse of adapted blood through adapted organs, the quiet mineral conversation of tissue that has learned to parse brine and dust instead of water and air.

She supervises a crew of fourteen. They are all younger than her—the older workers tend to migrate to the day shift, where the noise and activity distract from whatever the dust is doing to their interiors. Her crew members still have clear eyes, still sleep, still talk about leaving when their contracts expire. She does not discourage this. She remembers being twenty-two, new to the Ascendant, certain that the dust was a hazard rather than a habitat.

What she does, what no one has asked her to do but what she does anyway, is watch them. She watches for the moment when the selifrith stops being something they breathe and starts being something they *are*. It is a subtle transition. A change in the way they move—slower, more deliberate, as if they are learning to walk at the speed of the stone beneath them. A change in the way they speak—dropping volume, learning to say more with less, letting the dust carry the parts of language that wet air would require them to shout. A change in the way they listen—no longer turning toward sounds but *feeling* them, the vibration arriving through the floor before the air can carry it.

She lost two crew members last year. Not to death—to the illi.

It happened the way it always does. Renn and Vass, both twenty-six, both on the Ascendant for three years, both beginning to develop the grey film that indicates selisk adaptation. They started following the illi on their breaks—walking along the ridge lines, clicking softly, making sounds that were not language but were not random either. Mira saw it happening. She could have reported it. She could have had them reassigned to the interior, to work that would keep them away from the illi's routes.

She did not. This is the thing about the Ascendant: the rules say *do not follow the illi*, but the rules also recognize—as all rules here recognize—that some transitions cannot be prevented, only accompanied. Renn and Vass were *being asked*. The illi do not recruit—they do not have the concept—but they *call*, in frequencies that the selisk ear begins to perceive before the selisk mind begins to understand, and when that call is heard, the person who hears it has already begun the journey away from what they were.

Renn came back. After six weeks, he walked into the refinery, his eyes clear as glass, his hearing sharp enough to detect the benthic sketh in the deep shafts, his sleep gone, his body humming at the frequency of the stone. He could taste corthunite in the walls. He could feel the electrical potential in every vein beneath the city. He was, by any functional measure, a better miner than he had been before—but he was no longer entirely human, and everyone knew it, including him.

Vass did not come back. The illi led her past the ridges, past the last surveyor stations, out into the deep interior where the rise grows hotter and the stone begins to deform. She walked with them for nine days, according to the last crawler crew to see her, and she was *smiling*, they said, not the smile of happiness but the smile of recognition, as if she were seeing something she had always known was there.

Mira does not talk about Vass. But she leaves a cup of brine at the edge of the refinery every night, at the place where the illi gather, and in the morning the cup is empty, and the dust around it is arranged in patterns that might be footprints or might be words or might be nothing at all.

## The Children

The children of Verath are not born selisk.

This is important to state plainly, because visitors sometimes assume that the mineral adaptation is hereditary—that the children of dust-touched parents emerge from the womb already modified, already part of the Ascendant's slow metabolism. They do not. Human reproduction on the Ascendant follows the same carbon-water template it follows everywhere else. Infants are born wet. Helpless. Disgustingly organic.

But they adapt faster.

Seli Thessen—Mira's daughter, Kael's granddaughter, three years old and already walking with the deliberate pace of someone who has nowhere to be except where the stone wants her—has never tasted water. She was gestated on brine, born on brine, nursed on milk produced by a body that runs on brine. Her first solid food was mineral paste, the same paste the miners use to seal cracks in the shaft walls. She ate it without complaint. She digested it. She grew.

Her eyes are clear. Not grey—not yet—but there is something in the way she looks at the world that differs from the way lowland children look at it. She watches the dust. Not the way children watch falling snow or drifting seeds, with delight and the impulse to catch, but with *attention*. She is studying it. Learning its patterns. She is three years old and she is already listening to the Ascendant's respiration, already beginning to synchronize her own breathing with the settling and lifting of the selifrith.

Her mother finds this beautiful. Her grandfather finds this inevitable. The lowland health inspectors who review Verath's birth statistics every five years find it *concerning*, and they write reports about developmental anomalies and mineral exposure and the ethical implications of raising children in an environment that is, by any biological standard, hostile to human life.

The reports are filed. The recommendations are noted. Nothing changes, because nothing can change—because the Ascendant does not negotiate, does not accommodate, does not alter its nature for the comfort of organisms that evolved in water. The children born here will either adapt or they will not, and the adaptation rate, measured over the city's two-hundred-year history, is ninety-seven percent.

The three percent who do not adapt are not sick. They are not damaged. They simply remain *wet*—resistant to the selifrith's gradual replacement of organic tissue, unable to process the brine without assistance, their eyes clear and their sleep untroubled and their hearing limited to the frequencies that air carries. They grow up feeling like visitors in their own home. They leave, most of them, descending the rise to the lowlands where their bodies make sense, and they are happy there—or something like happy, something wet and loud and fast that the Ascendant's residents have trouble recognizing as joy.

Seli will not be among them. Already, at three, she has begun to hum. A low, constant tone that her mother can feel more than hear, that vibrates in the stone beneath their feet, that draws the illi to the windows of their quarters where they cluster and click in patterns that might be response or might be welcome or might be the first phrases of a conversation that will take decades to complete.

She will grow up speaking two languages: the wet language of human mouth-sounds, and the dry language of mineral resonance. She will think in both. She will dream in neither—because by the time she is old enough to articulate the distinction, she will have stopped dreaming entirely, her consciousness slipping instead into the slow geological awareness of the stone beneath her, the rise that never ends, the warmth that rises from depths where the corthunite glows.

She will be the third generation of Thessens on the Ascendant. She will be the first to be born *of* it rather than merely *on* it.

## The Engineers

Pael Corvan does not believe the corthunite is alive.

This is a controversial position in Verath, where the miners have spent generations developing a relationship with the mineral that looks—to Pael—suspicious like worship. He is the city's Chief Extraction Engineer, responsible for the processes that turn raw corthunite into usable power, and he approaches the mineral with the clinical detachment of someone who has spent thirty years refusing to anthropomorphize geology.

He is wrong, and he knows he is wrong, and he continues to be wrong because the alternative—accepting that the mineral *responds*, that it *chooses*, that it has something analogous to preference or will—would require him to reconsider the foundation of his professional practice. Pael extracts corthunite by applying heat and pressure according to precisely calibrated formulas. He does not *ask* it. He does not *negotiate*. He treats it as a resource, and the resource, by and large, cooperates.

By and large.

There was an incident, seven years ago, when a new vein was discovered in Shaft Twelve—the richest vein in Verath's history, according to the resonance surveys, enough corthunite to power the city for a century. The extraction team, following Pael's protocols, began processing. The first week yielded unprecedented output. The second week, the vein's discharge rate tripled. By the third week, the systems were overloaded, conduits melting, safety valves failing, and Pael gave the order to shut down extraction entirely.

The vein went quiet. Not depleted—surveys confirmed that eighty percent of the corthunite remained—but *refusing*. It would not discharge. The extraction team tried every protocol, every combination of heat and pressure and electrical initiation, and the mineral sat in the stone, inert, as if it had never stored a volt of potential in its crystalline matrix.

Vethar. Offended stone.

Pael does not use this word. He calls it *extraction resistance syndrome* and he writes papers about it that nobody in Verath reads because everyone already knows what it is. The miners know. The illi know. The corthunite, if it can be said to know anything, knows. Only Pael persists in the fiction that the mineral's behavior can be explained without recourse to intention.

He is a lonely man. Not socially—his position brings him into contact with every level of Verath's industry, and his expertise is respected—but *intellectually* lonely, holding to a framework that the Ascendant has spent three decades dismantling. His wife left twelve years ago, descended the rise to the lowlands, and her last letter asked him why he refused to listen to the stone, and he could not answer because the answer would have admitted everything he has spent his career denying.

He works late. The night shift, like Mira, though for different reasons. He works late because the quiet lets him think, and thinking is the one activity the Ascendant has not yet learned to replace with mineral awareness. He sits in his office, surrounded by resonance charts and extraction reports, and he *thinks*, deliberately, stubbornly, in the wet language of analysis and hypothesis and falsifiable claims.

And sometimes—late, late at night, when the factory thrum has dropped to its lowest ebb and the illi are dispersed and even the dust seems to settle more heavily—he hears something. Not sound. Not vibration. Something else. A *suggestion*, transmitted through the stone beneath his feet, through the selifrith in his lungs, through the grey film that has begun to develop at the edges of his vision despite his every effort to prevent it.

It sounds like a question.

He does not answer it. He has never answered it. But every night, the question becomes more specific, and every night, the cost of not answering becomes more clear, and Pael Corvan—Chief Extraction Engineer, rationalist, the last man in Verath who refuses to admit that the Ascendant is speaking—begins to understand that silence is itself a kind of response, and that the stone is patient, and that it will wait.

## The Remembered

There are people in Verath who are no longer people.

This is not the same as being dead. Death happens here, as it happens everywhere—mine collapses, equipment failures, the occasional fall from a ridge where the selifrith has accumulated too thickly for traction. The dead are buried according to whatever traditions they held in life, and their bodies are placed in the stone, and the stone accepts them, and the dust settles, and that is the end.

But there are others. Those who followed the illi and came back *different*. Those who worked the deep shafts until the benthic sketh were not something they heard but something they *understood*. Those who stopped sleeping and started humming and walked into the deep interior and were never seen again.

They are not dead. The miners know this because the corthunite tells them.

In Shaft Nineteen, which has been sealed for forty years after a vethar event that emptied the richest vein in Verath's early history, something moves. Not the benthic sketh—those vast creatures have their own routes, their own depths, their own indifferent passage through the stone. Something else. Something that registers on the seismic monitors as human-sized, human-shaped, but moving at the pace of mineral rather than flesh, centimeters per day, slowly migrating deeper into the rise.

The miners call them the *vethaskim*. The dust-who-remained. They do not speak of them to visitors. They do not speak of them to each other, except in the low, careful voices that the Ascendant's quiet has taught them to use. But they know—they *know*—that the vethaskim are not dead, that they have not died, that they have instead completed the transition that Kael Thessen is still making, that Seli Thessen will finish, that everyone who stays long enough will eventually approach.

What is it like, to be vethaskim? To have walked so deep into the Ascendant's metabolism that the boundary between organism and environment is not blurred but *gone*? The miners have theories. The engineers have no comment. The xenobiologists have filled entire journals with speculation that resolves into the admission that they cannot know, because the vethaskim do not return and the instruments that might reach them cannot survive the depths they have reached.

But sometimes—rarely, in the deepest shafts, in the quietest hours—a miner will hear something that is not sketh, not stone, not the subsonic groan of the rising continent. A rhythm. A pattern. A *voice*, speaking in the click-language of the illi, in the resonance-language of the corthunite, in the slow geological grammar of the Ascendant itself.

And sometimes—not often, but sometimes—a miner will answer. In a whisper. In a hum. In the language of dust and warmth and patience that the city has spent two hundred years learning to speak.

And the voice in the deep will fall silent, as if listening, as if the thing that was once human and is now something else is hearing, for the first time in years or decades, the sound of the world it left behind.

## The Whole

So. Who lives here?

The miners, with stone in their lungs and grey on their eyes, descending into shafts where the benthic sketh dream their slow mineral dreams. The refiners, with brine in their blood and dust in their voices, processing the corthunite that powers the city's patient industry. The children, born wet but adapting fast, learning to speak in frequencies that lowland ears cannot hear. The engineers, stubborn in their rationality, feeling the question in the stone and refusing—still refusing—to answer. The vethaskim, deep in the rise, no longer human, no longer *not* human, speaking in the language of the Ascendant itself.

The sketh, grinding. The vorel, walking. The illi, clicking their prayers along the power lines, tending the current they did not create but somehow sustain.

The dust. The selifrith, settling and lifting, carrying the memory of every footstep, every grinding, every whispered conversation, every discharge of corthunite, every vibration of the rise's endless upward motion.

The corthunite, glowing in its deep veins, storing its electrical promise, choosing when to give and when to withhold and when to go quiet in the face of taking without asking.

And the Ascendant. The rise itself. The continental rampart, ancient beyond stratigraphy, still climbing, still warming, still breathing its slow respiration through the medium of dust and stone and the bodies of everyone who has stayed long enough to become part of it.

They are not separate, here. That is the thing visitors never understand, the thing that cannot be explained, only lived. The people of Verath do not live *on* the Ascendant. They live *in* it. *As* it. The boundary between person and place is—like everything else here—gradual, patient, and too slow to perceive.

They are the Ascendant, and the Ascendant is them, and the dust breathes, and the stone rises, and the warm air moves across a continent that has never known rain, and the quiet holds.
