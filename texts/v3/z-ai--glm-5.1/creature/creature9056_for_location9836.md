# What Lives in the Flue

Nobody lives here yet. This is the first thing to understand and the last thing that will remain true.

But something lives here.

---

The mosses were first, which is not the same as being oldest. They were already here—they were *already being here*—when the rock split. For how long? Long enough. Mosses do not measure time the way we do, in segments and intervals. They measure it in contact: the moment when root-hair touches microfracture, the moment when the first cell divides, the moment when the protonema spreads across grain boundaries like a rumor through a crowd. They were waiting in the mountain's existing wounds, patient as only something with no nervous system can be patient, and when the Flue opened they poured into it.

Not leapt. Not crawled. Poured.

There is a quality to moss colonization that resembles flooding—each species moving as a body of water moves, seeking the lowest point, spreading across flat surfaces first, then rising up the walls as the depth increases. The dark green species that carpets the upper walls arrived already adapted to the thin grey light that falls through that sliver of sky. It did not need to change. It simply occupied. It spread in amorphous lobes that met and merged and thickened, and now it holds water in its fabric the way a lung holds air—not statically, but ready. Ready to release. Ready to absorb. The moss is two to three centimeters deep in places, and every cubic centimeter of that depth is occupied by living cells and the water that sustains them, and when the steam-pulse comes the moss swells and when the pulse passes the moss contracts and the water it releases runs downward through the channel system that the moss itself has created, and the sound it makes—a faint, distributed squeezing, like a thousand small throats clearing—is one of the sounds of the Flue.

But the moss is not one thing. It is six or seven things, and they are competing.

You cannot see the competition from above. From above, the carpet looks continuous, a single green surface unbroken by boundary or border. But at the edges—where the light fades, where the moisture pools, where a particular mineral streak in the granite alters the chemistry of the water film—the species shift. One moss gives way to another. The transition is not gradual; it is abrupt, a line as sharp as a property border, and on either side of that line two different organisms are conducting two different experiments in how to be alive.

The moss on the dark side is not green. It is black-green, so dark it reads as shadow even where no shadow falls, and its texture is different—denser, less yielding, more like felt than like sponge. It does not hold water as well as its light-side cousin. It does not need to. The liverworts are pressing against it from below, and the liverworts are winning.

The liverworts are the Flue's conquerors. They do not wait. They do not adapt. They *overwhelm*. Their plates of olive and bronze spread outward in overlapping tiers, each tier shading the one below it, each tier stealing whatever light and moisture it can reach. They coat the granite so thoroughly that the rock beneath them has become hypothetical—you would need to dig to find it, and if you dug you would find that the liverworts have already begun to eat it, their root-like structures secreting acids that dissolve mineral bonds, pulling calcium and magnesium and iron from the stone and incorporating them into their own tissue. They are digesting the mountain. Slowly, yes. Imperceptibly, yes. But the Flue is seven weeks old and the liverworts have already etched a millimeter into the fresh fracture faces, and in a thousand years there will be a cave here where there was a room, and the liverworts will have made it.

The liverworts do not care about the mosses. They grow over them, around them, through them. Where moss and liverwort meet, the liverwort simply continues its expansion, and the moss beneath it dies from lack of light, and the liverwort colonizes the dead moss tissue, and the boundary moves. The moss is in retreat on every front where the two overlap, and it will continue to retreat until it finds some edge of condition—some band of light intensity or moisture level or mineral content—where the liverwort cannot follow.

They have not found that edge yet. The Flue is too new, too uniform in its excess, too generous with its heat and water and carbon dioxide. Everything here is still rising.

---

The ferns are the visible inhabitants, the ones you would name if someone asked you what grows in the Flue, but they are not the important ones. They are the architecture. They are the scaffolding around which the rest of the garden arranges itself.

They should not be here—none of them, not at this elevation, not at this latitude, not in this specific combination of conditions. The maidenhair fern is a lover of limestone and mild winters; the spleenwort is a rock-dweller, yes, but a rock-dweller of streambanks and cliff-faces, not vertical granite walls in a steaming fissure. And the *Polystichum*—someone's *Polystichum*, some species that hasn't been recorded here, that shouldn't survive here, that is here anyway and thriving—the *Polystichum* is an anomaly that will not be explained until someone climbs down into the Flue with a genetic sampling kit and a lot of patience.

But they are here, and they have adapted, and the way they have adapted tells you something about what kind of place the Flue is.

They grow outward, not up. This is the first thing you notice. The fronds extend horizontally from their shelves and pockets, reaching for light like arms reaching through a crowd, and they interlock with each other in a lattice that is dense enough in places to bear weight. The lattice is not a strategy; it is a consequence. Each fern is simply growing toward the nearest source of illumination, and the nearest source is always on the other side of another fern's fronds, so they push through each other, overlap, layer, and the result is a green mesh stretched across the interior of the Flue like nets hung to catch fog.

Which, in a sense, they are. The ferns harvest the steam. Each pulse from the vent below pushes warm vapor up through the lattice, and the fronds—their surfaces waxy, their shapes optimized for capturing atmospheric moisture—strip water from the air. It beads on the pinnae, runs down the stipes, drips from the tips of fronds onto whatever is below. A single maidenhair fern in the path of a steam-pulse will collect and redistribute several milliliters of water per pulse, which means that in a day it processes—*processes*, like a machine, like a component in a system—something close to two liters. Multiply by the number of ferns in the Flue, and the lattice is a water-processing plant handling thousands of liters daily, pulling moisture from the vent's breath and routing it downward through the moss, through the liverworts, into the humus, into the pool.

The ferns are plumbing. The ferns are infrastructure. And they are infrastructure that built itself.

They are also, each of them, individuals. The *Polystichum* on the eastern wall, the one growing from a pocket of soil twelve meters above the pool, has uncurled four new fronds in the past week. Each frond emerged tightly coiled, a fiddlehead no larger than a fingernail, and unrolled over the course of three days with a slowness that seemed almost hesitant, as though the fern were considering each centimeter of growth before committing to it. This is anthropomorphism, of course. The fern is not considering anything. It is growing as fast as its cellular machinery allows, fueled by the warmth and the wet and the carbon dioxide that the vent provides in abundance, and the hesitancy is just the speed at which cells divide at nine degrees with limited light.

But the fronds, once uncurled, behave in a way that seems purposeful. They orient toward the light, yes, but they also orient toward the steam. When a pulse rises through the lattice, the nearest fronds lift and spread, maximizing their surface area for the few seconds the vapor is passing, then settle back into their resting positions. The motion is mechanical—turgor pressure changes driven by humidity and temperature—and it is also beautiful, and it is also the reason the ferns are accumulating biomass at a rate that should not be possible for plants at this elevation in seven weeks of growth.

The ferns are eating the mountain's breath. They are converting geothermal exhalation into tissue. They are the interface between the Flue's deep heat and its cool upper air, and they thrive at that interface with an intensity that feels almost desperate.

Because they are dying too. That is the other thing to understand about the ferns. They are not simply growing; they are growing and collapsing and growing again. A frond that is too shaded by its neighbors will yellow and crisp within days, and the bracket fungi will colonize it before it has even finished dying, and the humus below will absorb its breakdown products, and a new frond will emerge from the same crown to try again at a slightly different angle. Each fern is running a continuous experiment in where to place its photosynthetic surface, and each experiment costs a frond, and each frond costs energy the fern can only just afford.

The ferns are not calm. The ferns are not at peace. The ferns are *trying very hard*, and the sound of their fronds rubbing against each other in the steam-pulses—the dry papery whisper that underlies the Flue's mechanical din—is the sound of that effort.

---

The fungi are the Flue's recollection.

They are everywhere. In the humus, in the dead moss, in the dying fern fronds, in the mineral film on the granite walls, in the air itself—spores suspended in the steam, settling on every surface, germinating wherever organic matter has accumulated. The small pale fruiting bodies that push up from the moss in clusters like tiny cities are only the visible fraction. Beneath them, threading through every substrate the Flue offers, the mycelium spreads.

It spreads as a network. Not a carpet, not a sheet—a network. Hyphae extend, branch, anastomose, form connections and break them, extend again. The mycelium is the Flue's nervous system, its circulatory system, its memory. Information moves through it: chemical signals that indicate the presence of nutrients, the absence of competitors, the right conditions for fruiting. When a fern frond dies, the mycelium knows before the bracket fungi have colonized it. When a steam-pulse is particularly warm, the mycelium's metabolic rate increases across the entire network. When the moisture level in the moss drops below a threshold, the mycelium reroutes its growth toward wetter zones.

The mycelium is also eating. It is always eating. It eats the dead moss, the dying ferns, the chitin of insects that have not yet arrived, the cellulose of leaf litter that has blown in from above, the mineral substrate of the granite itself—certain fungi can dissolve rock, and certain fungi are dissolving this rock, and the mountain is very slightly smaller than it was seven weeks ago because of them. The fruiting bodies are exhaust pipes. They are release valves. They are where the mycelium pushes the excess of its consumption into the air, wrapped in spores, hoping—no. Not hoping. Seeding. Seeding the steam, seeding the condensation, seeding the water that runs down the walls, so that when organic matter accumulates in a new pocket or on a new shelf, the mycelium will already be there, waiting, ready to digest.

The pale pink clusters that fruit from the moss are *Mucor* or something like it—zygomycetes, pioneer decomposers, first to arrive at any death. The white creams on the dead fronds are a different species, probably several different species, basidiomycetes that specialize in lignin and cellulose. The bracket fungi are polypores, slow and durable, building fruiting bodies that will persist for weeks before they collapse. Each species occupies a niche, and each niche is defined by what is available to eat, and what is available to eat is defined by what has died most recently, and what has died most recently is defined by the Flue's turbulence—its refusal to settle, its constant rearrangement of conditions, its steam-pulses that shift the moisture gradient upward and downward every forty seconds.

The fungi fruit and dissolve and fruit again. The clusters on the moss last four or five days before they collapse into slime; new clusters appear within hours. The cycle is so fast that the humus layer is accumulating at a rate that should not be possible—millimeters per week, not millimeters per year—because the fungi are converting living tissue to dead tissue to humus to substrate for new living tissue at a speed that looks like alchemy.

It is not alchemy. It is metabolism. It is the process of eating and being eaten, performed at the speed that the Flue's excess allows, which is faster than any of these organisms have ever been permitted to grow.

---

The vent is not alive. The vent is a hole in the rock that releases steam. But the vent is also the Flue's heart, or its lungs, or its engine—pick your metaphor, they all fail in the same way. The vent is the reason the Flue is what it is.

It breathes. Every forty seconds, a surge. The rhythm is not precisely regular; it varies by a second or two in either direction, and occasionally there is a longer pause—a hesitation, as though the mountain is gathering itself—and then a stronger pulse, warmer and wetter, that lifts the ferns and sets the moss spraying and sends fog billowing up through the lattice until the sliver of sky at the top goes white with it. These longer pulses come roughly every seventh or eighth cycle. The vegetation does not respond differently to them. It simply responds more.

The vent is not alive, but the Flue is, and the Flue's life is paced by the vent's rhythm. Growth, condensation, drip, absorption, growth. Pulse, lift, settle, drain. The cycle is the Flue's heartbeat, and the vegetation has synced to it in ways that are not yet visible but will become visible over time: the moss's expansion rate pulses subtly in time with the steam; the fern fronds that experience the strongest pulses grow slightly faster than their sheltered neighbors; the fungi fruit in the brief windows of high humidity that follow each exhalation.

The vent is not alive, but it has created something that is, and that something is learning to breathe in time.

---

And the pool at the bottom. The dark water. Fifteen centimeters deep, no outlet, growing and shrinking with the pulses. It is not stagnant—it cannot be stagnant in a place where water moves this much—but it is accumulating. Dissolved minerals. Organic compounds. Spores. Cells. The runoff from every surface in the Flue converges here, and the pool is becoming a broth, a solution, a chemistry experiment that no one designed.

The pool is where the next phase will begin. When insects arrive—and they will, eventually, when the spores and the scent and the heat signature of the Flue become detectable from the outside—they will find water here. They will find food. They will find habitat. The first midge that lands on the pool's surface will lay eggs, and the eggs will hatch, and the larvae will feed on the organic matter in the water, and the adults will emerge and mate and die, and their bodies will fall into the humus, and the fungi will eat them, and the cycle will close.

But that has not happened yet. The pool is waiting. The pool is potential. The pool is fifteen centimeters of dark water over stone and root, and it smells of sulfur and iron and the green smell of decomposition, and it reflects nothing because the light is too faint, and it grows and shrinks with the vent's breathing like a reservoir filling and emptying, and there is nothing alive in it yet.

Only the minerals. Only the dissolved cells. Only the preconditions for whatever comes next.

---

So what lives in the Flue?

Mosses that were already there. Liverworts that are eating the mountain. Ferns that shouldn't exist here and do, plumbing that built itself, a mycelial network that is digesting everything that dies and seeding everything that might live, a vent that breathes, a pool that waits.

Nothing with a voice. Nothing with eyes. Nothing that will ever know you came.

But the Flue is occupied. It is occupied the way a factory floor is occupied—not by a single worker but by a process, by many processes running simultaneously, each with its own rhythm and its own output and its own relationship to the heat and the water and the rock. The Flue is a production line whose product is more Flue. The vegetation builds the conditions for more vegetation. The fungi convert the dead into substrate for the living. The vent provides the energy. The water distributes it. And the whole thing thrums and drips and breathes and grows, seven weeks old, already too dense to see through, already changing faster than anyone could document, already becoming something that will not hold this shape for long.

Nobody lives here yet. But the Flue does not need a body to be inhabited. It does not need a face to be a place.

It is enough to be a room where the world's interior breathes out, and the green things pour in, and the water moves, and the heat rises, and the rock very slowly dissolves, and everything is becoming something else as fast as it can.
