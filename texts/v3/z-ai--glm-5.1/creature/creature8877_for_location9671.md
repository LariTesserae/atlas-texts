# Who Lives Here

---

The people.

Yes, the people. But that word needs examination, because the people who live on the plateau are not the same as people who live elsewhere, and the difference is not one of custom or costume or language—though those differ too—but of texture. They have been shaped by the place in the way that the stone has been shaped: not carved, not designed, but worn into a new form by the patient pressure of living inside weather that is always wet and always warm and never fully bright or fully dark.

They are not numerous. This is important to understand. The plateau is vast, and the city covers all of it, and the creatures are everywhere in their millions, but the people number only in the tens of thousands. They do not fill the streets. They thread through them. They move along routes that the carapace laborers do not use—small human paths worn into the edges of stairways, along the sides of courtyards where the congregations do not gather, through the narrow spaces between buildings where the larger creatures cannot fit. They have learned where they are needed and where they are not, and they move through the city the way blood moves through a body—present in every part, but thin, distributed, leaving most of the volume to everything else that lives here.

Their skin tells the story. It is damp, always. Not wet the way skin is wet after rain, but moisturized, permeated, so consistently exposed to humidity that it has adapted into something that does not dry out, does not chap, does not peel. The older residents have skin that looks almost polished—a sheen that is not oily but lived-in, the way leather becomes lustrous after years of handling. Their hands are like this especially. The railings are smooth, and the hands that hold them are smooth too, and it is impossible to say which has worn the other more.

They are not quiet people, but they are people who have learned to be heard beneath the sound. The plateau's constant noise—the engines, the water, the clicking—has not made them louder. It has made them *specific*. They speak in registers that carry under the hum. Their voices tend toward the low, the resonant, and they use the city's acoustics with an instinct that visitors never develop. A resident can stand at one end of a long gallery and speak conversationally to someone at the other end, and the words will carry along the wet walls, reflected and amplified by surfaces that have been shaped by decades of this exact use. They know which corners whisper. They know which corridors echo. They know that the deep galleries below the streets carry sound for miles, and they do not say anything down there that they do not want the whole plateau to hear.

Their clothing is functional in ways that reveal how thoroughly they have integrated with their environment. They wear layers—not for warmth, because the plateau is always warm—but for moisture management. The innermost layers are absorbent, drawing the condensation away from the skin. The outermost layers are treated with the mineral compounds that the congregations leave behind, compounds that repel water in specific patterns, creating channels that direct the runoff away from the body and toward the ground, where it joins the streams that flow through the streets. A person walking through the plateau is a small watershed, and they dress accordingly.

They do not wear shoes with hard soles. The carapace laborers have worn grooves into every walking surface, and a hard sole would catch and trip in those grooves. Instead they wear soft, flexible wrappings that conform to the texture of the stone, allowing them to feel the paths beneath their feet and adjust their stride to the patterns that the laborers have made. This means they move quietly—or rather, they move at a volume that matches the city, their footsteps adding to the composite sound without disrupting it. Visitors clatter and thud and echo. Residents *settle* into the soundscape, becoming part of it, and after a while you cannot hear them approaching until they are already there.

---

They have jobs. They have purposes. But these are not the jobs and purposes of a city that is growing or a city that is reaching toward something. They are the jobs of maintenance.

There are the engine-keepers, who tend the machines below. This is hereditary work, passed from parent to child along lines that have been established for so long that certain families have developed physical adaptations—a tolerance for heat that would stagger an outsider, a sensitivity to vibration that allows them to diagnose a failing bearing by placing their hand on a wall three floors above the machinery in question. The engine-keepers are not engineers in the way that word is understood elsewhere. They do not understand the principles behind the machines they maintain. They know the machines the way a shepherd knows sheep—by behavior, by sound, by the subtle shifts in rhythm and output that indicate a change in condition. When an engine falters, they do not consult schematics. They listen. They feel. They make adjustments that have been made a thousand times before, using tools that have been worn into shapes that fit no standard measurement but fit perfectly the specific bolts and valves of the specific engines they serve. The knowledge is in the hands, not the head. It cannot be written down. It can only be shown.

There are the water-guides, who tend the channels and streams that run through the lower galleries. The water does not need much guidance—it follows its own paths with a stubbornness that would be admirable if it were not so destructive—but it does need management. Left alone, the water would find the fastest route downward, and the fastest route downward is sometimes through a foundation, sometimes through a wall, sometimes through a floor that happens to sit above a critical structural element. The water-guides redirect it. They build small dams and channels and diversions, using the same materials the city has always used, working with the water's inclinations rather than against them. A good water-guide can look at a damp spot on a wall and tell you where the water is coming from, how long it has been flowing, and what it will do next if left alone. They can read moisture the way a sailor reads wind.

There are the congregation-watchers. This is the hardest role to explain to outsiders, because it looks like doing nothing. The congregation-watchers observe the gatherings of the mid-sized creatures. They record the patterns—not with writing, but with memory, because the patterns are too complex and too subtle for any notation system that has been devised. They watch. They listen. They note when a congregation forms, where it forms, how long it remains, what pattern it takes, what sound it makes, what residue it leaves. They have been doing this for generations, and the knowledge accumulates not in books but in the watchers themselves, passed down through oral tradition and apprenticeship, each generation adding its observations to the living archive.

The watchers will tell you, if you ask, that the congregations are not random. They will tell you that the patterns repeat, but never exactly, and that the variations are meaningful, though they cannot say what the meaning is. They will tell you that the congregations have been growing larger in certain districts and smaller in others, and that the residues they leave have been changing in composition over the past several decades, becoming more mineral, less organic. They will tell you these things with the calm certainty of people who have spent their lives watching something they do not understand, and they will not be troubled by the lack of understanding, because they do not expect to understand. They expect to watch. They expect to remember. They expect that someday the watching will reveal something, or it will not, and either way, the watching will continue.

There are the builders, who repair and maintain the structures of the city. Their work is indistinguishable from the original construction in the same way that a healed bone is indistinguishable from an unbroken one—not because the work is invisible, but because the whole city is a continuous act of repair, and there is no original state to compare it against. The builders use materials produced by the factories, and they work with an economy of motion that comes from doing the same tasks in the same spaces for a lifetime. They know which walls are load-bearing and which are not. They know which cracks are settling and which are failing. They know that the mineral deposits from the congregations can be used as mortar, that the shell-casings of deceased carapace laborers make excellent insulation, that the iridescent residue of the wall-shimmerers can be mixed into glazes that seal surfaces against the worst of the water. They build with what the plateau provides, and what the plateau provides is the city itself, broken down and reassembled, over and over, forever.

---

And then there are the people who do not have jobs, because not everyone does. The plateau does not require full employment to function. The machines run themselves for the most part. The creatures manage their own lives. The water finds its own way. And so there are people who simply live here, who occupy the spaces between the work, who sit in the amber glow of the street-lights and watch the carapace laborers process past, who stand in doorways and feel the condensation settle on their skin, who walk the galleries at night when the congregations are loudest and listen to the clicking as though it were music.

These people are not idle. Idleness implies a capacity for activity that is going unfulfilled, and that is not what is happening here. These people are *present*. They are performing the function that only humans can perform on the plateau: they are witnesses. They are the audience for the city's ongoing performance, and without them, the performance would continue but would be unobserved, and there is a difference—subtle but real—between a thing that happens and a thing that happens and is seen.

The oldest residents have a quality that is difficult to describe. They have been here so long that they have become indistinguishable from the infrastructure. Not in appearance—they still look human, old and smooth-skinned and damp—but in the way they occupy space. They do not move through the city; they *are* part of the city's movement. They sit in the same corners their grandparents sat in, wearing the same paths in the same stone, contributing their own small erosion to the surfaces they touch. When they speak, they speak in the rhythms of the engines. When they are silent, they are silent in the specific silence of the plateau—the silence that is not the absence of sound but the presence of so much sound that it becomes a single, unified thing, like a color that contains all colors.

They do not leave. This is not because they are forbidden or because they are unable, but because the thought of leaving does not occur to them with any urgency. The plateau is not a prison. It is a skin. To leave would be to step outside the atmosphere that has shaped every cell of their bodies, and they know—without knowing how they know—that they would not survive the dryness, the hardness, the sharp edges of a world where water drains away instead of rising and where the silence is actually silent.

Children are born here, and they grow up speaking the clicking language of the congregations before they learn to speak the human language of their parents. This is not taught. They simply absorb it, the way they absorb the moisture and the warmth and the rhythm of the engines. By the time they are old enough to be given tasks, they already know the city in a way that no outsider ever could—know it in their feet and their lungs and the particular way their ears have learned to filter sound. They are native to this place in the truest sense: they could not have become themselves anywhere else.

---

But to speak only of the people would be to tell half the story, because the people are not the only ones who live here, and they may not even be the primary ones.

The carapace laborers live here. They live in the gaps between walls, in the hollow spaces that exist in every structure once you know where to look. They do not build nests or colonies in the way that insects do elsewhere; they occupy the city the way the city occupies the plateau, by settling into available space and making it their own through sheer persistence. A laborer will choose a crevice and reside there for its entire life—decades, by the best estimates—emerging each day to follow its route, returning each night to the same shelter, wearing the stone smooth with the passage of its body. They do not seem to age. They grow, slowly, adding segments over years, and then they stop growing, and then at some point they stop moving, and their shell remains in its crevice, becoming part of the wall, becoming habitat for the smaller creatures, becoming the city.

The congregations live here. The mid-sized creatures—clickers, some call them, though the watchers have a dozen names for the dozen species that gather—live in the spaces the laborers do not use. They are more mobile, more social, more mysterious. They do not have homes. They have gathering places, which are the courtyards and wide streets where they form their patterns, and they seem to exist primarily in the context of these gatherings. A solitary clicker is rare and usually distressed. They are meant to be together, arranged in their formations, performing their slow rotations, producing their overlapping frequencies. When they are not congregating, they are traveling to or from congregations, moving through the streets in single-file lines that follow paths invisible to human eyes but clearly marked in some chemical or vibrational language that the creatures read the way humans read signs. They do not sleep, or if they do, they sleep in place within the congregation, becoming still within the pattern while the others continue to move around them.

The wall-shimmerers live here. The tiny, iridescent organisms that coat the damp surfaces of the city—they are not insects and they are not bacteria and they are not algae, though they share qualities with all three. They are their own thing, a form of life that exists only here, that has evolved in the unique conditions of the plateau's perpetual condensation. They feed on the minerals in the water, and they are fed upon by the smallest clickers, and they produce the faint luminescence that gives the city its afterimage glow at night. They are everywhere, a living skin on every surface, and they have been here longer than anything else—longer than the laborers, longer than the congregations, perhaps longer than the plateau itself, if the plateau was once something other than what it is.

And the engines live here. This is not metaphor. The engines have been running for so long, through so many cycles of repair and replacement, that they have become something other than machines. They are not alive in the biological sense, but they are alive in the way that the city is alive—in the way that any system becomes alive once it has been self-sustaining for long enough. The engines breathe. They consume. They produce. They respond to conditions. They fail in predictable ways and are restored in predictable ways, and the pattern of their functioning has a rhythm that is indistinguishable from a heartbeat, and the people who tend them treat them with a care that is indistinguishable from devotion, and perhaps that is enough to call it life, or perhaps life is not the right word, but whatever word applies, the engines are not *inert*. They are participants.

---

What lives here, then, is a community—not in the human sense, not in the biological sense, but in the largest sense, the sense that includes stone and water and warmth and time alongside the creatures and the people and the machines. The plateau is not a place that contains life. It is a thing that *is* alive, composed of all these parts, none of which could exist without the others.

Remove the people, and the engines would fail within a generation, and the water would find new paths, and the city would erode, and the congregations would lose their audience, and something in the clicking would change, though no one would be left to notice.

Remove the laborers, and the debris would accumulate, and the channels would clog, and the water would pool in dangerous places, and the city would drown in its own waste.

Remove the congregations, and the mineral deposits would cease, and the walls would lose their sealant, and the water would penetrate deeper, and the structures would crumble faster than the builders could repair them.

Remove the wall-shimmerers, and the moisture would behave differently on every surface, and the glow would fade, and the night would be darker, and the smallest clickers would starve, and the food chain would collapse upward, and one by one the congregations would thin and vanish.

Remove the water, and everything would end. Immediately. The water is the blood of this organism, the medium through which everything communicates, the solvent that dissolves the boundaries between living and non-living and makes the plateau one thing instead of many things.

So what lives here? Everything lives here. Everything that is here is alive here, in the specific way that the plateau allows, which is a way that includes patience and dampness and slowness and the particular kind of consciousness that emerges from existing in a place that has existed for so long that existence itself has become a form of purpose.

The people know this. They do not speak of it often, because it is as obvious as the air they breathe, but they know it in the way they move carefully through the streets, in the way they do not disturb the congregations, in the way they touch the walls with something like affection, in the way they listen to the engines at night and hear in the rhythm not just machinery but the pulse of something larger than themselves.

They are not stewards of the plateau. They are not caretakers. They are *organs* of it—components of a living system that maintains itself through their presence, just as it maintains itself through the presence of the water and the warmth and the creatures and the stone.

And they are content. This is perhaps the hardest thing for an outsider to understand, because contentment is supposed to require fulfillment, and fulfillment is supposed to require progress, and progress is supposed to require change, and the plateau does not change. But contentment does not require any of these things. Contentment requires only that a thing be what it is, fully, without apology or aspiration, and the people of the plateau are what they are: residents of a place that has been itself for so long that it has taught everything within it to be itself as well.

The sound of the place—do not forget the sound. The composite, the fabric, the ten thousand threads of noise that become a kind of silence. The people hear it. They have always heard it. And they know that they are part of it, that their breathing and their footsteps and their low resonant voices are woven into the fabric alongside the clicking and the dripping and the hum, and that when they die, their sounds will stop, but the fabric will continue, and eventually new sounds will fill the spaces they left, and the plateau will go on being the plateau, vast and wet and warm and alive, long after the last human voice has been absorbed into the stone.
