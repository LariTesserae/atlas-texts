The people of Vath are damp.

Not wet — they are past wet, the way a stone in a river is past wet. They have been damp so long that the dampness is no longer a condition but a texture, the same way their skin is a texture, the same way their bones are a structure. Their skin is soft. Not the soft of ill use or disuse, but the soft of constant moisture, the way a river-stone becomes smooth, the way a fern-frond unrolling is soft because it has never needed to harden against anything. Their hands are supple. Their feet, bare on the wet stone more often than not, have soles that are thick but not calloused — padded, like an animal that has never walked on dry ground would be padded, adapted to the particular give of wet rock.

They smell of mineral. Of the stone itself, and of the peat they burn, and of the faint sweetness that rises from the fibrous mats when they are fresh-harvested. Their smell is not a human smell as lowlanders would recognize it. There is no sourness of dried sweat, because their sweat does not dry. There is no animal warmth to their scent, because they do not eat animals and they do not keep animals and they have forgotten, if they ever knew, what animal warmth smells like. They smell like the plateau smells: wet stone, growing green, slow water.

Their hair is the strange part. It grows long — there is nothing to cut it against, no practice of shearing, no animals to provide wool and therefore no precedent for cutting fiber from a living thing — and it mats. Not into dreadlocks, which require dryness and friction and intention, but into something else, something the plateau teaches it to do. The constant dampness, the mineral content of the water, the slow accumulation of calcium and iron from the seeps that bathe them each night — their hair becomes a thing like the mats in the channels. Fibrous. Firm. Slightly yielding. It grows past their shoulders and then it grows together, strand binding to strand through some process that is not quite biological and not quite mineral, until by adulthood their hair is a single mass, dark and dense, hanging past their shoulder blades like a cape made of something that is not quite cloth and not quite rope and not quite stone. They do not cut it. They do not wash it. The water does both, running through it each night as the condensation gathers, keeping it clean in the way that the channels are kept clean: by constant flow, by the patient work of water over fiber.

---

They move like people who are not watched.

This is the thing. This is the crucial thing. There are no animals on the Rise. No predators. No prey. No eyes in the dark, no ears in the brush, no assessment from other species about whether they are fit or weak or desirable or dangerous. And because there are no animals, there are also no mirrors in Vath — not because they lack the technology (they could polish stone to a sheen if they wanted, they could still the water in a pool) but because they lack the need. They do not check themselves. They do not practice expressions. They do not adjust their posture to project confidence or shrink themselves to avoid attention. Their faces are mobile when they speak and still when they are silent and they do not perform either state.

They move slowly. Not from weakness — though they are thin, though they are not robust — but from a kind of physical honesty. There is nowhere to rush toward. There is nothing to run from. The plateau does not reward urgency. The water flows at its own pace, and the mats grow at their own pace, and the ferns unroll in their own time, and the people who have lived here for generations have learned the lesson that the plateau teaches: slowness is not laziness. Slowness is accuracy. Slowness is the pace of a place where nothing hunts you and nothing escapes you and the only measure of time is the water's constant flowing.

Their gestures are small. A nod that barely moves the head. A hand lifted an inch from the thigh to indicate direction. A turn of the shoulders that means come or go or wait or this is here. They do not wave. They do not point emphatically. Their faces do not go wide with surprise or narrow with suspicion because the events that would prompt such expressions do not occur. Something new happens in Vath perhaps once in a generation — a channel shifts its course, a seep slows or quickens, a building stone finally cracks after years of water working its seams — and when it does, the people notice, and they adapt, and they do not make a face about it. Adaptation is not dramatic when the thing you are adapting to is a change in the rate of water flow.

---

Their voices.

Low, yes, the original description established that. But low does not mean quiet in the way that lowlanders are quiet when they are trying not to be heard. It means low in register, low in projection, low in the kind of resonance that comes from filling the lungs and pushing sound through the throat with force. They speak from the chest, but shallowly. They speak like people who are standing next to a sleeping child, except there are no children sleeping, except there are no children awake, except the child is the plateau itself, and they do not want to disturb it, not because it would harm the plateau but because it would harm the silence, and the silence is what lets them hear the water, and hearing the water is how they know the channels are clear, and knowing the channels are clear is how they know they will eat.

Their language — they have one, though it is so spare that a lowlander linguist might argue it is only a dialect of gesture and grunt — has no words for things that do not exist on the Rise. No word for fire that burns out of control. No word for predator. No word for prey. No word for dry. They have seventeen words for the texture of wet stone, or if not words then seventeen sounds, modifications of breath and palate that indicate whether the stone is seeping or slick or merely damp, whether the water on it is flowing or standing or condensing, whether it is drinkable or mineral or brackish. Their language is a map of moisture. It is a tool for reading the plateau. It is not useful for anything else.

They do not name each other with the extravagance of lowland naming. A child is born and the child is referred to by the sound of the water nearest where they were born, and this is enough. The seep at the north of town makes a different sound than the seep at the south. The pool at the center has a low hum. The cascade over the edge has a whisper that can be heard from a hundred yards away. A person is named for the water that was present when they arrived, and this tells you something about them — where they came into the world, which channel they are likely to tend, which part of the plateau they know best — and it does not tell you anything else, and nothing else is needed.

---

They do not pair in the way lowlanders pair.

This is not because they lack affection. This is not because they lack desire. It is because the economy of their lives does not require it. There is no property to combine. There is no labor to divide. There are no children to protect from predators, because there are no predators. A person can raise a child alone as easily as they can raise a child with another, because raising a child in Vath is not a matter of protection but of proximity — the child is near the adults, the child watches the adults, the child learns by being present, and which adults they are near shifts from day to day and season to unmarked season.

When two people desire each other, they desire each other. When they do not, they do not. There is no structure to formalize the desire or extend it beyond its natural span. They share beds for warmth — the peat fires are low and the nights are cold in the way that wet cold is cold, seeping into the bones — and sometimes the sharing becomes a habit and the habit becomes an attachment and the attachment becomes something that looks, from outside, like love. But they do not call it love. They do not call it anything. It is the fact of waking next to the same person and clearing the same channel and eating the same mat and sitting in the same condensation each evening, and it is not spoken of because it does not need to be spoken of, because it is visible in the way two people move around each other, the slight adjustments of path that mean I am going here and you are going there and we will meet at the pool.

The children grow up seeing this. They grow up seeing all of it — the couplings that form and unform, the steady ones that persist, the people who prefer solitude and the people who prefer company. They grow up without a model for what they should do when they grow up, because there is no model, because there is only the plateau and the water and the work that needs doing, and they will do it or they will not, and if they do not do it someone else will, and the water will flow regardless.

---

The old people.

The original description mentioned a graveyard, and unmarked graves, and the dead dissolving into the water cycle. But it did not describe the old people, and the old people are the ones who reveal the most about what it means to live in Vath, because they are the ones who have lived in it longest.

They are thin. They are all thin, the people of Vath, but the old ones are thin the way a reed is thin — not fragile, not brittle, but stripped to the essential structure. Their hair-masses have grown past their waists, dark and dense, some of them trailing behind when they walk like a second spine, a second shadow. Their skin has the softness of long dampness and the resilience of stone that has been wet for a thousand years. Their eyes are clear. Their movements are slower than the young, but they are not feeble. They are the slowness of something that has stopped hurrying because it has learned that there is nothing to hurry toward.

They do not give counsel. This is important. In lowland towns, the old are repositories of wisdom, they are the ones who remember how things were done, they are the ones who can say we have tried this before and it did not work. In Vath, there is no we tried this before because the thing that is being tried is always the same thing — clearing a channel, harvesting a mat, repairing a wall — and it always works, and it always has worked, and there is no variation in the method because the method is what the plateau taught and the plateau does not change its lessons. So the old people do not give counsel because there is no counsel to give. They do what they have always done. They clear channels. They harvest mats. They sit in the condensation. When they die, they are placed in the wet ground and the stone is replaced and the water flows over them and they become part of the cycle, and this is not a tragedy because it is not a change. It is a continuation.

The young people do not revere the old. They do not dismiss them either. They sit near them, sometimes, because the old people have a quality of stillness that is instructive, not as a lesson but as a demonstration. This is how to sit. This is how to be in a place without demanding that it be different. This is how to let the condensation form on your skin without wiping it away, because wiping it away would be work, and the water will dry when it dries, or it will not dry, and either way you will still be here, sitting, waiting for nothing, ready for the next thing the plateau asks.

---

There is a game the children play.

Or not a game, exactly. Games imply competition, and competition implies winners and losers, and there is nothing to win on the Rise and nothing to lose. It is an activity. It is a practice. The children — when they are not watching the adults work, which is most of the time, because watching is how they learn — gather at the central pool and they float things.

Leaves from the ferns. Strips of the fibrous mat. Small stones that they drop from the edge and watch sink. The pool is shallow and clear and the bottom is visible, and the children watch the things they have dropped settle or float or dissolve, and they watch the water flow in from the channels and out over the edge, and they do not talk while they watch because there is nothing to say, because the watching is the whole of it, because the pool is doing what the pool does and they are watching it do it, and this is enough.

Sometimes a child will divert a channel. Not to sabotage the water system — they would not, they know better, they have been watching the water-walker since they were small and they understand that the water must flow — but to see where it goes. To trace a new path with their finger in the stone. To watch the water find a new groove and fill it and flow along it and reach the pool by a different route. The children who do this are not scolded. The water-walker comes and clears the channel and the water goes back to its old path, and the child has learned something about how water behaves, which is the same thing the water-walker learned when they were a child, which is the same thing every person in Vath has learned, which is that water goes where it is directed and it will find the lowest point and it will flow over the edge and it will not stop flowing unless you stop it, and stopping it is harder than letting it go.

---

There is a sound the plateau makes at night.

The original description mentioned silence, but silence is not the absence of sound. It is the absence of competing sound, and in the absence of competition, every small sound becomes present, becomes itself, becomes a thing that exists in the ear rather than a thing that is drowned out by other things. The plateau at night is not silent. It is full of the sounds that the day's haze obscures: the drip of condensation letting go, the creak of stone adjusting to the cold, the whisper of water over the escarpment edge, the softer whisper of water emerging from seeps, the softest whisper of water moving through the channels, and beneath all of it, beneath the whisper and the drip and the creak, the low hum of the earth itself, the sound of the water table pressing against the stone, the sound of the plateau being a plateau, the sound of geology happening in real time, which is so slow it is almost not a sound at all, it is almost a feeling, it is almost a vibration in the bones, it is almost a knowledge that the ground beneath you is alive in its own way, doing its own work, making its own slow progress toward whatever it will become when it is no longer young, when its cliffs have softened and its stones have rounded and its seeps have found their final level and the water flows in its permanent channels and the people who live here — if people still live here, if the plateau still supports them, if the cycle has not been broken by something they cannot predict — will wake in the morning and clear the channels and harvest the mats and sit in the condensation and know that they are part of something that does not need them but includes them, that does not require them but permits them, that does not love them but does not reject them, and that this is enough, this is more than enough, this is what it means to be alive in a place that has found its balance and has no reason to change.

The people of Vath are that reason, or the absence of that reason. They are the proof that the plateau does not need them. They are the proof that they do not need to be needed. They are the thing that the plateau could lose and not notice, and they know this, and they do not mind, because to be noticed by the plateau would be to be acted upon by the plateau, and to be acted upon by the plateau would be to be changed by the plateau, and they are already what the plateau has made them, which is damp, which is quiet, which is slow, which is sufficient, which is alive in the way that the ferns are alive and the mats are alive and the water is alive — not because something is watching, but because the conditions permit it, and they have adapted to those conditions, and the conditions have not changed, and they are still here, sitting in the condensation, listening to the plateau hum.

---

This is who lives in Vath.

Not a people defined by what they lack — though they lack much, by lowland standards. Not a people defined by what they endure — though endurance is part of their story, the endurance of dampness, the endurance of quiet, the endurance of a diet that is monotonous and adequate. A people defined by what they are: a feature of the plateau, an outcropping of humanity in a landscape that did not invite them but did not refuse them, a colony of the same tenacious impulse that puts ferns in the seeps and mosses on the wet stone and mats in the channels where the water flows slow and steady and available.

They do not know they are strange. They do not know that lowlanders would find them strange, because they do not know what lowlanders would find. They do not know what they are missing, and they are not missing anything, and these are the same statement, here, on the Rise, where the water flows and the silence holds and the people are damp and the plateau endures and the night is full of the sound of water doing what water does, which is flow, which is seep, which is find the lowest point and go over the edge and become something else's concern, something below, something far, something that is not theirs and never was.
