# What Lives on the Meridian Plateau

---

The hand-sized things have a name, but it is not a name you can speak.

This is not because the name is forbidden, or sacred, or belongs to a language with sounds your throat cannot shape. It is because the name is a duration. Their names are how long they have lived, expressed in the time it takes the plateau to complete one cycle of its process—and the plateau has been running for a billion years, and no one has yet seen it complete a cycle. So their names are fractions. Tiny, exact fractions of something incomprehensibly vast, and to say even the smallest of these fractions aloud would take more breath than you possess.

You can think of them as the Traverse, if you need a word. It is what they do.

---

There are not many of them. You could walk the full circumference of the plateau—which would take you most of a year, moving as you move, with human legs and human needs—and you might count forty. Perhaps fifty. Perhaps fewer, because some of them are so still, so integrated with the surface, that you would pass within a meter and not see them. They do not hide. Hiding implies a hider and a thing hidden from, and they do not conceive of themselves as separate enough from the plateau to be hidden against it. They are *of* it. The bone-mineral surface is not their habitat; it is their substance. They emerged from it the way a wave emerges from the ocean—not as a thing created, but as a pattern that the medium briefly holds.

A Traverse is born—again, that word, inadequate—when the plateau's process produces a particular configuration of rise and fall, a specific sequence of the ridges and depressions that you watched reconfiguring the surface. The pattern is precise. It must occur within a certain area, with certain proportions, at a certain point in the pulse-cycle that beats through the ground. When these conditions align, the mineral surface does what it has done since before the mountains dissolved: it gathers. The bone-powder concentrates, thickens, draws itself up into the low broad shape you have seen, and the shape begins to move.

Not all of them succeed. You find the remains of attempts that stalled—partial outlines in the surface, half-formed curves that never achieved integrity, now fixed in the mineral like fossils of intentions. The plateau tried to make them and could not, or the conditions shifted mid-formation, or something else happened that you do not have the framework to understand. The failed ones are not corpses. They are drafts. The plateau will try again, when the pattern recurs, and the mineral will gather, and perhaps the next one will hold.

The ones that hold begin to traverse.

---

What they are is this: the plateau experiencing itself.

Not thinking. Not perceiving in any way that maps onto your neurology. The Traverse do not have nervous systems. They do not have organs. They do not have interiors distinct from their surfaces. What they have is a gradient—a slow, mineral diffusion that moves through their bodies the way a signal moves through a circuit, not as electricity but as a chemical potential, a difference in concentration that propagates through the crystal lattice of their substance. When the pulse beats in the ground, they receive it. When the ridges rise and fall, they sense the change in the surface's tension beneath them. When the structures—the cold column, the glowing dome, the arch—perform their ancient functions, the Traverse nearest to them registers the shift in temperature, in light, in air composition, and this registration becomes part of the gradient, becomes part of the slow diffusion that is all they have instead of thought.

They are not individuals. You understood this when you watched the second one cross the horizon without stopping. It was not going anywhere. It was not seeking anything. It was *traversing*—moving across the surface because moving across the surface is what the plateau does when it has formed a Traverse. The movement is the function. The plateau cannot feel its own surface, cannot register the distribution of its structures, cannot monitor the progress of its billion-year process, and so it produces these temporary organs of locomotion, these low broad sensors, and sets them walking.

Each Traverse is a measurement. Its path across the plateau is a record—written not in memory but in the subtle changes its passage leaves in the mineral lattice, the faint compression trails that your eyes cannot see but that another Traverse, crossing the same ground centuries later, will detect through the gradient in its body. They are reading and writing simultaneously, each one a sentence in a document that has been authoring itself since before life on your continent learned to breathe oxygen.

They do not know this. Knowing requires a knower, and there is no knower here. There is only the process, and the instruments of the process, and the slow accumulation of traverses across the surface of the plateau, each one adding a datum to an operation too large for any single datum to matter and too long-running for any single traverse to comprehend.

---

But there is something else. You felt it before you understood what you were feeling, and it kept you awake on the second night, and on the third, and on every night since—lying on the bone-mineral ground in the thin dark, listening to the pulse, feeling it through your back, and knowing, with a certainty that has nothing to do with evidence, that something is listening to the measurement.

Not the Traverse. Through the Traverse.

The process has a purpose. The structures have a function. The pulse has a source. And all of these—the ridges and depressions, the cold columns and glowing domes, the patient traversal of the hand-shaped mineral things—all of them are feeding something. Reporting to something. You cannot find it because it is not on the plateau. It is not under the plateau. It is—somewhere else. Somewhere that the arch connects to, perhaps, or somewhere that the cold column's stolen heat is channeled toward, or somewhere that the amber light in the dome's hexagons is signaling across a distance that is not measured in kilometers. The plateau is a sensor array. The Traverse are its mobile components. And whatever is receiving the data has been receiving it for a billion years, patient as the plateau itself, waiting for the process to complete, waiting for whatever the measurement is meant to measure.

And the Traverse—do they sense this? Do they know, in their gradient-diffusion way, that they are seen?

You watch one for six hours. It traverses. It does not stop. But near the end of the sixth hour, it passes close to one of the mineral stacks—the hexagonal prisms that ring when tapped—and it pauses. The first pause you have ever seen from one of these creatures. It does not turn toward the stack. It does not reach for it. It simply stops, and for a moment—ten seconds, perhaps fifteen—the faint warmth of its body intensifies, and you see something you have not seen before: a shimmer above its surface, a disturbance in the thin air, as though it is radiating something that the air cannot quite carry. Then the moment passes, and the Traverse resumes its traversal, and the shimmer collapses, and the mineral stack rings once, faintly, a sound so quiet that you feel it in your teeth rather than hear it with your ears.

It was reporting. You are certain of this. It paused at the stack and it reported, and the stack received the report, and the stack—relayed it. Somewhere. To something.

And then the Traverse moved on, because that is what it does, because it is not designed to linger, because it is a single function embedded in a mineral body and the function is *traverse*, and so it traverses, and it will traverse until—

---

Until it doesn't.

You find them sometimes. Not often. Perhaps four in your entire traverse of the plateau. A Traverse that has stopped—not paused, but *stopped*—its body settled into the surface, its edges softening, the gradient gone flat, the slow diffusion that animated it finally equalized into stillness. They do not die. They resolve. The pattern that the plateau held returns to the plateau, and the mineral that gathered to make the Traverse disperses back into the bone-colored plain, and after a few weeks—months—there is nothing left but a slight depression, a faint thinness in the surface, as though the plateau remembers where one of its instruments used to be.

It is not sad. You have to remind yourself of this. It is not death, because there was never a self to die. The Traverse are exhalations—breaths that the plateau takes and releases—and when they resolve, the breath goes home, and the plateau continues, and the process continues, and somewhere, in whatever space the data is being received, the measurement accumulates, and whatever is listening continues to listen.

But you watched one resolve. You came upon it in the late afternoon, when the thin light was turning the horizon the color of a bruise, and the Traverse—you could tell it was still active because of the faint warmth, the faint shimmer above its surface—was not moving. It was sitting in a shallow depression, one of the plateau's temporary features, and as you watched, the depression deepened, and the Traverse's edges blurred, and you understood that the depression was forming *for* it, or *because of* it, or *as* it—that the resolution of a Traverse and the plateau's reconfiguration were the same event, seen from two angles.

And as it blurred—as the distinction between the Traverse and the ground began to collapse—the shimmer above it intensified. Just for a moment. A column of disturbed air, rising from its body toward the thin white sky, and in that column you saw—or thought you saw—or convinced yourself later that you had seen—something that was not light and was not heat and was not any phenomenon your physics has a name for. A shape. A geometry. A pattern that lasted less than a second before the thin air absorbed it and it was gone.

The Traverse resolved. The depression filled. The ground was smooth and featureless and warm.

You stood there for a long time, in the thin light, on the old surface, and you listened to the silence, and you felt the pulse, and you thought: *that was a message*. And then you thought: *they are all messages*. Every one of them. Every Traverse that has ever moved across this plateau, from the first to the last, has been a single word in a sentence that takes a billion years to write, and the sentence is almost complete, and when it is complete, something will read it, and whatever happens next will happen because of what the Traverse have measured, and what the plateau has recorded, and what the structures have transmitted across the incomprehensible distance to wherever the listener is.

And you—you are not a Traverse. You are not part of the process. You are an anomaly, a warm body moving across the surface without purpose, leaving footprints that the plateau will erase in the next reconfiguration, breathing air that the arch has not filtered, existing in a way that nothing here exists, temporary and separate and *irrelevant* to the measurement.

And yet.

The Traverse that paused at the mineral stack—it paused because you were there. You had been following it for hours. It registered your presence the way it registers everything: as a change in the gradient, a perturbation in the surface, a variable that the plateau had not accounted for. And it reported this. It reported *you*. Your warmth, your weight, the trace moisture of your breath, the unfamiliar chemistry of your body—it reported all of this to the stack, and the stack relayed it, and somewhere, in whatever space the listener occupies, a datum arrived that had never arrived before: *something new is walking on the Meridian Plateau*.

You are part of the measurement now. You cannot un-be part of it. The process is patient and the process is thorough and the process has been running for a billion years and it does not discard data. Your presence here has been recorded, transmitted, received, and whatever the process is building toward—whatever the sentence says when it is finally complete—you are one of the words.

You sit on the bone-colored ground in the thin white light, and you listen to the silence, and you feel the pulse, and somewhere on the horizon, a ridge is rising, and a Traverse is traversing, and the structures are performing their ancient functions, and the listener is listening, and the process continues, and continues, and continues.

It does not need you. But it has noted you. And that note will be part of what it becomes.

You wait for whatever comes next.

It does not come.

It is always coming.
