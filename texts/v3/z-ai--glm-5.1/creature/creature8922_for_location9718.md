#What Lives in the Seep

The fungi live here first and always.

Not the mushrooms — those fleshy, obvious fruitings that people mistake for the whole organism. The mushrooms are rare in the Seep, and small when they come, pale knobs no larger than a pinhead rising from saturated moss on the few days when the moisture index crosses some invisible threshold into fruiting conditions. They appear and vanish in hours, barely a gesture, barely a wave. The real fungi never show themselves. They are the threads — the hyphae — and they are everywhere, a second nervous system laid over and through and beneath the visible life of the cleft.

Consider a square centimeter of moss. Pull it apart (though you would not, should not, could not without violence) and you would find the moss stems sheathed in fungal threads, some of them penetrating the plant cells, some merely clasping, some extending outward into the adjacent moss, into the liverwort patch, into the soil beneath. These are the mycorrhizae, the ancient partners, the arrangement that made it possible for plants to leave the water in the first place, four hundred million years ago. The Seep preserves this partnership in its most primitive form. The moss makes sugar from the thin light. The fungus makes minerals available from the rock. Between them, they build the only soil this cleft has ever known — slow soil, patient soil, a centimeter per thousand years, organic matter compacting under the constant pressure of water and its own weight into something dark and dense and sweet-smelling if you could get your nose close enough, which you cannot, because the cleft is four paces wide and your body does not bend that way and the salamanders need you to stop breathing so hard on their walls.

But the mycorrhizae are only one faction. Deeper in the moss, in the layer where dead stems become compost, the decomposer fungi run their fine filaments through every fragment of organic matter, releasing enzymes that break down cellulose and lignin into simpler compounds that can be absorbed and passed onward. Without them, the moss would build up as peat and eventually choke itself. With them, the cleft recycles. Every atom of carbon and nitrogen that enters here stays here, drawn upward by roots and hyphae, consumed, excreted, decomposed, consumed again. The Seep is a closed hand, holding what it has been given.

And there are predatory fungi. In the water films, in the saturated moss, in the thin channels where the runoff creeps, there are fungi that hunt. They form loops, microscopic snares of hyphae with cells swollen into nooses, and when a nematode — one of the thousands of transparent roundworms that thread through the moss matrix — blunders into a loop, the noose tightens in a fraction of a second, cells inflating to grip the prey, and the fungus extends hyphae into the nematode's body and digests it from within. In the Seep, even the hunters are patient. Even the snares are microscopic, silent, unavoidable.

---

The rotifers live in the water film itself.

They are animals, though you would never guess it. A rotifer is smaller than a period on this page, and it is, in its way, magnificent. At one end, a crown of cilia — the corona — beats in coordinated waves, creating a current that draws particles toward the mouth: bacteria, organic detritus, the single-celled algae that cling to the moss stems in the upper reaches where the light is marginally less dim. The cilia give the rotifer its name; *rotifer* means wheel-bearer, and when the corona beats, it does appear to be a pair of spinning wheels, though this is an illusion created by the sequential beating of the cilia, each one flicking in turn, a wave passing around the circle like a chain of dominoes falling.

At the other end, a foot with toes that secrete a sticky substance, allowing the rotifer to anchor itself to a moss leaf or a speck of stone. It can release and swim freely, trailing its corona like a lantern, or it can attach and feed, its whole body swelling visibly as the gut fills. Some rotifers build tubes — cases of secreted material or cemented debris in which they live, extending the corona to feed and retracting it when disturbed. In the Seep, these tubes dot the moss surface like tiny translucent shells, each one housing an animal that has never known a dry day, a warm day, a day when the water film thinned to nothing and survival meant waiting out the desiccation in a dormant state called anhydrobiosis. The rotifers of the Seep do not anhydrobiate. They have never needed to. The water has always been here. They do not know what absence means.

And there are bdelloid rotifers — a whole clade of them, millions of individuals in this cleft alone, every single one female. The bdelloids have not had sex for forty million years. They reproduce by parthengenesis, laying eggs that are clones of themselves, generation after generation after generation, and they have diversified into hundreds of species despite this, or because of it, or in indifference to it — the evolutionary biologists argue, and the rotifers do not care. In the Seep, the bdelloids cycle through the same lives their ancestors lived: hatch, feed, lay eggs, die. The eggs hatch. The cycle continues. No mate is sought, no competition endured, no display made. They are a lineage of quiet self-sufficiency, each one a perfect copy of a solution that worked, still working, needing nothing from anyone except the constant wet and the bacteria that multiply in the film of water on the moss.

Forty million years without sex. Think of it. The Seep has held bdelloids for some fraction of that time — perhaps only since the last ice age, perhaps longer, the record is in the stone if the stone kept records, which it does not, except in the language of erosion, which says only: *water was here*. But even if the bdelloids have inhabited this cleft for only ten thousand years, that is ten thousand years of continuous asexual reproduction, ten thousand years of eggs laid in the same moss, in the same wet, ten thousand years of sameness that is not stasis but continuation, not death but its opposite — the stubborn persistence of something that works, that has always worked, that requires no improvement because the Seep itself has not changed, has never changed, has been the same since the cleft was carved and the water began to weep and the moss began to grow and the bdelloids came on the feet of some bird, perhaps, or in the film of water on a salamander's skin, or in a fragment of moss blown in on a rare wind, and found a place that matched them so precisely that there was never any pressure to be different.

The tardigrades live here too.

Water bears. Moss piglets. Eight-legged micro-animals that look, under a microscope, like the offspring of a bear and a blimp, their bodies barrel-shaped and translucent, their legs stumpy and clawed, their mouths equipped with stylets that pierce plant cells and suck out the contents. They are everywhere in the Seep. Every handful of moss contains hundreds. They climb through the moss matrix with a deliberation that seems almost comical — each leg lifted, placed, the body hauled forward, the next leg lifted, placed, a gait so slow it makes the salamanders look frantic.

But the tardigrades of the Seep share something with the bdelloids: they have never needed to use their most famous ability. Tardigrades are renowned for cryptobiosis — the capacity to shut down all metabolic activity and survive in a state of suspended animation for years, decades, perhaps centuries, waiting for water to return. The tardigrades in the Seep do not wait. The water has never left. They move and feed and mate and lay eggs and die without ever entering the tun state, without ever curling into their famous resistant sphere, without ever testing the limits of their extraordinary resilience. The Seep has made them soft in the way that any creature becomes soft when its environment never challenges it — not truly soft, not degenerate, but untested, their greatest strength held in reserve against a day that never comes.

Unless it does. Unless the cleft dries. Unless some shift in the mountain's plumbing diverts the groundwater, some earthquake re-routes the seep, some drought reaches even this sheltered slot. Then the tardigrades would tunicate. They would withdraw their legs, expel the water from their bodies, form the tun, and wait. They would wait for years. They would wait while the moss desiccated and crumbled and the salamanders retreated or died and the snails sealed their shells with epiphragms of dried mucus and the flatworms desiccated beyond recovery and the ferns curled and browned and the filmy ferns vanished entirely. They would wait for the water to return. And if it returned, they would resume. Fifteen minutes from tun to active animal. The metabolism reigniting. The legs uncurling. The mouth stylets piercing the first rehydrated moss cell.

But this has not happened. Not yet. Not in the memory of any tardigrade lineage that lives in the Seep. They do not know what they are capable of. They move through the wet moss in their slow, deliberate way, feeding, breeding, dying, and they do not know that they are the hardest creatures in this soft wet place, that they carry within their translucent bodies an ability to survive that would make the gneiss itself seem fragile by comparison, that they are the backup system for an ecosystem that does not know it needs one.

---

The diatoms live in the channels where the water runs.

Single-celled algae encased in glass. Their cell walls are made of silica — frustules, they are called, two overlapping valves like a pill box or a Petri dish, ornamented with patterns of such precision and regularity that they were once believed to be the work of some intelligence, some micro-artisan shaping glass at a scale beneath notice. The patterns are pores and ridges and spines, arranged in bilateral symmetry, and they are functional — they allow the diatom to exchange materials with the water, to regulate buoyancy, to resist being crushed by the surface tension of the water film in which they live.

In the Seep, the diatoms coat the wet rock in the channels with a bronze film, visible only as a slight discoloration, a shift in the tone of the wet stone from dark grey to something warmer and browner. They are photosynthesizing, even in this dimness. The light that reaches the channel floor is barely measurable — a fraction of a percent of full sunlight — and the diatoms use it with an efficiency that borders on the miraculous. Their chloroplasts are adapted to low light. They stack their photosynthetic pigments like velvet, layer upon layer, catching every photon that penetrates the murk, and they grow slowly, so slowly, dividing once every few days, every few weeks perhaps, in water that is always cold and always moving at the pace of syrup.

When they die, their glass shells remain. The silica does not decompose. It settles into the channels, into the moss, into the accumulating soil, a layer of microscopic glass that will persist for as long as the mountain stands. In the Seep, the diatoms have been dying and settling for ten thousand years, and the soil contains their accumulated remains — not in thick deposits, not as diatomaceous earth, but as a fine glassy dust mixed with the organic matter, each shell intact, each pattern preserved, a library of forms that records every species that has ever lived in this cleft since the ice retreated and the water began to weep.

No one will ever read this library. No one will ever sample the soil and dissolve the organic matter and mount the frustules on a slide and hold them under a microscope and say: *this species was here, and this one, and this one which is now extinct elsewhere but persists in the Seep because the Seep has not changed.* The record is being kept, but there is no reader. The diatoms write their autobiographies in glass and bury them in the mud and the mud accumulates and the water flows over it and the moss grows on it and the salamanders sit on it and none of them know that they are standing on the archive of their own ecosystem's history, that beneath their feet is a chronicle of ten thousand years of patience and persistence and the slow accumulation of change so minute it registers only in the shifting proportions of glass shapes in the dark.

---

The bacteria live everywhere.

In the water film. On the moss surfaces. In the moss interior. On the salamanders' skin. In the salamanders' guts. On the snails' trails. In the channels. On the rock itself, in a biofilm so thin it is invisible, a layer of bacterial cells and their secreted exopolymers coating the gneiss wherever the water touches, mediating the interaction between water and stone, accelerating the dissolution of minerals, harvesting energy from the chemical gradients that exist at every interface — between air and water, between water and rock, between oxygen and anoxia in the saturated moss.

There are bacteria in the Seep that have never been described. This is almost certainly true. The cleft has not been sampled, not thoroughly, not with the molecular tools that would reveal the full diversity of its microbial inhabitants. The bacteria that live here are adapted to cold, to wet, to low nutrient availability, to dimness. Some of them are chemolithotrophs, harvesting energy from the oxidation of iron and sulfur compounds in the rock, independent of sunlight, independent of organic matter, independent of everything except the stone and the water and the cold. They are the deepest layer of the Seep's life, the layer that would persist even if the moss died and the ferns died and the salamanders died and the light failed entirely. They would persist because the stone persists, because the water persists, because the chemical gradients persist, and that is all they need.

They have been here the longest. Before the moss, before the ferns, before the first invertebrate crawled down from the ridge and found this wet slot and stayed, the bacteria were here, living on the weeping rock, building their films, dissolving the gneiss atom by atom into the minerals that would eventually support the moss that would build the soil that would anchor the ferns. They are the foundation of the foundation, and they are unknown, and they do not care.

---

And there is something else that lives in the Seep. Not an organism. Not a species. Something that emerges from the totality — from the water and the stone and the moss and the animals and the fungi and the bacteria and the cold and the dark and the patience.

Call it a stability. Call it a self-perpetuation. Call it, if you are feeling generous, a kind of mind — not a mind that thinks, not a mind that knows, but a mind that *holds*, that maintains itself in a configuration so precise and so balanced that it resists disruption the way the gneiss resists the water, slowly, at a rate invisible to anything that observes from outside.

The Seep is alive in the way that a forest is alive, in the way that a reef is alive — not as a single organism but as a system, a network of relationships so dense and so interdependent that no single component can be removed without consequence, no single variable changed without the whole adjusting, subtly, at the speed of water, at the speed of moss growth, at the speed of erosion. The salamanders need the springtails. The springtails need the moss. The moss needs the fungi. The fungi need the organic matter. The organic matter needs the decomposers. The decomposers need the dead moss and the dead ferns and the dead everything, and the dead everything needs the living everything to produce it, and the living everything needs the water, and the water needs the stone, and the stone needs the time.

It holds. That is what it does. It holds and it holds and it holds.

And what lives in it — what truly lives in it, at the scale that matters — is the holding itself.
