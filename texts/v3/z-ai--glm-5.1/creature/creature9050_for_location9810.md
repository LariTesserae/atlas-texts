# The Weeping Works

The people here are soft-spoken in the way that rivers are soft-spoken—they are not quiet, but their voices have been shaped by the necessity of moving around obstacles, of finding the channels where passage is possible. In the residential quarters, where the engine-roar drops to a constant murmur felt more than heard, they speak at what visitors call a murmur and what they call ordinary volume. Their hearing is not gone, not exactly, but it has been retrained, thinned in the registers where the pistons dominate and sharpened in the narrow bands where human speech still competes. They hear each other through the sound the way a fisherman reads a river by the movement of its surface—by disturbance, by exception, by the tiny interruptions in the pattern that indicate something living passes beneath.

Their skin tells the story of the place before they open their mouths. Everyone here carries the mineral film—calcium and iron and trace elements that the weeping-water deposits on everything it touches, and it touches everything. The film builds up over years, a fine pale scale on the backs of the hands, around the hairline, in the creases of the elbows and knees where sweat and weep-water pool and dry and pool again. The oldest workers, the ones who have spent decades in the deep houses where the humidity approaches saturation, have a pearlescent quality to their skin, almost like the inside of an oyster shell. It is not unhealthy, the physicians insist, though they admit they have no comparison group and no baseline. The mineral accretion doesn't seem to harm the tissue beneath. It simply coats it, the way it coats the pipes, the walls, the gutters. The body becomes another surface the water touches and marks.

They bathe, of course. The residential quarters have catch-basins fed by the cooler seeps, water that has traveled through the stone long enough to shed some of its heat and most of its mineral load. But bathing here is a temporary reprieve, not a reset. You step out of the basin and the air deposits the film again within the hour. The very young and the very old sometimes develop a condition the tenders call "crusting"—patches of thicker accumulation at the temples, the collarbones, the spine, where the skin's oils combine with the mineral water to form a dense, almost stonelike deposit. It can be softened and removed with prolonged soaking, but it returns. Some of the elderly have stopped removing it. They wear it like a second skeleton growing outward, pale and ridged, and when they move through the amber haze of the evening streets, they look like things partially petrified, halfway between flesh and the wet warm stone they walk on.

---

The children are born here, most of them. The works have never drawn immigrants in any number—only the occasional mechanic or engineer from the lowlands, contracted for a specific repair, who stays the length of the contract and then descends again, blinking in the dry thin air, complaining of the silence. The population is self-sustaining: born, raised, employed, aged, and buried in the same mineral cycle. The children grow up with the sound as their first environment. In the lowlands, infants are startled by sudden noise; here, the infants are startled by silence, which they encounter only in the deep maintenance tunnels, the old cisterns, the rare acoustic shadows where the engine-din cancels itself through some accident of reflection and distance. A crying infant will sometimes quiet immediately when carried toward the engine houses, settling into the vibration as though it has returned to the womb's own constant murmur. The mothers know this. The tenders know this. It is not discussed with the same urgency that lowland parents discuss their children's development, because here it is simply how development proceeds. The sound is. The children adapt. What else would they do?

The children play in the gutters when the flow is low, floating bits of debris—stone chips, the hollow stems of the reedy grass, occasional scraps of metal from the workshops—through the channel networks they have memorized with the devotion that lowland children give to street games and board games. They know the drainage system by heart: which channels connect, which weep-lines feed which gutters, which outflows run fast enough to carry a boat of folded polymer scrap all the way to the plateau's edge. They race their vessels. They bet on them. They have a whole taxonomy of watercraft—the long thin ones that run fast in straight channels, the broad stable ones that can navigate the rougher flows near the engine houses, the weighted ones that ride low and slow through the calcifying stretches where the mineral deposits have narrowed the gutters to a trickle. They know the system's rhythms better than some of the adult tenders. A child can tell you, by the sound of a particular engine's exhaust, whether the flow in Channel 17 will be high or low that afternoon. They learn it the way lowland children learn weather—by feel, by accumulation, by the patterned intuition that comes from living inside a system rather than observing it from outside.

The children also develop the mineral film, of course, but theirs is softer, newer, easily shed. They shed it and shed it again, and each time it reforms slightly different, tracking the changes in the water's mineral content as the seasons shift and the geothermal reservoir pulses. A child's skin is a record of the water's recent history, legible to anyone who knows how to read the subtle variations in color and texture—the orange flush that means iron has spiked, the white chalkiness that means calcium is dominant, the rare blue-green tinge that the old tenders associate with deep reservoir shifts and watch carefully, though they will not say exactly what they are watching for.

---

The tenders are the closest thing the works have to a professional class, though the distinction is less formal than it sounds. A tender is simply someone who has learned to read the system and respond to it—not to control it, never to control it, but to participate in its maintenance the way a gardener participates in a garden, by observation and adjustment, by learning what the system wants and helping it achieve that rather than imposing an external logic. The training is apprenticeship, long and informal. A young person who shows an aptitude—meaning an aptitude for patience, for pattern recognition, for the kind of non-verbal communication that the engine houses demand—will be taken on by an older tender and walked through the works slowly, over years, learning the weep-lines and the pressure points and the calcification patterns and the specific sound each engine makes when it is running correctly and the specific sound it makes when it is beginning to fail.

Failure here is not catastrophic. The engines fail the way organs fail in an aging body—gradually, with compensations, the load shifting to neighboring systems, the sound changing in ways that a skilled tender can detect weeks before the failure becomes functionally significant. The tenders do not repair engines so much as they ease them through their decline. When an engine finally calcifies beyond use—when the mineral deposits have narrowed its chambers and seized its pistons and sealed its valves into immobility—it is not removed. It is left in place, and a new engine is installed beside it, fed by the same water, exhausting through the same channels, gradually accumulating its own mineral burden and beginning the long process of becoming part of the stone. The works are full of dead engines, calcified into near-geological features, their original shapes still visible beneath the accretion like bones beneath skin. The tenders call them "the elders" and use them as anchor points for navigation, as landmarks, as the stable reference points in a system that is otherwise always shifting, always weeping, always slowly becoming something else.

The tenders have their own internal hierarchy, which is based not on authority but on attunement. The most respected tenders are not the ones who can fix the most problems; they are the ones who can sense the most subtle shifts in the system's equilibrium, who can predict a pressure change days before the gauges register it, who can hear in the compound sound of a dozen engines the specific note of a single component beginning to drift toward failure. These tenders—the ones the others call "deep-listeners," though the term is unofficial and somewhat reverent—are the closest thing the works have to leadership, though they do not lead in any conventional sense. They advise. They indicate. They write notes and pass them during the hand-signal conferences in the engine houses, and their notes carry weight because they have been earned, because they are grounded in decades of listening to a sound that never stops.

---

The residents who are not tenders—the ones who live along the edges, where the noise drops and the views open—still participate in the works, because the works are the only economy the plateau has ever had. They work in the workshops that fabricate replacement parts from the iron and steel that arrives by cart from the lowlands. They work in the distillation houses, where the mineral water is processed for export—the calcium and iron and trace elements that the lowland industries pay for, the warm alkaline water that the lowland spas bottle and sell as curative. They work in the kitchens and the dormitories and the few shops that supply the plateau's modest needs. But their relationship to the works is different from the tenders' relationship. They see the works from outside, as a fact of their environment rather than an object of their attention. They hear the sound as background rather than as information. They do not dream of engines.

They do dream of water, though. Everyone here dreams of water. Not the ocean, which most of them have never seen, but the specific water of the plateau—warm, mineral, rising—appearing in dreams as rooms that flood slowly from the floor up, as walls that sweat and breathe, as the feeling of standing on stone that has become soft and yielding underfoot. The dreams are not anxious, the residents say. They are not nightmares. They are simply the mind's way of processing what the body already knows: that the water is always there, below and within and around, that the stone is only a temporary arrangement of minerals that the water is slowly rearranging, that the distinction between solid and liquid here is a matter of time and patience and nothing more.

The edge-dwellers, as the tenders sometimes call them, have a social life that the tenders do not. The residential quarters have gathering halls—old structures, built in the imperial period, their walls thick with mineral scale, their floors gently heaved by the thermal expansion beneath—that host meals, discussions, the occasional musical performance. The music is percussion. No other form survives the engine-sound. Drums, metal sheets, the bare stone itself struck with bare hands or padded mallets, creating rhythms that layer over the engine-din and temporarily dominate it, or interweave with it, or comment on it in ways that the audience can feel in their chests even when they cannot distinguish the human-made sound from the machine-made sound. The musicians are among the most respected members of the edge-dwelling community. They are not tenders, but they have something of the tender's attunement—the ability to hear the system's sound and work within it rather than against it. A good musician can make the engines seem to participate in the performance, the pistons falling into phase with the drumbeat, the steam-exhausts sighing in syncopation, though whether this is real or perceptual no one can say and no one much cares.

---

The old ones are the ones who have been here longest, who have passed beyond the mineral film into something more thorough, more integrated. They move slowly, which is appropriate, because the works move slowly, because the time of this place is geological, and the old ones have adapted to that time. They sit in the warmest corners of the residential quarters—near the vents where the geothermal heat is strongest, where the stone floor radiates against their feet and the air holds the moisture like a held breath—and they speak rarely, and when they speak they say things that the younger residents find obscure, elliptical, difficult to parse. The old ones talk about the water the way other people talk about the dead: with familiarity, with a lack of sentimentality that can seem cold to outsiders but is simply the comfort of long acquaintance. They say the water is restless today. They say the water is thinking about something. They say the water remembers the time before the works, when the plateau was just stone and heat and pressure, and the water had to find its own way up, carving its own channels through the sandstone over millennia, and the weeping was slower then, more tentative, the way a child weeps before it learns that weeping is something it can do whenever it wants.

The old ones are not considered senile. They are considered deep-listeners who have stopped listening to the engines and started listening to the water itself, which is a different thing, a more fundamental thing, the thing that the engines are only translating into mechanical terms. The water has its own sound, beneath and within the engine-din, and the old ones can hear it because they have been hearing it for so long that it has become the ground on which all other sounds are built. They say the water is patient. They say the water will outlast the works. They say this without sadness, without any sense of loss, the way a person might say that the sky will outlast the buildings beneath it, which is simply the nature of sky and building, no tragedy involved.

The old ones die and are buried in the old cisterns, the ones that have been dry for centuries, sealed off from the active water system, their walls thick with mineral deposits and their floors deep with the sediment of ages. The bodies are laid in the sediment and covered with more sediment, and over the decades the mineral water finds its way in—through cracks, through weep-lines, through the slow penetration of moisture that is not quite liquid and not quite vapor—and begins the work of depositing calcium and iron and trace elements in the tissue, replacing the organic minerals with the minerals of the plateau, turning bone to stone, turning flesh to the same pale scale that coats the walls and the pipes and the living skin of the residents. The tenders say this is not burial but continuation. The dead do not leave. They calcify. They join the elders, the dead engines, the mineral deposits, the stone itself. They become part of the system they tended, the environment they inhabited, the slow wet warm process that has been running longer than any record and will run longer still.

---

There is a word that the residents use for themselves, a word that does not translate well into the lowland languages because it combines the concepts of resident and participant and patient and mineral, all at once, in a way that the lowland tongues have no category for. The word sounds like the sound the water makes when it finds a new crack and begins the slow process of widening it—a kind of soft exhalation, a release, a giving-in to the path of least resistance. When the residents say this word, they are saying: I belong to this place in the way that the water belongs to it, which is to say I am held by it and I am shaped by it and I will leave marks on it and it will leave marks on me and eventually the distinction between me and it will become meaningless, a matter of mineral content rather than identity, and I will be here in the stone and the water and the sound long after I have stopped being anything that the lowlands would recognize as a person.

They do not say this word often. They do not need to. It is evident in the way they walk, which is slow and deliberate and attuned to the heave of the pavement. It is evident in the way they touch the walls, which is frequent and casual and affectionate, the way you touch the shoulder of someone you have known so long that the touch is not communication but simply acknowledgment, a confirmation of continued presence. It is evident in the way they stand in the engine houses, eyes half-closed, bodies swaying almost imperceptibly with the piston-cycles, their breath timed to the exhalations of steam, their heartbeats—though this cannot be proven, though the physicians insist it is not possible—their heartbeats synchronized with the deep slow rhythm of the works themselves, the rhythm that is not mechanical or geological or biological but something that has emerged from the interaction of all three, a rhythm that belongs only to this place, that can be felt but not measured, that can be joined but not controlled.

The people of the Weeping Works are not trapped here. They are not exiles or prisoners or the desperate poor with nowhere else to go. They are people who have found their form, the way the works have found their form—not through effort but through the exhaustion of alternatives, through the slow patient process of being worn into the shape that the environment demands, and finding, at the end of that wearing, that the shape fits, that it is comfortable, that it is home. They stay because they cannot imagine leaving. They stay because the silence of the lowlands would be unbearable, a kind of deafness in reverse. They stay because the water is still rising, and the stone is still weeping, and the engines are still cycling, and they are part of it now, mineral and flesh and sound, indistinguishable in the long run from the warm wet walls they tend, the slow steady process they serve, the equilibrium that has found them and held them and will not let them go.
