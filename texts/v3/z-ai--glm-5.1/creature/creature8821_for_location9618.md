# What Lives Here

The people are the last thing you notice, and this is how they prefer it.

---

They call themselves a word that does not translate cleanly. The closest approximation might be *those-who-stay-when-leaving-was-possible*, but this is too long and too proud. Another attempt: *the-ones-the-plateau-has-not-broken*. But this implies a contest, and they do not experience their lives as adversarial. Perhaps the truest translation is simply *still-here*, spoken with the particular emphasis they give to the second syllable—a rising inflection that turns statement into wonder, as if they are reminding themselves.

There are not many of them. Perhaps two hundred across the entire plateau, though no census has ever been attempted and would be meaningless if it were. They live in groups of eight to fifteen—family is not quite the right word, nor is clan, nor is band—centered on the confluences where three or more dimples create something like abundance. Each group claims no territory. Territory implies boundaries, and boundaries imply something that can be owned, and the plateau has been teaching a different lesson for two billion years. Instead, they have *patterns*: routes between dimples, schedules of visitation, an oral-and-signed cartography so precise that a child of ten can navigate from one side of the plateau to the other using only cairns and the angle of the wind.

They are small. This is not malnutrition, though food is scarce. This is adaptation, executed across generations so gradually that no single lifetime could perceive it. The people of the plateau stand between one hundred and forty and one hundred and fifty-five centimeters tall, and their limbs are proportioned shorter than you might expect—compact, dense, difficult for the wind to catch. Their bodies have learned the same lesson the fauna learned: nothing large survives here unchanged. The-sound-that-hunts is the exception, and it pays for its size with a lifespan that seems, from the scattered evidence, to be brutal and brief.

Their skin is dark—not the darkness of equatorial ancestry alone but something denser, a brown that borders on black in certain light, as if they have absorbed the mineral heat of the stone they sleep against. The children are born lighter, and the darkening happens over years, slowly, like leather curing. The eldest among them have skin that seems to hold light rather than reflect it, and when they sit on the-body-that-remained in the afternoon they are nearly invisible, just a slightly different quality of warmth against the warm stone.

Their hands are what you notice, once you are close enough to notice anything. The fingers are long—longer than the proportions of their bodies would suggest—and the spaces between them are webbed halfway to the first knuckle. Not enough to call it a membrane. Enough to call it a silence. When they press their palms flat against each other, the webbing seals the gap, and the sign-language they use becomes a closed system, visible only to the one facing them. This is how they speak in the open, where the wind would tear words away. Their hands are instruments designed for a world that does not want to be heard.

---

They do not remember arriving. This is not the same as having always been here. Their stories—which they sign to each other in the shelter of the hummocks, the leaves thrumming overhead—tell of a time before the plateau, but it is a mythological before, not a historical one. In the stories, the before-place had silence in it. Not the absence of sound but the presence of a different kind of quiet, a quiet you could rest inside. They speak of this the way other cultures speak of Eden or the Golden Age: as a condition that was lost or given up or stolen, depending on which elder is telling the story.

They do not agree on how they came here. Some versions say they were driven. Some say they wandered. Some say they grew out of the dimples like the vegetation, which is clearly not literally true but points toward something they believe about themselves: that they belong to the plateau in the same way the hummocks do, that they are an eruption of life in a place that resists it, that their presence is as much a geological fact as the stone or the wind.

They do not leave. This requires emphasis. There are edges to the plateau—sheer escarpments where the worn-flat land falls away into lower terrain, into forests and rivers and places where the wind is merely weather. They know these edges exist. They visit them, sometimes, on purpose, to look down at the softer world below. And then they return.

You might call this entrapment. They do not. The word they use for home and the word they use for body are the same sign: both hands pressed flat against the sternum, fingers spread, a gesture that encompasses the self and the shelter and the ground beneath both.

---

A day in the life, then, though they do not measure days the way you do.

Dawn is the gentle time. The wind drops—not to silence, never to silence, but to a low moan that permits thought. The people emerge from their wind-shelters and press their bodies against the stone while it is still cool, absorbing the last of the night's chill before the sun brings the heat. This is when they touch each other most: foreheads to shoulders, palms to backs, long presses of contact that serve as greeting and reassurance and the simple mammalian need for warmth. They have a word for this that translates roughly to *the-reminder-that-we-are-not-wind*.

They check the dimples. This is the first task of every day, the checking, because the water is never certain. A dimple that held a pool yesterday may be empty this morning—drained through a new crack, absorbed by a thirsty hummock, simply evaporated in the night. They move between the depressions with an ease that speaks of countless repetitions, and when they find water they do not celebrate. They note it. Their hands move in the quick signing that passes for record-keeping: *the Throat is full, the Place That Swallows is half, the Wound Where Water Remembers is gone again*. Someone will carve a mark into a cairn nearby—a series of notches that only they can read, tracking the water's comings and goings across decades.

They drink. They fill the bladders they make from the inner bark of the hummock-vines, sealed with resin that seeps from the plants when they are cut. They water the small plots they tend in the lee of the shelters—patches of the broad-leaved vine that they cultivate for food, the leaves and stems edible when young, the roots starchy and dense. They do not call this farming. The word they use translates closer to *convincing*, as if they are persuading the plants to stay rather than commanding them to grow.

Midday is for shelter. The stone grows too hot to touch, and the wind rises to its full voice, and the people retreat into their half-domes and sleep. They sleep deeply here, more deeply than seems safe—a sleep that resembles hibernation more than rest, the metabolism slowing, the breath shallowing. They say the plateau sleeps with them, that the warmth from below lulls them, that the stone dreams through their bodies. They do not find this strange. They find it courteous.

Evening is for work. The wind drops again, briefly, and they emerge to repair what the day has damaged. A wind-shelter has lost three stones; they fit them back with practiced hands. A cairn has shifted; they realign it. A hummock has begun to lean toward a new dimple, and they must decide: follow it, or wait for it to return? This is a question with no right answer. They have signs for both choices, and they argue about it the way other cultures argue about politics or religion—passionately, at length, with elaborate hand-gestures that the wind cannot take from them.

Night is for stories. Gathered in the largest shelter, pressed close, the hummocks thrumming outside and the stone warm beneath them, they sign the old narratives. The one about the first person who learned to listen to the stone. The one about the-sound-that-hunts and why it runs toward the wind. The one about the hummock that walked so far it became a mountain again. The one they do not tell to outsiders, the one about the sound below the sound, the hum in the deep stone, the thing that was here before.

Their children watch and learn. The children of the plateau are born into the wind and do not know it is extraordinary. They learn to sign before they can walk. They learn to read the cairns before they can interpret the stars. They play games that teach survival without calling it teaching: who can find the hidden water, who can move closest to a hummock without disturbing the burrowers, who can stand in the open wind the longest before retreating. The toys they make reflect the world they know—the vine-figures with too many fingers, the miniature cairns, the tiny wind-shelters that they test against the breath of their own mouths.

---

They do not count their years the way you do. They have no calendar, no seasons, no reliable cycle of warmth and cold to mark the passage of time. Instead, they track the hummocks. Each great plant-cluster has a name and a history, and the people know where it was and where it is and where it is going. When a child is born, they note which hummock is nearest, and that becomes the child's anchor—not a birth-date but a birth-place, a moving coordinate that shifts as the humlock drifts. *I was born when the-Throat-Gatherer was still near the Wound*, they will say, and everyone understands the timeline, because everyone knows where the-Throat-Gatherer was and how long it took to get where it is now.

This means their sense of time is inseparable from their sense of place. The past is not a series of events but a series of locations, and to remember something is to remember where the hummocks were when it happened. They do not say *three years ago*. They say *when the-Singer was still close enough to hear the Wound's water*. Their history is written in the drift of vegetation, and it is precise, and it is accurate, and it is completely illegible to anyone who has not spent a lifetime watching the green things walk.

---

They bury their dead in the dimples that have lost their water.

This is practical—the cracked stone allows digging in a way the solid plateau does not—and it is something more. They believe, or they behave as if they believe, that the dead become part of the water-cycle. That the body, laid in a dry dimple, somehow calls the rain back. That the dead remember water on behalf of the living. When a dimple refills after a burial, they do not call it coincidence. They call it *returning*, and the sign they use is the same one they use for the morning mist condensing on the stone.

They do not mark the graves. The cairns are for the living—for navigation, for water-tracking, for the work of staying. The dead do not need markers because the dead are not gone. They are below. They are the warmth that rises through the stone. They are the hum that you hear when you press your ear against the-body-that-remained at night.

When asked about this—carefully, through intermediaries, after weeks of earning the right to ask—the eldest among them made a sign that the intermediary struggled to translate. The closest they could come: *the dead are what the plateau is dreaming*. And then another sign, quicker, almost dismissive: *we are what it dreams when it is awake*.

---

They have a relationship with the-sound-that-hunts that is difficult to categorize.

They do not fear it. They respect it the way you might respect a storm—something that cannot be controlled and must instead be anticipated. They know its patterns, or they know them as well as anything's patterns can be known on a plateau where everything moves. They can predict, with reasonable accuracy, which hummocks it will circle on a given night. They leave it alone. It leaves them alone. This is not a truce. A truce implies two parties who might otherwise conflict. This is simply an absence of overlap, a recognition that they occupy different niches in the plateau's ecology.

But they also track it. They follow its claw-marks in the thin soil, note its direction, estimate its speed. Not to avoid it—they already know where it is, usually—but to understand it. They have a story about the-sound-that-hunts that they tell differently from their other stories. In most of their narratives, the wind is a character—capricious, ancient, sometimes cruel, sometimes merely indifferent. But in the story of the-sound-that-hunts, the wind is not the antagonist or the environment. The wind is what the creature is *chasing*.

They believe—though believe is perhaps too strong a word for something they treat more as a working hypothesis—that the-sound-that-hunts is trying to catch the wind. That its endless running, its straight-line paths, its refusal to sleep, is pursuit. The creature runs toward the loudest sound because it believes it can reach the source. It cannot. It never will. But it runs anyway, because running is what it does, because it was made for this, because the plateau has taught it the same lesson it has taught everything else: that endurance is the only virtue that matters.

When the people tell this story, they make a sign that has no translation. The intermediary I spoke with held her hands in front of her chest, fingers spread and trembling, and said: *It means the thing that is beautiful because it will fail. It means the thing that is beautiful because it does not know it will fail. It means—* and then she stopped, because the wind had shifted, and she had to close her hands against it.

---

They are not unhappy.

This is important to understand, because the plateau is a place that seems to demand misery. The wind, the emptiness, the constant motion, the unreliability of water, the absence of comfort—by any external measure, this is a place that should produce a hard people, a bitter people, a people who dream of escape.

They do not dream of escape. They dream of the plateau. They dream of the hummocks drifting and the water pooling and the stone warm beneath them and the sound that never stops, and in their dreams the sound is not suffering but music—not human music, not melody or rhythm, but something older and larger, the sound of a world becoming itself across eons, and they are part of that becoming, small and temporary and essential.

They laugh. Their laughter is silent—a shaking of the shoulders, a widening of the eyes, hands that sign *funny* and *true* and *again*. They make jokes about the wind, about the water, about the-sound-that-hunts and its hopeless pursuit. They tease each other about which hummock they were born near. They tell stories about outsiders who came and could not tolerate the sound and left with their ears covered, and they tell these stories with something gentler than contempt—pity, perhaps, or the sadness of people who know that what they have cannot be shared because it cannot be explained.

They love. Their signs for love are many and specific: the love between partners who share a shelter, the love between parent and child, the love between friends who walk the cairns together, the love for the plateau itself, which is the largest love and the most difficult to translate. When they sign *I love you* to another person, they press their palm against their own chest and then reach out and touch the other's chest—*my body and your body and the stone beneath both*. When they sign *I love the plateau*, they simply stand still, feet flat, arms at their sides, letting the wind move through them.

---

There is more. There is always more. The way they make music by striking the-body-that-remained with stones, finding the resonant frequencies that the wind has already discovered, adding their own small rhythm to the plateau's endless drone. The way they paint with the mineral-heavy rain, red and orange and occasional startling blue, on the interior walls of their shelters, abstract patterns that might be maps or might be prayers or might be something for which no word exists in any language but theirs. The way they age—their hair turning not gray but white, their skin darkening further, their bodies shrinking until the eldest among them seem carved from the same stone they walk on, small and dense and warm to the touch.

The way they die. Quietly, usually. In their sleep, the metabolism slowing and then stopping, the breath shallowing and then ceasing, the body cooling against the warm stone. They find them in the dawn, already stiff, already seeming more like the plateau than like people. They carry them to the nearest dry dimple. They lay them in the cracked earth. They do not speak, because there is nothing to say that the stone does not already know.

And then they return to the work of staying.

The wind continues. The hummocks drift. The water pools and vanishes. The people endure, which is not the same as surviving, which is not the same as persisting, which is a word they do not use because it implies effort. They do not effort. They are. They are here, still-here, those-who-stay-when-leaving-was-possible, and they will be here when you are gone, and they will be here when the hummocks have walked to the plateau's edge, and they will be here when the wind finally—finally, after two billion years—finds somewhere else to go.

Or they will not. And the plateau will not notice. And the stone will hold the warmth of their bodies the way it holds everything: patiently, completely, until it too is worn away.

This is what lives here.
