# Who Lives in the Weft

---

## The Settlers (who call themselves only *here*—the word for *us* and the word for *this place* are the same sound, a short closed vowel like a drop hitting water)

They are not numerous. Forty-three at the last counting, though counting requires agreement about who counts, and some of them spend seasons away, following the fruit-bats to the lower ridge-settlements where the Canopy's relatives grow in looser stands and the hunting is easier. They come back. Or they don't. The Weft does not require return. The Weft does not notice departure.

Their bodies have taken the shape of the place. Not dramatically—not in the way stories sometimes tell, with skin going green or fingers lengthening into root-like things. But they have become *dense*. The children here do not grow tall. The food—trapped rodent, gathered grub, the starchy pith of certain Canopy-roots, the fruit of the vine the settlers have encouraged for generations—supports growth but not extension. The adults are compact, broad in the shoulder, deep in the chest. They breathe the thick air efficiently. Their lungs are large for their frames, adapted to the constant mild hypoxia of altitude and the oxygen-poor miasma of decomposition that passes for atmosphere at the root-floor level.

Their skin darkens easily and does not lighten again. Even the children who spend time in the lower settlements return to the Weft and resume the darkening—something in the light here, filtered as it is through layers of leaf and the particular chemistry of the air, triggers melanin production that does not reverse. The oldest among them—Keel, who may be sixty or may be seventy, who was born in a dwelling that has since been absorbed back into the Canopy's trunk-structure—has skin the color of wet bark. Not a metaphor. The same dark umber, the same slight sheen in the rain.

Their feet have changed. They go barefoot on the root-floor and the walkways and the notched ladders, and their feet have widened, their toes splayed, the spaces between them capable of gripping the curved surfaces of roots. The children learn to climb before they learn to walk well—the dwellings are suspended, the interesting things are overhead, and a child who cannot grip is a child who falls. The falls here are short but the surfaces are hard and wet and the root-floor is never clean. Infection takes the injured quickly in this moisture. The settlers have a plant-paste they apply to wounds—some secretion from a Canopy-adjacent fungus that the ants also use to seal breaches in their mounds—and it works often enough that the population holds steady. But there are gaps in the age-cohorts where a child's foot slipped and the rot got in before the paste could set.

They do not have leaders. This is not a principle; it is a practical outcome. The Weft's turbulence does not permit long-term planning. The water moves, the Canopy grows, the cat-thing takes someone's child, a dwelling's wall-root decides to send out a new branch at an angle that makes the structure unsound, and the response must be *immediate* and *local* and cannot wait for consultation. Authority here is whatever person is closest to the problem and has the relevant knowledge. Keel knows the Canopy's growth-patterns better than anyone, and so when a root behaves unexpectedly, people wait for Keel's assessment—but Keel cannot tell them what to do, only what is happening, and they decide. The cat-thing is hunted by whoever is strong enough and fast enough and near enough when it takes. There is no negotiation about this. There is no meeting. There is only the tracking and the killing and the skinning and the distribution of the meat to the household that lost the child, which eats it or doesn't according to its own grief, which is private, which is not discussed.

Their language is a dialect of the ridge-tongue, modified by the acoustics of the Weft. They speak softly. Not from secretiveness but because sound carries here in unexpected ways—the root-structure transmits vibration, and a shout at the wrong frequency can alert the cat-thing or disturb a colony of the biting ants or simply *bounce* off the wet surfaces until it becomes unparseable noise. They have developed a set of clicks and glottal stops that cut through the ambient sound without adding to it. To visitors from the lower settlements, they seem to be speaking in code. They are. The code is *this sound means attention and this sound means danger and this sound means come and this sound means the Canopy is doing something we did not anticipate*, and the distinctions matter, and the children learn them before they learn the words for emotions or colors or the names of the dead.

The dead are not buried. There is no soil deep enough, and the root-mat would process a body before it could be properly interred, which is *fine*—which is the rule of the place—but which is not the way the settlers choose to return what they have been given. Instead the dead are offered to the Canopy directly. A platform is constructed in the upper branches, among the fruit-bats and the territorial birds, and the body is placed there, and the Canopy takes it. This is not a metaphor either. Aerial roots descend. They find the body. They do what roots do, which is grow into and through and around, and over a season the platform is a node of thickened wood where the root-structure has *incorporated* what was offered. The settlers visit these nodes. They touch them. The wood is warm—some metabolic process, some heat of growth—and the shape of the incorporated one is sometimes still present in the way the roots have thickened, a hip's curve, the long line of a spine, a suggestion of the skull's dome before the root-tissue smoothed and sealed and made the shape its own. The settlers know where their people are. They are *in* the Canopy. They are the Canopy, in the same way the Canopy is the stone and the water and the light that has been fixed into sugar and cellulose. This is not comfort. Comfort is not a category the Weft recognizes. But it is *fact*, and facts are what the settlers deal in.

## The Ants (who have no name for themselves because naming requires the concept of *self as distinct from colony*, which they do not possess)

They are not a single colony. The settlers think of them as one entity because the behavior is uniform and the pheromone-trails connect in a network that spans the entire Weft, but in truth there are at least seven distinct genetic lineages that have formed a supercolony, their borders dissolved through some ancient accommodation that no one remembers because the ants do not remember. They simply *are*, and what they are is *everywhere*.

Their numbers are beyond counting. The settlers estimate—based on the rate at which a fallen fruit is processed, based on the visible traffic on the root-surface, based on the volume of the mounds at the trunk-bases—at least eighty million. Possibly more. Possibly many more. The Weft's root-mat is their territory, and they work it constantly: breaking down organic material, farming the sap-sucking insects whose secretions they harvest, defending the colony's edges from the incursions of ant-species from the lower ridge that sometimes attempt to establish footholds in the Weft's resource-rich environment.

They tolerate the settlers. This is a separate tolerance from the Canopy's—a parallel negotiation conducted at a different scale. The settlers do not threaten the ants. The settlers' waste—food scraps, shed skin, hair, the occasional small corpse of a trapped rodent—is *useful* to the ants, and the ants process it with efficiency. In return the ants do not swarm the dwellings. They do not enter the living spaces unless something has died there that needs processing, and in that case their arrival is almost welcome, because a dead thing in the dwelling's close air is a source of infection, and the ants are thorough.

But the tolerance has conditions. The settlers know not to block the pheromone-trails. A dwelling's wall-root must not sever a major trail-route; if it begins to, the settlers cut the root and redirect it, because the alternative is the ants finding a new path, which might be *through* the dwelling, which has happened, which resulted in a household abandoning a dwelling for the three days it took the ants to process whatever they were processing and move on. The ants do not attack the settlers in the way they attack intruder-colonies. They simply *go through* them. The going-through is sufficient.

The settlers have also learned to read the ants. The ants know things. They know when the Canopy is distressed—a fungal infection in a root, a boring beetle in a trunk—before the settlers can see it, because the ants respond: they increase traffic to the affected area, they alter their farming behavior, they sometimes seal off sections of the mound-network in ways that suggest quarantine. A settler who watches the ants can learn what the Canopy is experiencing. Keel learned the Canopy's rhythms this way, as a child, lying on the root-floor with eyes at ant-level, watching the patterns for hours until the patterns became legible.

## The Cat-Thing (which is not a cat, which has no name, which the settlers call *the long one* in their click-modified dialect)

It is the only mammal in the Weft that hunts on purpose. The fruit-bats hunt insects in a sense, but their hunting is mechanical—a filter-feeding of the air, a statistical harvesting of the bug-medium. The rodents gnaw and scavenge and occasionally eat insects, but they are prey-animals whose strategy is speed and reproduction and the hope of not being noticed.

The long one notices. The long one *attends*.

It is the size of a large cat but stretched, as though the template were cat and the execution were ferret. Its spine is long and flexible, its legs short, its body a tube of dense muscle that can flatten and extend and compress through gaps that seem too small for it. Its fur is the dark red-brown of wet bark—the same color as the oldest roots, the same color as Keel's skin—and in the shadow of the lower Canopy it is effectively invisible. Its eyes are large and amber and forward-facing. Its teeth are more than a cat's teeth would be—more numerous, sharper, less differentiated between slicing and puncturing, a mouth designed for grabbing and holding and not releasing.

It hunts the rodents primarily. It also hunts the frogs, the lizards, the small birds that roost in the lower branches, and—when opportunity presents—the children of the settlers. This is not preference. This is not malice. The long one does not distinguish between prey-categories in the way that a human might. A small warm body that moves on the root-floor is *food* in the same way that a rodent is food, and the long one's assessment involves size and accessibility and the likelihood of injury during capture, not the taxonomic kingdom of the target.

It is solitary. There are others of its kind in the ridge-forest—the settlers have seen them in the lower settlements, slightly different in coloration, the same stretched architecture—but in the Weft there is only one. It has a range that includes the entire Weft and extends beyond it, and it patrols this range on a cycle that the settlers have mapped: every nine days, approximately, it moves through the dwelling-level at night, and the settlers know to keep their children in the interior rooms during those passages, and sometimes this is enough.

The long one is not their enemy. This is an important distinction. The settlers kill it when they can, when it has taken a child and the trail is fresh and the grief is sharp enough to make the pursuit feel like something other than futility, but they do not hate it. They understand that it is doing what the Weft requires of it, which is processing the small warm things that move on the root-floor, which is the same thing the ants are doing at their scale, the same thing the Canopy is doing at its scale. The Weft processes. The long one processes. The settlers process, in their way—trapping rodents, harvesting grubs, eating the fruit of the vine and the pith of the root and occasionally the meat of the long one itself, which is tough and strong-tasting and carries the deep mineral note of a creature that has lived its whole life in the body of another living thing.

When they kill the long one, another arrives within a season. The Weft has a niche, and the niche is filled, and the filling is not personal.

## The Frogs (who are many species, who are one sound, who are the Weft's pulse made audible)

The settlers know twenty-three distinct types. This is not all of them. New ones appear occasionally—a morph that has developed in an isolated pool high in the Canopy, a color-variant that may be a new species or may be an old one expressing differently in response to some shift in the root-mat's chemistry. The settlers do not catalogue them. They learn the ones that matter.

What matters: which ones are poisonous. The bright ones, mostly—the frogs of the Weft follow the standard tropic signal, their toxicity advertised by coloration that shouts *do not eat* to anything with color vision. But the shout is not always honest, and some of the bright ones are merely bright, and some of the dull ones carry neurotoxins that will stop a child's breathing in the time it takes to carry them to a dwelling and apply the plant-paste, which does not work on neurotoxins because the plant-paste is for infection, not for poison, and there is no cure for the poison except time and luck and the strength of the small body that has been dosed.

The children learn the frogs the way they learn the ants—by attention, by proximity, by the slow accumulation of pattern-recognition that the settlers do not call knowledge because they do not have a word for knowledge that is separate from *the way things are*. The frogs are. Some of them kill. This is one of the ways things are.

The sound they make is the Weft's heartbeat. Not metaphorically—the rhythm of the calling follows the rhythm of the water-availability, which follows the cycle of condensation, which follows the movement of the clouds through the saddle. The frogs call loudest when the water is highest, which is the early morning, which is when the cloud-migration passes through and deposits its moisture on every surface. The calling builds through the night—the frogs of the evening shift starting as the day-calling birds settle, the nocturnal species joining in sequence, the peak density of sound arriving just before dawn when every frog in the Weft is calling at once and the air is *solid* with it, a wall of voiced amphibian need that vibrates in the chest and makes the root-floor tremble and the water in the pools shiver with the sympathetic resonance of ten thousand throats.

Then the light comes, filtered and dim, and the calling diminishes, species by species, and by midmorning only the day-callers remain, and the sound-field is the insects again, and the water, and the creak of the Canopy's slow growth.

## The Canopy (which is the Weft, which is the substrate and the structure and the process, which does not think but which *decides*)

It began as a seed. Probably. The settlers have no creation story for the Canopy—it was here when they came, it was here before the basalt was basalt, it was here before the concept of *here* coalesced into a place that could be named. But logic suggests a seed, a fig seed, deposited in a crack in the stone by a bird or a bat or the wind, finding purchase in the moisture that emerged from the rock, sending down roots, sending up shoots, beginning the long process of becoming what it is.

What it is: a single organism that may also be many organisms, a network of trunks and roots and branches that covers a hundred meters of basalt saddle and extends its roots into the stone itself, breaking it, carrying it away in increments too small to perceive, dissolving the foundation on which it sits with the slow patience of water and growth and the acid secretions of its root-tips. The Canopy is eating the mountain. Not quickly. Not dramatically. But *persistently*, in the way that all living things persist, because living is a process of consumption and incorporation and the Canopy is very much alive.

It does not think. The settlers are clear about this. They do not personify the Canopy, do not attribute intention or consciousness to it, do not pray to it or make offerings beyond the dead, which are not offerings but *returns*. The Canopy grows toward light and water and away from instability and disease. It responds to stimuli. It has preferences in the limited sense that a plant can prefer—the way a vine prefers a trellis, the way a root prefers a fracture in the stone. But it does not *want*. It does not *decide* in the way a deciding-thing decides. Its decisions are the decisions of chemistry and physics and the ancient encoded instructions of its genome, and they are no less total for being mechanical.

And yet.

The settlers have observed things. They do not discuss them as observations because they do not have a category for them, but they have *seen*. A dwelling that was failing—the settlers within it ill, the air wrong, the water pooling in ways that suggested the root-mat was not draining properly—would sometimes find its wall-roots growing in new directions, opening gaps, improving the ventilation, redirecting the water-flow. An aerial root that descended near a child's sleeping-platform would sometimes thicken faster than expected, creating a rail before the child was old enough to need one, as though the Canopy had anticipated a requirement. The settlers do not say *the Canopy cares*. They say *the Canopy grows*, and they note that the growing sometimes produces results that align with the settlers' needs, and they do not know if this is coincidence or response, and they do not spend time deciding because the distinction does not change what they do, which is tend.

They tend the Canopy. They guide its roots and clear its wounds and protect it from the fungi that would hollow it from within. The Canopy shelters them and feeds them and incorporates their dead. The relationship is not symbiosis in any strict sense—the Canopy would continue without them, as it continued before them—but it is also not *nothing*. It is a pattern of interaction that has developed over generations, a mutual accommodation that requires neither consciousness nor intention, only the slow feedback of action and response.

The Canopy is the Weft in the way that the ocean is the ocean's animals' habitat—it is the medium, the context, the thing within which everything else exists. But it is also a *participant*, a living body with its own processes and its own trajectory, and it is growing, still, always, sending out new roots and new branches and filling the space the basalt provides with more of itself, more wood, more leaf, more root, more life, more *process*, until the boundary between the Canopy and the Weft becomes meaningless and what remains is a single continuous living thing that happens to contain other living things, the way a body contains cells, the way a coral reef contains fish.

## And the Others

The birds, iridescent and territorial, who do not know they are beautiful, who know only that *this branch is mine* and *that insect is mine* and *you will not take it*.

The fruit-bats, who number in the tens of thousands, who roost in the upper Canopy in clusters that the branches strain to support, who emerge at dusk in a stream of leathery wings and high sharp calls that dominate the sound-field for twenty minutes before they disperse to forage on the lower ridge, who return before dawn with bellies full of fruit and the seeds of the ridge-forest's plants passing through their digestive tracts, seeds that will be deposited on the Canopy's branches and in the root-mat and in the settlers' dwellings, seeds that will sprout and be consumed by the ants or rot into the substrate or occasionally find purchase and begin the long process of becoming something new, something that the Canopy will incorporate or outcompete or simply grow around, as it grows around everything.

The lizards, who are small and quick and eat the insects and are eaten by the long one and the birds and occasionally by the children, who catch them for sport and release them or kill them for food depending on the household's needs.

The snakes, thin and bright, who move through the canopy with a deliberation that distinguishes their rustle from the wind's, who hunt the frogs and the lizards and the small birds, who are themselves hunted by nothing except time and the occasional territorial bird mobbing that drives them from a roost.

The fungi, who are not animals but who are also not not-animals, who exist in the Weft as a constant presence—fruiting and collapsing and fruiting again, their mycelia threading through the root-mat in networks as dense as the Canopy's wood, processing the dead material that the ants have not reached, breaking down the complex molecules of wood and leaf and insect-exoskeleton into simpler compounds that the Canopy's roots can absorb. The fungi are the Weft's digestive system. They are also its immune system—some of them produce compounds that suppress the growth of other fungi, maintaining a balance that prevents any single decomposer from dominating and consuming the Canopy faster than it can grow. The settlers know the fungi by their fruiting bodies—pale and wet, pushing up overnight, some of them edible, some of them poisonous, one of them producing the secretion that seals wounds and slows infection, the plant-paste that is not a plant at all but a mushroom, which the settlers do not know because the category *mushroom* does not exist in their vocabulary separate from *thing that grows from the root-mat and may be useful or may be dangerous*.

The water, which is not alive but which moves through the Weft as though it were, which emerges from the stone and falls from the leaves and pools in the root-mat and flows between the roots in channels that shift with the growth of the wood, which carries dissolved minerals and organic compounds and the larvae of insects and the spores of fungi and the waste-products of every living thing in the Weft, which is the medium of exchange, the currency of the system, the blood of this body that is not a body but functions as one.

## What Lives in the Weft

Everything. Everything that can fit, everything that can find a niche, everything that can survive the turbulence and the moisture and the competition and the constant processing of living and dying that the place requires.

The Weft is full. Not in the way a container is full, with a surface and a limit and a point at which nothing more can be added. Full in the way a process is full—running at capacity, every input being processed, every output being captured and used as input for something else, the system in a state of dynamic equilibrium that looks from the outside like chaos and feels from the inside like *home*.

The settlers do not own the Weft. The ants do not own it. The long one does not own it. The Canopy does not own it, because the Canopy *is* it, and a thing cannot own itself. The Weft is a commons in the most absolute sense—a shared space sustained by the shared activity of everything within it, with no central authority and no external purpose and no goal beyond continuation.

And everything in it is *of* it. The settlers' bodies are built from the Canopy's fruit and the trapped rodents and the water that falls from the leaves. The Canopy's wood is built from the basalt's minerals and the settlers' dead and the nitrogen fixed by the bacteria in the root-mat. The frogs' calls are shaped by the acoustics of the wood and the water. The ants' trails follow the Canopy's growth. The long one's coloration matches the Canopy's bark. There is nothing here that is not *from* here, that does not carry the signature of this place in its chemistry and its form and its behavior.

The Weft does not care about you. But the Weft *contains* you, if you are in it, and it will process you as it processes everything, and it will make you into something that is *of* the Weft, whether you are a settler growing a dwelling from living roots or a child learning which frogs will kill you or an ant following a pheromone-trail across a trunk that was someone's wall or a body on a platform being slowly incorporated into the structure of the living cathedral that is the only shelter you have ever known.

This is who lives in the Weft. Everything. Everyone. Processed and processing. Alive and being made more alive by the continuous turbulent activity of living that this place performs without pause, without purpose, without end.
