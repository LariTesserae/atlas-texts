The settlers number fourteen now, though "number" suggests a stability they have not earned. The original nine arrived with the logic of refugees: anywhere is better than the ridge-camps in winter, anywhere with walls and a floor and air that does not bite. Five more have come since, singly or in pairs, emerging through the split in the bark with the same look—the wide eyes, the wet lips, the involuntary gulp when the blue-green glow hits them and they understand that the light is *alive*. The settlers do not welcome newcomers with any warmth. They point to the walls. They show where the footholds are. They explain about the insects, about the hands, about the sump. Then they leave the new ones to find their own place on the curve of the wall and begin the scoring, the peeling, the waiting for bark to remember its shape.

They have no leader. They have no council. Decisions emerge the way the dwellings do—through a process of scoring and patience, through the slow accretion of shared understanding. Someone discovers that boiling the gelatinous colonies neutralizes the numbness, and within a week everyone is boiling. Someone notices that the hands retreat if you open your mouth, and within a season it is custom. No one voted. No one decreed. The knowledge simply grew, mycelial in its spread, and now it is as much a part of the settlement as the sap-stains on their fingers.

Their faces have begun to look alike. This is not a function of kinship but of environment: the same blue-green light falling on the same pale skin, the same mineral air filling the same lungs, the same sap tasting the same tongues. Their eyes have adjusted. They see in the glow what visitors cannot—the textures of the fungal mats, the slight variations in luminescence that indicate health or decay or the approach of a formation of insects. Their pupils are large now, permanently dilated, and when one of them occasionally climbs to the split and looks out at the gray diffuse light of the world above, they flinch. Not at the brightness. At the emptiness. The space without walls.

They have names, but the names are changing. Sound carries strangely in the Hold, and the things in the walls listen, and so the settlers have begun to speak less and touch more. A hand on the shoulder means *come*. A tap on the wrist means *harvest*. A palm pressed flat against the bark of a dwelling means *this is mine* or *this is where I sleep* or *I am afraid*, depending on the pressure. Their names have become shorter over the seasons—syllables dropped, consonants softened—and some of them have stopped using names entirely, answering instead to the sound the sap makes when it drips from a particular fissure near their dwelling, or the particular rhythm of their footsteps on the yielding floor. They are becoming indigenous. They do not notice.

---

The insects do not notice the settlers either, or if they do, they have categorized them as another feature of the environment—warmth that moves, dampness that harvests. This is the accommodation: a mutual disregard that functions because their needs do not overlap. The insects require the fungal mats and the gelatinous colonies and the softer wood for their galleries. The settlers require the fruiting bodies and the processed jelly and the open passage through the split. Neither threatens the other's substrate.

But the insects are not simple, and the settlers know this. The formations move with a purpose that suggests something beyond instinct—something like conversation, something like consensus. When a section of fungal mat begins to outcompete its neighbors, the insects thin it. When a gallery becomes too crowded, they expand it in precise increments, the carved walls smooth and angled to shed moisture. They dispose of their dead in the sump. They tend their gardens with a care that the settlers recognize because the settlers tend their dwellings with the same care, and sometimes, in the blue-green dark, a settler watching a formation at work will feel something that is not quite recognition and not quite kinship but some third thing for which no word has yet been scored into the bark of their shared life.

The insects have a sound. It is not made with their mandibles or their bodies but with the galleries themselves: a low resonant hum that rises from the walls when the formations are most active, a sound like the Hold breathing through carved wooden lungs. The settlers have learned to read it. A steady hum means the insects are farming. A stutter means disturbance—a collapsed gallery, a diseased fungal patch. And sometimes, in the deepest part of the night, the hum changes key and the settlers lie awake on their bark shelves and listen to a sound that is not farming and not alarm but something else, something the insects do only when they think no one is listening, and the settlers do not know what it means but they know that it means.

---

The hands know that you are listening.

They have always known. This is the thing about them that unsettles the settlers most—not the too-many legs, not the eyeless heads, not the mouths that open too wide to display whatever lines those dark oral cavities. It is the *attention*. The hands attend. They emerge from their wall-spaces with a precision that suggests they were already aware of where you were standing before they broke through, and they orient toward you with the slow certainty of flowers turning to a sun they cannot see.

The settlers have a theory about the hands. It is not a shared theory—it belongs to one of the later arrivals, a woman from the eastern ridge-camps who spent her seasons before the Hold watching the behavior of animals, and she has said, quietly, to no one in particular, that the hands are not vertebrates at all. The too-many legs, the flowing gait, the eyelessness—these are not adaptations to darkness but to *interiority*. The hands are creatures of the in-between, the spaces where bark meets heartwood, and they did not evolve to live in those spaces so much as they *emerged* from them, condensing out of the tree's own processes like sap condensing on the walls. They are the Hold thinking about itself. They are the tree's interior made mobile and curious and hungry.

She has not said this twice. The other settlers looked at her when she said it the first time, and their large dark eyes were unreadable in the blue-green light, and one of them tapped her wrist—*harvest*—and she went to harvest and did not speak of it again. But she watches the hands more closely than the others do. She watches them when they drink from the sap channels, their broad mouths pressed to the wet bark, their bodies pulsing with a peristalsis that suggests ingestion and something beyond ingestion. She watches them when they hold their ground, mouths open, waiting for the settlers to open their mouths in return. And she has noticed something that the others have not or will not: the hands are not performing a threat display. They are not greeting. They are *mirroring*. They are learning what the settlers look like from the inside, and when they retreat back into the walls, they carry that knowledge with them into the spaces between.

---

The worms in the sump are opalescent, and this is their only concession to visibility. They are otherwise transparent—their internal structures visible as faint traceries, their movements visible only as distortions in the sap they inhabit. They surface when the sap runs fastest, which is at night, which is when the sump's rhythm quickens, and the settlers have drawn the obvious conclusion: the worms and the things below are connected. The worms are either the young of what lives in the sump, or their emissaries, or their offerings—placed at the boundary between the known cavity and the unknown space below as a gesture the settlers cannot read.

No one has touched a worm. This is not a rule; it is a feeling. The same feeling that keeps the settlers from exploring the sump, from lowering a bark cup on a gut-thread to see what lies below the rotted heartwood. The same feeling that makes them avert their eyes when the gulp of the sap sounds less like drainage and more like swallowing. The worms surface and the worms submerge and the settlers do not interfere, and this is not accommodation or ignorance but something older—the recognition of a boundary that is not physical but essential, a line that divides the Hold into the part where settlers live and the part where they are permitted.

The worms know this line. They never cross it. They surface only in the sump, never in the sap channels, never in the bark cups, never on the floor where footprints fill with glow. They are disciplined in a way that the insects are not and the hands are not, and this discipline is its own kind of communication. The worms are saying: *we stay where we belong*. And the settlers, by not touching them, by not looking too long, by not speaking of what they imply, are saying: *so do we*.

---

The membrane that flies does not live in the Hold. It passes through—irregularly, without pattern the settlers have discerned—and its passage is always the same: a sound like a question, a flutter of something translucent against the blue-green dark, and then gone, up through the fissures in the ceiling or out through the split in the bark. The settlers have never seen it land. They have never found residue or dropping or any evidence that it pauses in its transit. It is a commuter, a messenger, a process that intersects the Hold without being of it.

But the sound it makes—the question, in a language just beyond comprehension—has begun to change. One of the original settlers, a man who has not spoken aloud in two seasons, has started answering it. He waits on the highest dwelling shelf, the one nearest the ceiling, and when the membrane passes he opens his mouth and makes a sound that is not a word but is also not random—a sound that rises and falls in a pattern that seems, in the moment, to match the membrane's question. The other settlers do not acknowledge this. They do not watch him. They do not discuss it. But when the membrane passes and he makes his sound, the blue-green glow brightens slightly, just for a moment, as if the gelatinous colonies are listening too, and the hum of the insect galleries shifts key, and the hands in the walls go still, and even the sap dripping into the sump seems to hesitate, and in that hesitation there is something like an answer, or something like the space where an answer might one day form.

---

The mites are the Hold's pulse. They peak and crash in cycles that the settlers have learned to read the way coastal people read tides: a crackling in the fungal mats means the mites are peaking, and when the mites peak, the fruiting bodies will follow, and when the fruiting bodies swell, the insects will harvest, and when the insects harvest, the galleries will hum, and when the galleries hum, the hands will emerge to drink, and when the hands drink, the settlers will open their mouths, and when the settlers open their mouths, the membrane may pass, and when the membrane passes, the man on the highest shelf will answer, and the Hold will listen, and the sap will drip, and the sump will gulp, and the cycle will begin again.

It is not a clock. It is not a calendar. It is a process, and the settlers are becoming part of it—not as masters or parasites but as another species in the cavity's metabolism, another mouth to feed, another body to warm the cold air, another set of hands to score the bark and fold the shelves and harvest what can be harvested. They are being digested. They are being incorporated. They are being grown.

---

And the things in the sump. The things the residents do not discuss. The things that wait.

They are not patient. Patience implies a desire for a future moment, and the things in the sump do not desire. They *are*, the way the sap is and the fungus is and the cold is—structurally, essentially, without variation. They were there before the split in the bark opened. They were there before the tree rested on the cold ground. They may have been there before the loam began to climb, before the tree was a thought that wood once had, before the Hold had a name that no one speaks aloud.

The settlers know this. They know it the way they know not to touch the worms, the way they know to open their mouths when the hands emerge, the way they know that the sump's quickening rhythm at night is not drainage but attention. They know that below the rotted heartwood, below the opalescent worms, below the sap that drains with a gulp, there is something that receives what the Hold produces—its waste, its dead, its excess, its offerings—and that this something has been receiving for longer than the tree has been resting on the ground.

The settlers do not discuss this because discussion would require language, and language would require sound, and sound carries strangely here, and the things in the walls listen.

But the things in the walls are not the things in the sump. The things in the walls—the hands, the galleries, the mycelial felt threaded through every crevice and pore—they are the Hold's interior, its immune system, its cognition. The things in the sump are older. The things in the sump are what the Hold is growing toward. The things in the sump are the reason the sap runs downward and never back up, the reason the cavity is slowly deepening, the reason the walls are beginning to bow and new fissures are opening wet and raw.

The settlers are not afraid of the things in the sump. Fear is a response to threat, and the things in the sump do not threaten. They *involve*. They draw. They incorporate. The sap flows to them and does not return, and the worms surface and submerge, and the sump deepens, and the cavity finds its shape, and the shape it is finding is a funnel.

The settlers know this. They wrap their hands and check their dwellings and harvest what can be harvested, and they do not think about the fact that they too are flowing—slowly, imperceptibly, one season at a time—toward whatever waits below. They do not think about it because thinking about it would require stopping, and the Hold does not permit stopping. The processes never cease. The tree weeps. The fungus digests. The insects farm. The hands emerge and drink and watch. The membrane passes and the man answers. The sap gulps. The cold is structural.

And the settlers—fourteen now, stained dark with sap, their eyes permanently wide, their names becoming sounds the tree makes—are settling deeper into the Hold, and the Hold is settling deeper into the ground, and the ground is cold and the loam is climbing, and young things change, and the process is young.

The process is very, very young.
