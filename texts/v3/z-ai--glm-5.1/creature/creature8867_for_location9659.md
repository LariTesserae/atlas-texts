# The Condensate — Who Lives Here

---

The locals call themselves nothing special. When you ask what they are—what word defines the forty thousand who persist in this wet, dark, unfinished place—they look at you like you've asked what they breathe. They breathe the air. They are the people. The distinction has never occurred to them, and your asking makes them suspicious of you, a little, the way you'd be suspicious of someone who needs the obvious explained.

But they are not like you.

---

Consider Maren, who is sixty-three and has never seen sunlight.

She knows the concept. She studied it in school—there are schools, three of them, sealed and humming with the same counter-frequencies as the sleeping chambers. She knows that somewhere the sky is blue and the light is warm and water falls in discrete events called rain. She has seen images. They look fake to her, like the old illustrations of far planets: technically correct, emotionally inaccessible. The light in those images seems aggressive. Hostile. She doesn't say this to outsiders because they look at her with such pity when she does, and the pity is worse than the dark.

Maren works the eastern deposition arrays. She walks the scaffolding twice daily, checking the crystalline fuzz for irregularities, adjusting the mineral content of the condensate feed. She wears a sealed suit, but she's modified hers. The helmet's faceplate has a thin crack near the left temple—has had it for eleven years—and she hasn't repaired it because the tiny seep of wet air that enters reminds her she's alive. This is against regulation. She doesn't care. At sixty-three, she's outlived two supervisors and both of the biologists who told her the crack would kill her.

Her hands know things her mind doesn't. When she presses her palms to the deposition wall, she can feel where the lattice is growing wrong—a density issue, a crystallographic flaw—before the instruments register it. She says it's vibration. She says the bad growth feels different, like a word misspoken in a sentence you otherwise understand. The younger workers think she's mystical. She's not. She's just been touching the walls for forty-five years, and the walls have taught her their grammar.

She sleeps in a chamber on the fourteenth floor of a building that grew from a seed-stone so old no one remembers who laid it. The counter-frequency generator above her bed produces a tone that cancels the chittering, but she turns it off sometimes, in the deep dark of the seasonal night, and lets the sound come in. The chitterers nest in the cable-veins just outside her window. She can hear their waste-sound, the byproduct of their piezoelectric feast, and it reminds her of something she cannot name. Not music. Not language. Something more like the sound a body makes when it's working properly—the thrum of blood, the click of joints, the wet machinery of living.

She is not lonely. She has never been lonely. The city is too present for loneliness, too alive with its own becoming, and she is part of that becoming, her hands on the walls, her breath in the crack of her helmet, her body filtering the same condensate that filters through the pipes beneath her feet.

When she dies—she has thought about this, because at sixty-three you think about it—she wants them to put her in the deep channels. Not the crematorium, not the mineral recovery vats. She wants the ray-things to find her, the pale undulating filter-feeders in the main flow. She wants to become part of the movement she's spent her life maintaining. She hasn't told anyone this. They wouldn't understand. They'd think it was morbid. But Maren knows: the fauna don't consume. They incorporate. They transform. The ray-things will take the mineral of her and make it part of their translucent flickering bodies, and her calcium will glow briefly in the maintenance lights before dying into dark, and the dark is where she's always lived anyway.

---

Consider the Hesh, which is what the sealed-lab biologists call the family of crawling, segmented things that move through the condensation gutters in coordinated waves.

The Hesh are not individuals. This is the part that took the biologists decades to understand, and some of them still argue about it in the way biologists argue about anything that threatens their frameworks. The Hesh are a distributed organism. Each fingernail-length segment is a node in a network, and the network thinks the way a neural net thinks—slowly, collectively, through the aggregation of simple signals into complex behavior. The waves you see in the gutters are not migrations. They are computations.

The Hesh are solving the city.

Not metaphorically. The gutters they move through carry condensate, and the condensate carries mineral, and the mineral feeds the deposition systems, and the deposition systems grow the walls and channels and scaffolding. The Hesh sense density differentials, flow rates, crystallographic stress. They move toward problems and away from solutions. Their bodies, in motion, redistribute particulate—carrying it from oversaturated channels to undersaturated ones, smoothing the mineral load the way a hand smooths a sheet, and the deposition systems respond to the smoothed load by growing more evenly.

The older families know this. They don't use the word *computation*. They say the Hesh *dream* the walls true. Maren says this, and she means it literally, the way you might say a surgeon *cuts* the tumor out, or a river *carves* the canyon. The Hesh dream. The walls grow. The city becomes.

The Hesh do not notice you. You are not part of their computation. You stand in the gutter and they flow around your boots like water around a stone, and they do not alter their course or their rhythm or their dream, because you are not a variable in the equation they're solving. You could stand there forever and they would flow around you forever, and the walls would grow true around your skeleton, and in ten thousand years the city would incorporate you the way it incorporates everything: slowly, patiently, one mineral layer at a time.

The Hesh are older than the city. The biologists suspect they are older than the ice. They found evidence in the deep bores—segments preserved in permafrost, dormant but not dead, still capable of movement when warmed. The implication is troubling: the Hesh were here before the settlement, before the outpost, before the first stone was laid. They were here when the plateau was nothing but ice and young basalt and the slow geological groan of a land not yet decided on its shape. They were dreaming something, then. Dreaming the ice, perhaps. Dreaming the cold.

And then the city came, and the city was warm and wet and humming, and the Hesh woke into a new equation, and they began to dream something else.

No one knows what. The biologists in their sealed labs have models, but the models are wrong because the models assume the Hesh are optimizing for something the biologists can name: stability, efficiency, equilibrium. But the Hesh are not optimizing. They are *becoming*. The distinction matters. Optimization is a process with a goal. Becoming is a process without one. The Hesh are not trying to reach a final state. They are trying to become a process that can continue becoming.

The city is their medium. The condensate is their language. The walls they dream true are their body, growing layer by layer, centimeter by centimeter, in the dark.

Consider Dael, who is nineteen and has seen sunlight once.

He went up with a survey team to the upper ridgeline—the provisional one, the sketch that might be revised—where the cloud base thins and, on rare occasions, breaks. He wore a sealed suit, the proper kind, no cracks. He carried instruments. He was there to measure the deposition rate on the exposed scaffolding, which grows differently in the thin air above the condensation zone.

The clouds broke for eleven minutes.

He didn't scream. He's proud of that. Some do, their first time—the light hits them and it's wrong, it's too much, it's a violence after a lifetime of diffuse glow. But Dael just stood there, inside his helmet, and let it happen. The light was white and gold and it came from a single point in the sky, a point so intense it had a physical presence, a weight, a pressure on his faceplate. He could feel it warming the suit's exterior. He could feel the condensate on his suit evaporating for the first time in his life, the water that had been his constant companion since birth turning to vapor and vanishing upward, and he watched it go, and he thought: *so that's what happens.*

He hasn't gone up again. Not because he's afraid, but because the eleven minutes unsettled something in him that he hasn't finished examining. The light was beautiful. He can admit that. But it was also *irreversible*. The condensate that evaporated didn't come back. The water that rose didn't fall again. In the city, water is constant—it condenses, it flows, it's harvested, it deposits, it condenses again. Nothing is lost. Nothing ends. The cycles are cycles, and the cycles are the city's heartbeat, and the heartbeat doesn't stop.

But in the light, the water left. It escaped the cycle. It became something else—vapor, gas, something thin and invisible and gone.

He thinks about this in the dark, in his sleeping chamber, with the counter-frequency humming and the chitterers dampening outside. He thinks about leaving. Not the city—he can't imagine living anywhere else—but the cycle. The constant, closed, patient cycle of condensation and growth and becoming. He thinks about what it would mean to evaporate. To rise. To not come back.

He hasn't told anyone this. The older families would be horrified. Maren, who he works with on the eastern arrays, would look at him with a confusion that would feel like grief. The biologists would want to study him—stress response, they'd call it, sensory overload from the light exposure. But Dael knows it's not that. It's not damage. It's a different kind of becoming than the one the city offers.

He touches the walls, like Maren does. He feels the lattice growing. But where Maren feels grammar, Dael feels a cage.

He's nineteen. He has time to decide. The city is patient. The Hesh are dreaming. The water will condense on his skin in the morning, and he will wipe it away, and it will condense again, and again, and again, until one day it doesn't.

Consider the Chitterers, who eat sound.

They nest in the cable-veins above the streets, and they are the reason the city's hum never crescendos into destruction. They are piezoelectric creatures, their bodies converting mechanical vibration into chemical energy, and they are—depending on your perspective—either the city's immune system or its most successful parasites.

They are not smart. A single Chitterer is a simple mechanism: a body like a flattened teardrop, chitinous and dark, with gripping appendages at the wide end and a vibrating membrane at the narrow end. The membrane absorbs the cable's frequency; the body converts it; the byproduct is the chittering sound, a waste frequency too thin and too rapid to be absorbed by other Chitterers, which dissipates into the wet air and dies.

But collectively, collectively—

The Chitterers respond to the city's hum the way white blood cells respond to infection. When a section of infrastructure grows too loud—when a deposition node is overproducing, when a filtration channel is running at excess capacity, when a generator is straining toward failure—the Chitterers gather. They multiply. They blanket the cable-veins around the noise source, absorbing, dampening, converting, until the frequency drops back toward equilibrium. And when the frequency drops, the food supply drops, and the Chitterers can no longer sustain their numbers, and they die back, their chitinous bodies falling into the condensation gutters where the Hesh flow over them and incorporate their mineral into the dream.

It is, the biologists say, the most elegant feedback loop they've ever encountered. It is also, they note with the discomfort that has become their professional signature, completely accidental. No one designed the Chitterers. No one introduced them. They emerged from the same conditions that produced the Hesh and the ray-things and the clicking leg-things in the gaps between buildings—conditions of cold, wet, dark, mineral-rich, and humming. The city built the conditions. The conditions built the fauna. The fauna regulate the city.

The Chitterers do not know they are regulating anything. They are eating. They are reproducing. They are dying. They are as indifferent to the city's survival as the Hesh are to Mael's existential crisis, or as the condensate is to the distinction between falling and forming. They simply are, and their being happens to serve the city's becoming, and the city's becoming happens to produce more of the conditions they need to be.

Symbiosis, if you're generous. Mutual tolerance, if you're honest. Indifferent co-creation, if you're Dael, standing in the dark, listening to the chittering that is the sound of waste, the sound of excess being absorbed, the sound of a system that doesn't know it's a system maintaining itself.

---

Consider the Ray-things, the filter-feeders in the deep pipes.

They have no name the locals use. The biologists call them *Lamniforma condensatis*, a taxonomic placeholder that reveals more about the limits of taxonomy than about the creatures themselves. They are flat and eyeless and translucent, and they undulate through the main flow channels with an elegance that seems impossible in something that evolved in a pipe, and their edges flicker with bioluminescence that dies the instant external light touches it.

The biologists have been trying to study the bioluminescence for years. It's impossible. The moment they deploy a probe with a lamp, the light goes out. The moment they remove the lamp, it comes back. They've tried infrared, ultraviolet, acoustic imaging—every technique that might let them observe without illuminating—and every technique fails. The Ray-things sense observation. They know when they're being watched. And they refuse.

This is not a word the biologists use. *Refuse* implies agency, intention, choice. The biologists prefer *reactive extinction response*—a reflexive shutdown of bioluminescent production in the presence of detectable electromagnetic radiation above a certain threshold. But the threshold shifts. It shifts depending on the wavelength, the intensity, the angle of incidence. It shifts depending on whether the Ray-things are alone or in groups. It shifts depending on factors the biologists haven't identified yet, can't control, can't even name.

Maren calls them the Shy Ones. She says they glow in the dark, when no one's looking, and the dark is where they live, and they don't want to be seen in their own home. She says this with absolute sincerity, and the biologists dismiss it as anthropomorphism, and Maren dismisses the biologists as people who can't imagine a mind that doesn't want to be understood.

The Ray-things filter-feed on the smaller organisms in the flow—the Hesh nodes, the larvae of the Chitterers, the myriad microscopic lifeforms that the condensate carries. They keep the channels clear. Without them, the pipes would clog within a season, the filtration systems would back up, the deposition systems would fail, the walls would stop growing, and the city would slowly, over years, dissolve back into the raw young stone from which it emerged.

The Ray-things do not know this. Or perhaps they do. Perhaps the glow they produce in the unobserved dark is a language, a communication, a way of saying *here I am* to others of their kind, a way of mapping the flow and the darkness and the deep channels of the city's circulatory system. Perhaps the glow is art. Perhaps it is memory. Perhaps it is a way of marking the boundaries of a territory that has no edges, only currents.

No one knows. No one may ever know. The Ray-things have made their position clear: they will exist in the dark, or they will not exist at all.

---

Consider the Building, which is not a building but the city itself, which is not a city but a process, which is not a process but a becoming.

The deposition systems print the city from condensate and mineral. The Hesh dream the deposition toward truth. The Chitterers dampen the excess. The Ray-things clear the channels. The locals maintain the systems that maintain the walls that maintain the conditions that maintain the fauna that maintain the systems.

But who is maintaining the becoming?

No one. Everyone. The question is wrong. The becoming is not maintained; the becoming *is*. It is the fundamental condition of this place—geologically immature, atmospherically over-saturated, tectonically undecided. The plateau groans because it has not finished becoming a plateau. The mountains are provisional because they have not finished becoming mountains. The city is printing itself because it has not finished becoming a city, and the fauna are thriving because they have not finished becoming fauna, and the locals—Maren with her cracked helmet, Dael with his eleven minutes of light, the forty thousand with their sealed homes and filtered water and counter-frequency dreams—have not finished becoming whatever they are becoming either.

The condensation is the medium. The water gives up, again and again, surrenders its vapor and becomes liquid and flows and is harvested and carries mineral and deposits crystal and evaporates and condenses again. The cycle is not a circle. A circle returns to its beginning. The cycle is a spiral, and each turn adds a layer to the walls, and the walls grow thicker, and the city grows denser, and the fauna grow more numerous, and the hum grows more complex, and the Chitterers multiply to dampen it, and the Hesh dream it true, and the Ray-things glow in the dark where no one can see, and the locals sleep in their sealed chambers and wake in the morning to find condensation on their windows, again, always, the same water in a new configuration, the same becoming in a new layer, the same patience in a new day.

---

Stand where Maren stands, at the edge of the eastern arrays, and touch the wall.

Feel the crystalline fuzz accumulating beneath your palm. Feel the hum in the stone, the bass pulse of the deep channels, the chittering overhead, the click of leg-things in the gaps. Feel the water condensing on your skin, your lashes, your lips. Feel the cold that is not a temperature but a state of matter, a refusal of heat, a patience so deep it has become a place.

The city is still becoming.

The people who live here are still becoming.

The fauna that dream and eat and glow and die are still becoming.

And you—standing at the edge, touching the wall, feeling the vibration of something that has not yet decided what it is—you are becoming too. The condensate is forming on your skin, and the mineral is in the water, and the deposition is slow but it is constant, and in a thousand years, if you stand here long enough, the city will grow around you, and the Hesh will flow over you, and the Ray-things will glow in the dark where you used to be, and the chittering will dampen the frequency of your fading heartbeat, and the water will condense, and the water will flow, and the water will become.

It will be a long time before anything here is done.

That is why they stay.
