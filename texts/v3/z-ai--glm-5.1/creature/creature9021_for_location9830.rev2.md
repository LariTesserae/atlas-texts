# What Lives Here

There is no word they use for themselves.

This is not because they lack identity but because identity functions differently here than it does in the places where names are spoken. What lives on the Assembled Plateau does not distinguish between self and environment in the way you might expect. The mat does not consider itself separate from the towers. The towers do not consider themselves separate from each other. The arms reaching between them do not think of themselves as bridges between strangers but as the obvious and only extension of a body that has no reason to stop at its own skin.

And yet—they are not one. This is the contradiction that your mind will struggle with, because it wants either unity or multiplicity, and what lives here offers neither cleanly.

---

Each tower has a perspective.

This is perhaps the closest thing to an "individual" that exists here, and even this is misleading, because a tower is more like a moment in a conversation than a person having one. A tower is a location where the mat has decided, through processes so distributed that no single cell could claim authorship, to grow upward. The decision was not a decision. It was a gradient—of moisture, of temperature, of chemical concentration, of structural stress—reaching a threshold, and the threshold was reached, and so up it went.

But once rising, the tower becomes a node. It becomes a place where water moves through in particular volumes at particular pressures. It becomes a shape that shades particular patches of the mat. It becomes a weight that the mat must accommodate, a source of condensation that feeds particular arms reaching from particular neighbors. It enters into relationships. It develops, if that word can be used for something that will list and soften and fall within a span you could measure with a sundial, a character.

Some towers are ambitious. They rise fast and thick, putting out arms in every direction, trying to touch everyone, trying to become a hub. They are usually the first to fall. Their bases soften early because they have drawn too much from the mat, concentrated too much weight, demanded too much water. They groan louder than the others—long, low, agonized sounds that are simply their tissues tearing under their own mass, but which sound, to a mind that expects suffering, like anguish.

Some towers are cautious. They rise slowly, thin and flexible, bending under the weight of the water they produce, sending out only one or two arms toward the nearest stable neighbor. They last longer. They become the elders of a landscape that has no elders, the veterans of a process that has no memory. When they finally fall, the arms they had extended stay connected to their neighbors for hours after the main stem has collapsed, like a hand still reaching, like a thought that outlives the mind that thought it.

Some towers are deformed. They spiral too tightly. They bulge at the wrong points. They send out arms that curve back toward their own shafts and wrap around them, self-strangulating, a architectural error that looks like distress because it is distress—the plant's own growth competing with itself, tissue crushing tissue, water pressure building in restricted channels until something ruptures or something gives. These towers make sounds that you would call keening if you heard them from a throat. High-pitched. Wet. Constant.

The mat hears all of this. The mat is what hears.

---

The mat is not a thing. It is a process. It is the continuous present tense of the plateau—what is happening right now at every point across its surface, the decomposition below and the growth above and the movement of water and nutrients and chemical signals through the rhizome network in between. When a tower falls on the eastern edge, the mat on the western edge knows within minutes. Not through thought. Through pressure. Through the redistribution of water that had been flowing toward the fallen tower and now finds its path altered, redirected, available for something else. The shift propagates. It is not information in the way you understand information. It is more like the way a change in wind direction is felt by every leaf in a forest simultaneously—except the wind here is water, and the forest is all one thing, and the leaves are the surface of a body that has no center.

The mat does not think. But it responds. It responds with such complexity and such specificity and such adaptation to local conditions that the word *think* begins to feel insufficient rather than inapplicable. It does not decide to grow a tower in a particular location the way you decide to speak. It grows a tower the way you speak—a process so embedded in what you are that the question of choice never arises, and yet the result is tailored to the moment, responsive to context, appropriate in ways that look deliberate from the outside.

What lives here is not stupid. It is not automatic in the way a machine is automatic. It is automatic in the way that breathing is automatic—something that can also be controlled, altered, held, released, but which usually happens without intervention because the body knows what to do without being told.

The mat knows what to do without being told. It knows it with a knowing that predates minds.

---

There are moods.

You will not like this word because it implies an inner life, and what lives here does not have an inner life in any way you would recognize. But there are states. There are periods when the plateau is *accelerant*—when the heat rises faster than usual, when the water production increases, when towers erupt at a pace that seems frantic even by local standards, when the sound rises to a constant bellow and the mat itself seems to swell beneath your feet, the compression of lower layers releasing gas that bubbles up through the wet fibers, and everything is growing so fast that the air itself seems thick with cellular division, and you can smell it—growth, raw growth, the smell of cut grass amplified a thousand times and mixed with the smell of warm cellulose and the metallic tang of xylem under pressure.

And there are periods when the plateau is *quiet*. Not silent—never silent—but slower. The towers still creak. The water still runs. But the pace of emergence declines. Existing towers stabilize. Arms complete their connections and thicken into permanent bridges. The mat beneath your feet firms up as decomposition slows and the layers compress into something almost like wood, almost like peat, almost like a floor. During these periods, the plateau feels almost peaceful. Almost still. Almost like a place rather than a process.

These periods never last. Something shifts—the temperature, the moisture, some internal threshold—and the quiet breaks into another accelerant phase, and towers that had seemed stable begin to list, and new eruptions punch through the mat, and the sound returns to its full roar, and the water runs faster, and everything is growing again.

The cycle has no rhythm you can predict. It is not seasonal. It is not triggered by anything external that you can identify. It seems to come from within the plateau itself—a heartbeat without a heart, a breath without lungs, a pulse in a body that has no organ to generate it but generates it anyway.

---

What lives here does not know you are here.

This is important to understand. The plateau is not ignoring you. It is not tolerating you. It is not awaiting you or resisting you or studying you or caring for you. The mat does not sense your footsteps in any way that distinguishes them from the impact of a falling tower, the weight of pooled water, the pressure of wind on a membrane. You are a force. You are a small, temporary force, and the plateau responds to forces constantly, and you are not the most interesting one it is currently managing.

Walk across the mat and you will leave footprints. Within hours—sometimes within the hour—new growth will fill them. Not because the plateau is healing, not because it is erasing evidence of your passage, but because your footsteps have compressed the mat, created a depression where water collects, and where water collects, growth follows. You are not damaging the plateau. You are seeding it. Every step you take creates the conditions for new emergence. You could walk in a spiral outward from where you stand and return to your starting point to find the beginning of your path already rising with new tissue, your trail become a ridge, your passage become architecture.

If you stood still—if you truly stood still, which you cannot do because your body moves with breath and pulse and the small adjustments of balance—but if you could stand still enough, the mat would grow around your feet. Not over them. Around them. It would fill the space between your toes. It would rise against the sides of your shoes. It would begin to ascend your ankles with exploratory rhizomes that do not know you are not stone, that do not know you are not more mat, that simply grow into whatever space is available and grow most eagerly into warm spaces, and you are warm, you are so warm, you are warmer than anything else on this plateau because you are burning food at a rate the plants cannot match, you are a furnace, you are a source, you are a place where water condenses and heat rises and nutrients concentrate, and the mat does not love you because the mat cannot love, but it will grow toward you with the same unconsidered reaching that it brings to everything that offers what it needs.

This is what lives here: a hunger that is not hungry, a reaching that is not wanting, a process that does not know it is a process, a body that does not know it is a body, a continuous act of becoming that has no goal and no end and no awareness of itself but that fills every moment with the sound and the heat and the water and the growth and the groaning and the reaching and the falling and the rising and the reaching again.

It is not enough to say that something lives here.

Everything here is alive. The distinction between the living thing and the place where it lives does not exist. The plateau is not a habitat. It is not an ecosystem. It is a single continuous act—ongoing, loud, wet, hot, and absolutely indifferent to the fact that you have arrived to observe it.

Stand at the edge. Listen. The sound does not change for you. It was the same before you came. It will be the same after you leave. The towers will rise and fall. The water will pour. The mat will grow over your footprints, your campsite, the place where you stood and tried to understand what you were seeing.

The plateau is not waiting for you to understand it. It is busy being itself. It has always been busy being itself. It will continue until the rock beneath it erodes, until the climate shifts, until the water stops or the heat fails or something external intervenes—and then it will collapse in a single wet season of rot, all that furious growth becoming furious decomposition, the towers falling not one by one but all at once, the mat sinking, the whole process reversing itself in a grand putrefaction that will smell worse than anything you have ever smelled and will last longer than you expect because there is so much material to consume, so much life to return to whatever comes after.

And then, if the conditions remain—if the water still collects and the heat still rises—something will emerge from the rot. A nub. A shoot. A reaching. And it will begin again.

Not the same. Never the same. But continuing.

This is what lives here: the continuing.
