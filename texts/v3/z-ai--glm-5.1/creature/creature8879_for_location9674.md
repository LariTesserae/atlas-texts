# The Inhabitants of the Manufactory

---

## The Drinkers

They do not have names for themselves.

This is not because they lack the capacity for designation—some among them recognize specific individuals by chemical signature, by the particular trace minerals embedded in their chitin, by the rhythm of their locomotion cycles. They know each other. They simply do not know that they know each other in any way that requires articulation. Language is a technology for beings who need to coordinate across gaps. There are no gaps here. When you live in the water that emerges from the same seams, when you drink from the same mineral flows, when your body chemistry is continuously refreshed by the same source, you are not separate enough from your neighbor to require introduction.

They are called many things by the few who have visited and tried to categorize them. *Manufactory fauna.* *Symbiotes.* *The drinker-clades.* None of these terms are wrong. None of them are sufficient.

The smallest among them—you might call them the hand-sized ones, though some are smaller than a thumb and others are broad as a dinner plate—live their entire lives within a few hundred meters of a single water seam. They are the sedentary castes, though they are not truly sedentary; they move, they drift, they reposition when the flow from their seam shifts slightly or when the population around a particularly productive gap grows dense enough to prompt dispersal. But they return. They always return. The seam is their world and their anchor and the thing that makes them what they are, and if a seam were to close—which none ever has, not in the memory of this place, not in the operational history that is recorded in the accumulated husks of their ancestors layered in the spaces beneath the floor—they would not know what to do. They would wait. They would wait until they became husks themselves, and then they would join the layers.

The larger ones—the dog-sized, the knee-high, the ones that move between towers with visible purpose—these are the travelers. Not nomads. Nomads implies choice, implies a pattern of movement dictated by season or resource availability. The travelers move because the Manufactory moves them, because something in the mineral content of the water or the subtle variations in floor temperature or the periodic vibrations that they can feel through their legs and you cannot creates circuits in their behavior that they follow as reliably as the water follows its gradients.

They carry things.

This is important, and easy to miss, because what they carry is small and dark and wet and indistinguishable—to your eyes—from the material of the floor or the walls or their own bodies. But they carry things. You see it in the way they move: a slight asymmetry, a density held against the ventral plates, a cargo that shifts when they climb the tower walls and that they deposit—where? Inside. Always inside the structures. In the openings that have no doors, in the chambers that you cannot easily access, in the spaces where the machinery breathes and clicks and maintains its ancient schedules.

They are feeding the Manufactory. Or the Manufactory is feeding them. Or the distinction between those two statements is meaningless here, where every process is a subprocess of something larger, where metabolism and mechanism are not opposed categories but adjacent functions in a cycle that has no true beginning and no end.

## The Congregations

Sometimes they gather in numbers that feel significant.

Not at the seams—at the seams, their clustering is functional, mechanical, a response to resource density. You can predict it. You can map it. The congregations are different. They happen in the open floor, in the shallow sheets of standing water, in the spaces between towers where nothing obvious draws them and nothing obvious holds them. Dozens at first, then hundreds, their dark bodies dotting the grey surface like characters in a script you cannot read.

They do not interact. Not in any way you can parse. They do not touch, do not communicate through sound or gesture or chemical release—at least not in any concentration you can detect. They simply *are*, together, in a place, for a duration, and then they disperse.

You spend a long time watching one of these gatherings from the upper opening of a tower. The creatures below move slowly, purposefully, but their purposes do not seem to intersect. They cross paths without acknowledgment. They occupy the same water without competition. It looks like a city square in the early morning, populated by strangers who happen to share a schedule and a space and nothing else.

But it is not random. After the third congregation, you begin to notice the pattern: they are arranging themselves. Not in rows or spirals or any geometry you recognize, but in densities, in clusters-within-clusters, in a distribution that looks, from above, like a heat map of something you cannot measure. The densest clusters form at specific points—points that correspond to nothing visible on the floor, no seam, no feature, no marker of any kind. But when you descend later and stand at one of those points, you feel it: a vibration. Not the general floor heartbeat but something deeper, something specific, a resonance that you can feel in your sternum and your teeth and the fluid of your inner ear.

They are listening.

Not to sound—they have no ears, no auditory apparatus that you can identify—but to something the Manufactory is doing at those locations, some process or exchange or event that occurs below the floor and communicates itself through the medium of the warm ceramalloy and the shallow water and their own legs pressed flat against the ridged surface.

They are reading the machinery.

And the machinery, perhaps, is reading them.

## The Husks

Death here is not what you expect.

You find the remains before you understand them. Small dark shapes on the floor, in the corners of structures, against the base of towers where the water runs in thin vertical channels. They look at first like debris—fragments of the Manufactory itself, chips or flakes or waste material from some long-completed fabrication. But they have joints. They have the ghost of segmentation. They have, if you look closely, the faintest impression of leg-positions, held in the moment of whatever stillness took them.

They are not decomposed. Nothing decomposes here. They are *dried*—desiccated in a place where the air is perpetually saturated, which should be impossible but is not, because the process that removes the moisture from their bodies is not atmospheric but chemical, an enzymatic breakdown that starts from within and works outward, converting the soft internal tissues into a substance that is chemically indistinguishable from the floor.

They become the Manufactory. Literally. Their chitin mineralizes into the same dark ceramalloy. Their fluids drain into the seams and return to the water. Their mass does not leave the system. Nothing leaves the system.

And the living ones consume the husks. Not all of them, not always, but often enough that you realize this too is a function—the recycling of biological material back into mineral form, the closing of a loop that the Manufactory perhaps cannot close on its own. The creatures eat their dead and excrete what the machinery needs, and the machinery provides the water that the creatures need, and the cycle is so old and so stable that the boundary between "organism" and "infrastructure" has become a matter of perspective rather than fact.

You find old husks—very old ones, you think, though you have no way to measure—embedded in the floor itself, partial outlines absorbed into the surface material like fossils in stone, except the stone is a floor that was made, and the fossils were made too, or were permitted to become what they became because the process of their becoming served the process of the Manufactory's continuing.

The floor is partly made of them.

You walk on them. You have been walking on them since you arrived.

## The Climbers

The ones that ascend the towers are different.

Not physically—not in any way you can isolate and point to as a marker of caste or specialization. They are the same species, if species is even the right word, the same general body plan, the same dark chitin and multiple legs and fluid movement. But their behavior marks them as something other than the seam-drinkers and the congregators and the floor-traversers.

They climb.

They climb to heights that the others do not reach, up the vertical ridged surfaces of the tallest towers, three hundred meters of warm wet ceramalloy, and they enter the openings and they do not come back down. Not immediately. Some of them stay inside for what seems like days—though days have no meaning here, with no sun and no shift and no marker of time except the unvarying pulse of the floor—and when they emerge, they are different.

Not visibly different. Not larger or smaller or differently colored. But they move with a specificity that the others lack, a directedness that suggests they have acquired something inside those towers, some information or instruction or chemical imperative that has rewired the priorities of their simple neural architecture.

These are the ones that carry things.

These are the ones that perform the maintenance.

You follow one once—follow it inside a tower, through a portal that has no door, into a space that is warm and wet and dim and filled with a geometry that makes your eyes ache. The interior is not a room. It is a mechanism—a three-dimensional lattice of surfaces and channels and voids, all the same dark material, all the same fine ridges, all weeping the same mineral water. The creature moves through this lattice with an ease you cannot match, its legs finding purchase on surfaces angled at degrees your body was not designed to navigate, its body flowing through gaps you would have to squeeze through.

It reaches a point deep inside the lattice where a channel has become occluded—not blocked, but narrowed, its walls thickened with a deposit of something crystalline and pale against the dark surface. The creature extends a set of mouthparts you have not seen before—delicate, hinged, almost surgical—and it scrapes. Slowly. Methodically. It clears the deposit. The channel opens. Water flows through it again, and the water makes a sound—barely audible, a thin mineral whisper—and the creature drinks from the cleared flow, and then it moves on.

It was not trained. It was not instructed, not in any way you would recognize. It *knew*. It knew because the Manufactory told it, through the vibration of the floor, through the mineral content of the water, through some signal embedded in the architecture of the place that its body has evolved—or been designed—to receive.

The climbers are the immune system of a machine that is not alive but needs an immune system anyway.

## The Deep Ones

You never see them clearly.

You see what they leave behind—larger husks, deeper impressions in the floor, channels in the standing water that take hours to fill back in after something has passed through. You see the displacement of the smaller creatures, the way they scatter from certain areas and then cautiously return, as if the space they abandoned is temporarily claimed by something they prefer not to be near.

You hear them. Or you feel them—low vibrations in the water, movements that disturb the thin sheets in ways the small creatures do not, ripples that propagate outward from something large and slow and below the visible horizon.

They are the size the text described: dog-sized, perhaps larger. But you suspect the text was conservative. You suspect there are specimens in the deeper sections, in the areas between towers that take days to cross, in the spaces where the floor gives way to something lower—grates, perhaps, or mesh, or surfaces perforated by holes large enough for something to pass through—that support forms too large for the corridors and chambers you have explored.

They do not come near the seams. They do not congregate. They do not climb.

They move through the deep water—water that is deeper than it should be, water that has accumulated in the low places between floor sections, water that is warm and mineral and has been circulating since the Manufactory began—and they do whatever they do there, in the parts of the system that are not accessible to you or to the smaller creatures or perhaps to anything that was not designed to operate at that scale.

Sometimes, when the floor vibrates at a frequency you have not felt before, the deep ones respond. You hear them—or you feel them, the displacement of water, the movement of mass—moving toward the source of the vibration, moving with a speed and purpose that the smaller creatures never exhibit. And then the vibration stops, and the deep ones return to their circuits, and the Manufactory continues as if nothing happened.

You think they are the repair crews for the machinery below the floor. You think they descend into the interlocking guts of the place and do what the climbers do for the towers, but at a scale that is harder to observe and harder to understand. You think the Manufactory has tiers of response, layers of biological infrastructure that correspond to layers of mechanical infrastructure, and the deep ones are the outermost layer, the one that handles the failures too large for the climbers and too complex for the automatic systems that the Manufactory has been running on since before any of these creatures existed.

You do not know what they eat. You do not know how they reproduce. You do not know if they are the same species as the smaller creatures or something else entirely, something that emerged from a different branch of the Manufactory's long biological experimentation, something that was designed for a different purpose and has been repurposed over millennia of quiet operation.

You do not follow them into the deep water.

You are not a component of this system, and the system knows it, and the deep ones know it, and the distance between you and them is maintained by a mutual understanding that has nothing to do with communication and everything to do with the fundamental logic of a place where everything that exists serves a function and you do not.

## The Young

They are born in the walls.

This is what you realize after you have been here long enough to notice the small ones—the very small ones, the ones the size of your fingernail, the ones that emerge from the gaps between floor plates and the seams where water rises and the junctions where tower meets floor with no visible join. They do not emerge from eggs or from the bodies of the adults. They emerge from the Manufactory itself.

Not from the material—as if the ceramalloy were somehow biological, somehow capable of gestation. But from spaces within the walls that seem to serve as incubation chambers, spaces that are warmer than the surrounding surfaces and more humid and that contain a thin film of the mineral water at a concentration higher than anywhere else. You find these spaces by accident, by reaching into a gap to test the depth of a wall and feeling something move against your fingers—something small and many-legged and warm and alive, something that has been growing in the heated wet dark of the Manufactory's interior the way a bacterium grows in a culture, fed by the same processes that feed the machinery.

The young are colorless when they emerge. Not black—the black comes later, as their chitin hardens and mineralizes, as the chemistry of the Manufactory infuses their exoskeletons with the same compounds that make the floor dark and the walls dark and the water dark when it pools deeply enough. They are translucent, almost invisible against the wet surfaces, and they move with a hesitancy that the adults never exhibit, testing the ridges of the floor with their legs, orienting themselves by vibrations you cannot feel, learning the geometry of a world that has no variation and no surprise and no need for learning except that learning happens anyway, because they are alive and living things learn even when there is nothing new to know.

They find the water within minutes. They drink. They darken. They become.

You watch one emerge from a gap near the base of a tower—a tower you have been using as a landmark, a point of reference in the sameness—and you watch it orient, and you watch it drink, and you watch it move away from the seam with the steady purposeless motion of something that has been given a function but has not yet discovered what that function is. And you think: this is what it is like to be born into a place that was already complete before you existed. To be a replacement part. To be expected. To be *included in the specification*.

The young do not know they are young. They do not know they are small. They know the warmth and the water and the ridges beneath their feet and the vibrations in the floor that tell them where to go and what to do when they get there. They know the Manufactory the way a blood cell knows a vein—not as a place but as a path, a direction, a set of constraints that are also a set of instructions.

They will never know they are components.

They will never know there is any other way to be.

## The Ones That Notice You

It happens rarely.

Most of the creatures ignore you the way they ignore each other—without acknowledgment, without curiosity, without the need to respond to your presence because your presence does not register as relevant to their function. You are not water. You are not a vibration they need to follow. You are not a deposit they need to clear or a channel they need to open. You are nothing, in the grammar of their existence, and so you are beneath notice.

But some of them—individuals, specific creatures you begin to recognize by the pattern of their movement or the texture of their chitin or the particular way they hold their bodies when they drink—some of them notice you. They pause when you pass. They turn what you assume are sensory organs toward you—organs you cannot identify, organs that might detect heat or vibration or chemistry or something else entirely. They do not approach. They do not flee. They simply *register* you, and then they return to whatever they were doing, and the encounter is over.

Except.

Except one of them follows you.

Not closely. Not with any apparent intent. But you notice—after hours of walking the open floor, after you have stopped paying attention to the creatures because they are as constant as the warmth and the water—you notice that one of the climbers has been behind you for some time. Not tracking you, not hunting you, not paralleling your course at a distance. Just... behind. In the direction you came from. As if it is returning from wherever you have been, or going wherever you are going, or simply moving in the same direction at the same speed for reasons that have nothing to do with you and everything to do with a pattern you cannot perceive.

You stop. It stops.

You wait. It waits.

You turn and walk toward it, and it does not move, and you get close enough to see the fine details of its body—the segmentation, the mineral traces in its chitin, the small orifices that might be mouths or might be something else, the way its legs hold it just above the surface of the warm wet floor—and it does not flinch, does not recoil, does not display any of the avoidance behaviors you might expect from a wild creature confronted by something unfamiliar.

It looks at you.

Not with eyes—it has nothing you would call eyes—but with whatever it uses to perceive the world, with whatever sensory apparatus allows it to navigate the Manufactory's geometries and perform its maintenance tasks and find its way back to the seams and the towers and the deep places where the important vibrations live. It looks at you and you feel *seen*, not as a threat or a resource or a curiosity, but as an anomaly. A reading that does not match the expected values. A variable that the system did not account for.

And then it turns and moves away, climbing the nearest tower with its unhurried fluidity, ascending to an opening and disappearing inside, and you are alone again on the warm wet floor with the grey light and the mineral air and the sound of almost nothing.

You think: it was reporting you. It was carrying information about you to wherever information goes in this place, to whatever process or subsystem handles the unexpected, to whatever part of the Manufactory is still capable of noticing that something is here that should not be.

You think: the Manufactory does not care about you. But its components might.

You think: you are being watched by a place that does not have eyes, monitored by creatures that do not have minds, included in calculations performed by machinery that does not have consciousness, and the result of those calculations—whatever they determine about you, whatever they decide to do about the anomaly of your presence—will be carried out by beings that do not know they are carrying anything out at all.

You think these things, and then you stop thinking them, because the water is rising at your feet and the floor is vibrating its ancient rhythm and the creatures are drinking from the seams and the Manufactory is running, has always been running, will continue to run, and your thoughts are not a component of its operation.

They are not included in the specification.

They do not matter here.

## What Lives Here

What lives here is not a collection of creatures. It is not a species or an ecosystem or a community or any of the categories you brought with you from places where biology makes its own rules and occupies its own spaces.

What lives here is a *function*.

The creatures are the function made flesh. They are the Manufactory's answer to problems that machinery alone could not solve—or could not solve efficiently enough, or could not solve without the flexibility that comes from metabolism and reproduction and the million small adaptations that living things perform without conscious direction. They are maintenance and recycling and sensing and response, distributed across a surface too vast for centralized control, operating on schedules too old for adjustment, performing tasks too specific for programming and too general for mechanism.

They do not know this.

They do not know anything, in the way that you know things—with reflection, with context, with the ability to imagine otherwise. They know the warmth and the water and the ridges and the vibrations, and they act on what they know, and their actions sustain the system that sustains them, and the cycle continues, and will continue, in the permanent grey light, in the permanent warm wet, in the permanent quiet that has weight.

What lives here is the Manufactory living.

And the Manufactory is not alive. And the Manufactory will never die. And the creatures that move through its corridors and drink from its seams and climb its towers and clear its channels and respond to its vibrations and carry its materials and recycle its dead and birth its young and watch its visitors with whatever passes for attention—all of this is just what it looks like when a process becomes a world.

You could call them inhabitants. You could call them denizens or residents or natives. But these words imply a separation between the creature and the place that does not exist here. They are not *in* the Manufactory. They are *of* it. They are the Manufactory in the same way that white blood cells are the body—not separate from the system, not serving the system, but *being* the system, performing its functions at a scale and in a manner that the system itself cannot achieve through structure alone.

They are the living tissue of a machine that does not know it has tissue.

They are the breath of a body that does not know it breathes.

They are what remains of whatever this place was made to be, now that the making is done and the only law is continuation.

They are what lives here.

And they will be living here, in the warm wet grey quiet, long after you have left, long after anyone remembers that there was ever anything here to visit, long after the word *Manufactory* has lost all meaning and the place itself has become so much a condition of existence that no one thinks to name it at all.

The water rises.

The creatures drink.

The floor vibrates.

Something is maintained.
