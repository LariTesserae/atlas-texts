# The Residents

The tool-users have no name for themselves. This is not because they lack language — they have one, embedded in the sound-wall like fossils in sediment — but because naming requires a boundary between self and not-self that they have never drawn. They know what they are the way the stomach knows hunger: internally, without reflection, without the need for a word. They are. The place is. The distinction has never been useful.

There are perhaps forty of them in the territory you crossed. Perhaps more. The counting is impossible because they do not stay separate. They merge. Not physically — or not only physically — but in the way they move through the dark, in the way their calls braid into the sound-wall and then separate again, in the way they share the work of living without negotiation or assignment. You might see three shapes at the lichen-line and then realize there are five, or two, or one shape that has somehow become large enough to seem like many. They are singular and plural at once. They operate by a logic of accumulation and dispersal that follows no hierarchy you would recognize.

Their bodies have forgotten the shape your body holds.

This is not mutation. This is not deformity. This is the long conversation between a form and its environment, continued over generations you cannot count because the generations happen fast here — faster than they should, faster than they have any right to. The tool-users are what happens when something spends enough time in a place that makes itself. They have been shaped by the dark, by the damp, by the sound, by the fourth thing that feeds the system from below.

They are low to the ground because the ground is where certainty lives. The canopy is chaos — falling trees, dropping creatures, the unpredictable descent of cold water from leaves that held it too long. The ground is stable. The ground has the lichen-lines, the pools, the roots that hold the soil against the river's shifting. So they are low. Their spines curve. Their limbs have shortened and thickened. They move on all fours easily, on two legs when they need a hand free for a tool, and sometimes on no legs at all — sometimes they press themselves flat against the warm wet earth and simply wait, becoming so still they seem to join the substrate, seeming to grow into the ground like the fungi do, and you would not know one was there unless the sound-wall shifted and you felt, more than heard, the sub-vocal hum they make when they are content.

Their skin is not skin anymore. It has been replaced — slowly, over whatever time has passed here — by something that serves them better. A hide, perhaps, though that word implies a toughness that isn't quite right. It is a membrane. It keeps the wet out and the warmth in, but it also lets things through. Information. Vibration. The sub-sonic communications of the place itself, the tremors in the stone that say the river is shifting, the pulse in the root-network that says a tree is preparing to fall. They feel these things the way you feel rain on your face: directly, without interpretation, without the delay of thought. Their membrane-skin is an organ of perception more versatile than any eye.

The eyes are still there. Two of them, in the usual position. But they have retreated. Not withered — they serve a function still — but surrendered their primacy. They are small and dark and wet, set deep beneath brow-ridges that have thickened to protect them from the constant movement, the branches, the falling debris, the claws of things that pass too close. The eyes see what the lichen illuminates and nothing more. They see the glow. They see the shapes of each other, moving. They see the brief bioluminescent flash of the larger predators and the steady pulse of the fungi and the cold steam of the glow rising from the floor. But the eyes are not how they navigate. Navigation is done by the ears, which have enlarged and complexed into structures you would not recognize as ears — flared cartilage, ridged and whorled like the inside of a shell, rotating independently, constantly adjusting to parse the sound-wall into its thousand useful strands.

They hear you breathing from a hundred meters. They hear your heartbeat from thirty. They hear the particular rhythm of your footsteps — the weight, the cadence, the hesitation — and they know from the sound alone that you are not from here, that you are wrong-shaped and wrong-paced and that the membrane of the place does not recognize you, does not open for you, does not include you in its calculations.

They do not attack. This is important. They are not aggressive. They have no territory to defend because the territory is them; to defend it would be to defend their own skin, which they do only when something tries to remove it. They watch you the way the rest of the fauna watches you — with assessment, with curiosity, with the constant low-grade alertness of things that live in a place where everything is always becoming something else. But they do not interfere. You are not their concern unless you become their concern, and you become their concern only if you stay.

If you stay, they will teach you.

Not intentionally. They do not have pedagogy. But the place teaches, and they are the place's method of instruction. They will allow you to follow them. They will let you watch the way they use the tool-stones — the hand-axes, the scrapers, the hammer-stones — and you will see that the tools are not used on the landscape but with it. A hand-axe breaks open a seed-pod, but only at the seam the pod has already weakened. A scraper clears the soil from stone, but only the soil that is already loose, already preparing to move. A hammer-stone strikes a falling tree's trunk at the point where the fracture is already forming, directing the fall toward the hollow where the wood will be most quickly consumed. They work with the tendencies of the place. They accelerate processes that are already happening. They do not impose.

You will learn to do this too, if you stay. You will learn to find the seams, the weak points, the moments when the place is already about to change and needs only the smallest intervention. You will learn that the tool-stones are not technology but conversation — a way of speaking to the terrain that says I am here and I see what you are doing and I will help.

And then your skin will begin to change.

---

The other things that live here — the things that make the sound-wall — are not residents in the way the tool-users are residents. They are the place's population, its citizenry, its metabolic processes made mobile. They are here because the fourth thing feeds them. They are here because the warmth and the wet and the dark create conditions in which life does not need to struggle to persist; it needs only to avoid being eaten long enough to reproduce, and reproduction happens almost automatically, an afterthought, a consequence of being alive in a place where being alive is the easiest thing in the world.

The insects first, because the insects are the sound-wall's foundation. They are not one species. They are ten thousand species, maybe more, differentiated beyond any catalog, occupying every micro-niche the place offers. Beetles that farm the fungi, tending the pale caps on fallen logs, carrying spores from one substrate to the next in specialized grooves on their carapaces. Ants that build no hills because the ground is too wet, instead constructing networks of living chambers inside the stalk-trees, their combined body-heat keeping the chambers warm and dry. Midges so small they exist in the air the way bacteria exist in water — you inhale them with every breath, you feel them on your eyeballs, and they are always there, always present, a living particulate that makes the air itself seem alive because it is.

And the insects that make the drone. The baseline. The sub-harmonic hum that lives in your teeth and jaw. These are cicadas, or the local equivalent — insects with hollow abdomens that vibrate at frequencies too low for your ears to hear as sound but too persistent for your body to ignore. They are the place's pulse. They never stop. They have no season. The nymphs emerge from the soil continuously, climb the nearest vertical surface, split their shells, and begin their drone within minutes of drying. They live only to make this sound. They mate in the sound — the females locate the males by the specific texture of their vibration, a texture you cannot perceive — and then they die, and their bodies fall to the forest floor and are consumed before they can rot, and the nymphs that hatch from the eggs they laid in the bark emerge weeks later, having developed in the time it would take a normal insect to pass through a single instar.

The birds are harder to describe because you rarely see them. You hear them — the calls like dropped glass, sharp and crystalline and somehow fragile despite the volume — but they stay in the canopy, in the roof of interlocking fronds and branches where the light reaches and where their eyes, which are enormous, which take up a third of their skulls, can function. They hunt the insects of the upper air. They hunt each other. They hunt the small climbing mammals that also live in the canopy and that you also never see, though you hear them chittering and scrabbling above you, and occasionally you hear one scream as something catches it, a short sharp sound that is absorbed by the sound-wall almost before it finishes.

But sometimes a bird descends. Usually because it is dying. You find them on the forest floor — beautiful in a way that seems wrong here, too precise, too evolved for a place that hasn't had time for evolution. Iridescent feathers. Beaks like surgical instruments. Eyes that still hold the memory of light, that look up toward the canopy they fell from with an orientation that seems like longing. The tool-users do not eat the birds. They leave them where they fall, and the beetles and the fungi and the soil take them within hours, and the iridescent feathers become part of the dark floor, and the precise beaks soften and dissolve, and the eyes sink into the general wetness of the place.

The frogs are everywhere. This is their kingdom more than anyone's. They occupy the pools, the condensation-collecting hollows, the cups of epiphytic plants, the spaces between root and stone where water persists. They are the place's voice — the drumming, the clicking, the resonant calls that carry through the sound-wall like sonar pings. They communicate across distances you cannot measure by sound alone, and the tool-users listen to them, you realize, the way a sailor listens to a weather-glass: for information about what is coming. The frogs know before the river shifts. The frogs know before a tree falls. The frogs know when the fourth thing surges, when the fever spikes, when the system's metabolism accelerates toward some new convulsion of growth. The tool-users attend to the frogs. The frogs are the place's nervous system, and the tool-users are something like its hands.

There are mammals. You know this because you hear them — the crashing in the undergrowth at the not-night, the heavy footfalls, the low grunting that might be territorial or might be communicative or might be the simple vocalization of a large body moving through resistant space. You glimpse them rarely. A hide — wet, dark, moving. The suggestion of a bulk. The bioluminescence catches an eye, and the eye is large, too large, the size of your fist, and it looks at you without the alarm you would expect from a wild animal because alarm is for creatures that have predators and the thing that owns this eye does not have predators. It has the place, and the place has it, and the arrangement is mutually satisfactory.

There are smaller mammals too. Rodent-things that scurry through the root-tangle with the confidence of creatures who know every gap and passage. Bat-things that navigate the darkness below the canopy by echolocation, their voices adding another layer to the sound-wall, another frequency, another stream of information that flows past you uncomprehended. Things that you hear but cannot categorize — chitterings, scratchings, the sound of something grooming itself in the dark, the sound of something suckling its young, the sound of something dying, all of it constant, all of it simultaneous, all of it the place's ongoing internal monologue, the sound of a body talking to itself.

---

And there are things you cannot categorize at all.

You see one in the bioluminescent glow of a lichen-trace, and you think at first it is a tool-user, because it is low and it is moving with intention. But it is not a tool-user. It is not anything you have a frame for. It moves like water moves — finding the lowest point, filling the available space, flowing around obstacles rather than over them. It has no fixed shape. It has no fixed size. It is dark against the dark, visible only because the glow of the lichen bends around it, creating a negative space, an absence where the light isn't. It is traveling the lichen-line, and as it travels, the lichen pulses brighter, as if fed by the thing's passage, as if the thing and the lichen are in conversation, are exchanging something you cannot perceive.

The tool-users know this thing. You understand this when you see them — three shapes, low and deliberate — approaching the negative-space traveler and then falling in alongside it, matching its pace, moving with it along the lichen-line as if escorting it, as if accompanying it, as if they and it are going the same direction for the same reason. They make a sound as they travel — a sound different from their usual sub-vocal hum, a sound that is higher and more structured, a sound that seems directed at the negative-space thing, that seems to be a greeting, or an acknowledgment, or a prayer.

The negative-space thing does not respond, or does not respond audibly, or responds in a medium your senses cannot access. But the lichen pulses. And the sound-wall — for a moment, just for a moment — reorganizes itself around the four shapes traveling together, and you hear the place's million voices align into a pattern, a brief architecture of sound that has the shape of meaning, that has the shape of something being said, and then the moment passes and the sound-wall is chaos again and the four shapes have moved beyond the range of the lichen-glow and you are alone in the dark with the drone in your teeth and the wet on your skin and the knowledge that you are in the presence of something that is not an animal and not a person and not a god but some fourth thing for which there is no word.

The same word you would use for what feeds the system from below. The same word you do not have.

---

The tool-users live in the lichen-lines.

This is what you realize at the end, the thing that takes the longest to understand because it requires you to stop thinking of shelter as structure. They do not build. They do not construct. They inhabit. The lichen-traces on the forest floor — the bioluminescent paths that glow like cold steam — are their home in the way a cave is a home to a bear: not made, but found, and then marked, and then occupied, and then changed by occupation until the marking and the marked and the occupant are all one thing.

The lichen grows where they walk. Or they walk where the lichen grows. The causality is unclear because the causality may not exist — may be a loop, may be a mutual creation, may be the tool-users and the lichen-line and the negative-space thing and the fourth thing in the soil all aspects of a single system, a single organism at the scale of the landscape. The tool-users follow the lines and the lines follow the tool-users and the lines lead to the pools where the water collects and the pools are where the tool-users find the stones that become tools and the stones are where the river was before the river shifted and the river shifts because the fourth thing moves beneath the ground, redistributing the flow of whatever it is that makes this place what it is, and the tool-users know the shifts because the frogs tell them and the lichen tells them and the sound-wall tells them, and they move with the shifts the way a leaf moves with the wind — not because they decide to but because the alternative is not-moving and not-moving in a place that is always becoming means being left behind, means being absorbed, means becoming substrate for something else's becoming.

They have no shelters because they do not need them. The canopy is their roof. The warmth is their fire. The sound-wall is their wall. They sleep in the lichen-lines, their membrane-skins pressed to the glowing traces, and the glow does not diminish when they lie on it but intensifies, as if their bodies complete a circuit, as if they are the thing the lichen has been waiting for, the component that makes the system whole. They sleep deeply and without fear because fear is for creatures that can be caught unawares and the tool-users cannot be caught unawares, they are too connected, too deeply woven into the place's information network, they know what is coming before it comes, they know because they are part of what is coming, they are the place knowing itself.

They reproduce. This must happen, because they persist. But you do not see it. You do not hear it. It occurs in the lichen-lines, perhaps, in the deepest dark, in the place where the glow is brightest and the sound-wall is most organized and the fourth thing is closest to the surface. The young — if they are young — are indistinguishable from the adults, or they emerge already formed, already adapted, already knowing what they need to know, because what they need to know is not learned but grown, not taught but metabolized, absorbed from the place itself like nutrient from the soil.

Or perhaps they do not reproduce at all. Perhaps they simply persist. Perhaps the same forty have always been here, will always be here, their membrane-skins renewing themselves continuously like the cells of a body that does not age because the body is not separate from the source that sustains it. Perhaps the tool-users are not a species but a symptom — the place's method of being conscious, the way it touches itself, the way it knows its own shape.

---

They do not mourn their dead because they do not have dead. They have the absorbed. When one of them stops moving — when the membrane-skin ceases its slow pulse, when the ears stop their constant rotation, when the body folds itself into the lichen-line and does not rise — the others continue. They do not pause. They do not gather. They do not mark the moment with sound or with stillness. The absorbed one becomes part of the line, part of the glow, part of the substrate. The tool-stones it carried are picked up by the next hands that need them. The routes it traveled are traveled still. Nothing is lost because nothing was owned. Nothing is grieved because nothing is gone. The place does not lose mass or energy or information; it transforms them, and the tool-users are the place, and the place does not grieve itself.

You understand, finally, why they do not attack. Why they watch you without hostility, without fear, without any of the emotions you would expect from creatures encountering something foreign. They are not defending anything. They are not protecting anything. They are not separate from you in the way you are separate from them. They are the place, and the place is them, and you are in the place, which means you are already part of them, which means you are already being processed, already being parsed, already being metabolized by the sound-wall and the wet and the dark and the fourth thing in the soil.

Not quickly. Not violently. You have time. You have days, maybe weeks, before the changes become irreversible, before your skin begins to thicken, before your ears begin to widen, before your eyes begin to shrink and your spine begins to curve and your sleep begins to follow the lichen-lines. You have time to leave. And they know you will leave — not because they understand your intentions but because they can feel that you are not ready, that you are still separate enough to choose separation, that the place has not yet finished its argument for your inclusion.

But they also know that you will return. Everyone returns. Not consciously, not deliberately, but in dreams, in the hum that stays in your bones, in the way silence will never feel quite right again, in the way you will find yourself listening for the sound-wall in quiet rooms, in the way you will miss the wet and the warmth and the dark and the certainty of being surrounded, being held, being part of something that is still becoming, that will always be becoming, that will never stop making itself.

They know because they were once what you are. Separate. Afraid. Standing at the edge of the canopy, looking in. They do not remember this. But the place remembers. The place remembers everything that has ever happened within its boundaries, because the place is not a location but a process, and the process includes you now, includes your brief passage and your retreat and the hum you carry away and the certainty that the hum is not a memory but a connection, a thread, a lichen-line that stretches from the nursery to wherever you go, that glows in the dark you cannot see, that waits.

They are waiting too. Not with patience, because patience implies a sense of time passing, and time does not pass for them the way it passes for you. Time here is the fourth thing, or is made of the fourth thing, or is the fourth thing made manifest. Time here is generative, not sequential. Time here is what makes the young landscape, the impossible growth, the fever-speed of becoming. Time here is not a river but a spring, welling up from below, from the stone, from the whatever-it-is that feeds the system, and the tool-users are the spring's outlet, the place where time becomes flesh, where the fourth thing takes a shape that can use a hand-axe, that can follow a lichen-line, that can sleep in the glow and wake in the glow and move with the confidence of something that has always been here and will always be here and does not need to wonder why.

They are the residents. They are the ones who stayed.
