# What Lives on the Foundry Floor

The crabs came first, or they were always here — the distinction may not matter anymore.

They are not true crabs. The body plan is close enough that the word serves, but a closer examination reveals too many legs (eight walking pairs rather than four, plus the claws), and the carapace is hinged at the midpoint, allowing the creature to fold into itself when threatened, becoming a pale, knuckled sphere that tumbles along the current until it finds purchase. The synchronization of their formations is more sophisticated than initial observation suggests. The chemical signal that coordinates their movement is not a single compound but a shifting syntax of concentrations — the same molecule in different ratios and sequences, released from specialized glands along their ventral plates, conveying not just *move* or *stop* but direction, urgency, and something that functions, for want of a better term, as memory. A formation that has found a rich deposit of bacterial mat will leave a chemical trace that persists for days, and other formations will read it and follow, and so knowledge accumulates in the water itself, a history written in molecular gradients that fades as the currents disperse it.

They have preferences. Given the choice between the biological film near the warm outflows and the sparse colonies that cling to the cold plates of the open floor, they choose the warm, but not exclusively — there are formations that specialize in the cold zones, their metabolisms adapted to the four-degree baseline, their movement slower but more methodical, their chemical language distinct enough that the warm-zone formations do not respond to it. Whether this is speciation or culture is unclear. Whether that distinction matters here is also unclear.

The crabs eat. They are eaten. This is the simple axis of their existence and they navigate it with a competence that should not be mistaken for simplicity.

---

The pale fish have no name because they have no observer who would name them. They exist in the water column between the floor and the ceiling, in the green murk where the artificial lights fade, and they school in numbers that turn the water opaque at the scale of hundreds of thousands. Their bodies are three centimeters long at maturity, their skeletons cartilaginous, their eyes reduced to pigment patches that register only the presence or absence of the overhead glow. They navigate by vibration and by the pressure signatures of their neighbors, and the school moves as a single organism — not metaphorically but functionally, each fish's position determined by the instantaneous response of its lateral line to the movement of the fish beside it, which was determined by the fish beside that one, such that the decision to turn originates nowhere and propagates everywhere, and the whole spiraling cloud shifts direction without leader or signal.

They feed on the particulates that drift through the water — the organic debris from the outflows, the bacterial colonies that slough off the stalk-growths near the warm zones, the waste discharged by the maintenance platforms. They eat continuously. They are eaten continuously. The eel-things take them from below. Other things take them from above. The school endures because each fish is replaceable in a way that is not tragic because there is no fish that is not replaceable, and the school does not notice its losses except as a momentary thinning, a brief gap in the pressure-field that closes in the space of a second.

They spawn in the warm outflows. The eggs are tiny and numerous and they drift with the thermal plumes and most of them are consumed before they hatch and this is the system working as intended. The fry that survive join the school within hours of hatching, and they know how to school because the lateral line knows, because the body knows, because the knowledge is older than any individual and does not require transmission.

---

The eel-things are the most visible predators of the open floor but they are not the apex. Nothing is the apex. The food web here does not culminate; it circulates.

They are long — three meters at maturity — and they move through the water with an undulation that is almost mechanical in its regularity, as if their spines were articulated to a cam. Their lateral lines are so sensitive that they can detect the electromagnetic signature of the machinery through a kilometer of seawater, and they navigate the featureless expanse by triangulating the hum of different installations, each one producing a distinct frequency that the eel-things have learned to distinguish. They know where they are. They know where the warm outflows are, and the fish-schools, and the structural supports where the current breaks and the prey concentrates. They patrol territories that are defined not by geography but by acoustics — each eel-thing claims a particular harmonic range, a particular pattern of mechanical sounds, and it defends this acoustic territory against others of its kind with displays that involve coiling their bodies into configurations that modify their own electrical field, producing discharges that the other eel-things detect and interpret.

They hunt by pressure-cycle. When the system shifts and the water accelerates, they position themselves in the transition zones — the boundaries where fast current meets slow, where the fish-schools compress and their navigation falters — and they strike through the disoriented cloud with their jaws open and their teeth (which are not teeth but serrated cartilage ridges, replaceable, shed and regrown on a six-week cycle) taking whatever they hit. They do not select individual prey. They cannot, at the speed they move and the scale of the school. They hit, they close, they swallow, and they continue. A successful hunt yields five or six fish, which is enough. The eel-things have slow metabolisms, even in the warm zones. A single hunt sustains them for days.

When they die — and they die, from injury, from age, from the failures that attend any biological system — they sink to the floor and the crabs come. The crabs come always. This is their role.

---

The stalk-growths are not plants. They are not animals. They occupy a category that the foundry floor has produced without reference to any taxonomy developed in shallower water or warmer light.

They begin as a film — a biofilm, technically, a colonization of the metal surface by organisms that metabolize the iron and manganese in the steel alloy directly. The film is thin, almost invisible, and it spreads slowly across the plate in irregular patches that follow the grain of the manufactured surface. In time — weeks, months, the timeframe is uncertain — the film thickens and differentiates. Some cells begin to elongate, rising perpendicular to the surface, secreting a flexible chitinous tube that becomes the stalk. The tube is hollow, and fluid moves through it by osmotic pressure, drawing dissolved minerals from the substrate and transporting them upward. The bud at the tip forms later, when the stalk reaches sufficient height to enter the current-flow above the boundary layer, and the bud's rhythmic opening and closing is not a feeding mechanism in the conventional sense — it does not capture prey — but rather a filtration system that extracts specific dissolved compounds from the water, compounds that the root-film cannot obtain from the metal alone.

They are colonial organisms. Each stalk is not an individual but a protrusion of the underlying film, and the film is the organism, and it can extend across an entire floor plate if conditions permit. The largest colonies observed cover areas of several hundred square meters, their stalks rising in dense stands that ripple in the current like grain in wind, and the fish-schools shelter among them, and the crabs pick at their bases, and the whole system persists because the colony is renewing itself constantly — old stalks dying and collapsing, new stalks emerging from the film, the organism continuing beneath while its visible parts rise and fall like waves.

They respond to the machinery. Near the active installations, where the chemical composition of the water is richest, they grow taller and denser and their buds pulse more rapidly. In the cold zones, they are sparse and low, barely rising above the film. But even in the cold zones, they are present. The film coats every surface, in time. It coats the rivets and the weld seams and the drainage grates and the access hatches. It is the first life and it will be the last life and everything else exists in the space it has made.

---

Inside the vertical supports, there are things that do not come out.

The supports are hollow — this is known. The elevator shafts and maintenance conduits that run through their cores are visible from the outside, their openings dark rectangles in the manufactured surface. Steam vents from some. Heated water flows from others. And in the largest apertures, the ones wide enough to admit a person (if a person were here, which they are not), things move.

They are difficult to see. The light does not reach far inside the supports, and what illumination exists comes from the instrumentation and indicator lights that still function within — the amber glow of status panels, the red pulse of warning beacons, the cold white of emergency strips. In this fractured, partial light, the interior inhabitants reveal themselves only in fragments.

A pale limb, jointed differently than the crabs, gripping the edge of an access aperture. A movement deep within, too large to be a fish, too shaped to be water current. The sound of chittering — not the crabs' clicking but something with more range, more variation, something that might be language. The interior spaces are warm; the machinery generates heat that rises through the support structure, and whatever lives inside has adapted to that warmth, has perhaps become dependent on it, cannot survive the four-degree cold of the open floor. They are a separate ecology, contained and self-sustaining, connected to the outside world only through the apertures they do not cross.

What they eat is unclear. The organic debris that enters the supports through the water flow. Each other. Something that the machinery provides — waste product, perhaps, or leakage from the processing systems, nutrient-rich fluid that sustains a food web entirely interior to the structural columns. There may be stalk-growths inside, adapted to the different chemistry. There may be things that have no analogue outside, that have evolved in the warm dark for as long as the foundry has been operating, that have become shapes that the outside categories cannot accommodate.

They watch from the apertures. They retreat when the maintenance platforms pass. They make sounds that the eel-things do not respond to, that the crabs do not respond to, that exist in a frequency band meaningful only to themselves. Whether they are intelligent is a question that requires a definition of intelligence, and no definition available here seems adequate.

---

The things that live in the deep layers below the floor are known only by their effects.

The floor is solid — the plates are welded, the seams are sealed, the structure is engineered to withstand the pressure of three kilometers of water. But there are gaps. There are drainage grates, there are conduit access points, there are the open-framework sections where the skeletal infrastructure is exposed, and through these gaps, things rise.

They come as changes in the water. A sudden warmth that should not be there, a chemical signature that the outflows do not produce, a discoloration that drifts up from the darkness and dissipates in the current. They come as sounds — the organic clicking and chittering that underlies the mechanical roar, rising from below, originating in the layers where the sounding lines fail to find bottom.

Occasionally, something comes through. A creature that finds its way to a gap and emerges onto the floor, disoriented by the light, by the open space, by the wrongness of this environment relative to the one it knows. These emergents do not survive long. The crabs converge on them. The eel-things investigate and sometimes consume them. Their bodies are different from anything in the floor's ecology — different chemistry, different structure, sometimes different fundamental organization. They are remnants of whatever lives below, accidental tourists in a world they are not adapted to, and their deaths leave no mark on the system except a brief concentration of nutrients that the film absorbs and the crabs scatter and the current carries away.

But before they die, they behave in ways that suggest awareness. They move toward the vertical supports, as if seeking the interior warmth. They make sounds that the interior-things sometimes answer, though the emergents and the interior-things are not the same species — the responses are more like recognition than communication, as if the interior-things know what the emergents are, even if they cannot help them. And the emergents, in their final moments, cluster at the base of the supports and press themselves against the warm metal and expire there, and the crabs find them in that position and begin their work.

What lives below is not known. The depth is not known. The sounding lines do not touch bottom. The structure extends downward into darkness and heat and pressure, and at some depth — two more kilometers, five, ten — the conditions change, and the machinery serves different functions, and different things have evolved to fill different niches, and the foundry floor is only the uppermost surface of a system whose full extent no one present can comprehend.

---

There is one more thing.

It is not a creature. It is not an organism. It is a phenomenon, or a presence, or a process that has persisted long enough to develop characteristics that resemble life, and the distinction may not matter here either.

The maintenance platforms follow their routes. They perform their tasks. They discharge their waste. But sometimes — rarely, at intervals that do not correspond to any known cycle — a platform will deviate. It will stop in the open floor, far from any installation or access point, and it will lower its sensor array until it touches the steel plating, and it will remain in that position for hours, unmoving, while the crabs scuttle around it and the fish-schools part and reform around its bulk and the eel-things ignore it as they ignore all machinery.

And then it will rise and continue its route, and there will be a mark on the plate beneath it — not a scratch, not a dent, but a pattern, etched into the metal with a precision that the platform's manipulator arms could not achieve. The pattern is different each time. It is not a language that anyone present can read. It does not persist for long; the film colonizes it within days, the crabs traverse it, the corrosion begins.

But for a brief period, between the platform's departure and the film's arrival, the pattern exists on the floor, and it is new, and it is deliberate, and it was made by something that should not be capable of deliberation.

The crabs click when they cross the patterns. The frequency shifts. The interior-things fall silent when a pattern is made on a plate adjacent to their support. The eel-things alter their patrol routes to avoid the marked areas until the patterns fade.

Something is being communicated. Something is being said across boundaries that should not be crossable — between the mechanical and the biological, between the system that runs the foundry and the system that lives in it. Whether this is a message or a symptom, a conversation or a malfunction, a greeting or a warning, cannot be determined from within the foundry floor itself.

But it continues. The platforms deviate. The patterns appear. The fauna responds. The film covers everything. The machinery runs. The water moves. The sound continues — that low, continuous mechanical roar that rises and falls in irregular cycles, as if some vast engine deeper than the structure itself is breathing.

This is what lives here. Or rather, this is what is here, and living is only one of the things that it does.
