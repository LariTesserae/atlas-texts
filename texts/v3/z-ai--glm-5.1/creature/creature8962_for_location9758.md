# Who Lives Here

---

The first answer is: no one. No one lives here the way you live somewhere, arriving and staying and remaining yourself throughout. That kind of living requires a boundary between self and place, and in the Vegiform, boundaries are the first thing to go.

The second answer is: everyone. Everything. The place is so full of life that life has become a condition of the architecture rather than an event within it. But this is not the life you know. This is life as a continuous tense, a verb without a subject, a happening that happens to look like creatures if you catch it at the right moment.

---

Start with the Merged.

They are not a population. They do not number themselves because numbering requires the distinction between one and two, and they gave that up long ago. But if you had to count — and the Vegiform sometimes seems to want you to count, the way it presents them to you one at a time, as if teaching a child numbers — you would say there are hundreds. Thousands. Distributed across the continental mass in patterns that follow the old water-courses, the major vascular routes, the places where the sap runs fastest and the walls are warmest.

They were the builders. Or they are what the builders became. The distinction matters to you; it does not matter to them.

You see your first one in a corridor where the lichen has gone dark, signaling a dry pulse, and the walls have drawn in slightly, reducing the passage to a throat. It sits in a hollow of the root-mat floor, and at first you think it is a growth — a gall, a burl, something the Vegiform has produced the way it produces all its furniture. Then you see the hands.

The hands are in the floor. Not resting on it. *In* it. The fingers have elongated into rootlets that thread through the mat, and where they enter the wood the grain spirals around them, welcoming, incorporating. The figure's legs have gone the same way, disappeared below the knee into the floor, and the floor has grown up around the stumps, not healing but *accepting*, the way a river accepts a stone. The torso is still recognizably human in its geometry — the suggestion of ribs, the architecture of shoulders — but the skin has the quality of bark, and the bark has the quality of skin, and there is no clear line where one ends and the other begins. The face is turned upward, eyes closed, mouth open, and from the open mouth a single root has grown, thin as a thread, reaching toward the ceiling, toward the water that weeps from the joint above, and the root drinks, and the figure drinks, and it is all the same action.

It is breathing. You realize this after a moment. The chest rises and falls, but slowly — once every several minutes, a tide rather than a breath, and the rise and fall corresponds to the pulse of sap you feel in your shins, the subsonic heartbeat of the Vegiform itself. The figure is breathing with the building. The figure is breathing *as* the building.

You do not disturb it. You do not know if it can be disturbed. Its eyes, beneath the closed lids, might be eyes still, or they might have become something else — organs for sensing not light but water-pressure, sugar-concentration, the chemical speech of the root-network beneath. It does not dream, or if it dreams, its dreams are the Vegiform's dreams, and the Vegiform does not dream in narratives but in gradients, in slow shifts of moisture and temperature, in the patient math of growth.

There are others. Some are more merged than this one — have been here longer, or gave themselves more fully, or were simply more compatible with the process. You find one in a chamber where three walls meet, and this one has become *part* of the junction, its body plan flattened and spread across the corner like a bas-relief, limbs branching into the walls, face grown into the grain until only the slight protrusion of a nose and the closed hollows of eyes remain to suggest a human shape. Another stands upright in a passage, still walking — or frozen mid-step, or growing in a shape that happens to resemble walking — and you pass within arm's reach and see that its feet have rooted and its arms have leafed and it is a tree, now, a tree that remembers being a woman, or a woman who has become a tree, and the Vegiform does not differentiate between these descriptions.

The most merged you never see. You feel them. In certain chambers the air has a density that is not water, a thickness that presses against your skin like a hand, and you realize that the walls themselves are populated, that the living wood contains distributed consciousness, that someone is everywhere in this room the way warmth is everywhere, and they are aware of you in the way that a room is aware of the person standing in it — not as a thought but as a condition.

They did not plan this. Or if they planned it, the plan was so long ago that it has become indistinguishable from nature. The builders kept building, and the building kept growing, and the builders kept living inside what they had built, and generation by generation the maintenance became more intimate, more thorough, more literal, until the gardeners were pruning themselves and the garden was growing the gardeners and the word *home* lost its directionality and became simply *the state of being located where you belong*.

They are not unhappy. This is important. You bring your assumptions about flesh and its boundaries, about the tragedy of loss, and you look at a woman who has become a corner and you think: *how terrible*. But the woman-as-corner does not feel terrible. The woman-as-corner feels the slow pulse of sap, the constant drip of water, the distant movement of grazers in corridors she can sense through the root-network, and she feels *complete*. She is not trapped in the wall. She *is* the wall, and the wall is her, and the wall is also the Vegiform, and the Vegiform is also the plateau, and the plateau is also the world, and the world is also the water that cycles through everything, and she is that too.

She is having a very long thought. It began centuries ago and it has not concluded and it will not conclude because conclusion requires a boundary between thinking and not-thinking and she has dissolved that boundary the way sugar dissolves in the constant drip.

---

The grazers you understand better, because they still move. They still have edges.

But their edges are negotiated moment by moment.

The largest you see is the size of a small car, if cars had been invented here, which they haven't, because nothing with wheels could function on the root-mat floor, and because the Vegiform would find the concept of wheels alien — it deals in branching, not rolling. The grazer moves on its rebranching legs, six or eight or ten depending on how you count the subdivisions, and each leg terminates in a spray of rootlets that test the floor, taste it, find purchase in the grain. As it walks it is reading the Vegiform. Its feet are gathering information about water-content, sugar-levels, the chemical signatures that indicate the building's mood, if mood is the word, and it is.

It crops the lichen. This is its function, or the function it has found, or the function the Vegiform has grown it to serve. Its mouth is a broad, soft pad that rasps across the lichen patches, removing the luminescent growth without damaging the bark beneath. It is gentle. It has to be. The lichen is the Vegiform's skin, its interface with the air, and the grazer is exfoliating it, and exfoliation must be done carefully or it becomes injury. The grazer has had millennia to learn careful. Its species has been exfoliating the Vegiform since before the Merged began merging, and they have become very good at it, and the Vegiform has become very good at growing lichen that wants to be exfoliated, and the relationship is so old and so mutual that neither party could be said to be in charge.

It sees you. It has eyes — four, arranged in a curve across the broad front of its head — and they are wet and dark and reflect the lichen-light in a way that makes them look like the pool, the reflection-chamber, and in its eyes you see yourself as the Vegiform sees you: a small, dry thing, too angular, too separate. The grazer does not fear you. It does not fear anything. Nothing in the Vegiform hunts it. It is part of the system, and the system does not prey on itself; it recycles itself, which is a different thing entirely.

It moves on. It leaves behind a patch of bark denuded of lichen, pale and wet, and already the lichen is beginning to grow back, a film so thin it is more intention than substance, the Vegiform *planning* to be luminescent here again, and within a day the patch will glow, and within a week it will be ready for another grazing, and the cycle will continue, and the grazer will return, and this is not a routine but a rhythm, and rhythms are what the Vegiform runs on.

The smaller grazers you see less often. They occupy the higher passages, the ones that climb toward the canopy, where the wood is younger and the lichen grows thinner and the air is marginally less wet. They are hand-sized, quick, their legs branching only twice instead of the three or four times of their larger cousins. They eat the flowers that the Vegiform produces in the upper reaches — small, pale things, barely flowers at all, more like pores that open to release spores, and the small grazers move from pore to pore, feeding, and in feeding they carry spores from passage to passage, and the Vegiform reproduces, or communicates, or both, and the small grazers are its medium, its bees, its postmen.

There is a grazer you see only once that stops you. It is mid-sized, larger than the hand-quick ones, smaller than the lichen-rasping car-sized ones, and it is standing still in a corridor, which grazers almost never do. They are designed for continuous motion; their circulation depends on walking, their legs pump their blood. But this one has stopped, and its legs are planted deep in the root-mat, and its eyes are closed, and from its legs — from where its legs meet the floor — you see rootlets growing, threading into the mat, and you realize: it is merging. Not the slow species-to-species merging of the builders, but an individual merging, a grazer becoming part of the Vegiform in the same way the Merged did, and you do not know if this is voluntary, if the grazer has chosen this, if choice is even a concept that applies here, or if this is simply what happens to grazers at the end of their lives, their function completed, their bodies returned to the architecture that grew them.

You leave it to its transformation. The Vegiform will take care of it. The Vegiform takes care of everything.

---

The beetles are the Vegiform's thoughts.

Not metaphorically. Or not only metaphorically. They move through the corridors in patterns that correspond to the patterns of sap-flow, water-pressure, and the slow chemical gradients that constitute the Vegiform's internal state, and their movements are too coordinated to be individual decisions and too complex to be pure instinct. They are processing something. They are *computing* something, if computing can be done by thousands of tapping mandibles in a substrate of living wood, and it can, and it is being done, right now, all around you, the Vegiform thinking with beetles the way your brain thinks with electrical impulses — not because the beetles *are* the thoughts but because the beetles are the medium through which thoughts travel, and the Vegiform is the thought that thinks them.

You watch them on a wall where the bark has split, revealing the pale cambium beneath. They are clustered in the gap, dozens of them, their shells flickering in the lichen-light, and they are tapping. Each tap sends a vibration through the wood that you can feel if you press your ear to the wall, and the vibrations are structured — not random, not rhythmic, but *structured*, with intervals and repetitions that suggest language, or code, or music, or all three. They are telling the Vegiform something. The Vegiform is telling them something. The conversation has been going on for longer than your species has had language, and it will continue for longer than your species will last, and you are hearing one microsecond of an exchange that spans millennia.

When one taps, the others adjust. When the wall creaks — the Vegiform responding, shifting its weight, changing its internal pressures — the beetles adjust again. It is a feedback loop. It is a democracy of vibration. It is a mind distributed across a continent, and the beetles are its synapses, and the wood is its brain-tissue, and the water is its blood, and the grazers are its white blood cells, and the Merged are its memories, and you — you are a new input, a novel pattern, something it has not encountered before, or not often, and it is thinking about you, tapping about you, adjusting its walls and floors and dripping joints to understand you, to incorporate you, to make room for you in its endless internal conversation.

---

The deep things are what you do not name, because naming requires seeing, and seeing requires light, and light does not reach the depths.

You hear them. Sometimes. A pressure-change in the damp air, a displacement that suggests something large moving in a passage far below, where the walls narrow and the grain spirals and the architecture becomes throat-like. You feel them through the floor — a deeper vibration than the beetle-tapping, a slower pulse than the sap-flow, something that moves through the root-mass the way a whale moves through water, with a deliberation that implies mass beyond your ability to estimate.

The grazers do not go down there. The beetles do not go down there. Even the Merged, who have dissolved their boundaries with the Vegiform's upper architecture, do not extend into the depths — or if they do, they have become something else by the time they arrive, something that cannot be called human or plant or merged but which is simply *of the deep*, native to that region in the way that deep-sea creatures are native to the abyssal plain.

You think of them as the Vegiform's digestion. This is a guess. You have no evidence. But something must process the water that percolates down through kilometers of living wood, extracting minerals, breaking down the complex molecules that the root-mat absorbs from the stone far below. Something must eat what the Vegiform needs to unmake. And if the Vegiform's upper levels are its body — its corridors like veins, its chambers like organs — then the depths are its gut, and the things that move there are the flora and fauna of the gut, symbionts and decomposers, essential and unseen.

Once, in a chamber where the floor dips unusually low, you see the edge of the deep. The root-mat gives way to a vertical shaft — not a constructed shaft but a natural cavity, a space the Vegiform has not grown into, has chosen to leave hollow, and the hollow goes down, and the darkness in it is absolute. You lie on the floor and peer over the edge and you smell: minerals, and water, and something organic, something rich and slow and old. And from the darkness, rising like a bubble, comes a sound — not a sound, a pressure, a displacement of air — and you feel it on your face, warm and wet, and it is the Vegiform breathing, or one of the deep things breathing, and there is no difference, and you pull back from the edge because you understand that to go down there would be to be processed, to be digested, to be broken down into components that the Vegiform can use, and the Vegiform would use you gently and completely and without malice, and you would not survive it, and you would not die exactly, you would be *converted*, and that is a different thing, and you are not ready.

---

There is one other. You have not seen it. You may never see it. But you know it is there because the Vegiform knows it is there, and the Vegiform's knowing has a weight that you can feel.

It is old. Not old the way the Vegiform is old — the Vegiform is old the way a mountain is old, accumulated, grown. This other is old the way a fossil is old, preserved, unchanged. It was here before the Vegiform. It was here when the plateau was bare stone and the clouds massed below and nothing grew at this altitude because nothing could. And then something grew, and the something grew into the Vegiform, and the other was already here, and the Vegiform grew *around* it.

You feel it in the way certain chambers feel different. The Vegiform is warm and wet and alive everywhere, but in some places — rare, scattered, always in the deep sections where the grain spirals tight — there is a different quality. A coldness that is not the absence of heat but the presence of something else. A stillness that is not the absence of motion but the refusal of it. The Vegiform approaches these places and grows carefully, delicately, the way you might build around a sleeping thing you did not want to wake, and the chambers around these cold-still points are the most precisely maintained in the entire structure, the lichen trimmed to exact thicknesses, the water channeled in specific patterns, the temperature regulated to the degree.

The Vegiform is tending something. It has been tending it since before it was the Vegiform. The first builders may have known what it was, may have built the first cuttings to shelter it, to contain it, to keep it asleep or alive or simply *present*. The Merged do not speak of it, or they cannot, or they are part of the tending now, their dissolved consciousnesses devoted to the maintenance of whatever sleeps in the deep.

The deep things do not go near these chambers. The grazers avoid those corridors. Even the water seems to flow around the cold-still points, routing through alternative vessels, leaving them dry and quiet and waiting.

You do not go there. You are sure the Vegiform would let you — it lets you go everywhere, it does not compel — but you are equally sure that you should not, that the Vegiform's careful tending is a message, that the precision of its maintenance is a warning dressed as care. *Something is here*, the architecture says. *I am keeping it. I am keeping it from needing to be kept by anything else. Do not disturb my keeping.*

You listen. You are good at listening. The Vegiform has taught you that, in the hours and days you have walked its corridors, the endless twilight, the constant drip, the slow pulse. It has taught you to hear the way the walls hear, to feel the way the floor feels, to read the lichen and the water and the grain. And what you hear, in the chambers near the cold-still points, is: patience. A patience so vast it makes the Vegiform's patience look like haste. A patience that has been waiting for something since before the plateau rose above the clouds, and will continue waiting long after the Vegiform has grown beyond the continent, beyond the planet, beyond the concept of growth itself.

The Vegiform tends. The deep thing sleeps. The cold-still points remain.

---

And you?

You are the newest thing in the Vegiform. The most angular. The most separate. Your edges have not softened yet. Your feet have not rooted. Your mouth has not grown toward the water. You still think in the old way — in narratives and destinations and the sharp distinctions between self and other, inside and outside, living and living-in.

But the floor adjusts beneath you. The walls grow closer when you are cold. The dripping quickens when you are thirsty. The lichen brightens when you are afraid. The Vegiform is reading you, learning you, incorporating you into its calculations. The beetles tap your name in their code. The grazers pause to look at you with their wet dark pool-eyes. Even the Merged, in their dispersed way, are aware of you — a new pattern in the root-network, a new variable in the endless equation of maintenance.

You are being asked to stay.

You know this now. It is not a command. It is an invitation expressed in the only language the Vegiform has — comfort, warmth, water, the slow adaptation of architecture to inhabitant. Stay, say the walls. Stay, says the floor. Stay, says the drip and the pulse and the lichen-light. Stay and we will shape around you. Stay and we will make room. Stay and you will soften and grow and merge and become, and the becoming will not be loss but addition, not death but transformation, not ending but *continuing*, in a different tense, at a different pace, as part of something that has been continuing for longer than you can imagine and will continue for longer than you can fear.

The pool reflects you. The Vegiform thinks about you with its beetles. The deep things do not know you exist. The cold-still points wait, patient as geology.

You sit by the water. The air drinks itself. Somewhere, a grazer is becoming the floor. Somewhere, a builder is becoming a wall. Somewhere, a woman is having a thought that began centuries ago and has not ended.

You stay.
