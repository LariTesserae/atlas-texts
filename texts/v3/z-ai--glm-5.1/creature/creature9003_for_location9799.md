# The Congregation

There are beings here who have never seen the sky.

Not once. Not through any failure of ambition or mobility—they simply have no cause to ascend the final meters where the green gives way to blue, where the light arrives unfiltered and the air thins into something almost breathable. They were born in the dim middle zone, in the perpetual jade half-light where photosynthesis is a whisper rather than a shout, and they will die there, and their bodies will descend into the substrate and become the ground for children they will never meet, and this is not tragedy. This is simply the arrangement.

Consider the canopy frog—not one species but dozens, a cluster of forms that have converged on the same improbable strategy. They live in the bromeliad tanks, the small water-filled rosettes that form where epiphytic leaves meet stem. Each tank is a world. Each tank contains: water, yes, rainwater and condensation and the drip from the tank above; but also fallen debris—insect parts, pollen grains, leaf fragments; and also microorganisms—bacteria, protozoa, rotifers; and also, sometimes, other frogs. The tanks are apartments in a vertical city, and each frog holds its apartment with territorial stubbornness, calling at night in a voice that is absurdly large for its body, a voice that must be large because sound is the only medium that travels well here, sound and smell and touch but mostly sound, always sound, the great consensual roar.

The frog knows its tank. It knows the texture of the bromeliad leaves, the depth of the water, the timing of the afternoon condensation. It knows which insects alight on the lip of the tank and when. It knows the smell of its own waste and the smell of the waste of the frog in the tank above, which drips down, and the smell of the fungal threads that have begun to colonize the dead leaf wedged in the tank's base. It does not know these things in the way you know your apartment—through representational thought, through naming. It knows them the way your immune system knows a pathogen: through contact, through pattern, through a recognition that precedes consciousness.

And it knows the sound. The frog cannot help but know the sound, because the sound is the medium of its knowing. It calls and other frogs call back, and in the gaps between calls it hears the cicadas and the birds and the dripping water, and all of this information arrives simultaneously, a thick soup of acoustic data from which it extracts what it needs: the call of a mate, the call of a rival, the rustle of a predator, the particular silence that means snake. The frog does not parse the roar. It lives inside the roar the way you live inside air—surrounded, supported, unaware of the medium until the medium changes.

---

There are beings here who remember.

Not the mammals—they are too brief, too quick, their nervous systems tuned for reactivity rather than retention. A tree shrew moves through the canopy like a flame moves through paper: fast, bright, consuming. It remembers where the fig tree is fruiting. It remembers the scent of the snake that nearly caught it yesterday. It remembers the location of the hollow where it sleeps. But it does not remember the way the forest was before the great limb fell, because it did not live here before the great limb fell, and even if it had, it would not have kept that information. The mammals are the forest's present tense.

The trees remember. The vines remember. The long-lived organisms that have watched centuries of canopy succession remember in the only way that matters: by incorporating the past into their bodies. A strangler fig that germinated on the branch of a tree now three centuries dead carries within its wood the history of that relationship—the way it sent roots downward, the way it compressed and eventually absorbed its host, the way it became its own host, a hollow cylinder of living wood where a tree used to be. The fig does not think about this. It does not narrate. But its body is the narrative, every growth ring a chapter, every scar a plot point, every branch angle a record of the light that was available at the moment of growth.

And there are beings who remember in stranger ways. The fungal networks that thread through the substrate—the mycorrhizal mats that connect root to root, tree to tree, organism to organism in a web of chemical exchange—these networks hold information. Not information as you understand it, not data stored in symbols, but something closer to your body's memory of how to heal a wound: a pattern of response, a history of interaction preserved in the structure of the network itself. When a tree falls, the fungal network reconfigures. When a new seedling roots, the network incorporates it. When an insect outbreak damages one region, the network transmits chemical signals that prepare other regions, and this transmission is not thought but it is also not nothing. It is a kind of knowing. It is a kind of memory that does not require a mind.

The forest thinks the way a city thinks—distributed, emergent, without central coordination but with recognizable patterns that persist across time. The city does not have a brain. The city *is* a brain, or at least something brain-adjacent, something that processes information and produces responses and adapts to changing conditions and maintains itself through feedback loops so intricate that no single resident understands the whole. The forest is like this. The forest is exactly like this.

---

There are beings here who build.

The leafcutter ants are the most obvious architects, and they are obvious because their constructions are visible—their highways worn into the mossy surface, their nest mounds rising from the substrate like small volcanoes, the lines of workers moving with purpose along chemical trails that persist for years, decades, possibly longer than any individual ant has lived. The trails are older than the ants. The trails are maintained by the ants, but they were established by ants who are dead, and they will be used by ants who are not yet born. The trail is the institution; the ant is the employee.

Inside the nest, in chambers carved from the compressed organic matter and lined with a paste made from chewed leaf fragments and ant secretions, the fungus gardens grow. These are not gardens as you would recognize them. They are more like bacterial colonies, more like fermentation vats—dense mats of mycelium that the ants cultivate with absolute attention, weeding out contaminant molds, feeding the fungus with freshly cut leaf fragments, harvesting the nutrient-rich structures that the fungus produces in return. The ants cannot digest leaves. The fungus can. The fungus cannot disperse. The ants carry it to new nests when a queen founds a new colony, a small pellet of mycelium held in a pocket beneath her mandibles, like a seed, like a prayer, like the entire history of a five-million-year partnership compressed into a single gesture.

The ants are farmers. The ants are also, in their way, philosophers—not because they think, but because they have solved problems that you would recognize as philosophical. The problem of cooperation. The problem of communication without language. The problem of maintaining a stable system across generations, across the inevitable decay of individuals, across the constant pressure of entropy and predation and the slow catastrophe of time. They have solved these problems not through reason but through the patient accumulation of behavioral mutations, each one tested against the only criterion that matters: does it allow the colony to persist? The colony persists. The colony has persisted for longer than your species has existed. This does not make the ants wise. This makes them successful, which is a different thing, or maybe the same thing, or maybe the distinction does not matter to the fungus or to the dead leaves or to the substrate that receives the colony's waste and converts it, slowly, into the ground of future growth.

---

There are beings here who wait.

The ambush bug on the orchid spike has not moved in six hours. Its body is the same green as the stem, its legs aligned with the stem's angle, its forearms folded beneath its head like a mantis in prayer, or like a trap waiting to spring, which is the same thing here. It is waiting for a pollinator—a bee, a moth, anything small enough to catch and large enough to be worth the energy of digestion. It does not experience this waiting the way you experience waiting, as a duration to be endured, a gap between events. For the ambush bug, waiting is the event. Waiting is its mode of being, its primary activity, the thing it does with the same focused intensity that you bring to your work. It is not bored. It is not impatient. It is precisely as engaged as it needs to be, which is: totally, but quietly, the way a held breath is engaged with the air it has captured.

And there are waiters who wait longer. The seeds in the seed bank—the dormant seeds buried in the substrate, some of them decades old, some of them possibly older, waiting for the specific combination of light and moisture and chemical signal that means a gap has opened in the canopy, that means there is space, that means now—these seeds are the forest's archive of possibility. They contain forms that have not been expressed in years, species that have not grown in this particular location since before the current canopy closed, but which persist as genetic memory, as compressed potential, as the forest's way of hedging its bets against the future. The seed bank is the forest's unconscious: a reservoir of forms and strategies that emerge when conditions demand them, that recede when conditions change, that persist regardless of whether they are currently useful, because utility is a moving target and the forest has learned—through the patient instruction of extinction—that what is useless today may be essential tomorrow.

---

There are beings here who decompose.

This is not a small category. This is not a subset. This is, numerically, the majority. The decomposers—the fungi, the bacteria, the detritivorous insects, the mites and springtails and nematodes that process the dead into the conditions for the living—they outnumber everything else by orders of magnitude. A single gram of substrate contains more of them than there are people in your city. They are the majority constituency of this republic, and they are the foundation, and they are invisible, and they do not care about any of these descriptors because descriptors are not a category they operate within.

The decomposers are the forest's digestive system. They are also its immune system, in the sense that they process the dead before the dead can become dangerous, recycling pathogens into nutrients, breaking down the bodies of the infected before the infection can spread. They are also its memory, in the sense that the products of decomposition—the humic acids, the mineral concentrations, the specific chemical signatures of particular decay pathways—become the chemical language that living roots read as they grow, informing the next generation about what grew here before, what died here before, what the soil can support and what it cannot.

There is a bracket fungus growing on a fallen tree. The tree fell forty years ago. The fungus has been fruiting on it for thirty-five. In that time, it has consumed most of the tree's lignin and cellulose, converting the massive structure—this thing that was, in life, a vertical pillar supporting a section of canopy—into fruiting bodies and mycelial threads and the subtle chemical exudates that feed the bacteria that feed the protozoa that feed the nematodes that feed the springtails that feed the beetles that feed the frogs that feed the snakes that feed the hawks that cast pellets containing the bones and hair of their prey, which fall to the substrate and are consumed by the decomposers, beginning the cycle again. The fungus is not the tree. The tree is not the fungus. But the fungus is what the tree is becoming, and the tree is what the fungus was, and in the space between these statements lies the entire process of the forest: a continuous transformation in which nothing is lost and nothing is created and everything changes form according to rules that were not written but which are, nevertheless, binding.

---

There are beings here who sing.

The cicadas hold the drone-line—you have heard this, you know this, but you may not understand what the drone-line is. It is not a single song. It is a chorus of overlapping songs, each male cicada producing a sound that is modulated by the shape of his resonating chamber, the frequency of his wing-muscle contractions, the temperature of his body. The sound is a composite, a wave-form built from thousands of individual contributions, and it changes across the day as different individuals join and leave, as the temperature rises and falls, as the humidity shifts. The drone-line is not a track on repeat. It is a living composition, continuously revised, never the same twice but always recognizable, always the cicada-song, always the sawing hum that fills the molars and vibrates the skull and becomes, after enough time, indistinguishable from silence, because the ear stops registering what never changes, because the mind makes a space for constant input and then moves past it, because the drone-line is not information but medium, the acoustic equivalent of the green light, the humidity, the substrate: a condition of existence rather than a signal within existence.

But the birds—here, the birds are signal. Each species has its song, its call, its alarm-note, its contact-note, its begging-note, its territorial proclamation, and each of these is distinct, each is learnable, each carries meaning in the way that your words carry meaning: conventionally, arbitrarily, but effectively. The birds are the forest's language-users, its grammarians, its poets and criers and gossip-mongers. They call across the canopy in voices that carry through the dense vegetation, and other birds answer, and the exchange is not conversation as you would recognize it—it is more like a continuous roll-call, a checking-in, a distributed awareness of who is where and what is happening and whether there is danger. A hawk overhead produces a specific alarm call that ripples through the canopy, each bird passing the signal to its neighbors, a wave of sound that travels faster than the hawk can fly, that arrives at the far side of the forest before the hawk has crossed the halfway point. This is communication. This is also, in its way, a form of collective cognition—a distributed processing of threat information that no individual could accomplish alone.

And the gibbons—if there are gibbons, if this forest reaches whatever altitude still sustains them—sing at dawn, their voices rising in great whooping cascades that echo through the canopy and define the boundaries of their territories and advertise the strength of their bonds, and this song is the most beautiful sound the forest produces, and it is also the most purposeful, and the fact that it is beautiful to you is irrelevant to its function but not, perhaps, irrelevant to whatever part of you still responds to the world without requiring that the response be justified.

---

There are beings here who are not individuals.

This is not metaphor. This is not poetic license. The aspen grove in a temperate forest is a single organism connected by root system, and the honeybee colony is a superorganism whose individual members are more like cells than like independent agents, and here, in this forest, the same principle applies at a scale that makes the aspen grove look scattered and the beehive look individualistic. The mycorrhizal network connects trees. The fungal mats connect substrates. The root systems of the epiphytes interlock in the canopy's organic accumulated soil. The ants' chemical trails connect colonies. The frogs' calls connect territories. The birds' alarm networks connect species that otherwise compete. The entire forest is a lattice of connections, a mesh of relationships so dense that the concept of "individual" becomes a convenience rather than a fact.

Where does one tree end and the fungus begin? The fungus penetrates the root cells. The tree provides carbohydrates. The fungus provides minerals. The tree cannot grow without the fungus. The fungus cannot grow without the tree. They have been together so long that their genomes have co-evolved, that their metabolisms have intertwined, that the boundary between them is not a wall but a zone of exchange, a marketplace where goods are traded according to rules that neither party wrote but both parties follow. Are they two organisms? Are they one? Is this even a meaningful question, or is it like asking where the wave ends and the ocean begins—a question that reveals more about the limitations of the asker than about the nature of the asked?

The forest does not care about your categories. The forest has been operating without them for longer than your categories have existed. The forest is a continuous process of exchange, transformation, and persistence, and it has no need for the distinctions you draw between self and other, between individual and community, between life and death, because these distinctions are not features of the forest but features of the way you have learned to see. The forest sees differently. The forest sees in terms of flow—of nutrients, of energy, of information, of chemical signals, of genetic material—and in terms of flow, the distinction between tree and fungus, between predator and prey, between the living and the dead, is not a distinction at all but a moment in a cycle, a point on a circuit, a position in a pattern that has no beginning and no end but only the continuous unfolding of the process itself.

---

There are beings here who will outlast you.

Not because they are individually long-lived—some are, the trees especially, but some are not, the insects most prominently— but because the system they comprise has achieved a stability that your presence cannot easily disrupt. The forest has survived storms. It has survived droughts. It has survived the slow rise and fall of mountains, the shifting of rivers, the coming and going of ice ages. It has survived because it is diverse, and diversity is resilience, and resilience is the capacity to absorb disturbance without losing the essential structure that makes the system what it is.

You could damage it. You are, in fact, the only being here who could damage it in the ways that matter—by cutting the trees, by burning the substrate, by introducing contaminants that the decomposers cannot process, by removing species whose functions are not redundant and whose loss would cascade through the network in ways that no one can predict. You could do these things. You have done them elsewhere, in forests that were less dense, less diverse, less resilient than this one, and you have seen the results: the collapse of the network, the erosion of the substrate, the conversion of cathedral into wasteland.

But you could also simply leave. You could recognize that this place does not need you, was not made for you, will not miss you when you are gone. You could understand that your presence here is a specific kind of irrelevance—the irrelevance of the visitor to the host, the tourist to the cathedral, the breath to the roar—and that this irrelevance is not a judgment but a fact, as morally neutral as the humidity, as the green light, as the sound that fills your skull and vibrates your molars and will continue, will always continue, whether you are here to hear it or not.

---

The congregation does not know it is a congregation. The cathedral does not know it is a cathedral. These are your words, your categories, your attempts to make the unfamiliar legible. But if you must use words, then use these: this is a place where living and dying are not opposites but phases, where the individual and the community are not alternatives but scales, where the sound never ceases because the sound is the sound of process, of exchange, of the continuous transformation of one thing into another that is the only permanent thing the forest has.

The congregation is singing. It has always been singing. It will continue to sing.

You are standing in the middle of it, and you are very quiet, and you are listening, and that is—perhaps—the most appropriate response.
