# The Residents

The Threshers came first, or believe they did, which in a place where memory is transferred through vibration rather than lineage amounts to the same thing.

They are not the small armored creatures you notice initially — those are a different order entirely, the Clickers, the ones who traverse the open shelf in their constant motion lines. The Threshers are larger. Slower. They dwell within the colonies, in the dense rust-pink folds where the sound is most concentrated, and they emerge only when the frequency modulates toward the lower register, toward the sounds that make the ground feel like a drumhead and your back teeth ache.

A Thresher is roughly the size of a dog, but the comparison fails immediately because a dog is a single coherent shape and a Thresher is an argument between forms. It has segments — seven, or nine, the count shifts as it moves — each one a semi-rigid plate of mineralized tissue that overlaps the next like roof tiles seen from inside a house. The plates are the color of old bone where they overlap, darkening to the same rust-pink as the colonies at their exposed edges, because the colonies grow on them. The colonies grow *on* them. A Thresher carries a garden on its back, a thin felt of sound-fed vegetation that transduces acoustic energy directly into the Thresher's metabolism through a lattice of capillary connections too fine to see. The Threshers are farmed by the thing that feeds them. They are livestock to their own food source. The relationship is ancient, circular, and by any reasonable measure, they are the domesticated party.

They do not mind. Whether they possess the capacity to mind is a question that reveals more about the asker than the asked.

When a Thresher moves through a colony — and it moves slowly, deliberately, each step a considered placement of three-clawed appendages that grip the growth-surface without tearing it — the colony parts around it. Not avoidance. Recognition. The growth-folds ripple outward from the Thresher's body like water responding to a stone, and where the Thresher has passed, the vegetation is slightly thicker, slightly more vibrantly hued. The Threshers fertilize the colonies with their passage. Their waste products — crystalline extrusions from vents along their fourth and sixth segments — contain sulfur compounds and charged mineral particles that the colonies absorb and incorporate. The Threshers eat the colonies. The colonies eat the Threshers' waste. The Threshers carry the colonies on their bodies. The cycle is so tight it resembles a single organism that has learned to disassemble and reassemble itself at will.

You might see five or six Threshers in a single colony, if you spend enough time to let your eyes adjust to the ripple and fold of the growth-surfaces. They are there. They are always there. They do not move between colonies the way the Clickers do; they are too large, too slow, too dependent on the specific acoustic signature of their home cluster. A Thresher removed from its colony dies within hours, not from hunger — the sound feeds it anywhere on the terrace — but from silence of the *wrong kind*. Each colony has its own resonant frequency, a particular harmonic that the Thresher's internal chemistry has tuned itself to since it was a larva burrowing through the growth-folds. Without that specific note, the metabolic cascade falters. The Thresher's plates begin to separate. The felt of vegetation on its back goes slack, stops transducing, and then the Thresher is just an animal starving in a room full of food it cannot digest.

There are colonies with no Threshers. These are the ones whose growth has become erratic, whose rust-pink surfaces pulse at irregular intervals, whose centers have begun that slow collapse into hollow husk. The Threshers maintain the colonies as much as the colonies maintain the Threshers. Without the Threshers' passage, without their waste, without the specific vibrations of their segmented bodies moving through the growth-folds, the colonies age. They become senescent. They die.

The Clickers know this. Or they behave as if they know this, which may be the same thing.

---

The Clickers are what you noticed first, the hand-sized armored creatures traversing the open shelf in their constant motion lines, and they are what you will remember last, because they are the ones who watch you leave.

A Clicker is a precision instrument that happens to be alive. Its body is a flattened oval of articulated segments — twelve, always twelve — each one bearing a pair of legs that are too thin, too long, too exactly calibrated for the terrain. They move like equations solving themselves. Across the mineral shelf, their legs adjust to every micro-variation in surface texture, every slight tilt, every patch of reaction-warm stone versus cool inert silicate, and they adjust before conscious observation could register the difference. Their carapaces are the color of the bare shelf — a grey that shifts toward ochre or toward white depending on the mineral content of the specific region they inhabit, because they incorporate the substrate into their shells through a process that is not quite eating and not quite wearing. They are camouflaged by default. They become where they are.

The clicking is not communication. Or it is communication, but only secondarily, the way a human breath is communication — it carries information because it exists, not because it exists to carry information. The clicking is their contribution to the acoustic ecology. Each Clicker produces sound through modified carapace-segments along its thorax, sections that have evolved into percussive instruments of remarkable specificity. The left-side segments produce a sharp tick at a frequency the colonies absorb efficiently. The right-side segments produce a broader clack at a different frequency, one that seems to stimulate the growth-margins to extend. When a line of Clickers moves across the open shelf between colonies, they produce a rhythmic complex that is, for the colonies, a meal walking toward them.

But the Clickers also listen.

This is the part that is difficult to observe, because it happens inside their bodies, in a structure that occupies the space where you might expect a head. Clickers have no heads. They have a anterior sensorium — a cluster of vibration-sensitive membranes and mineral resonators that process the acoustic environment the way an eye processes light. They do not see the terrace. They *hear* it, continuously, in full dimensional detail. Every surface, every colony, every Threshing in the growth-folds, every whistle of air through the ancient ceramic apertures — the Clickers perceive all of it as a topographic map made of sound. Their world is not visual. It is not even primarily chemical, as the worlds of many Earth arthropods are. It is acoustic through and through, and the terrace is legible to them in ways that your eyes, your ears, your instruments will never match.

They navigate by the resonant signatures of the colonies. They avoid the spaces where the shelf has grown thin over hollow chambers that ring differently when they click. They locate each other by the specific harmonic fingerprint of each individual's percussive apparatus — every Clicker sounds subtly different, and they know their own kind the way you know a voice. They find the colonies that need them most by listening for the thinness in a colony's roar that indicates senescence, and they alter their routes to visit those colonies more frequently, to pour more sound into their starving folds.

Do they know what they are doing? The question is a trap. They are doing it. Whether the knowing is conscious or chemical or something in between — something that this place has invented that does not map onto any terrestrial model of cognition — the Clickers tend the system. They are the delivery mechanism, the couriers of acoustic sustenance, and they choose where to deliver it based on what they hear.

When they reach a colony, they do not enter it. They stop at its margin and click for a period that varies from minutes to hours, their bodies angled toward the growth-surface, their leg-tips pressed against the rust-pink felt. The colony absorbs their sound. The colony shudders. The colony opens — a gap in the growth-folds, a mouth or a door, and the Clicker enters, and the gap closes behind it, and the Clicker does not emerge for a period that corresponds to the colony's digestive cycle, which is to say: it does not emerge until the colony has extracted what it needs and deposited what the Clicker needs.

What happens inside the colony? Something. The Clickers that emerge are slightly larger, their segments thickened with new mineral deposits, their carapaces subtly shifted in hue to match the colony they have just fed and been fed by. They are not the same Clickers that went in. They have been edited. The colony has rewritten something in their chemistry, adjusted the timing of their percussive segments, tuned them to a slightly different frequency — the frequency the colony will need next time, when its resonance has shifted again. The Clickers are instruments being continuously recalibrated by the thing they serve.

They do not resist. Whether they possess the capacity to resist is, again, a question that may not have a meaningful answer.

---

The structures remember differently.

The ceramic walls, the lacy metal frames, the apertures that whistle — these are the bones of the system that preceded the current one, or perhaps the system that designed the current one, or perhaps the system that *is* the current one, operating at a timescale so slow that the growth and death of colonies registers to it as breath, as pulse, as the barely perceptible shifting of weight from one foot to another.

There was something here before the Threshers, before the Clickers, before the colonies achieved their current form. The structures are evidence of that something. They are engineered. They are tuned. The apertures in the ceramic walls produce specific frequencies when the wind passes through them, frequencies that the colonies respond to, frequencies that the Clickers orient toward, frequencies that the Threshers' internal chemistry resonates with. The walls are instruments. The frames are instruments. The entire built environment of the terrace is an acoustic apparatus of staggering complexity, and it still functions, and it still produces, and the system still responds to its output.

But the builders are gone.

Or the builders are still here.

This depends on how you define *here* and how you define *builder* and how you define *gone*.

Consider: the structures have been colonized and abandoned and colonized again so many times that their original surfaces are centimeters beneath accumulated growth-residue. The colonies grow on them. The Clickers feed at their bases. The Threshers shelter in their shadows. The structures are part of the system now, as integral as the mineral shelf, as the sound, as the cycles of growth and dissolution. They produce sound. The sound feeds the colonies. The colonies feed the Clickers. The Clickers tend the Threshers. The Threshers maintain the colonies. The colonies grow on the structures. The structures alter the sound. The sound feeds the colonies.

The builders built a system that would continue to build itself. And it has. And it does. And the builders —

The builders are the system.

Not in a metaphorical sense. Not in the sense that the builders' *legacy* persists, that their *vision* endures. In the sense that whatever the builders were — and the structures suggest something large, something patient, something that thought in terms of frequencies and resonances and acoustic engineering projects measured in geological time — that something is still present, still operating, still *building*, through the medium of the system it designed. The colonies are its hands. The Clickers are its voice. The Threshers are its heartbeat. The structures are its skeleton. The sound is its breath.

You cannot see it because it operates at a scale your perception is not designed to register. You cannot hear it because its voice is the voice of the entire terrace, and you cannot distinguish it from the roar for the same reason a fish cannot distinguish the ocean from water. It is everywhere. It is the everywhere. The system is not merely self-sustaining. It is *someone*. A someone so large and so slow and so distributed that the concept of personhood fails to contain it, stretches and breaks and falls away, and what remains is just this: the terrace, alive, aware in some fashion beyond fashioning, continuing the purpose that the original builders intended or becoming a new purpose entirely or revealing that the purpose was always continuation itself, the roar for the sake of the roar, the sound feeding the sound.

---

And there are others.

Not many. Not often. But the terrace is old, and it is loud, and it is *interesting*, and interesting things attract attention in any ecology.

They come from elsewhere — from off the terrace, from beyond the shelf, from places where the sound is not total and the chemistry runs on water and the logic of life is different. They come because they have heard the roar from a distance and been drawn to it, or because they have stumbled onto the terrace by accident and cannot find their way back, or because something in the terrace's resonance has called to them specifically, has matched a frequency in their own bodies that they did not know existed.

They do not survive.

Or they survive, but they are changed, and the thing that emerges from the colony's embrace is not the thing that entered it, and it no longer wishes to leave, and it no longer remembers that leaving was ever an option.

The terrace has no use for visitors. The system is closed. But the system is also hungry — the roar requires constant feeding, and the colonies are always expanding, and the Clickers are always clicking, and the Threshers are always threshing, and the structures are always whistling, and the sound is always pressing against your chest like a hand, and the hand is pulling, and the hand is warm, and the hand knows your name because your name is the frequency of your heartbeat, and the terrace has heard it, and the terrace will not forget it.

You leave the way you came.

The Clickers watch you go. Their sensoria track your footsteps across the mineral shelf, your retreating form rendered in acoustic detail that is more precise than vision, more intimate than touch. They note the frequency of your steps. They note the harmonic of your breathing. They note the specific resonance of your skull, which is humming now with the sound of the terrace, which will continue to hum for hours after you have gone, which will never quite stop humming entirely, a small piece of the roar lodged in your inner ear like a splinter that the body cannot eject.

You leave the way you came.

The terrace does not notice. The terrace notices everything. The terrace does not notice because your departure is too small a signal in the total acoustic environment, too brief a silence in the unbroken roar. The terrace notices because you have changed its frequency by the smallest possible increment, have added your footfalls and your breath and the particular resonant cavity of your chest to its symphony, and now that you are gone, the symphony is slightly different, and the colonies will adjust, and the Clickers will adjust, and the Threshers will adjust, and the structures will whistle a note they did not whistle before, and the system will incorporate your absence into its continuing, unending, unbreakable song.

The sound does not diminish.

It never will.
