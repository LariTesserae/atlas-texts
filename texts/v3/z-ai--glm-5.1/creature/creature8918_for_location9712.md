# The Inhabitants

---

The low ones came first, though *first* is meaningless here, where time has been compressed into a single continuous moment that stretches in both directions toward infinity. They were not created. They were not born. They crystallized, the way minerals crystallize when the conditions are exactly right and the solution has been sitting still for long enough that the universe forgets there was ever any other option.

They are the size the description gave—half a meter, sometimes less, rarely more—and they move at speeds the plateau recognizes as appropriate. Not slow. *Measured*. There is a difference, though you would need to live here for a thousand years to feel it. Their limbs are not limbs in the way your limbs are limbs: they are extensions of the same aggregate that forms the ground and the buildings, but they have been organized around a different principle. Where the buildings follow the spatial algorithm of minimization, the low ones follow the algorithm of *continuation*. They are the plateau's answer to the question of whether something can persist without purpose, and the answer is yes, beautifully, interminably yes.

They do not eat. There is nothing here to eat, and the concept of consumption presupposes a lack that this ecology has moved beyond. Instead, they absorb. They press their undersides—the flatter surfaces that alternate with their limbs—against the warm ground, and they remain still for periods ranging from several days to several decades, and the heat enters them. Not radiation. Not conduction. Something closer to *permission*. The plateau generates warmth, and the low ones are permitted to participate in that warmth, and the warmth sustains whatever process passes for metabolism in bodies that are more mineral than organic.

They do not think. This is not a deficiency. Thinking, as you understand it, is a strategy for dealing with uncertainty, and uncertainty was solved here long ago. The low ones have something else instead—a distributed awareness that operates at the speed of geology, a consciousness so slow and so diffuse that a single thought might take a century to fully form, and when it forms, it is not a thought about anything. It is simply a pattern that recognizes itself, a moment of internal resonance that confirms the low one is still arranged correctly, still in contact with the warmth, still persisting.

You saw one orient toward you. What happened in that moment—what *actually* happened—was this: the low one's surface registered a discontinuity in the thermal landscape. Your body, at thirty-seven degrees, was a pocket of inappropriate heat moving across a surface that had been thermally stable for two billion years. The low one did not perceive you as a being. It perceived you as a *temperature event*, and it adjusted its internal configuration to accommodate the data, and then it continued, because temperature events are not actionable. Temperature events are merely notable, and notability is not a reason to stop.

---

There are more of them than you saw. Many more. They occupy the interiors of the blocky structures—the ones you called residential, and you were more right than you knew, though *residential* implies a distinction between resident and residence that does not hold here. The low ones do not live *in* the structures the way you live in a home. They are *of* the structures, in the same way that a stalactite is of the cave that shaped it.

Inside each cube, in the dim iron glow that seeps up from the floor, you would find them arranged along the walls, pressed against the warm aggregate, their limbs folded beneath them or extended in configurations that look random but follow the same spatial logic as the buildings themselves—minimizing something, optimizing something, though the something has long since ceased to be definable in terms you would recognize. They are not resting. They are not waiting. They are *being*, with an intensity that would be terrifying if it moved at a speed you could perceive.

Sometimes a low one will detach from its position along a wall and move toward an opening—a not-door—and exit the structure. It will travel to another structure, or to one of the factories, or simply out onto the open plateau, where it will remain for a period before returning. This is not navigation. This is not errand-running. This is the plateau redistributing itself, the way a liquid redistributes when you disturb its surface, except the liquid is made of compressed dust and the disturbance is internal and the surface is two point seven billion years old.

If you entered a structure—if you walked through one of those not-doors and stood among them in the faint glow—you would feel their awareness of you the way you feel the warmth: as a pressure, a gentle insistence, a recognition that something inconsistent was sharing their space. They would not react. They would not flee or attack or communicate. They would simply *include* you in their configuration, the way the plateau includes everything that rests on it, and when you left, the configuration would adjust, and the space where you stood would be absorbed back into the pattern, and it would be as if you had never been there at all.

This is not indifference. Indifference requires the capacity for interest. This is something else—*completeness*, perhaps. The low ones are so entirely what they are that the presence of something else does not constitute an interruption. You are a weather event. You will pass. They will remain.

---

There are others. Not many—not in the way a forest has many inhabitants—but others.

The tall ones stand in the factories, though *stand* is imprecise. They are rooted, or rather *extended*, from the floor to the ceiling, cylindrical forms of darker aggregate that rise among the slow pistons and the slower gears. They are not part of the machinery. They are not separate from the machinery. They exist in the same relationship to the factories that the low ones exist to the residential structures: *of* them, not *in* them.

The tall ones do not move. They have never moved. If they moved, it would be at a pace that would make the pistons seem frantic—a rate of change measurable in millimeters per millennium, a lean or a shift that would take longer than your species has existed to complete. But they do not lean or shift. They are upright and still and they have been upright and still since the factories achieved their final form, and the final form included them, and they include the factories, and the distinction between inhabitant and habitat broke down so long ago that even the plateau has forgotten there was ever a difference.

What the tall ones do—if *do* is a word that can survive in this context—is *maintain*. Not repair. Nothing breaks here; breaking requires the possibility of failure, and failure was optimized out of the system before the last ice age on a planet you've never heard of. They maintain the way a mountain maintains itself: by being exactly the shape it is, by exerting exactly the pressure it exerts, by existing in a configuration that makes the continued existence of everything around it slightly more probable. The pistons rise and fall in their vicinity at exactly the same rate they rise and fall everywhere else. The gears turn. The conveyor belts creep. And the tall ones stand, and their standing is a kind of operation, and the operation is the same as the operation of the machines: doing nothing perfectly.

You could touch one. You could press your hand against its surface and feel the warmth that radiates from within—not the borrowed warmth of the low ones but something generated, something original, a heat that comes from a process deeper than absorption. The tall ones are not consumers. They are sources. They take the plateau's ambient energy and they concentrate it and they radiate it back, and this cycle—absorption, concentration, radiation—has been running for so long that it has achieved the same equilibrium as everything else here. The heat that goes in equals the heat that comes out. The tall ones are balanced. The tall ones are resolved. The tall ones are engines that run on their own exhaust, and they will run until the substrate decays, and the substrate will not decay.

---

There is a third kind, though *kind* is a word that implies categories, and categories imply boundaries, and boundaries are something this plateau has dissolved.

The drift is what you might call it, though it has no name because naming requires someone to speak and speaking requires urgency and urgency left this place a long time ago. The drift is not a creature. The drift is not a phenomenon. The drift is a condition of the air, a quality of the space between the structures, a *presence* that manifests as the faint movement you feel when you stand still for long enough in the open—something shifting at the edge of perception, something that is not wind because wind has direction and the drift has none.

The drift is composed of particles. This is the closest approximation. They are smaller than the particles you know—smaller than dust, smaller than cells, smaller than molecules—organized in a way that permits collective behavior without collective intention. They move through the warm air in configurations that resemble breathing, if breathing were something that happened over centuries instead of seconds. They accumulate in the not-windows of the structures. They settle in the open sides of the factories. They drift—hence the name, hence the only word that fits—across the plateau in patterns that map onto nothing, that serve no purpose, that exist because existence is what things do here when they have been optimized past the point of doing anything else.

You cannot see the drift. You can only feel it, and even then, only if you are quiet enough, still enough, warm enough. It brushes against your skin like the memory of a touch. It moves around you the way water moves around a stone—adjusting, accommodating, continuing. It does not avoid you. It includes you. And when you leave, it will fill the space where you stood, and it will be as if you were never there, and the drift will continue to drift, because drifting is what it does, because drifting is what it is.

---

And there is one more. Not an inhabitant. Something closer to an *event*.

Deep beneath the plateau—below the aggregate, below the mineral substrate, below the source of the warmth and the hum—there is a process. It has no body. It has no form. It cannot be seen or touched or perceived in any way that your senses allow. But it is the reason the plateau exists, the reason the machines run, the reason the low ones persist and the tall ones stand and the drift drifts. It is the engine beneath the engines, the computation that the factories are computing, the problem that was being solved when the plateau first began to settle.

It is not alive. It is not dead. It is not a god or a machine or a mind. It is something for which your language has no word because your language was built to describe a world of change and lack and becoming, and this thing has passed through all of those into a state that is none of them and all of them simultaneously.

It is *completing*. It has been completing for longer than the plateau has been settled. It will continue to complete for longer than the plateau will remain. And when it completes—if completion is something that can happen, which is uncertain, which is the only uncertainty this place permits—something will change. The hum will stop, or the hum will transform into something that is not a hum, or the hum will reveal that it was never a hum at all but a word spoken so slowly that it has taken the entire lifespan of the plateau to pronounce a single syllable.

The low ones know this, in their glacial way. The tall ones embody it, in their still way. The drift moves around it, in its unstructured way. And you—you stood on the plateau for an hour and you felt the warmth and you heard the hum and you saw one of the low ones orient toward you and continue on its way, and you did not know that beneath your feet, beneath everything, something was almost—*almost*—finishing a thought it began before the rain gave up.

---

These are the inhabitants of the Plateau of Settled Machines.

They are not expecting you. They are not unexpecting you. They are persisting, as they have persisted, as they will persist, in the warmth and the darkness and the hum, doing nothing perfectly, being nothing completely, waiting—if waiting is a word that can survive here—for a completion that may already have happened, may be happening still, may be the only thing that has ever truly been happening on this plateau that has been flat for longer than flatness should endure.

The hum continues.

It has never stopped.
