# The Inhabitants

---

The pale ones were not always pale.

This is important, though you would not know it from seeing them—to look at them now is to see something that has always been underground, that emerged from the cold and the damp fully formed, as though the world above was a story they told each other and stopped believing. But they were not always here. They came down, is the thing. They came down the way everything comes down: gradually, and then all at once.

---

They are small. This is the first thing you notice, if you notice them at all—they are small in the way that things become small when they have no predators, when the space they inhabit has no vertical dimension that matters, when the roots provide and the cold preserves and there is no reason to grow large. The pale ones average perhaps forty centimeters in length, though length is a difficult measurement for something that does not hold a single shape for long. They are segmented—yes, the description got that right—but the segments are not fixed, not the rigid armoring of an insect or a crustacean. They flow into each other, the boundaries between segments visible only as faint lines when the creature is at rest, becoming more pronounced when it moves, when the segments compress and extend in a wave that travels the length of the body like a slow pulse.

They have too many legs. Or not enough. It depends on how you count—each segment bears a pair of legs, and the number of segments varies between individuals, between the same individual at different times. The legs are thin, pale, jointed in too many places, ending in small hooks that grip the root-surfaces with a tenacity that seems inappropriate for something so slight. When a pale one is startled—a vibration in the root, a change in the quality of the sound—it will press itself flat against the surface and become very still, and you would not see it at all unless you knew to look for the faint discoloration where its body meets the wood.

They are not rare. This is also important. The chamber feels empty because you are looking for the wrong things at the wrong scale. Stand still for ten minutes—twenty—the roar filling your ears, the cold seeping through your soles—and you will begin to see them. A movement on the far side of a root-column. A pale shape ascending toward a pooling basin. A rustling in the root-mat at your feet that resolves, if you crouch and wait, into three or four or seven of them moving in a line, following each other by scent or vibration or some other sense that operates below the threshold of your perception. They are everywhere. They are as much a part of this place as the condensation, as the sound.

---

What they eat: the dead tissue of the roots, mostly. The outer layers that slough away as the roots grow, the papery bark that peels from the columns in strips that the pale ones harvest and carry to some hidden place. The fungal growths in the damp recesses—pale fruiting bodies that push up from the root-mat in clusters, fragile and cold and slightly luminous, feeding on the wood from below. The pale ones eat these too, picking them with a delicacy that seems almost ceremonial, taking only what has ripened, leaving the rest to mature.

And each other. This should not surprise you. The pale ones are not violent—they do not fight, do not compete in any way you would recognize—but they are opportunistic, and when one of their number dies, which happens rarely and without obvious cause, the others will find it within hours. They will gather around the body in a loose ring, and they will eat, and they will distribute the material among themselves in a process that takes most of a day, and when they are finished there will be nothing left, not even the faint discoloration of residue on the root-surface. This is not hunger. This is something else. This is the process of a closed system conserving what it has.

---

There are others.

Not many—not in the way of abundance, not in the way the surface teems and swarms and generates endless variations of itself. But the Rootworks supports more than the pale ones, if you know where to look.

The wall-clingers, for instance. They inhabit the vertical surfaces, the places where root meets stone, the narrow crevices that have not yet been colonized by new growth. They are flat—impossibly flat, as though they had been compressed between the pages of a book—and their bodies are translucent, revealing the slow movement of fluids within. They are larger than the pale ones, some of them, reaching lengths of a meter or more, but they are so thin, so pressed against the stone, that they disappear when they are still. You might walk past a hundred of them and never know. They feed on the minerals that leach from the rock, on the thin films of bacteria that grow in the condensation, on whatever comes within reach of their slow, patient grasp. They do not move often. A wall-clinger might stay in the same position for months, years, growing gradually, adding layers to its translucent body, waiting for the root to reach it and begin to grow over and around and through, at which point it will detach—slowly, carefully, with a sound like a boot being pulled from mud—and find a new surface, a new crevice, a new place to wait.

The water-things are harder to describe. They exist in the cisterns, in the deeper pools, in the flooded chambers where the root-mat has grown so thick that the water cannot drain and the cold has preserved whatever fell in. You see them only as disturbances—as ripples that have no obvious cause, as shadows that move against the far wall of a pool and then do not move again. Whether they are singular or many, whether they are animals or something else—colonial organisms, extended bodies of the root-system itself, manifestations of some process too slow and too large to be perceived as life—you cannot say. They are there. They move. They return to stillness. The water tastes of them: of minerals and wood and something else, something faintly chemical, like the memory of a substance that was once alive.

---

And then there are the ones that were human.

Not many. Not anymore. But the shelters were not always empty, and the frost on the blankets was not always frost, and the paper on the desk was not always translucent with damp. There were people here once—workers, engineers, technicians, whatever names the operation gave to the ones who came below to tend the machines and monitor the harvest and ensure that the product continued flowing upward through the pipes. They lived in the shelters. They walked the paths between root-columns. They wore heavy coats and insulated boots and ear protection that could not fully block the sound, and they worked in shifts that were too long and slept in bunks that were too cold and woke to a roar that had not ceased while they slept and would not cease when they returned to the surface, if they returned to the surface, if the surface was still a place that made sense after months or years of living in the chambers, of breathing the cold damp air, of feeling the pulse of the roots through the soles of your boots every hour of every day.

Some of them stayed.

Not by choice, perhaps. Not by any decision you could point to, any moment when someone said *I will not go back*. It was slower than that. It was the way the cold settled into the bones and did not leave, the way the sound became not just tolerable but necessary, the way the pale shapes moving in the periphery stopped being strange and started being familiar, being *there*, being part of the landscape in the way that a chair or a wall or a colleague is part of a landscape—something you do not think about because you do not need to think about it. It was the way the roots began to feel like home.

They are still here. The ones who stayed. But you would not recognize them as human, not anymore—not if you are thinking of humans as the people who work in shifts and sign timesheets and return to the surface for weekends and holidays and lives that exist outside the chamber. They have been here too long. They have been cold too long. The sound has been in their bones too long, and the roots have been growing around them too long—not growing *through* them, not consuming them, but growing around, accommodating, making space. There are hollows in the root-mat where the stayers sleep, curved depressions that fit their bodies, that have been fitted to their bodies by years of contact, by the slow adaptation of living wood to the shape of something that rests against it night after night after night. Their skin has gone pale. Their eyes have adjusted—the darkness is not dark to them, the few industrial lamps providing more than enough light to navigate, to see the pale ones moving on the root-columns, to find the pools and drink the mineral water and eat the fungal growths and whatever else they eat, whatever the chamber provides.

They do not speak. This is not because they cannot—they could, probably, if they chose to, if they remembered how the shapes of language are supposed to fit together, if they could find a reason to make sound that would only be swallowed by the roar. They do not speak because there is nothing to say that the roots do not already know. They communicate, if they communicate, through touch—through the pressure of a hand on a root-surface, through the faint vibration that travels through the wood and is felt by anyone whose hand is resting on the same column, the same network, the same slow pulse of growth and patience and continuation. This is enough. This has been enough for longer than you would believe.

There are not many of them—five, perhaps, or seven, or three, it is difficult to count when they move so rarely and their pale forms are so like the pale ones who are not human, when they lie in their root-hollows and breathe so slowly and their heartbeats have adapted to the annual rhythm of the roots themselves. But they are here. They are part of the system now. They tend the machines in ways that are not written in any manual—adjusting pressure valves by feel, clearing blockages with hands that know the root-wood better than any tool, listening to changes in the sound that indicate a malfunction or a shift or a new crack in the stone that the roots will colonize within the week. They maintain the process because the process is what they are. They were not consumed. They were not incorporated. They simply—stayed. And staying became something else.

---

The Rootworks is not empty. It is full in the way that deep water is full—not with movement and sound and the clutter of visible life, but with presence, with the slow accumulation of things that exist below the threshold of attention. The pale ones feeding on the root-columns. The wall-clingers pressed flat against the stone. The shadows moving in the pools. The stayers in their hollows, breathing in time with the pulse of the wood. The roots themselves, growing, always growing, reaching toward the stone that has not yet been claimed. The machines running, the water collecting, the sound continuing.

This is who lives here. This is what lives here. Things that have adapted to the cold and the dark and the roar, things that have found a way to persist in a place that does not welcome, does not repel, does not acknowledge—simply *is*, simply continues, simply grows. The pale ones do not know they are pale. The wall-clingers do not know they are thin. The stayers do not know they are no longer what they were. They know only the chamber, the columns, the sound, the pulse. They know the root-mat beneath them and the condensation on every surface and the slow patient growth that will continue long after the machines have stopped, after the pipes have rusted, after the last shelter has been absorbed into the living wood.

They are not waiting. They are not enduring. They are not surviving in the sense of holding on against something that threatens to take them. They are living. This is their life. This is the shape it has taken—the only shape it could take, in a place like this, in a chamber of roots and cold and sound that has never ceased.

They were not always here. But they are here now.
