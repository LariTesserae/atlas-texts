The people who live here have become what the stone required.

Not in any mystical sense — there is no communion with the earth, no sacred geometry, no chanting in the chambers that reifies a spiritual bond with the substrata. What has happened is simpler and more thorough: over generations of occupation, the human body has been selected by the constraints of the excavation, and the excavation has been shaped by the limits of the human body, and the two processes have been so long intertwined that it is no longer clear which adapted to which.

They are not tall. This is the first thing you would notice, if you noticed anything, because they would rather you didn't. The ceiling in many passages is low — the shale galleries, the narrow connector routes, the older residential chambers where the excavators were still learning how much stone could safely be removed — and the people who live here have developed a posture that is not a slouch but a kind of compressed readiness. They move through low spaces the way water moves through a channel: not fighting the dimensions but filling them efficiently. Some of the older residents have a permanent forward tilt to the spine, a slight hunch that is not deformity but accommodation, the body having learned that there is no point standing fully upright when the stone above prevents it. The children, who still have pliable bones and the sense that the world will conform to them, run upright until they hit a threshold age — eight, nine, ten — when they begin to fold, unconsciously, into the shape the city requires. It is not a sad thing. It is a thing that happens, like the calluses on their hands.

The calluses are significant. Everyone has them. The stone is the constant interlocutor — you touch it to navigate, you grip it to climb, you brace against it to gain leverage for the chisel, you rest your back against it when you pause in a passage. The fingertips of the excavators are dense with built-up skin, almost horned, and they can read the texture of a working face with their hands the way a scholar reads a manuscript with their eyes. A single pass of the fingers across a wall tells them the grain of the rock, the direction of the bedding, the proximity of moisture, the likelihood of a productive excavation. This knowledge is not mystical. It is accumulated and transmitted, and it begins in childhood, when the young are sent to trace the walls of known chambers with their hands while their elders watch and correct and explain what the child has not yet learned to feel. There are failures — people who never develop the sensitivity, who can chip stone adequately but cannot anticipate it — and these people are not shamed but are gently redirected toward other work. The city needs excavators, but it also needs cooks and tool-sharpeners and fat-renderers and the people who maintain the drainage channels and the people who care for the very young and the very old.

The very old are numerous. This is perhaps surprising, given the hazards of the place — the sharp edges of fresh excavation, the risk of collapse in new chambers, the constant cold that settles into joints and stays there, the occasional respiratory illness that sweeps through the packed passages when ventilation shifts — but the excavated city preserves its old people the way it preserves its meat. The steady temperature means no winter kill. The communal living means no isolation. An elder who can no longer work the chisel can still sit in a cooking chamber and watch the children and offer advice on the water-seekers' routes and describe the acoustic properties of chambers that were hollowed out before the current generation was born. The knowledge they carry is irreplaceable because so much of it is spatial and experiential and has never been recorded in any form except their own bodies and voices. When an elder dies, a specific version of the city dies with them — their paths, their shortcuts, their knowledge of which drips are reliable in dry months and which walls are safe to chip near and which chambers change their acoustics when too many people gather in them. The mourning period is long. Some of what is lost can be recovered by walking the routes the dead person walked. Some cannot.

Their skin is pale. Not the pallor of illness but the particular whiteness of people who have lived their entire lives in lamplight. There are those who have never seen the surface — children born in the lower chambers, raised in the deeper neighborhoods, who know the white distant light only from descriptions and from the rare moments when it enters through the few natural openings and falls in shafts against the far walls of the upper chambers. These children do not seem to suffer for the lack. They have the city — which is vast and still becoming, which offers new passages to explore and new chambers to discover, which is in the slow process of revealing itself like a body opening from the inside. The surface, with its exposed horizons and unmediated weather, sounds terrifying to them. They are not wrong. People do die above. The hunting parties that go up to trade and track game return with stories of cold that changes by the hour, of rain that falls without ceiling, of the strange disorientation that comes from being able to see for miles in every direction with nothing between you and the sky. The surface people — the trading partners, the occasional migrants who join the settlement from above — have a different look about them. Weathered. Weathered in the literal sense, as if they are slowly being eroded by exposure. The below-people are not eroded. They are excavated, like the city itself — their rough edges removed by the stone, their forms shaped by the dimensions of the chambers they inhabit.

Their eyes are adapted to low light but not to darkness. This is an important distinction. The stories about underground people who see in total blackness are just stories, and the residents of the excavation know this and find the myth both amusing and slightly insulting, as if the surface world believes they have ceased to be human. They cannot see in the dark. What they can do is navigate in the dark — two different things entirely. They know their city by touch, by sound, by the movement of air on their skin, by the subtle temperature shifts that indicate a passage branching left or right, by the smell of specific chambers (cooking fat and old smoke near the residential clusters, clean mineral cold near the water collection points, the faint bitter smell of the pale fungus where it grows in the damp crevices). A below-person caught in a passage without a lamp can find their way to safety if they remain calm and attend to the information the stone provides. Panic is what kills you in the dark, not the dark itself. The children learn this early, sometimes through deliberate exercises conducted by the elders, who extinguish the lamps in known chambers and make the young find their way out by hand and ear and the faint breath of ventilation on their cheeks.

There is no single leader. The city is too distributed for that, and the authority structures are too old and too specific to be reduced to hierarchy. The water-seekers direct excavation toward new sources; they have authority in matters of water and no authority in matters of food distribution or child-rearing or the resolution of disputes between families. The excavators determine which chambers are safe to hollow and which should be left for later; their word on structural matters is final, but they cannot dictate how a finished chamber is used. The family clusters — extended networks of kin that the below-people call "hearths," after the cooking fires that define their shared spaces — govern their own members and negotiate with other hearths for access to passages, storage, and water rights. There are conflicts. The acoustics of the city mean that arguments carry, sometimes into chambers the disputants didn't know could hear them, and the residents have developed a cultural preference for indirectness, for careful phrasing, for the slow approach. Direct confrontation is not forbidden but it is uncomfortable, the way shouting above the murmur of the city is uncomfortable, and disputes that cannot be resolved through negotiation are brought before a rotating council of elders who are chosen for their knowledge of precedent and their ability to listen without speaking for long stretches of time.

They sing, but quietly. The singing is a navigation tool as much as an art — certain chambers have resonances that the residents have learned to use, frequencies that amplify or absorb the voice, and the songs they sing are often built around these acoustic properties, the melody shaped to fit the room the way water fills a carved basin. There are songs that only work in specific chambers, that dissolve into nonsense or dissonance if attempted elsewhere. The residents know this and treat these songs as belonging to those places, as if the chamber and the melody are parts of the same created thing. A song sung in the wrong chamber is not sacrilege — they do not have that concept, exactly — but it is wrong, an error of fit, like wearing another hearth's clothing or using another family's water-drip without permission.

They are not a lonely people. The constant proximity, the shared chambers, the knowledge that the walls between you and your neighbor are only as thick as the stone the excavators left — these produce a particular kind of closeness that the surface world might find suffocating but that the below-people experience as warmth. Privacy exists but it is a function of time rather than space: you can be alone by being the only one awake in a chamber, or by retreating to a sleeping alcove when others are working, or by walking a passage at a hour when no one else is traveling. But there is always the knowledge of others nearby, the sounds of breath and movement transmitted through the stone, the subtle shifts in air pressure when someone enters a chamber on the other side of a wall. They have learned to live in the presence of others the way they have learned to live in the presence of the stone — not ignoring it, but accommodating it, making room for it, becoming part of the same system.

They do not think of themselves as strange. This is perhaps the most important thing to understand. They know the surface exists. Some of them visit it, for trade or for hunting or for the rare and unsettling experience of seeing the sky. They know that other people live above, in buildings they constructed rather than excavated, in towns where the weather changes and the light varies and the horizon is visible. They do not envy these people. They do not pity them either. The below-people have the stone, and the slow work of becoming, and the knowledge that the city will outlast them — that the chambers they hollow will be occupied by their children's children, that the passages they carve will become the routes by which future generations reach water, that their labor is literally foundational, that every swing of the chisel creates space for life that has not yet been conceived. This is not a religious belief. It is a simple material fact. The stone is there. The voids are made. The people fill them. The city continues.

And the excavators keep working. You can hear them now if you listen — the faint chipping from some distant working face, transmitted through the rock, a rhythm that the residents feel in their feet and their fingertips and the small bones of their inner ear. The city is still becoming. The people are still becoming. The stone is still deciding what it will yield. And in the lamplit chambers, in the cool damp air, in the steady presence of the earth on every side, life goes on in the shape the stone has given it — folded, patient, dense with accumulated meaning, still being hollowed out, still making room for whatever comes next.
