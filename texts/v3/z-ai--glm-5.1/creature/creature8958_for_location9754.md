The people who came to the Plateau of Convergence came for reasons they could not fully articulate, and this is the first thing you must understand about them. They did not come to conquer or to extract or to worship, though their presence resembles all three at different moments. They came because the city was here. The city was here before them — this is not in dispute among them, though everything else is — and it was empty, and it was waiting, and they had the particular kind of restlessness that mistakes an open door for an invitation.

They are not scientists. Or rather, some of them were scientists once, in the way that someone who hasn't painted in twenty years is still a painter. The scientific method requires hypotheses, and the plateau resists hypothesis. You can measure the water — they have, they do, in cisterns marked with numbers that correspond to nothing published — but you cannot say why it weeps. You can map the structures — and their maps are beautiful, obsessive things, constantly revised — but you cannot say what they are for. The city provides. The city maintains. The city's processes are invisible and total and after a certain point, you either accept this or you leave.

Most leave. This is the second thing.

The ones who stay share a quality that is hard to name but easy to recognize. Call it tolerance for the indeterminate. Call it comfort with function without understanding. They are the kind of people who can live in a house without knowing how the plumbing works — not because they lack curiosity, but because they have learned to locate curiosity elsewhere, in the lived experience of a place rather than its mechanism. They are not unbothered. They are bothered in a way that has become atmospheric, like the mist — so constant it no longer registers as separate from the air.

There are perhaps three thousand of them at any given time, spread across a city built for millions. They cluster. Not from fear — there is nothing to fear here that the plateau itself does not already embody — but from the simple mammals' preference for the sound of breathing that isn't one's own. They form neighborhoods in the way water forms pools: finding the natural depressions, the sheltered places, the lee of certain towers where the wind loses its edge. They have not built. The city cannot be built upon in any way that sticks; the material rejects foreign matter like skin rejecting a splinter, slowly pushing it to the surface over weeks or months. Instead, they have *inhabited*. They have claimed rooms and corridors and platforms by the simple act of being in them repeatedly until the space accepts the shape of their living.

Their dwellings are recognizable by the small accumulations. A pile of rocks at an entrance, meaning *someone lives here*. A strip of fabric hung across a threshold, meaning *privacy is requested*. A bowl left outside a door — and the bowls are made from the city's own material, because nothing else survives the cold and the wet; you learn to shape it by finding the places it wants to break — meaning *I have enough to share*. The city's corridors are wide and many, and the lighting (sourceless, even, slightly warmer in the inhabited sections, though no one will confirm this) creates the impression of a hospital at three in the morning: functional, clean, aware of the bodies it shelters.

They eat what the city offers. This is the part that disturbs visitors most, and the part that the residents speak of least. The city's cisterns collect water, but certain cisterns — and there is no visible difference between them — also collect something else. A film. A residue. It's organic, or near enough: proteinaceous, faintly sweet, the color of the stone when the stone is wet. It gathers in the same way the moisture does, condensing from some process deep in the structure that no one has accessed or wants to access. The residents call it *sustenance*. They do not call it food. They spread it on flat pieces of the city's material, which becomes soft when heated and hard when cold, and they eat it slowly, because it is rich and because eating is one of the few activities here that rewards haste.

Some of them have tried to grow things. The soil between the structures is thin and mineral and perpetually damp, and the plants that naturally occur here — the tough dark grasses, the leather-leaved herbs — are not cultivatable. They grow where they grow and no elsewhere. But there are depressions in the rock where sediment collects, and in these, with effort, certain residents have coaxed alien green things: small pale vegetables, leaves like paper, roots that taste like the plateau tastes — wet stone and nothing. The crops fail more often than they succeed. The gardeners persist. They are among the most respected members of the community, not because their work succeeds but because it represents the refusal to become entirely dependent on the city's opaque generosity.

Children are born here. Not many — the cold and the damp and the strange diet do not encourage fertility — but some. The children are the third thing, and perhaps the most important.

They are not like the children of the lowlands. This is not a deficiency; it is an adaptation. They are quiet from the beginning, not because they are taught to be but because noise is simply not their first language. They learn to move across the wet rock before they learn to walk on dry ground, and as a result they have a particular gait — wide-based, deliberate, constantly aware of footing — that marks them even when they visit the territories below. Their eyes adjust to the sourceless light so completely that full darkness is frightening to them and full sunlight is painful. They do not startle at animals. They have grown up alongside the grazers and the predators and the pale corridor-things, and they regard them with the same mild tolerance the animals show each other: *you are here, I am here, we are both still, this is acceptable.*

The children play, but their play is strange to outsiders. They build structures from the city's material — the material allows itself to be broken when children are the ones breaking it, another unexplained tolerance — and then they watch the city repair itself. This is a game that takes days. They mark a wall, chip a piece, carve a symbol, and then they wait, and the city slowly, slowly undoes their work, pushing the material back into wholeness with the same patient force it uses to reject foreign matter. The children have learned to read the speed of repair as a kind of communication. *The city wants this to heal quickly*, they say. *The city doesn't mind this being here for a while.* They have developed a taxonomy of the city's responses that is more nuanced than anything the adults have produced, and when asked how they know what the city wants, they look confused by the question. *You can feel it*, they say. And when pressed: *It's like the wind. You can't see it but you know which way it's going.*

The oldest among the residents — not old in years, necessarily, but old in the plateau's terms, which are measured in how many seasons of condensation you've watched — speak of the city as though it were a living thing, but they do not personify it. It does not want or feel or plan in the way humans want and feel and plan. It *processes*. It maintains. It adjusts. When the plateau shifts, as it does, the city shifts with it, and the residents must shift too, and this is simply the condition of living here: you are always slightly realigning. The floor of your dwelling may be two degrees different from last year. The corridor you used to reach the eastern cisterns may have narrowed or widened. You adapt. You don't think about it the way you don't think about your own cells replacing themselves. It happens at a rate below noticing unless you pay attention, and the people who stay are the people who can pay attention without becoming anxious about what they notice.

There are those who have tried to communicate with the city directly. They stand in the corridors at night when the vibration is strongest, and they press their hands against the warm seamless walls, and they think — or speak, or sing, depending on the tradition — into the material. Nothing responds, at least not in any language. But some of them report that after these sessions, the light in their dwelling is slightly warmer, or the cistern near their quarter yields more of the sweet residue, or the wind outside their entrance shifts to block the rain. The community treats these reports the way lowland communities treat prayer: with respect, with skepticism, with the unspoken understanding that causation is not correlation but that correlation is not nothing either.

The predators know the people. This is worth stating plainly because it is the relationship that visitors find most difficult to witness. The predators — the long-bodied, silent-coordinated hunters that work the plateau's ridges — do not hunt humans. This is not because they cannot, and it is not because they have been taught not to. It is, as best as anyone can determine, because humans are not food here. The city's residue, the mineral film the grazers eat, the pale corridor-things, the burrowers — these are the plateau's food web, and humans are not part of it. The predators regard people with a flat, assessing gaze that contains no malice and no fear, only the calm recognition of *not-prey*. This is unnerving. A predator that fears you is manageable; a predator that ignores you is a mirror.

The people, in turn, have learned to read the predators' movements as information. When the predators shift their hunting grounds, it means the grazers have moved, which means the mineral film has shifted, which means the water is emerging from a new fracture somewhere, which means the plateau is settling in a particular direction. The predators are the plateau's early warning system, and the people who have been here longest watch them with the same attention they give the weeping rock. *The land is thinking*, they say when the predators change pattern. *We should think too.*

Visitors come, though not often. The plateau is remote and the ascent is long and the city — for all its accommodations — does not welcome newcomers. Not hostilely. The material simply doesn't soften for them. The corridors are colder. The light is dimmer. The cisterns near their quarters run dry. The city isn't rejecting them; it's failing to recognize them, and a city that does not recognize you cannot maintain you. Most visitors leave after a few days, chilled and damp and unsettled by the quiet and the wet stone and the people who move through the corridors like they were born there, which, in some cases, they were.

Those who stay beyond the first week begin to change. Their skin takes on the gray cast of the basalt. Their movements slow. They start to eat the residue without being shown it, finding the productive cisterns by some instinct that has no obvious source. They learn the animals' names before they learn the residents' names, because the animals are more visible and more consistent. They stop flinching at the predators' gaze. They begin, without meaning to, to weep at the same rate as the rock — a thin film of moisture on their faces in the cold, which they wipe away and which returns, which is not tears but looks like tears, which is the plateau's moisture finding another surface to condense on.

After a month, they are residents. They do not remember the moment of transition. It was not a decision. It was a settling, like sediment in a depression, like water finding its level, like the plateau itself still finding its final shape.

At night, the residents sleep in the city's warmth, and they dream. Their dreams are not of the plateau — the plateau does not need to be dreamed about because it is already everywhere — but of the lowlands, of heat and noise and the chaos of places where the land has finished forming and everything is already what it will be. They wake from these dreams relieved to be here, in the quiet, in the wet, in the still-becoming. They press their hands to the warm walls. They feel the vibration. They feel the city noticing them, not with love or with welcome but with the simple acknowledgment of a system that has registered their presence and will maintain them while they remain.

They are not the city's children. They are not its worshippers or its subjects or its experiment. They are its inhabitants in the oldest sense: they dwell here. They have found a place that tolerates them and they have chosen to be tolerated, and in the exchange — the city gives warmth and water and sustenance; the people give nothing the city needs but nothing it rejects — there is a balance as precise and as indifferent as the balance between the grazers and the predators, between the condensation and the stone, between the plateau and the slow tectonic force that is still, after all these millions of years, pushing it up.

The quiet, the people will tell you if you ask, is not the absence of something. It is the presence of patience. The plateau is patient. The city is patient. The water is patient. The predators are patient. And the people — the ones who stay — they have learned the patience of things that are still becoming, that have not yet arrived at their final shape, that weep from fractures they cannot see and eat from processes they cannot name and sleep in walls that breathe with a vibration too low to hear.

They live here. It is enough. It has to be enough, because the plateau does not offer anything more, and it does not need to, because what it offers is the chance to exist in a place that is still deciding what it will be — alongside a city that is still deciding what it will do — among animals that are still deciding what they will tolerate — and to be, yourself, in all that uncertainty, still deciding too.

The quiet is the sound of all that deciding, happening at once, at a pace too slow for hearing.

You listen anyway.
