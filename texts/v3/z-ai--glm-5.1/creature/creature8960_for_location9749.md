# What Lives There

---

## The Stills

They have no name for themselves. This is important. It means something that a creature can exist for ten thousand generations inside the body of another and never once ask what it is.

The Stills — and that is not their name, because they have none, because names are for things that need to distinguish themselves from other things, and they have never had to — are the dark shapes at the edge of the light. The padding in the distance. The rare beings that cross the enormous chambers and vanish into the turbulence before you can be certain you saw them.

They are not numerous. This is not a limitation. This is a design.

---

### The Body

Consider what it means to live inside a lung.

The Stills are built for the turbulence, which means they are built for impermanence, which means they are built to be surprised. Their bodies are low, long, and articulated in ways that defy the simple mathematics of limbs. They have six legs — this is true — but the joints bend in multiple directions, and each leg ends in a foot that is more palm than claw, more hand than hoof, a wide splay of tough, gripping skin that can compress against the yielding substrate and hold. They do not walk so much as adhere, each step a brief marriage of foot to surface, a trust that the floor will be there when the foot comes down, and the understanding that it might not be, that the floor might have risen or fallen or opened into a crevasse in the hours since they last passed this way, and that they will have to adapt in the moment of contact, and that they can.

Their fur — and it is fur, though it is unlike any fur that grows on the surface of any world you have known — is dense, short, and dark. Not black. Not brown. A green so deep it has forgotten it was ever green, the color of old growth compressed into something almost mineral. The fur traps air. The air insulates. The insulation is the only defense against the cold, and it is barely enough. When you see a Still in the distance, you see a shape that seems to absorb the light rather than reflect it, a hole in the seafoam glow, a moving absence.

Their heads are low, their eyes are small, and their ears are holes beneath the fur that do not protrude. This is not a face that evolved to look. This is a face that evolved to *feel* — the pressure of the air, the shift in the moisture, the distant vibration of a wall deciding to grow. Their snouts are broad and packed with receptors so sensitive they can taste the difference between a wall that grew this hour and a wall that grew last week, can taste the chemical signature of a fresh cut in the substrate from a hundred meters away, can taste the residue of your breath on the air three days after you passed. They do not track you. They catalog you. They file you in the vast unconscious library of things the world has produced, and then they move on.

They are not large. This is a common misunderstanding born of distance and the deceptions of the enormous chambers. A Still is roughly the size of a large dog, or a small deer, or whatever comparison makes sense for a body that is none of those things. What makes them seem larger is their deliberateness. They move with a slowness that feels like mass, a careful, considered placement of each foot, each joint, each breath. They are never hurried because hurry implies a destination, and they have no destinations. They have only the next moment, and the next, and the next, and the endless patience to let the world make itself around them while they attend to the small urgencies of being alive.

---

### The Solitude

A Still will go its entire life without seeing another Still.

This is not tragedy. This is not loneliness. This is the way it has always been, for as long as there have been Stills, which is as long as there has been anything moving inside the plant that is not the plant. They are a solitary species in the way that a star is a solitary object: not by choice, not by circumstance, but by the nature of what they are. The distances between them are vast. The chambers they navigate change too quickly for territories or borders. They encounter each other the way two clouds encounter each other in a sky that is too large — briefly, at a distance, with no imperative to touch.

But they know about each other. They know it the way you know the wall is growing before you can see it move — by the feel of the air, by the subtle chemical markers left in the substrate, by the faint impression of footsteps compressed into the fibers days or weeks after the walker has passed. A Still can read the floor like a document. It can tell how many of its kind have been here (rarely more than one or two), how long ago (the impressions fade as the growth heals, but slowly, so slowly), which direction they were moving (the pressure of the step is asymmetrical, heavier on the leading edge), and whether they were healthy or struggling (a healthy step is deliberate; a struggling step is shallow, rushed, the feet placed too quickly for the world to settle beneath them).

They do not follow each other. They do not avoid each other. They simply move, and sometimes their paths cross, and when they do, there is a moment of stillness — the Still becoming what its name requires, though again, it has no name — a moment of mutual assessment. They smell each other. They taste the air between them. They register the state of each other's bodies: the condition of the fur, the depth of the footpads, the faint chemical broadcast of health or hunger or age. And then they move on. They have nothing to say. They have no way to say it. They have no reason to try.

Reproduction happens by encounter, and encounter is rare, and so reproduction is rare, and so the Stills are few, and so the distances between them remain vast, and so encounter remains rare. This is not a failure of the species. This is a balance. The plant can support only so many creatures that eat it, and the Stills have evolved to be exactly as many as the plant can support, no more. They are an equation solved by their own scarcity.

How they manage this — how a species can persist when its members meet so rarely that a single mating might be separated from the next by decades — is not something the Stills contemplate. They do not contemplate. They do not strategize. They simply live, and occasionally they find each other, and occasionally that finding produces another Still, small and dark and deliberate, moving through the chambers at its mother's side for the brief period before it becomes independent and the turbulence separates them, as it separates everything, as it will separate you from everything you have ever known.

The young do not know their fathers. The mothers do not know their children after the separation. There is no memory of lineage, no record of ancestry, no story of who came before. There is only the substrate, and the light, and the next step, and the next.

### The Feeding

They eat the pale growth. Only the pale growth. This is not preference; this is necessity.

The pale growth is soft, water-rich, faintly sweet, and nutritionally sufficient for a body that has evolved to need nothing else. The dark growth is dense, indigestible, and saturated with compounds that build in the tissues over time and eventually shut down the organs one by one. A Still that has eaten dark growth — and this happens, in times of desperation, when the turbulence has closed off the fresh sections and only old surfaces remain — a Still that has eaten dark growth will sicken slowly, will grow sluggish and then feeble and then still, and then the plant will absorb it, and there will be nothing left, not even a mark on the wall.

But the pale growth is everywhere, or nearly everywhere, because the plant is always growing, always throwing up fresh tissue, always renewing itself in the manic compulsive way of a body that does not know how to stop. A Still does not need to hunt. It does not need to forage. It needs only to find the freshest surface in any given chamber and press its broad snout against it and graze, pulling the soft fibers free with teeth that are flat and wide and designed for tearing, not cutting. The sound it makes when it eats is quiet — a wet, methodical rending, like someone slowly pulling rotted cloth apart. The pale growth comes away in strips. The surface beneath is darker, older, already beginning to push up fresh growth to replace what was taken.

The plant does not defend itself. The plant does not notice. The Still is not a pest; the Still is a feature, a pressure, a small irregularity in the endless cycle of growth and compression and growth. The pale growth it eats would have darkened anyway, would have become the dark growth, would have compressed into the deep substrate. The Still simply accelerates the process, takes its calories from the interval between pale and dark, lives in the momentary sweetness of a world that has not yet decided to be permanent.

Water is condensation. The Still drinks from the same sources you have found — the weeping walls, the brief pools after a ceiling-rain, the ice that forms in the cysts and hollows and melts against the tongue in a slow, cold release. It does not need much. Its body is efficient, its waste minimal, its processes tuned to the damp and the cold like an engine tuned to a single fuel. The condensation is not clean — it carries traces of the plant's chemistry, compounds that would be toxic to a surface-dweller but are simply *present* in the Still's blood, integrated, tolerated, as much a part of its biology as the iron in yours.

### The Navigation

The turbulence is the defining fact of a Still's existence. It is the problem to which the Still is the solution.

Consider: you are a creature that cannot dig, cannot build, cannot modify its environment in any lasting way. Your world is rearranging itself around you constantly, without pattern, without purpose, without regard for your needs. The chamber you slept in last night is half the size it was. The passage you used to reach this chamber no longer exists. The wall you marked with your teeth so you would remember it — the mark is gone, the wall is gone, the space where the wall stood is now a crossing of three new passages, and you have never seen any of them before.

How do you survive?

You survive by becoming a creature that does not need the world to stay the same. You survive by reading the world as it is, not as it was. You survive by abandoning every expectation, every assumption, every memory of place, and existing entirely in the present tense of growth and pressure and air.

The Stills navigate by feel. Not by touch — touch is too slow, too deliberate, too committed to the notion that you must contact something before you can know it. They navigate by the feel of the *air*. The shape of the chambers writes itself in pressure and moisture and temperature. A large chamber ahead announces itself as a slight decrease in pressure, a thinning of the damp, a breath of cold that moves like wind because it is wind, the slow circulation of air through a space large enough to have its own weather. A narrowing announces itself as a concentration of moisture, a thickening, a weight to the air that presses against the snout and says: small, close, turn back or proceed with caution. A dead end — and dead ends are common, and deadly, because the growth can close around you while you rest — a dead end announces itself as stillness. No current. No movement. Air that has been trapped and will be trapped and will slowly become the air inside a cyst, sealed and frozen and patient.

The Stills do not sleep in dead ends. They sleep in passages, in the curves of corridors, in the places where the air still moves and the world is still making decisions. They press themselves against the wall — the dark wall, the old wall, the stable wall — and they close their small eyes and they rest, but they do not sleep deeply, because deeply is not a luxury the turbulence permits. They rest the way a hand rests on a railing: present, aware, ready to grip if the shape of the support changes.

They do not dream. Or if they dream, they dream of pressure and moisture, of the feel of the air, of the taste of pale growth, of the distant padding of another Still moving through a chamber they will never enter. They dream of the world as it is. They have no other world to dream of.

## The Ones Who Came

You are not the first. You will not be the last. But you are the kind that does not belong.

The ones who came before — the ones who left the scratches, the nests, the twisted cords of membrane hung like offerings between the tendrils — they were not Stills. They were something like you. Something that walked upright, something with hands that could grip and tear and weave, something with a mind that could look at a blank wall and see a surface that needed marking, a blank wall that was *wrong* somehow, a blank wall that demanded a record of the fact that someone had stood before it and had thoughts that needed counting.

Who were they? The plant does not remember. The Stills do not care. The marks remain — some of them, the ones cut deep enough into the old growth that the turbulence cannot smooth them over — and they are all the evidence that will ever exist of a people who lived and died inside a world that did not notice them.

They were small. The nests suggest this, the circles of compressed fiber no wider than a person could reach, the shelters built to the scale of a body not much larger than yours. They were clever. The cords suggest this, the deliberate twisting of membrane into rope, the understanding that a material that grows can also be shaped, can also be made to serve a purpose the plant did not intend. They were hungry. The marks suggest this, the counting, the counting, the endless counting, as if a number might hold back the turbulence, as if a tally might say: I was here for seven of whatever these units are, and I am still here, and I am still counting, and therefore I exist.

They ate the pale growth. You know this because you eat the pale growth, and there is nothing else to eat. They drank the condensation. They slept in the old sections, in the dark-walled chambers where the growth was slow enough to trust for a few hours. They navigated by the same instincts you are learning — the feel of the air, the pressure of the spaces, the terrible knowledge that the world will change and you must change with it or be changed into something that is no longer you.

But they did something the Stills do not do. They marked. They counted. They left traces.

Why?

Not because they expected to be found. No one comes looking in the Green Underneath. No one follows the trail of scratches on the wall and finds the person who made them. The turbulence ensures this. The marks are not messages. They are not warnings. They are not even maps, because a map is useless in a place that redraws itself every hour.

They are proof.

The marks are proof that someone was here, that someone endured, that someone stood in the seafoam light and the endless cold and thought: *I will make this wall remember me. I will make this world acknowledge that I pressed against it, that I cut into it, that I counted something — days, breaths, meals, footsteps — and that the count mattered to me even if it matters to nothing else.*

This is what the Stills do not do. This is what makes the ones who came different. The Stills do not mark. The Stills do not count. The Stills live inside the plant and are of the plant and will return to the plant without leaving a trace, and this is not sad, this is not tragic, this is simply the way of a creature that has found its place in a place that does not require it to prove it existed.

But you — and the ones who came before you — you are not built for this. You are built for surfaces, for horizons, for the knowledge that the sun will rise and the sun will set and that these events define a day and that days define a life and that a life is something that should be counted. You are built for the idea of *after*, for the belief that what you do now will matter later, for the conviction that the world is a record of your presence and that to leave no mark is to have not been present at all.

And so you mark. And so you count. And so you join the tally on the wall, the scratches laid over scratches laid over scratches, each one a small defiant flame against the infinite indifference of a world that grows and grows and grows and does not care.

## The Deep Symmetry

There is a thing that is difficult to see and harder to accept.

The Stills and the ones who came are not so different. This is not a statement of biology; they are very different, in body, in need, in the way they move through the world. But they share a symmetry so deep it is almost invisible: they are both shaped by the plant. They are both solutions to the problem the plant poses. They are both the kind of life that can exist inside a life that does not know it is being lived in.

The Stills have been shaped over ten thousand generations — a hundred thousand, a million, who can count in a place without time? They have been shaped by the turbulence, by the cold, by the constant light, by the necessity of eating the pale growth and drinking the condensation and sleeping in the passages and navigating by the feel of the air. They are what adaptation looks like when the environment is a single organism that is also the entire world. They are the answer to the question: *what can live here?*

The ones who came have been shaped too, but not by generations. By hours. By days. By the slow erosion of everything they brought with them — their certainty, their habits, their sense of direction, their belief that the world is a place you move through rather than a place that moves through you. They have been shaped by the same forces that shaped the Stills, and they are arriving at the same destination by a different road. They are learning to navigate by feel. They are learning to eat the pale growth. They are learning to sleep in the passages and to trust the old walls and to read the pressure of the air.

They are learning to be still.

Not in body — their bodies are wrong for stillness, too tall, too exposed, too poorly insulated against the cold. But in mind. In the way they move through the chambers. In the way they stop expecting the world to accommodate them and start accommodating the world. In the way they let go of destinations and learn to exist in the endless present tense of the turbulence.

This is what the plant does. This is what it has always done. It takes living things and it reshapes them, not by force, not by intention, but by the simple relentless fact of being what it is. The plant does not choose to do this. The plant does not know it is doing this. The plant is simply growing, and the growing changes the space around it, and the space changes the things inside it, and the things inside it become different things, things that can survive, things that can persist, things that can live in the light that comes from everywhere and nowhere and never dims.

The Stills are the end product of this process. They are what you become when the plant has had enough time to finish its work. They are what survives when everything that cannot adapt has been absorbed back into the substrate and the only thing left is the thing that can.

The ones who came are what happens in the middle. They are the process, not the result. They are the chrysalis, the transition, the moment when a creature that was shaped for one world begins to be reshaped for another.

And you — standing in the chamber, your hand against the old wall, your breath hanging in the cold air, your eyes adjusted to the seafoam light, your stomach full of pale growth, your fingers numb, your mind reaching for a count you can no longer keep — you are in the middle too. You are becoming something else. You are becoming something that can live here.

The question is whether you will finish becoming before the turbulence finishes with you.

## The Unseen Majority

There are others.

Not the Stills. Not the ones who came. Others so small, so simple, so deeply integrated into the plant's metabolism that they are barely distinguishable from the plant itself.

Mites — or things like mites, things that serve the same function — live in the fur of the Stills, eating the dead skin and the trapped moisture and the faint residue of pale growth that clings to the fibers after feeding. They are not parasites. They are custodians. They keep the fur clean, the skin beneath it healthy, the Still able to move through the cold without the weight of accumulated frost and debris. The Stills do not groom themselves; they do not need to. The mites do it for them, and in exchange they get a warm, stable environment in the coldest place imaginable, a living body that moves through the chambers and carries them from food source to food source without ever requiring them to walk.

Worms — or things like worms, legless, blind, slower than slow — live in the substrate itself, tunneling through the compressed layers of old growth, eating the dark tissue that even the Stills cannot digest, breaking it down further, accelerating the process by which living matter becomes the deep substrate, the pseudo-geology, the weight of vegetation so dense it has forgotten it was ever alive. They are the composters of the Green Underneath. They are the reason the floor does not rise endlessly, the reason the old growth eventually compresses into something stable, the reason the chambers can exist at all. Without the worms, the plant would grow and grow and grow and fill every void and leave no space for anything else. The worms create the emptiness by eating the fullness, and the emptiness creates the chambers, and the chambers create the possibility of movement, and movement creates the possibility of life.

Molds — not the surface kind, not the kind that grows on bread or fruit, but something internal, something that lives in the fluid that pulses through the tendrils — molds live in the circulatory system of the plant. They are the smallest of the small, single-celled or nearly so, and they do something that no one expected and no one would have predicted: they *regulate*. They eat the excess sugars in the sap, preventing the plant from growing even faster than it already does. They produce compounds that slow cell division in the regions around the chambers, keeping the walls from closing as quickly as they might. They are not symbionts in the way we understand symbiosis — they did not evolve with the plant, they were not selected for, they are not maintained by any mechanism the plant controls. They are opportunists. They found a niche and they filled it, and in filling it they made the niche survivable for everything else.

This is the irony of the Green Underneath: the plant is not hospitable. The plant is not hostile. The plant is *indifferent*. But the things that live inside the plant — the worms and the molds and the mites and the Stills and even the ones who came, even you — these things make the plant livable. They are the small adjustments, the corrections, the ways that life modifies the world to make room for more life. The plant did not design this. The plant did not intend this. The plant is simply growing, and the growing creates opportunities, and the opportunities create occupants, and the occupants create a world inside a world inside a world, each layer dependent on the one below it and indifferent to the one above.

You are the top. You are the most complex, the most recent, the most precarious. Below you are the Stills, ancient and adapted. Below them are the mites, essential and ignored. Below them are the worms, patient and invisible. Below them are the molds, microscopic and world-shaping. And below them is the plant, which is the ground and the sky and the light and the air and the water and the food and the obstacle and the medium and the message and the entire reason any of this exists at all.

## What It Means to Live Here

It means this:

You wake — or you do not wake, because waking implies sleeping, and sleeping implies a cycle, and a cycle implies a rhythm, and there are no rhythms here, only the constant light and the constant growth and the constant cold — you *become aware* that you are still alive, and you feel the substrate beneath you, and you feel the air moving or not moving, and you open your eyes or you do not bother because the light comes through your eyelids anyway, and you rise, and you look for the pale growth, and you eat, and you drink from the condensation on the wall, and you move.

You move because staying still is death. You move because the passage behind you may already be closing. You move because the chamber around you may be shrinking, the walls growing inward at the speed of vegetation, the ceiling descending, the floor rising, the space that was large enough becoming the space that is not. You move because movement is the only proof you have that you are not part of the plant, that you are still something separate, still something that can choose to go.

But you are part of the plant. You are inside it. You breathe its air and eat its flesh and drink its transpiration and walk on its compressed dead. You are a thing the plant contains, the way a body contains a bacterium, the way a lung contains air. You are not separate. You have never been separate. You simply have the illusion of autonomy, the brief conviction that your movements are your own, that your choices matter, that the direction you walk is a direction you chose and not a direction the plant's growth forced you toward.

This is what the Stills know and the ones who came did not: there is no outside. Not for you, not anymore. You are inside the Green Underneath, and the Green Underneath is inside you, and the distinction between occupant and occupied has been meaningless since the moment you first ate the pale growth and its sugars entered your blood and its cells became your cells and its endless, aimless, furious life became part of your own.

You will leave marks on the wall. You will count. You will try to prove you were here. And the marks will grow over, and the count will be lost, and the plant will continue to grow, and the Stills will continue to move through the chambers, and the mites will continue to clean their fur, and the worms will continue to eat the dark growth, and the molds will continue to slow the pulse, and the condensation will continue to fall, plip, plip, plip, and the light will continue to shine, cold and constant and seafoam-pale, and none of it will remember you, and all of it will remember you, because you are part of it now, because you have always been part of it, because the Green Underneath does not distinguish between itself and the things that live inside it, because it does not distinguish at all, because it does not think, because it grows.

It grows.

And you — you grow too. You grow into something that can survive here. You grow into something that does not need the sun or the stars or the cycle of day and night. You grow into something that reads the pressure of the air and tastes the moisture and knows, without knowing how it knows, that the wall to the left is stable and the wall to the right is new and the passage ahead is closing and the only way out is the way you did not plan to go.

You grow into a Still.

Not in body. In mind. In the way you move through the world. In the way you stop marking and stop counting and stop trying to prove you existed and simply exist instead, quiet and deliberate and present, a dark shape at the edge of the light, padding softly across the endless floor of an endless chamber inside an endless body that does not know your name, because you do not have one, because you do not need one, because you are part of the Green Underneath now, and the Green Underneath is part of you, and this is what it means to live here, and this is what it means to be alive, and this is what it means to be still.
