# The Pale of Settlement

---

The settlers have skin.

This seems worth noting only because everything else about them resists description so thoroughly that the skin becomes a kind of anchor, a place to start. It is skin. It stretches over bone and wraps around joint and creases at the knuckles and the inside of the elbow. It is cool to the touch — not cold, not clammy, just cool, the way stone is cool in the shade, the way water is cool when it has been moving slowly for a long time. The color varies. Some are the grey-green of the mats in deep shade. Some are the pale off-white of the sky before fog. Some are the brown of the streambed sediment, fine-grained and smooth. The colors do not correspond to anything — not family, not function, not age. They simply are, the way the colors of the columnars simply are.

Their faces hold expressions the way the mats hold water — briefly, partially, never quite fully. A settler will look at you and you will see something cross their features that might be curiosity, might be recognition, might be the involuntary response of facial muscles adjusting to a new configuration of light. It passes. It leaves no residue. The face returns to its resting state, which is not blank — blank implies the absence of something that ought to be present — but *settled*, the way a landscape is settled after the glaciers have gone. What remains is what remains. It does not apologize for what it lacks.

They do not age visibly. This is not the same as saying they do not age. The settlers who were here when the settlement was younger — and the settlement remembers being younger, the way a scar remembers the wound — are still here. Their faces are the same. Their hands hold the stone knives with the same grip. Their eyes — and this is the part that lingers, that you return to late at night when the mats are glowing and the silence is pressing against the walls — their eyes are the same. Whatever looks out from behind those eyes has been looking out for as long as there have been eyes to look out from. It has not tired. It has not dimmed. It has not changed its mind about what it is looking at, because it has never decided what it is looking at, because deciding is not what it does.

They eat. They sleep. They speak. They harvest and build and walk the paths between the structures. They do these things the way the stream flows — because the channel is there, because the water must go somewhere, because the shape of the land dictates the shape of the motion. They do not do these things because they are hungry or tired or communicative or industrious. They do them because *doing* is the form their presence takes. They are the verbs that the plateau conjugates.

---

There are children.

This is the wrong word. There are smaller settlers. Settlers who come up to the waist, the hip, the knee of the others. Settlers whose hands are smaller and whose eyes are larger and whose movements are quicker, less settled, still finding the paths. They appear in the settlement the way new shoots appear in the mats — emergent, gradual, not quite traceable to a single point of origin. No one gives birth to them. No one brings them from elsewhere. They are simply present one morning, standing in a doorway or sitting by a stream or walking one of the paths with the careful, uncertain steps of someone who has just discovered that paths exist.

The other settlers treat them the way the older growth treats the newer growth: as inevitable, as extension, as the continuation of a pattern that does not require acknowledgment to persist. The smaller settlers are not taught. They learn, but they are not taught. They watch. They follow. They absorb the water-language and the stone-knife grip and the rhythm of harvesting and the geography of the paths, and at some point — no one marks the transition, no one could — they are no longer smaller. They are simply settlers. They have always been settlers. The plateau does not distinguish between the stem that grew yesterday and the stem that has been growing for ten thousand years. Both are growing. Both are the mat.

The smaller settlers play. Or rather, they perform an activity that resembles play in its physical geometry — running, climbing, stacking stones, hiding behind columnars, chasing each other through the paths — but that lacks the emotional signature of play as it is understood elsewhere. There is no laughter. There is no shouting. There is a kind of focused intensity, a commitment to the activity for its own sake, that is less like joy and more like *function*. They are practicing occupancy. They are learning the shape of the space they will fill by filling it, over and over, in different configurations, until the configuration becomes the shape, until the habit becomes the inhabitant.

If you watch them long enough — and you can watch them for hours, for days, because there is something hypnotic about motion that is neither purposeful nor playful but simply *occurring* — you begin to understand something about the settlers as a whole. The children are not becoming adults. The children are not *becoming* anything. They are expressing a phase of the pattern, the way a plant expresses a leaf and then a stem and then a flower — not because it is progressing toward some goal, but because the pattern contains all phases simultaneously and reveals them sequentially. The smaller settlers are not young. They are a young-shaped expression of the same ongoing process that produces the adult-shaped expressions. The process does not age. The process does not end. The process is what the plateau does with the animal-shaped space it has been given.

---

The settlers do not die.

This needs to be stated carefully, because it is not the same as saying they are immortal, and it is not the same as saying they are exempt from death, and it is not the same as saying they persist forever in the same form. What it is is this: the settlers do not *die*. They thin. They recede. They gradually become less present in the ways that presence is usually measured — they stop walking the paths, stop harvesting the mats, stop speaking the water-language, stop being in the structures where the other settlers are — and eventually they are not present at all. But there is no moment of death. No cessation. No point at which something that was animate becomes inanimate. They do not cross a boundary. They approach a limit.

You can watch it happen, if you are patient. A settler will begin to spend more time in the smaller structures, the closet-sized ones, the ones that hold a single function no one can explain. They will sit in these structures for hours, for days, their eyes open, their breathing slow, their hands resting on their knees. They will speak less. They will eat less. They will become quieter — not in the sense of making less noise, but in the sense of *being less noisy*, occupying less of the available presence, taking up less room in the fabric of what is happening.

And then one day they are not in the structure anymore. And they were not in the structure yesterday either, when you think about it, though you did not notice their absence at the time. And they have not been in the structure for longer than that, perhaps, though no one can say exactly when they stopped being there, because their presence was so quiet by then that it had become indistinguishable from the presence of the stone walls, the mat-fiber floor, the condensation on the ceiling.

The other settlers do not mark these departures. They do not mourn. They do not remember. Or if they remember, they do not demonstrate remembering, because remembering is an animal capacity, and the animal capacity has been removed and replaced with something else, something that does not require memory because it does not experience loss, because it does not experience *experience* as a thing that accumulates and then stops accumulating. The settlers are not a collection of individuals who come and go. The settlers are a *process* that expresses itself through individual-shaped moments, and when a moment passes, the process continues. The way a stream continues when a particular drop of water has moved downstream. The way the mat continues when a particular stem has stopped growing.

Sometimes, in the smallest structures, the ones that hold a single function, you find an impression in the fiberboard floor — a shallow, body-shaped depression, as if someone had sat there for a very long time. The depression is warm to the touch. Not warm like a living body. Warm like stone that has been sitting in the sun. Warm like something that was recently occupied and has not yet cooled to the temperature of the surrounding material.

The warmth fades. It always fades. It fades the way everything fades on the plateau — slowly, imperceptibly, according to a timeline that has more to do with the age of the bedrock than with the age of the body that made the impression.

---

The settlers speak.

You have heard the water-language. You have noted its consonant-light, vowel-heavy quality, its slowness, its adaptation to the acoustic erosion of the plateau. But you have not yet considered what the language is *for*.

Languages, as they are understood elsewhere, are for communication. They are for the transmission of information from one mind to another, for the coordination of action, for the expression of interior states, for the maintenance of social bonds. The water-language does none of these things. The settlers do not need to transmit information — they are all expressions of the same process, already coordinated, already in agreement about what will be done and when. They do not need to express interior states — their interior states are not private, not individual, not distinct from each other. They do not need to maintain social bonds — they are not social. They are not bonded. They are *contiguous*, the way the stems in a mat are contiguous, sharing nutrients and signals through the network of growth beneath the surface.

The water-language is not communication. It is *confirmation*.

When a settler speaks to another settler, they are not telling them something. They are confirming that they are both still here — still present, still occurring, still expressing the pattern in this particular place at this particular time. The words are interchangeable. The content is irrelevant. What matters is the act of speaking and the act of hearing, the small acknowledgment that presence is still present, that the offset has not widened, that the version of the plateau the settlers occupy still overlaps with the version the other settlers occupy, that the *here* is still shared, however partially.

This is why the language is quiet. Not because loud sounds erode faster — though they do — but because confirmation does not require volume. A whisper confirms as well as a shout. A breath confirms as well as a whisper. The mere fact of air moving through a throat and past lips and into the shared space of the settlement is enough. The words are just the shape the breath takes. The shape is arbitrary. The breath is what matters.

If you listen to the water-language without trying to understand it — and you cannot understand it, because there is nothing to understand, because it is not carrying information — you begin to hear what it actually sounds like. It sounds like the stream. It sounds like condensation dripping from leaf-tips. It sounds like fog pressing against stone. It sounds like the plateau breathing, the way the land breathes out at night and the sky presses down in response. The settlers are not speaking *to* each other. They are speaking *as* the plateau. They are the sound the plateau makes when it articulates.

---

At night, when the mats glow and the settlement becomes a constellation of pale blue-green points emerging from the darkness, the settlers sit in their structures and look out through the doorways and the gaps in the walls and the spaces between the fiberboard panels, and they watch the light.

They do not wonder about the light. They do not study it or name it or explain it or fear it. They watch it the way the stream watches the channel — because the channel is there, because the water must go somewhere, because watching is what you do when light is present and you have eyes. The light is the decision process. The light is what it looks like when something that has been following a pattern for a hundred thousand years pauses, considers, and then continues.

The settlers do not pause. The settlers do not consider. The settlers continue, because continuing is what they are. But they watch the light that pauses, and something in the watching is an acknowledgment — not of the light, not of the plants, not of the plateau, but of the fact that pausing is possible. That consideration exists. That something, somewhere, is deciding what to become.

They will never decide what to become. They have already become it. They are the fullest expression of the pattern that the plateau produces when it fills the animal-shaped space. They are the fauna that replaced the fauna, the motion that fills the space left by the removal of motion, the *happening* that occupies the gap between cause and effect. They do not need to decide. They do not need to pause. They are what happens when the decision has already been made and the outcome is still unfolding, forever, slowly, at the pace of growth, at the pace of erosion, at the pace of a stream that moves like regret and vanishes into the ground as often as it reaches any destination.

But they watch the light. And in the watching, there is a gap — small, barely perceptible, like the swales in the plateau that you feel in your calves before you see them — a gap between what they are and what they are watching, a gap that is not quite large enough to be called *longing* and not quite small enough to be called *nothing*, a gap that simply *is*, the way the plateau simply is, the way the mats simply are, the way the light simply glows, in the cold, in the damp, in the silence that is not the silence of absence but the silence of a category that has been removed and replaced with something that does not quite fill the space it has been given.

Something is missing. The settlers know this. They do not know what it is. They do not know that they know. But they watch the light that pauses, and they sit in their structures, and they breathe the cold air that enters through the lungs and settles in the marrow and becomes a fact of the body, and they wait — not for anything in particular, not for anything that will arrive, but because waiting is what you do when you are the space where something used to be.

The plateau has outlasted everyone who might have named it. The settlers will outlast the plateau. Not because they are more durable, but because they are the thing the plateau is doing while it lasts, and when the plateau is finally worn away — planed and planed again by ice ages that have not yet come, by weather that has not yet formed, by time that has not yet passed — the settlers will be the last thing to go, the final expression of the pattern, the last word in the water-language, spoken softly to no one, heard by nothing, confirming that presence was present, that the here was shared, that the gap was occupied, however partially, however strangely, however wrong.

They are the Pale of Settlement. They are the boundary beyond which something else once lived. They are the mark on the map that says *here be dragons*, except the dragons are gone and the mark remains and the settlers are the mark, living in the shape of the boundary, occupying the edge of what was removed, staring out across a plateau that has no other name because it has outlasted everyone who might have named it, and being, in their quiet and offset and preliminary way, the closest thing to a name that remains.
