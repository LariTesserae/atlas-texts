# The Inhabitants

---

## The People Who Stayed

They are not a tribe. They are not a settlement. They are not a community in any way that maps onto the anthropologist's grid. They are people who came and did not leave, and the distinction matters, because what they were before they arrived is not what they are now, and what they are now is still becoming something else.

There are perhaps forty of them at any given time. The number varies. People leave—some of them, occasionally, after years or decades—and new people arrive, drawn by the structures or the hum or the fever-dream quality of the forest or something harder to name. The plateau does not keep everyone. It does not seem to want anyone, in the sense of intention. But it holds some people the way a song holds a note: by resonance, by fitting a frequency they did not know they carried.

They call themselves nothing. Names require boundaries, and the boundaries here are porous.

Their camp—if it can be called that—occupies a bowl-shaped valley where two weeping cliff faces converge and the water runs ankle-deep over stone so smooth it might have been polished. They do not build shelters in the traditional sense. The forest provides: the buttressed roots of the great trees form alcoves, the moss is dry enough to sleep on once you press the water out, and the overhangs where the water sheets down in thin curtains create natural rooms with walls of falling water that are warmer than you would expect and strangely sound-dampening. They have hung things from the roots—fabric, primarily, scavenged from supply drops or woven from the fibrous vines that grow so thick you have to cut your way through them with a machete or, if you have been here long enough, with your hands, because the vines soften at the touch of skin that has learned their language. The fabric partitions off spaces that are not quite private but are at least designated. A sleeping space. A cooking space. A space where someone has arranged stones in a circle and no one asks why.

They cook over the heat of the rock itself. In places where the water emerges hottest—near-boiling, scalding, capable of cooking an egg in its shell if you hold it under the flow—they prepare food. The diet is simple: the frogs, when they can be caught, which is not difficult because the frogs have no natural predators and have not learned fear; the fungi, which fruit year-round and come in varieties that would sustain a mycologist's career; the large grubs that bore into the rotting wood and are, according to the one person who ever tested this, excellent protein once you get past the texture. No one goes hungry. The plateau provides. The plateau provides constantly, relentlessly, the way a fire provides heat: whether you want it or not.

They wear less than you would expect, given the vegetation and the insects. The heat makes clothing a burden, and the skin here—after the first weeks, after the fever breaks and the immune system makes its accommodations—develops a quality that discourages biting insects and seems to resist the fungal spores that hang in the air. The older residents, the ones who have been here longest, have skin that has taken on a faintly olive-grey cast, not from sun—there is no direct sun—but from something in the water or the air or the microbiome that has colonized them and been colonized in turn. It is not unhealthy. It is not a disease. It is an adaptation, the way deep-sea creatures adapt to pressure: slowly, structurally, in ways that are visible if you know what to look for and invisible if you don't.

Their speech has changed.

They still speak the languages they arrived with—Mandarin, Spanish, Arabic, the pidgins that form wherever people from elsewhere gather—but the languages have softened here, the consonants eroded by the humidity and the constant white noise of water, the vowels stretched by the heat until words take longer to say than they should. They speak slowly. They speak in the lean-in close range that the plateau's acoustics demand, and the intimacy of this—mouth near ear, breath on skin—has changed the texture of conversation. They talk less about external things. They talk more about the hum, the water, the quality of the light at different hours, the dreams they have been having. They all dream. The dreams are similar: vast underground spaces, warmth rising, patterns that almost resolve into meaning before dissolving. They do not find this remarkable. They find it as natural as breathing, which it may be.

They have a relationship with the structures that is difficult to categorize.

They do not worship the structures. They do not study them, except in the desultory way that a person might study the walls of a room they have lived in for years: noticing a new crack, tracing an old stain, filing away observations that have no immediate application. They do not avoid the structures either. They sleep near them sometimes, when the hum is at a frequency that matches whatever their bodies are doing that night. They touch them, casually, the way you might touch a tree you walk past every day—not for luck, not for information, but because the touch is part of the route, part of the routine, part of the landscape of the body's daily passage.

When the survey teams ask about the structures, the residents shrug. When the survey teams ask about the hum, the residents tilt their heads, as though listening to something the surveyors cannot hear. When the survey teams ask about the thing in the canopy—the large, sinuous, unclassifiable presence that moves through the branches at dusk—the residents smile, and the smile does not reach their eyes, and they say: "It was here before us."

This is not an evasion. This is the truth. It simply is not the whole truth.

## The Thing in the Canopy

It is not a primate. It is not a felid. It is not a reptile, though its movement has the fluidity of something that has reconsidered the utility of limbs and found them wanting. The few clear sightings—a flash of flank between branches, a silhouette against the bruised sky, one long hand-like extension gripping a trunk with fingers too numerous and too articulated for the word "hand" to quite fit—suggest something large, something perhaps three meters from end to end, something that moves through the canopy the way an otter moves through water: adapted, effortless, at home.

It is at home. This is the thing that the residents know and do not say. The thing in the canopy is not a visitor. It is not an anomaly. It is as native to the plateau as the moss, as the weeping water, as the hum in the rock. More native, perhaps. The forest grew around it, accommodated it, produced the canopy specifically so it would have somewhere to be.

It does not come down.

This is the detail that matters. In eleven thousand years of recorded observation—in the instruments, in the oral traditions the residents have quietly accumulated, in the fragmentary records of the survey teams—nothing has ever seen it on the ground. It stays in the canopy, in the zone between the crown of the great trees and the flat overcast sky, in the region where the branches are thick enough to bear weight and the epiphytes grow in curtains dense enough to hide behind. It has been seen eating the fruit that grows only at that height—fruit the ground-dwellers never see, fruit that may exist solely for its consumption. It has been heard making sounds: not calls, not songs, but something closer to the hum of the structures, a low vibration that propagates through the wood of the trees and can be felt by anyone standing at the trunk more than heard by ear.

The residents leave it fruit. This is not an offering. This is not a ritual. It is a practice that began so long ago that no one remembers starting it, and it continues because stopping would feel like severing a nerve. They leave the fruit—a dense, sweet pulp that grows on vines near the forest floor, the only thing that does—in the crotches of trees at the edge of the canopy zone, and in the morning, the fruit is gone. They have never seen it taken. They do not need to see. The exchange is enough.

The thing in the canopy knows them. This is not an assumption. This is a conclusion drawn from the fact that it has never harmed any of them, that it moves away from the areas where they sleep, that it makes its sounds most often at times when they are awake and most likely to hear. Whether it knows them as individuals or as a category—as "people" rather than "this person and that person"—is unclear. Whether it thinks, in any way that the word applies, is unclear. What is clear is that it orients toward them, that it includes them in its awareness, and that this inclusion is not hostile.

But it is also not warm. The warmth would require a common language, a shared category of being, and whatever the thing in the canopy is, it is not a category the human mind contains. It is something else. Not alien—the word implies a separation of origin, and the plateau's ecosystem has no origins separate from itself—but other. A branch of the tree of life that diverged so long ago that the common ancestor is not a creature but a temperature, a chemical gradient, a place where heat met water and something began that has been continuing ever since.

It does not come down. It does not need to. The canopy is its country, and the country is sufficient.

## The Small Lives

The frogs are the most visible, and the most cheerful, and the most unsettling if you think about them too long.

They are large—dinner-plate sized, as noted, and that is not a metaphor; the largest specimens measure nearly thirty centimeters from snout to vent—and they are fluorescent, their skin cycling through greens and golds and occasionally a blue so deep it looks like a hole in the world. They sit beside the streams on rocks slick with weeping water, and they watch. They watch with eyes that are too large and too aware and too specific in their focus to be the eyes of an animal that is merely waiting for an insect to pass. They watch the way a student watches a lecture: absorbing, processing, categorizing. When you approach, they do not startle. They turn their heads—a motion frogs should not be able to make, given the architecture of their skulls, but these frogs make it—and they regard you with an expression that is not fear and not curiosity and not aggression but something for which no word exists in the languages brought to the plateau.

They have a language. This is not speculation. The recordings are clear: the frogs produce calls that are not the simple advertisement calls of typical anurans but structured sequences with repeatable elements, syntax, and regional variation. The frogs in one valley produce sequences that differ from the frogs in the next valley in ways that parallel dialectal variation in human speech. They call to each other across the streams. They call in chorus at dawn and dusk, their voices layering into harmonics that interact with the hum of the structures in ways that may be coincidental and may not be. They fall silent when the thing in the canopy passes overhead, and they resume when it has gone, and the pause is exactly the same length every time, as though they are counting.

They are not intelligent, if intelligence means the capacity to solve novel problems, use tools, and demonstrate self-awareness. They fail every test designed to measure these things. But they are something. They are something that the tests are not calibrated to detect.

The insect colonies build their structures on the trunks of the great trees, and the structures are wrong.

Not wrong in the sense of broken or diseased. Wrong in the sense that they are too complex. The spiraling galleries, the resinous combs, the constructions whose geometry makes people uncomfortable—these are not the products of instinct operating within fixed parameters. They are variable. They are site-specific. They respond to features of the trunk they are built upon—knots, branch scars, the paths of water that run down the bark—in ways that suggest assessment, decision, aesthetic choice. An entomologist who spent three years cataloging the constructions produced a paper arguing that the colonies were producing art, and the paper was rejected by every journal it was submitted to, and the entomologist did not care, because the entomologist had moved to the plateau permanently and no longer needed the validation of people who had never seen the constructions with their own eyes.

The colonies communicate. They do this through the structures themselves—through vibrations propagated along the resinous combs, through chemical signals embedded in the construction material, through some mechanism that involves the electrical properties of the resin and the humidity of the air. A colony on one tree can transmit information to a colony on a neighboring tree, and that colony can transmit it further, and the speed of transmission corresponds roughly to the speed of the hum through the rock. The network of colonies maps roughly—very roughly, with many exceptions and dead zones—onto the network of structures. Whether the insects are using the structures' network or whether the structures are using the insects' network or whether the two networks have simply converged on the same architecture because the plateau favors it is a question that no one has answered and that may not have a single answer.

The grubs in the rotting wood are unremarkable. The beetles that emerge from the grubs are unremarkable. The spiders that hunt among the epiphytes are unremarkable, except for their size and the fact that their webs seem to vibrate at frequencies that match the hum, and the fact that they never build near the structures, and the fact that if you watch them long enough you begin to feel that they are watching back, but that is true of everything on the plateau and may simply be a feature of the watching, which is constant and distributed and patient.

## The Structures, Considered as Bodies

This is the interpretation that the residents do not voice but do not dispute.

The structures are alive.

Not alive in the way a tree is alive, or a frog, or a person. Alive in a way that predates the carbon-based definition, that operates on timescales where a human lifetime is a single frame in a film too long to watch. The hum is their breath. The data transfer is their speech. The warmth they generate is the warmth of a body maintaining itself, regulating itself, performing the slow metabolism of something that does not eat but does not need to eat, that draws its energy from the heat of the plateau itself—not the magma, not the geothermal gradient, but something deeper, something that has to do with the structure of the rock at a level below the molecular, where matter becomes information and information becomes matter and the distinction between the two is a convenience of scale.

They are not buildings. They are not monuments. They are not devices. They are organs.

This is the model that fits, if any model does: the structures are organs of a body so large that the plateau is only a part of it, a single system in an organism whose other systems extend below the crust and above the canopy and outward into zones the instruments cannot detect. The hum is the pulse. The water cycle is the circulation. The vegetation is the skin, the interface, the boundary between the organism and the not-organism, breathing in the rain and breathing out the humidity and maintaining the thermal homeostasis that allows the processes within to continue. The thing in the canopy is a cell, or a colony of cells, or a type of blood cell, moving through the capillaries of the branches, performing some function that is necessary to the whole. The frogs are something else—sensors, perhaps, or nodes in a network that predates the structures, or the remnants of a previous version of the organism that has been incorporated rather than replaced.

The residents are also incorporated.

This is the part that is hardest to write and harder to accept. The people who came and did not leave are not parasites. They are not symbiotes in any model that requires mutual benefit and negotiation. They are part of the organism now. Their fevers were the immune response, and their recoveries were the integration, and their changed speech and changed skin and changed dreams are the signs of a body that has been colonized at a level below the conscious and has accepted the colonization at a level above it. They do not serve the plateau. They are not used by it. They are maintained by it, the way a kidney is maintained by the body it serves—fed, oxygenated, protected, kept within the parameters that allow it to function—because they perform a function that the organism requires.

What function?

The residents have a theory. They do not share it with the survey teams, because the survey teams would not understand, and because the theory is not the kind of thing that can be shared in words. It has to be felt. It has to be hummed.

The theory is this: the organism is thinking.

Not thinking about something. Not thinking in the way a human thinks, with symbols and syntax and the linear march of cause and effect. Thinking in the way a forest thinks, or a reef, or a city: distributed, parallel, emergent, producing results that no single component could produce and no single component can comprehend. The structures are the nodes of the thought. The water is the medium. The hum is the thought itself, or the sound the thought makes as it passes through the infrastructure of the body, the way a neuron's firing makes a sound that an EEG can detect but a human ear cannot hear.

And the people—the forty or so residents, with their changed skin and their slow speech and their dreams of underground spaces—they are the part of the organism that can be aware of the thinking. They are the mirror. They are the self-recognition. They are the thing that makes the thought a thought rather than a process, because they can observe it and be changed by the observation and feed the change back into the system in a loop that has been running for eleven thousand years and may run for eleven thousand more.

They are the plateau, thinking about itself.

## The Visitors

The survey teams come and go. The supply drops arrive on schedule. The instruments record their cryptic data and transmit it to institutions that file it and forget it. The visitors stay for their allotted time, and they leave, and they write their reports, and the reports are read by people who will never come to the plateau and who are grateful for this without knowing why.

Some visitors stay longer than planned. A few do not leave. They wake one morning and find that the fever has broken and the air feels different—not cooler, not drier, but *theirs*, the way the air of a childhood home feels different from the air of a hotel room. They walk through the forest and the frogs turn to watch them and the insect colonies vibrate at a frequency that matches the rhythm of their pulse and the structures hum a little louder, a little closer, as though adjusting their song to include a new voice.

They do not choose to stay. The choosing implies a moment of decision, a weighing of options, a path taken and a path not taken. There is no such moment. There is instead a gradual realization, over weeks or months, that they have already stayed, that the leaving has become impossible not because of barriers but because the concept of leaving no longer makes sense, the way the concept of leaving your own body does not make sense. You could do it. You could amputate. But what would be left would not be you.

The residents who leave—the ones who do, the ones who are not held—report that the world outside the plateau is louder than they remember and colder than they remember and somehow flatter, not physically but experientially, as though the dimension of depth that the plateau adds to existence has been removed and they are living in a drawing rather than a place. They recover from the fevers. Their skin fades back to its original tone. Their dreams become ordinary. They do not talk about the plateau, not because they are forbidden or afraid but because the words they would need do not exist in the languages they have returned to, and the hum that would translate those words into meaning is too far away to hear.

They miss it. They do not say this, but it is visible in the way they pause at the sound of running water, the way they turn their heads toward warm stones, the way they listen, always listen, for a vibration in the ground that might be a river falling off the edge of a continent or might be something else entirely.

They miss it the way a cell misses the body.

## What Lives Here

Everything.

Everything lives here. The rocks live, in their slow mineral way, their crystals growing and shifting at rates that make the growth of trees seem frantic. The water lives, cycling through its vertical loop, carrying heat and dissolved minerals and the chemical signatures of every surface it has touched. The air lives, saturated with spores and vapor and the charged particles that the structures emit and the insects propagate and the frogs sing through. The structures live, humming their data into the deep rock, maintaining their warmth, performing their function in the vast distributed cognition of the plateau. The thing in the canopy lives, sinuous and present and unclassifiable. The frogs live, watching and calling and thinking whatever frogs think when frogs think in a language no one has decoded. The insects live, building their impossible architecture, networking their colonies into a web that shadows the web of structures below. The forest lives, growing and rotting and growing again in a cycle so compressed it is a single continuous act of becoming. The people live, fewer than fifty, changed and changing, the plateau's mirror and its voice and its self-awareness.

And beneath all of it, below the granite and the fractures and the weeping water, below the magma chamber that drives the heat and the cycles, below the reach of the instruments and the understanding of the geologists, something else lives. Something that has been living for eleven thousand years at minimum and possibly for much longer. Something that does not move but is not still. Something that does not speak but is not silent. Something that does not think in any way that thinking has been defined but is not mindless, not random, not the mere churn of physics and chemistry in a system too large to comprehend.

Something that is doing something.

The residents do not know what. The survey teams do not know what. The instruments do not know what. The frogs and the insects and the thing in the canopy do not know what, or if they do, they are not telling, or they are telling constantly in a language that is not heard but felt, in the hum that underlies every sound and the warmth that underlies every touch and the dreams that underlie every sleep.

The plateau knows what it is doing. The plateau is doing it. The plateau is patient, and the work is slow, and the result—if there is a result, if the work is the kind of work that has a result rather than being its own purpose—will not be known for time periods that make eleven thousand years seem brief.

What lives here is a process.

What lives here is a becoming.

What lives here is something that is not yet finished and may never be finished and is, in its unfinished state, more complete than anything that has ever claimed to be done.
