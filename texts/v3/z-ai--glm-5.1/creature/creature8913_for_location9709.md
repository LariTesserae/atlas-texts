What lives here has learned to be wet.

Not the kind of wet that a creature minds. Not the emergency of a caught-in-the-rain, the temporary disaster of a fur slicked wrong. What lives here has passed through minding. The rats do not shake themselves dry because there is no dry to shake toward. The heron has become a collector of condensation — its feathers dark with it, weighted with it, and still it stands, carrying the water the way the concrete carries it, because there is no option that is not carrying it.

But you asked who lives here. Not what. And the distinction matters, though not in the way you might expect.

---

There is a person. Or there was. Or there is the shape that a person left behind, the way a hand leaves a print in concrete that has not yet cured.

The boots by the generator house are real enough. Size unknown — they have shrunk or the story of them has shrunk, and now they sit beneath the exhaust vent where the heat dries nothing but has made the rubber soft and strange, the color of a bruise that is trying to decide whether to heal. They are not empty in the way that abandoned things are empty. They are empty in the way that a shell is empty — with the understanding that something was here, was shaped by this container, and has withdrawn without discarding it.

Someone maintained this place. Someone does maintain it. The diesel tank is not infinite and it has not run dry. The generator was serviced recently enough that its oil is still the amber of fresh use, not the black of long neglect. The conduit's welds were checked — there are chalk marks at the joints, white lines that have not yet washed away in the condensation, though everything washes away here eventually, given enough time, and there is so much time, and it passes so strangely.

But you will not see this person. Not because they are hiding — hiding implies an effort to not be seen, a decision made fresh each moment — but because they have become part of the place's metabolism. They come when the yard does not observe them. They go when it does. They exist in the gaps between the generator's pulses, in the moment before a droplet releases, in the heron's blind spot which is also the heron's entire visual field because the heron does not turn its head and so cannot see what moves behind it.

If you stood here long enough — and you could, because nothing would stop you, because the yard does not repel or welcome, it simply persists — you might catch the evidence of them. A tool replaced in a different position. A bootprint in the mud that the rats have not yet crossed. A smell beneath the diesel and the algae and the slow rot of standing water: soap, perhaps, or the mineral tang of a body that has learned to sweat efficiently.

But you would not see them. They have become the yard's maintenance the way the yard has become the water's maintenance — a system that holds, that returns, that does not require acknowledgment.

---

The rats know them. The rats know everything that moves through here because movement is the only currency that matters in a place where nothing changes. The rats have a path that skirts the generator house's eastern wall, and when the person moves through, the rats adjust — not fleeing, not freezing, but shifting like water around a stone, and then resuming. They have an arrangement. The person leaves nothing edible in the open. The rats leave the person's things alone. It is not a treaty. It is simply what has always been true, which is the only kind of agreement that holds here.

The heron does not know them. The heron does not know anything that does not enter the water within striking distance. The heron is a device for waiting that occasionally produces a kill, and it does not differentiate between the person and the conduit and the sky, because none of these things are fish.

The geckos know the person's hands. They know the specific warmth of fingers that have been working near the engine. They do not flee from this warmth. They turn toward it, briefly, the way a plant turns — not with intelligence but with the simple following of a resource. And the person lets them. The person stands still when the gecko works its way across their knuckles, and this is the closest thing to companionship that the Condensation Yards offer, and it is enough. It is more than enough. It is exactly what is here.

---

What the person thinks about is unknown, and perhaps unknowable, and perhaps the question itself is wrong. Does the pumping station think about water? Does the conduit think about the weight it carries? The person has been here long enough that thought may have become something other than what it is in drier places — something more like the process the yard performs, a circulation of attention through fixed pathways, a processing of the same inputs over and over until the processing itself is the point.

They check the generator. They check the tank. They check the seals on the conduit. They walk the perimeter in a pattern that the gravel remembers as a faint discoloration, a path worn not by repetition but by the oils from their boots mixing with the stone. They stand in the doorway of the pumping station and look out at the yard and the yard looks back with its flat grey-yellow gaze and nothing is exchanged because nothing needs to be exchanged.

They sleep somewhere. Not here — the yard does not offer a dry place, a quiet place, a place where the generator's knock would not enter the chest and rearrange the rhythms of rest. But nearby. Close enough that when the generator's sound changes — and it does change, it must change, because it is a machine and machines are not the perfect perpetual motion that the yard pretends to be — they would hear it. Would feel it. Would know that the yard has shifted, however slightly, from the configuration it holds.

And they would come. Because that is what lives here: not a person in the way you might imagine a person, with a history and a name and a reason, but a function that has grown a body around itself. The yard needs maintenance. The maintenance needs a maintainer. The maintainer has become the kind of thing that can persist in a place where the water never stops and the light never changes and the heat holds everything in its mouth without biting down.

---

There is no loneliness here. That is important to understand, or at least important to state, because the assumption of loneliness is so strong when you look at a place like this — a single heron, a single person, a single engine, all of them performing their single functions in a space so small you could know it completely in an hour. But loneliness requires the concept of company, and company requires the concept of leaving, and leaving requires the concept of somewhere else to go, and somewhere else requires the concept of change, and the Condensation Yards do not change.

The person is not lonely. The person is complete in the way that a gear is complete — not because they lack nothing, but because the question of lacking requires a machine they are not part of, and the machine they are part of is running. The diesel knocks. The water condenses. The rebar rusts in its slow patient way. The person checks the generator, checks the tank, walks the perimeter, lets the gecko cross their hand, and stands in the doorway, and is held.

---

If you came here — and you could, because there is nothing stopping you, no fence, no gate, no warning sign, only the gradual understanding that you are entering a place that does not care about you — you would see the yard as it is. Wet. Loud. Young and already finished. The heron. The rats. The water on every surface, trembling with the engine's pulse.

And if you stayed long enough, you might see the person. Or you might not. They might be behind the generator house, checking something that does not need checking, or inside the pumping station, standing in the doorway's reverse, looking at the wall where the water traces its slow maps down.

But you would feel them. The way you feel the heat — not as a thing that arrives but as a condition that was already here. The yard is maintained. The yard is lived in. The yard holds its breath, and something holds it with it, and the holding is the closest thing to purpose that this place has, and the purpose is enough, and the water keeps coming, and the diesel knock counts out the time that does not pass, and the gecko finds the warm hand, and the heron stands, and the person is here.
