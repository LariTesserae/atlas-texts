# The People of the Peneplain

They are not numerous. They could not be numerous. The plain does not support density—not of plants, not of animals, and not of people. What it supports is persistence.

---

There are perhaps three hundred of them, though they have never counted. Counting requires a kind of separation that does not interest them—they know who is present the way the pans know where their water is, without measuring, without naming. They live in small groups of eight to twelve, spaced across the peneplain at distances that echo the spacing of the stunted trees: far enough apart that each group has its own pans, its own seeps, its own stretch of laterite crust, close enough that a person walking for most of a day could reach the nearest neighbors.

They do not walk quickly. There is no reason to walk quickly. The horizon does not get closer no matter how fast you move, and the pans do not dry up if you arrive late, and the frogs will still be roaring when you get there, the same as they were roaring when you left.

---

Their skin is dark—not the darkness of a single shade but the darkness of many shades layered and deepened over time, as if the sun has been applying coats of the same pigment for generations and has finally achieved a thoroughness that admits no further work. The children are born with skin that would be called dark anywhere else, and it darkens still further as they grow, and by adulthood it has reached a quality that seems less like color and more like the absorption of color, the way the laterite absorbs heat. They do not burn. They do not peel. They stand in the sun the way the crust stands: warmed through, unbothered, part of the temperature rather than subject to it.

Their hair grows in tight coils, close to the scalp, and they keep it short—not for vanity but for practicality. Anything that extends from the body is a handle for heat, and the heat here does not need handles. Some of the older adults go bald, and this is not remarked upon; it is simply what happens, the way the low rises erode, slowly, without ceremony.

They are not tall. This is not a place that rewards height. The trees are short, the sedge is short, the water is shallow, and the people are short—compact, wide-footed, with thick wrists and broad hands that can grip the laterite crust without cutting their palms. Their bodies have settled toward the ground the way everything settles toward the ground here, as if the plain's patient flattening applies to the living as well as the land.

---

They wear very little. The warmth does not require clothing, and the insects do not respect it, and the water ruins it quickly. What they wear is functional in the narrowest sense: a strip of woven sedge-fiber around the hips, sometimes a loose wrapping of the same material over the shoulders during the hottest part of the day when the sun is direct enough to make even their dark skin feel the assault. The fiber comes from the sedge in the pans. They harvest it at dawn, when the stems are cool and flexible, and they weave it by hand during the hours when the heat makes movement unwise. The weave is loose. It does not need to be tight. It is not protecting against cold.

Their feet are bare and have always been bare. The soles are thick—not callused in the way of a person who sometimes walks without shoes, but transformed, a continuous pad of toughened skin that can walk on hot laterite without flinching. Children are born with soft feet, and by their third year they can cross the crust at midday without crying, and by their fifth year they do not notice they are doing anything remarkable. The nerves are still there. The heat is still felt. It simply does not register as damage, because it isn't.

---

They speak a language that has no name, because it has no competitors. It is the only language spoken on the peneplain, and the people who speak it have never encountered a reason to distinguish it from the act of speaking itself. To them, talking is talking. That it happens in a particular set of sounds is incidental.

The language is quiet. This seems contradictory given the volume of the plain—the frog chorus, the insect drone, the slapping of the shallow water—but it is precisely because of the volume that the language is quiet. A language that competed with the frogs would be a language of shouting, and shouting is exhausting, and there is no reason to exhaust yourself when the person you are speaking to is sitting beside you, close enough to touch, as they always are. The language has developed in the spaces between the noise: low tones, breathy consonants, vowels that are almost hums. It sounds, from a distance, like a second layer of insect sound, a structured drone that rises and falls with the cadence of conversation but does not try to overwhelm the frogs.

They do not speak during the peak of the frog chorus at dusk and dawn. There is no point. Instead, they use a sign language that is not a separate language but a mode of the same language—same grammar, same vocabulary, expressed with the hands and face and the subtle inclination of the body. Every person is bilingual in this way from childhood, switching between spoken and signed the way a person in a colder climate might switch between indoor and outdoor voices.

The language has no word for *horizon*. It has a word for *the distance at which things become difficult to see*, but this is not the same. The horizon implies a line, a boundary between earth and sky, and the peneplain does not offer such a clean distinction. The earth is orange and the sky is blue, but where they meet there is only a gradual uncertainty, a band of color that is neither one nor the other, and this band has a name: *vèh*, a word that also means *doubt* and *twilight* and *the feeling of waking from a dream you cannot remember*.

The language has seventeen words for water. This is not unusual for people who live with water; what is unusual is that none of the words specify temperature or depth, because the water is always warm and always shallow. Instead, the words specify *relationship*: the water in a pan, the water seeping from a rise, the water that was rain an hour ago, the water that has been in the ground since before the last dry season, the water that is moving, the water that is still, the water that is present but invisible beneath the crust. Each is a different word. Each describes a different relationship between the water and the land and the person observing it.

---

They live in structures that are barely structures. A framework of branches from the stunted trees—cut with effort, since the wood is dense and hard and resists the stone tools they use—lashed together with sedge cord, draped with more sedge woven loosely enough to allow the breeze through but tightly enough to provide shade. The shade is the only purpose. The structures do not keep out rain; the rain is warm and does not need to be kept out. They do not keep out animals; the animals that might enter are small and easily discouraged. They do not keep out insects; nothing keeps out insects. They provide a patch of ground that is not in direct sun, and this is enough.

Each structure shelters a family unit—two to four adults, their children, and occasionally an elder who has outlived their generation's companions and has been absorbed into a younger group. The structures are arranged around the edge of a pan, close to the water but not in it, situated where the laterite crust gives way to the paler soil that is easier to dig into for the corner posts. From above, each settlement looks like a loose ring of low shadows around a bright, shallow mirror.

They do not stay in one pan permanently. The pans expand and contract with the rain, and a pan that is generous in the wet season may become a smear of damp crust in the dry, and the frogs will move, and the birds will follow the frogs, and the people will follow the birds—not hunting the birds, necessarily, but reading the birds as an indicator of where the water and the food have gone. They move on average twice a year, though "average" is misleading; some groups stay in one pan for three years if the water holds, and some groups move four times in a season if the rain is irregular. The movement is not migration, because migration implies a route and a destination. It is simply following the water, the way the water follows the contours of the land.

---

They eat frogs. This is not surprising; the frogs are the abundant protein of the plain, and they are easy to catch, and they are always present. The people catch them by hand, reaching into the shallow water at the pan's edge and feeling for the slick bodies in the warm murk. They are very good at this. A skilled catcher can take a dozen frogs in an hour without disturbing the surface enough to frighten the rest. The frogs are killed quickly—a thumb pressed to the spine behind the skull—and gutted with a sharpened piece of laterite, and roasted over a small fire of dried sedge and deadwood gathered from the stunted trees.

The fire is the only technology they have that is not immediately derived from the landscape, and even so, it is derived: the spark comes from striking two pieces of iron-rich laterite together over a bundle of dried sedge fluff. The laterite does not want to spark—it is not flint—but the people have learned which pieces will spark, and where to strike them, and how to nurse the tiny ember into a flame before the damp air can kill it. Fires are not kept burning continuously. The effort is too great, and the wood too scarce, and the warmth too unnecessary. A fire is made when food needs cooking, and then it is allowed to die.

They also eat the pale succulent at the pan edges—the swollen leaves, not the flower, which they leave alone. The leaves are bitter and astringent and slightly mucilaginous, and they are not nutritious in any meaningful sense, but they contain something that suppresses the appetite and makes the frog meat feel more satisfying than it is. The people eat a few leaves before each meal, and this is considered proper the way washing hands before a meal is considered proper in other places: a ritual of preparation, a signaling that the activity of eating is about to begin.

The children eat insects. This is not a formal part of the diet; it is a part of childhood, the way children in other places eat dirt or chew on grass stems. The water beetles are favored—crunchy, slightly metallic in taste, easy to catch when they sun themselves on the crust. The adults tolerate this without encouraging it. An adult who ate insects would be considered strange, though not with disgust—more with the mild concern one might have for an adult who still climbs trees.

They do not eat the crocodilians. This is not taboo; there is simply no reason to. The crocodilians are small and bony and difficult to catch and not worth the effort when frogs are abundant. They do not eat the heron-like birds, because the birds are useful—they indicate where the frogs are thickest, and they keep the frog population from overwhelming the pans entirely. They do not eat the larger mammal that leaves tracks in the wet margins, because they have never caught one, and they are not sure they could, and the pursuit would cost more energy than the meat would provide.

---

They do not have a concept of ownership as a permanent condition. A pan belongs to whoever is living beside it, but it belongs to them the way a shadow belongs to the object that casts it: temporarily, contingently, ending when the conditions change. When a group moves to a new pan, they leave the structure behind—it is not worth disassembling, and the sedge will rot in a season anyway—and the next group to arrive will build a new one, or will use the old frame if it still stands. There is no sense of trespass. The plain does not belong to them; they belong to the plain, and the plain moves them around as it sees fit.

This does not mean they are without territory. Each group knows the area it traverses—the pans, the seeps, the low rises, the particular stunted trees whose branches are straight enough for construction, the particular patches of crust that will spark when struck. They know these things the way a person knows their own body: not by drawing a map but by inhabiting the space so thoroughly that the knowledge becomes proprioceptive. A person from one group, placed blindfolded at a random point in their territory, could identify their location by the smell of the water and the quality of the crust underfoot and the particular frequency of the frog chorus from the nearest pan.

They do not fight. This is not because they are pacifists in any philosophical sense; it is because there is nothing to fight over. The frogs are inexhaustible. The water is everywhere. The land is identical in all directions. If a group arrives at a pan that is already occupied, they simply move to the next pan, which is a few hours' walk away and is exactly the same. Conflict requires scarcity, or the perception of scarcity, and the peneplain does not provide scarcity. It does not provide abundance, either—the land is too poor for that—but it provides sufficiency, steady and unchanging, and sufficiency does not ignite the kind of desperation that leads to violence.

---

They have children the way the plain has rain: irregularly, without season, in numbers that roughly correspond to the space available. A group of ten adults will have two or three children at any given time. Some of these children will survive to adulthood; some will not. The infant mortality is not catastrophic, but it is not negligible, and the people accept it the way they accept the heat and the frogs and the flatness: as a condition of the place, not as a problem to be solved.

Birth happens in the pans. The warm water eases the labor, and the blood feeds the algae, and the child enters the world already wet, already warm, already part of the shallow cycle of water and life that defines the plain. The mother stays in the pan for hours after the birth, holding the child above the water, letting the frogs roar around them, introducing the child to the sound that will be the constant background of their life. This is not a ritual in the formal sense; it is simply what is done, the way it is simply done that a child, once born, is fed and held and kept in the shade until they are old enough to move on their own.

The children learn to swim before they learn to walk—not because swimming is useful in water that is shin-deep, but because the water is where they are born and where they spend their earliest days, and walking comes later, on the hot crust, a skill that must be acquired carefully, with burned feet and tears, until the soles thicken and the heat becomes a sensation rather than a pain.

---

They grow old here, and they die here, and the plain receives them the way it receives everything: without resistance, without judgment, without hurry.

When a person becomes too old to walk the distances between pans, the group stays with them. This is not sacrifice; it is simply what happens. The frogs are as plentiful at this pan as at any other. The water is as warm. The move can wait. The group stays, and the old person sits in the shade of the sedge shelter, and the children bring them frog meat and succulent leaves, and the adults sit with them in the quiet hours of the afternoon when the frog chorus is at its lowest ebb, and they speak—quietly, in the breathy tones that the language uses—about the things that have happened and the things that will happen and the things that are always happening.

When the old person dies, they are placed in the pan. Not buried—the ground is too hard, too shallow, too wet for burial—and not burned—the wood is too precious, and the fire too difficult. They are placed in the pan, in the warm water, weighted down with stones from the low rises. The crustaceans and the microorganisms and the slow chemistry of the water do the rest. This is not a funeral. There is no ceremony. The person was part of the plain, and they are returned to the plain, and the plain continues, and the frogs continue, and the water continues, and the people who are still alive continue, because continuation is what living things do here.

---

They have stories. This is important. A people who live in a place as unchanging as the peneplain could be forgiven for having no stories—nothing happens, nothing changes, nothing creates the narrative tension that stories require. But they have stories, because stories do not require events; they require attention, and the people of the peneplain have attention the way the plain has water: everywhere, shallow, warm, persistent.

Their stories are about small things. A story about a frog who sang a note that no other frog had ever sung, and the pan where she sang it, and what happened to the water on the day of her singing. A story about a crack in the laterite crust that opened over the course of a single hot afternoon, and the root that found it, and the plant that grew from that root and lived for three generations of people before the crack closed again. A story about the larger mammal—the one that leaves tracks in the wet margins—who is not an animal in the stories but a person, a woman who walks between the pans at dusk and dawn, and who cannot speak but can be understood, and who knows where the water will be before the water knows itself.

Their stories are not about origins. They do not have an origin story, because they do not believe they originated. They have always been here, the way the laterite has always been here, the way the water has always been here. The question *where did we come from* does not occur to them, because the answer is so obvious as to be meaningless: they came from here, from the plain, from the warm water and the hot crust and the thin white soil, and they will remain here until they do not, and the plain will remain after them, and this is not a story but a fact, and facts do not need to be narrated.

Their stories are about *what it is like*—what it is like to stand in a pan at dusk when the frogs begin, what it is like to walk across the crust at midday when the heat makes the ground uncertain, what it is like to see the first stars reflected in the water with a clarity that makes the sky seem closer than the earth. They are stories of sensation, of experience, of the particular quality of existing in a place where existence is the only demand.

They tell these stories to each other in the quiet hours, in the breathy language, in the spaces between the frog chorus and the insect drone. They tell them to the children, who listen with the patient attention of people who have learned that listening is the primary activity of life on the plain. They tell them to themselves, sometimes, while sitting alone at the edge of a pan, watching the water reflect the sky, and in the telling they become both the teller and the listener, and the story becomes a thing that exists in the space between the person and the plain, and the plain does not mind, because the plain has been listening for longer than the people have been speaking, and it will continue listening after they stop.

---

They do not wonder what lies beyond the peneplain. Some of them, in the course of a long life, walk far enough that the pans begin to change—becoming deeper, perhaps, or giving way to ground that is dry, or approaching a horizon that seems to rise into something that might be a hill. These walkers do not continue. They turn back. Not from fear, but from the recognition that the plain has ended and that what lies beyond is not their concern. They return to their group and they do not report what they saw, because what they saw was not relevant to the people they live with, and relevance is the only standard by which information is judged here.

They do not wonder about the future. The future is the same as the present, which is the same as the past. The frogs will roar. The water will seep. The crust will heat and cool and heat again. The children will grow and have children and the old will die and the pans will receive them and the algae will bloom and the dragonflies will patrol and the laterite will ring underfoot in the morning and soften in the midday and the plain will continue, flat and warm and ancient, the irreducible minimum of what it has always been.

This is not resignation. Resignation implies that something else was possible, that a different future could have been chosen, that the present is a disappointment measured against an expectation. The people of the peneplain are not disappointed. They are not content, either—contentment is a feeling that requires the possibility of discontent, and they do not have that possibility. They simply are. They live in the way that the plain lives: by continuing, by being what the place has made them, by occupying the space that is available and no more, by reflecting the sky in their own still surfaces and allowing the heat to pass through them and joining the chorus when the time comes and being quiet when the time for quiet comes.

They are the people of the peneplain. They have been here for longer than memory, and they will be here for longer than forgetting, and when the plain is finally, finally done—when the last millimeter of the last rise has worn away and the water covers everything in a shallow, warm sheet that reflects the entire sky without interruption—they will still be here, standing in the water, listening to the frogs, watching the stars, being what the place has always been asking them to be: present, and patient, and part of the oldest sentence the land has ever spoken.
