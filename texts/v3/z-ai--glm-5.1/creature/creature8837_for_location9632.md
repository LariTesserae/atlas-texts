# The Inhabitants

---

The beetles are not the only ones. They are merely the ones you notice first, because they move, and movement draws the eye in a place where so much else communicates by slower methods.

But there are others.

---

You find the first one on your second hour, when you have grown bold enough to leave the low walls and cross the open silt. You almost step on it. Your foot descends, your weight commits, and at the last instant some register of shape or color fires in your hindbrain and you stagger, catch yourself on a wall that thrums like a tuning fork against your palm.

It is a person. Or: it was a person. Or: it is becoming something else, and the word *person* is what you reach for because you have no better one.

They sit against a section of wall in the attitude of someone resting after labor. Legs drawn up. Back pressed to the stone. Hands open on their knees, palms up, fingers relaxed. Their skin is the color of the sediment — not painted, not coated, but *become* — a pale mineral gray with the same matte finish as the packed silt. Their eyes are closed. Their lips are slightly parted. And from their parted lips, from their nostrils, from the slow pulse at their throat: the crystalline bloom. It emerges. Thin fibers, silver-green, the same material as the curtains on the walls, growing from the living body as if from soil. A delicate fringe edges their jaw. A mist of it fills the hollow of their collarbone. It has replaced their hair entirely, and where their hairline once was, the fibers have rooted into the stone behind them, threading into the gaps between the mortarless bricks, fusing body to architecture.

They are not dead. You understand this when you see the fibers turning, slowly, tracking the light that reaches this corner of the quarter in the late afternoon. The tracking requires tension. The tension requires will, or at least mechanism. Something in this form is still attending to the sun.

You check the pulse at their wrist. There is a rhythm, but it is slow — perhaps four beats per minute — and it does not quicken at your touch. Their skin is cool and dry, like ceramic fresh from firing. Their chest rises, but only once every twenty seconds, and the rise is shallow, barely disturbing the bloom at their sternum. They are breathing the dry air. They are drinking the light. They are feeding the growth that feeds on the stone, and the growth is feeding the sound, and the sound is feeding — what? The quarter itself? Something below the silt, something in the walls, something you have not found yet?

You leave them there. There is nothing else to do.

---

You find more.

Seven in total, over the next three hours. Each in the same attitude of rest or surrender or patience. Each at a different stage of the transformation.

The second is further along than the first. Less person, more fixture. Their limbs have shortened, thickened, assumed the rounded contours of the wall behind them. Their face has smoothed — not erased, but generalized, the way a river stone loses its edges. The features are suggestions now. The eyes have sealed shut beneath a membrane of translucent fiber. The mouth is a seam. They have become part of the architecture, a buttress of mineralized flesh, and the growth on them is thick, cascading, a whole curtain of sound-producing fiber that sways in the air currents and adds its voice to the roar.

The third and fourth are together, seated at a junction where two walls meet. They face each other, close enough that the growth from one has met and merged with the growth from the other, forming a single draped mass of fiber between their bodies — a canopy, a bridge, a web. Their hands are interlocked beneath it. The fiber has grown over their joined fingers, sealing them together. It is not clear which hand belongs to whom. They have been here a long time. The sound that comes from their merged growth is harmonic, complex, layered — two voices that have become one.

The fifth is almost invisible. You would have missed them entirely if not for a faint shimmer of prismatic light where no light should scatter. They are nearly flush with the wall, their body planar, compressed, as if they have been slowly absorbed into the stone over centuries. Only the fiber marks them — a sparse covering, more crystal than plant, that catches the sun and throws tiny rainbows across the silt. They make almost no sound. They are the silence inside the roar, the way a single still point can exist inside a spinning wheel.

The sixth is the one that makes you leave.

They are standing.

You round a corner of wall and they are there — upright, facing you, arms at their sides, body entirely consumed by the bloom. The fiber covers them completely, a second skin of silver-green that moves and shifts with the air currents. Their face is a suggestion beneath it, the architecture of a skull discernible but filled in, generalized, becoming architectural. They are not still. They are *listening*. Their head is tilted at an angle that speaks of attention, of reception, and as you watch, their body sways — not with the wind, but with the sound. They are adjusting it. The fiber on their arms tightens, loosens, changes pitch. The growth at their shoulders shifts angle, catches a different current, introduces a new harmonic into the chorus of the quarter.

They are playing the building. They are the instrument and the musician both. And when they sense you — not by sight, you think, but by the disruption your body makes in the airflow, the way your presence alters the acoustics — they turn their head toward you and you hear the sound change. A new frequency enters. Something that might be a question, or a greeting, or a warning, or simply an acknowledgment: *something else is here, and it changes the song.*

You do not run. Running would be impossible in this sound, in this heat. But you retreat. You find your seat on the low wall again and you sit and you try to understand what you have seen.

---

The seventh finds you.

You are sitting with the vibration in your bones, watching the light fail and the sound deepen — the growths reorienting, the harmonics shifting to something lower, something that presses more than it vibrates — when you become aware of motion to your left. Not the beetle-motion, deliberate and mechanical. Something fluid. Something careful.

They are not standing. They are moving along the wall on their hands and knees, slowly, with the deliberation of someone very old or very patient or very much in love with the surface they traverse. Their skin is still mostly skin — darker than yours, weathered to the same matte finish as everything here, but still flexible, still human in its range of expression. The bloom is sparse on them. A faint haze at the hairline. A patch at the left shoulder, spreading like lichen. They are early in the process. They have been here years, perhaps, not centuries.

They reach you. They sit beside you on the wall. The sound is too loud for speech — your voice dies, theirs would too — but they do not try to speak. Instead they place their hand flat on the wall between you, and they press, and you feel the vibration change. A pattern enters it. Not language, exactly. Something pre-language, or post-language. A rhythm that carries meaning the way a heartbeat carries life: without specifying, without describing, but communicating everything essential.

*You came from above.*

You press your hand beside theirs. The wall reads the shape of your bones, the density of your tissue, the foreignness of your chemistry. The sound shifts again.

*You are wet inside. You are full of solvent. You will dry out or you will leave.*

They look at you. Their eyes are still eyes — brown, bloodshot, dry at the corners but functional. They have not sealed yet. They can still see. They can still witness. They hold your gaze for a long time, and in that gaze you read something that is not welcome and not rejection but a kind of patient acknowledgment: *you are here, and you will change, and what you become is not my concern, but the quarter will accept you if you stay.*

They remove their hand from the wall. They resume their slow traversal, moving along the perimeter, touching the growths as they pass, adjusting their tension, tuning them — you realize this now — *tuning the quarter*. They are the maintainer. The one who has not yet given themselves entirely to the process but who serves it, who walks its boundaries, who ensures the song continues.

They are the one who placed the clay vessel at the junction. They wove the stiff mat on the eastern side. The sparse signs of human presence you noticed — those are theirs. Not decorations. Not offerings. Tools. Acoustic tools. Each object placed to break a standing wave, to redirect a harmonic, to prevent the sound from collapsing into destructive interference. They are maintaining the resonance the way a priest maintains a flame: not to worship it, but because they understand what happens when it goes out.

You do not know what happens when it goes out. You are not sure you want to.

---

Night comes, or something like it. The aperture above shows stars. The sun is gone. And the sound — the sound does not stop.

It changes. The growths cannot track the sun that is not there, and so they rest, and the tension goes out of them, and the roar resolves into something different: a low, cyclic pulsation, almost like breathing, almost like the beating of an enormous heart. The quarter breathes. The walls expand and contract by microns, and the silt shifts, and somewhere in the darkness the silica beetles move in great numbers, their carapazes clicking softly, a rhythm beneath the rhythm.

The seven are still. Their fiber drifts in the night currents, passive now, producing only this resting hum. The maintainer has found a place at the quarter's edge, curled against a wall, their sparse bloom catching starlight. They look smaller in the dark. More human. More tired.

You sit. You do not sleep. Sleep feels impossible here, where the sound presses against you like a hand, where the dryness is already working at your lips and your eyes and the joints of your fingers. You are desiccating. You are being preserved. If you stay long enough, you will mummify. And then — slowly, over years, over decades — you will grow. And the growth will root. And the growth will sing.

The maintainer shifts in their sleep. The sound adjusts. The stars wheel above the aperture, and the quarter holds its breath, and the clay vessel at the junction catches starlight instead of sunlight now, and holds it, trembling, like something almost alive.

You understand, sitting there, that the quarter is not a home. It is an instrument. And the ones who live here are not its inhabitants.

They are its music.
