What lives here lives provisionally.

This is the first thing to understand. Nothing in the basin has arrived with the assumption that it will stay. The pale amphipods that tumble through the warm shallows do not build shelters. They do not attachment themselves to surfaces or construct tubes from sediment the way their cousins in older waters do. They move constantly — not from restlessness but from the knowledge, encoded in whatever passes for their collective memory, that the channels shift. The stone softens and hardens on its own schedule. A pool that is deep today may be shallow tomorrow, may be stone again next century. To commit to a location here is to be buried or exposed or slowly dissolved by the water that raised you.

So they move. The amphipods move in loose clouds, their pale segments catching what little light there is, their legs finding purchase on stone that is still deciding its texture. They are not social in any way that implies affection or hierarchy. They are social the way a market is social — each individual following the same chemical signals toward the same resources, their movements creating patterns that look from above like coordination but are really just convergence. When they feed, they feed in masses, their bodies overlapping, their mouthparts working at the bacterial scum or at whatever organic film has accumulated on a submerged surface. They do not compete. There is enough, for now, because the water brings more, always more, from somewhere deeper.

But they do eat each other. This is not predation in the organized sense. It is opportunity. An amphipod that is injured or molting or simply slow becomes a concentration of nutrients in a place where nutrients are the only currency, and the others converge on it with the same chemical inevitability that draws them to the bacterial films. The dying amphipod does not struggle in any recognizable way. It is consumed while it is still alive, and its movements become part of the feeding motion, and then it is gone, and the cloud moves on.

---

The blind isopods are different.

They move in formations — not lines, not clusters, but something between the two, a distribution that maintains consistent spacing as they travel. You watched them for an hour once, trying to understand the pattern. The spacing is not uniform. It follows rules you cannot discern, distances that vary based on something invisible — perhaps chemical, perhaps vibrational, perhaps some quality of the stone beneath them that they can sense and you cannot.

They follow gradients. This much is clear. When the water shifts, when a new seep opens or an old one slows, the isopods reorient. They move toward whatever is richest in the minerals they need, and they move away from whatever is depleted. They do this collectively, the formation rotating or shifting direction without any visible signal, without any individual that you could point to and say *that one decided*. The decision emerges from them the way the basin's shape emerges from the argument between water and stone — a slow accumulation of small adjustments that produces a result no single entity intended.

They are longer than the amphipods, flatter, more deliberate. Their legs move in waves down their bodies, and the effect is less like walking and more like flowing. When they reach a food source — a particularly thick film of bacteria, a dead amphipod, a patch of mineral deposits that has accumulated on a submerged ledge — they fan out across it, their spacing tightening, and they feed in silence. They do not fight. They do not jostle. They feed until the resource is diminished, not gone, and then they reform their formation and move on.

You have never seen an isopod die. You have never seen one molt, or reproduce, or rest. They may do all of these things in the deeper channels where the water moves with more conviction and the shapes are larger. What you see is the fraction of their existence that passes through the shallows, the visible portion of a cycle that extends into parts of the basin you cannot reach.

They do not notice you. Or if they notice you, they do not adjust. You have stood in their path and watched them flow around your boots like water around a stone, their formation parting and rejoining without hesitation, without curiosity, without any acknowledgment that you are something different from the other obstacles they navigate daily. You are not a predator to them. You are not anything to them. You are topography.

---

The larger shapes in the deeper channels are what the basin is still deciding about.

You have seen them only as displacements — subtle changes in the way light behaves (or fails to behave) at the water's surface, a bulge or a movement that suggests mass below. They do not surface. They do not need to. Whatever they are, they have found a way to exist in the channels that the water has carved through stone that is still soft enough to carve, and they do not come up because what they need is down there.

The clicking comes from them. You are certain of this, though you cannot be certain why you are certain. The sound is too regular to be geological, too varied to be chemical. It rises from the deeper pools and from the channels below the stone, and it has a rhythm that suggests communication — or at least the kind of repeated signaling that, in other contexts, other species, would be called language.

The clicks are low and wet. They sound like joints popping, like tendons shifting in sockets that are not quite aligned. They sound like something that is not entirely comfortable in its own body, or that has only recently acquired a body and is still learning its tolerances. When several of them click at once — when the sound comes from multiple points in the same channel — there is a pattern, a call and response, or a chorus, or an argument. The tempo varies. The intervals vary. You have sat in the dimness and listened for hours and you cannot find the structure, but you are certain the structure is there. It is simply a structure that does not resolve in the time you have to listen.

They are patient, whatever they are. They have oriented themselves to the basin's tempo more completely than anything else here. The amphipods move quickly because they are small and their lives are short and the channels shift. The isopods move at their deliberate pace because their formations require consistency. The things in the deeper channels move at the pace of the stone itself — slowly, incrementally, their bodies pressing against channels that are still forming around them, their passage part of the carving process. They may be large. They may be growing larger. They may be, in some sense, the basin becoming aware of itself.

You do not know. You have not seen. You have only the clicking, and the shapes that are not shapes, and the knowledge that the basin extends deeper than you can go, warmer than you can measure, and that whatever lives down there has had longer to become what it is than anything in the shallows.

---

The gecko-things understand something the others do not.

They understand the air. They understand the interface between the wet and the merely damp, the surface of the stone and the surface of the water and the space between. They live on the walls because the walls are where the two media meet, and they face into the dimness because the dimness is where the insects fly, and the insects are what they eat.

They are translucent. You can see the dark masses of their internal organs through their skin, shifting and compressing as they breathe, as they digest, as they wait. Their eyes are two dark spots that do not seem to focus on anything visible. They sense light the way a wound senses pressure — as an awareness of damage, of something external that might require response. In the basin's perpetual dimness, this sense is mostly dormant. They rely instead on vibration, on the subtle shifts in air movement that announce an insect's passage, on the chemical signals that drift from the pools below.

They wait. This is their genius. They wait with a stillness that the stone might envy, their bodies flattened against the wet walls, their limbs splayed in positions that look uncomfortable but are clearly sustainable. You have watched a gecko-thing hold its position for six hours, its skin slowly absorbing the condensation that formed on its back, its eyes dark and unfocused, its entire being oriented toward a patience that you cannot comprehend. And then — movement. A blur. A wet sound. And the gecko-thing has an insect in its mouth, and its jaws are working, and then it is still again, and the stillness continues as if the interruption never happened.

They do not interact with each other. You have seen them on adjacent walls, within meters of each other, and they do not acknowledge the other's existence. They are not territorial because territory implies a relationship to space that they do not have. They are simply present, in the way that a mineral deposit is present, and they will continue to be present until they are not, and the basin will continue without them or with them, and the insects will continue to rise from the pools, and the cycle will continue, and the gecko-things will wait.

They are the basin's most successful inhabitants because they have asked the least of it. They do not require the channels to remain stable. They do not require the formations to harden. They require only a wall and moisture and the occasional insect, and the basin provides these things constantly, reliably, as a byproduct of its own formation. The gecko-things have found the niche that will persist no matter what the basin decides to become. They are adapted not to the basin's current state but to its process.

---

But there is something else. Something you have not named because you do not know if naming is appropriate, if the thing you are sensing is a thing at all.

The basin has a feeling. This is not the same as having a mind. You do not believe the basin is conscious in any way that would be recognizable to consciousness. But it has a feeling — a quality of attention, perhaps, or a quality of tendency, that is more than the sum of its geological and hydrological processes. The water does not simply flow. It *persists*. The stone does not simply subside. It *yields*. The life does not simply exist. It *insists*.

The basin wants. This is the closest you can come to describing it. It wants in the way that a chemical reaction wants equilibrium, in the way that a wound wants healing, in the way that water wants level. It wants to be something, and it does not know what, and the wanting is visible in every surface and audible in every sound and tangible in the warm moisture that presses against your skin from every direction.

The creatures that live here have aligned themselves with this wanting. The amphipods follow the nutrients because the nutrients follow the water because the water follows the basin's formation, and the formation is the shape of the wanting. The isopods move in their formations because the formations are a response to the gradients, and the gradients are a response to the basin's slow redistribution of its own mass. The things in the deeper channels have gone further — they have made themselves part of the process, their bodies another variable in the equation the basin is solving. And the gecko-things have found the one constant, the one thing the wanting will always produce, and they have attached themselves to it with a patience that mirrors the basin's own.

Nothing here is separate from the wanting. The industrial remnants — the pumping station, the walkway, the control panel — these are the only things that resisted it, and the basin has been processing them ever since, incorporating their metal and their logic into its own, dissolving what it can dissolve, subsuming what it can subsume, waiting for the rest to become soft enough to absorb.

You are being processed too. You know this. The moisture is finding every seam in your clothing, every gap in your skin. The warmth is making you porous. The sound is making you receptive. You are not as different from the stone as you would like to believe. You are being worn down, slowly, at a pace that you cannot perceive but that the basin can. In a century, the stone where you stood will be slightly smoother. In a millennium, it will be unrecognizable. And the basin will still be forming, and the wanting will still be present, and whatever lives here then will have aligned itself with whatever the basin has become.

---

What lives here lives in negotiation.

Not with each other — the amphipods do not negotiate with the isopods, the gecko-things do not negotiate with their prey — but with the basin itself. Every creature here exists at the basin's pleasure, and the basin's pleasure is change, and so every creature here exists in a state of continuous adaptation. Not the slow adaptation of evolution, though that is happening too, but the moment-to-moment adaptation of a living thing to a living place. The channels shift, and the amphipods shift. The gradients move, and the isopods move. The insects rise, and the gecko-things strike. The basin deepens, and the things in the channels grow, or change, or become something that the basin has room for.

This is why there are no plants here. Plants commit. They root. They stay. They make an argument for permanence in a place that has not yet agreed to be permanent, and the basin will not honor that argument. The bacterial films persist because they do not commit — they spread and retract and spread again, following moisture, following minerals, following the basin's temporary moods. The scum on the pools persists because it floats, because it can move, because it is as liquid as the water it lives on.

What lives here lives provisionally, and this is not a weakness. This is the only strength the basin respects.

You are provisional too, though you keep forgetting. You tell yourself you are passing through, but the basin does not recognize the category. You are here, and being here is being processed, and being processed is becoming part of the wanting. Your breath adds to the moisture. Your warmth adds to the warmth. Your passage disturbs the insects, feeds the gecko-things, displaces the amphipods, sends vibrations through the water that the deeper things might sense and respond to with clicks that you cannot interpret.

You are not outside the basin. You are not observing it. You are inside it, and it is inside you — the moisture, the warmth, the sound, the dimness. You are becoming part of what lives here, which is becoming part of what is forming here, which is becoming part of what will be here when the basin finally decides.

The decision will not be reached soon. The clicking continues. The water continues. The slow subsiding continues. And what lives here continues to live, provisionally, patiently, in the warm dark wet of a place that is still becoming itself, and will be becoming itself long after everything currently alive within it has been dissolved and redistributed and incorporated into whatever the basin decides to become next.
