The Held do not have faces the way you have faces. They have toll-patterns.

A child is born and the bronze sounds its figure — five strikes in a rhythm that will never belong to anyone else — and from that moment the child is more its bell-shape than its bone-shape. You know your mother by how she sounds coming down the lane. You know your lover by the interval between their steps. Faces are for mirrors and the rare still tarn, and mirrors here are unreliable at best (the Archive has ruled on mirrors twice; both rulings are kept in locked drawers), but a bell-pattern is a fact you can stand in. The Held carry themselves like music they are always half-hearing, and they know one another the way the town knows its roads: by recurrence, by the proof of returning.

They are pale people, and not only from the whitewash. The light here has been stripping color from things since before the mountains finished failing. The Held have adapted to the spectrum that remains: they see seventeen kinds of grey, and their word for gold covers what you'd call gold, and also the particular light that comes through fog at midday, and also the feeling of hearing your own bell-pattern answered by someone who has learned it by heart. Their eyes are large and quiet. They do not blink at rate you'd find comfortable. Something about watching — about the need to attend, to hold the world visible — has slowed the reflex, and a Held citizen will look at you with a steady, unflinching attention that is not aggressive but is not friendly either; it is structural. They are keeping you real. It is the only courtesy that matters here.

They dress in felted moss and woven grass and, always, white. The whitewash goes on the roofs and walls, and it goes on the people: a pale clay mixed from the kaolin pans two days' walk north, worn on the forearms and the forehead and the shoulders, refreshed each morning at the wall-basins where the drip-hour's catch is kept. A white figure on the wall at evening is not decoration; it is a proof, a small broadcast: *I am here and I am being seen and therefore I continue.* The Held do not have a word for orphan that means "without parents." Their word for orphan means "unwatched."

Children are raised by the lane. This is not a metaphor. The roofed lanes of the Hold are the children's first territory, and a child learns to walk under cover before it learns to walk in the open. The lanes see everything — that is their purpose — and a child in a lane is a child being attended to by the architecture itself. The oldest children, the ones who have grown tall enough to touch the lane-roofs with their palms, are the ones who begin to walk the bell-lines, checking the posts, learning to read the silence-holes. By the time a Held citizen is twelve, they know the town's ringing the way you know your own language: not as information but as the medium through which information becomes possible.

They do not lie, but not from virtue. Lying requires a confidence about what is real that the Level does not support. A Held citizen will say *the fog came from the east* or *the third bell-line went mute at mid-morning* or *my grandmother's toll-pattern had seven strikes, not six,* and each of these is an offering into the shared attention, a small rope thrown across the thin country. To lie would be to weaken the rope. They have stories — the Archive is full of them, knot-recorded, bell-recorded, kept in the deep peat-cellars where the centuries stay cool — but the stories are always about things that happened, and they are always told the same way, because variation in a place like this is not creativity; it is erosion. The Held understand what the rest of the world forgets: that repetition is not the enemy of meaning but its preservative.

They marry by bell-matching. Your toll-pattern and another's have to sound well together — not identical, not clashing, but related, the way two notes make a chord that neither could make alone. The matchmaker is the Archivist, who knows every pattern alive and many that are dead, and who listens for the harmonic. When two patterns match, the marriage-bells ring the combined figure, and from that point the couple exists as a chord, a small proof that agreement is still possible. Children inherit fragments of both patterns, recombined; the Archivist composes the new toll at birth, listening for what the child's cry wants to become.

They farm, but farming here is a strange negotiation. The soil is peat and moss and the cold refusal to rot, and what grows wants to grow — the green is *insistent,* remember, it does not know the world has finished — so the farmer's work is less cultivation than arrangement. You do not coax a crop from reluctant ground; you persuade an eager, directionless abundance to concentrate itself in particular places. The farms are five miles apart, each one a white cairn and a bell-line and a family maintaining its knot of reality in the thin country. The farmers walk their bounds each morning, touching the cairns, speaking their toll-names aloud — not prayer, not incantation, just the simple physics: *a voice heard is a place confirmed.* They harvest the moss for fuel, the grasses for rope and felt, the cushion-plants for their medicinal warmth. They do not harvest the fruit; the fruit is not for them. No one has ever been able to say why, but the fruit is not for them.

The old people are the most real people in the Hold. This is not sentiment; it is mechanics. An old person has been seen for longer. Their toll-pattern has been ringing for decades, woven into the town's chord so thoroughly that removing them would leave a silence the size of a building. The oldest woman in the Hold — her name is Tollen, which is not a name but a title, meaning *the one who is rung* — has a pattern that the Archive says has been sounding for a hundred and twelve years, and when she walks the roofed lanes, the bells on the walls ring her figure without anyone striking them. They have learned it from the air. She is so attended-to that reality keeps her as a load-bearing wall. Her death will be a structural event. Scaffolding will be required.

The dead are kept in the Archive, not as bodies but as patterns. Every citizen who has ever lived has their toll-pattern recorded in the knot-registry, and on certain days — the frost-bloom, the Long Rain — the bell-tower sounds the patterns of the absent, a long roll of figures that takes most of the morning. The dead are not gone; they are simply not being rung. The Held do not fear death the way you might. They fear being forgotten by the bells, which is a different thing and a slower one. A person who is no longer attended to does not die; they thin. They become provisional. They join the ranks of things that the Level is quietly de-creating, and the wind picks up their shape and carries it to the gullies, where it will cry out in their voice once a season until someone answers, and no one answers, because the rule is the rule: *once is weather; twice is a person.* The dead get one call, and then they are archive.

There are those who thin before they die. The Held have a word for this: *untolled,* meaning a person who has stopped being seen. It happens to farmers who stay too long at the distant posts, or to travelers who lose the bell-lines in fog and walk too far into the thin country. An untolled person is still present — still breathing, still standing — but they have begun to flicker. Their shadow doubles. Their voice acquires an echo that comes slightly too late, as if the sound is having trouble agreeing to exist. The cure is attention: bring them home, ring their pattern, whitewash their shoulders, keep them in the roofed lanes for a season. Sometimes it works. The Archive records the cases where it does not. Those names are entered in the Registry of the Absent alongside the wolves and the bees and the river that used to leave the plain.

The Archivist is the most important person in the Hold, and the least visible. They live in the deep archive, below the peat-cellars, where the oldest records are kept in the cold slow time that preserves all things here. The Archivist rarely comes above. They have assistants — bell-listeners, knot-readers, the young people with good ears who can transcribe a toll-pattern from a single hearing — but the Archivist themselves works in the deep, maintaining the records, composing the new tolls, making the rulings that keep the town coherent. The last ruling was forty years ago: *the tarns are to be enjoyed, not consulted.* The one before that: *a shadow that moves against the sun is to be regarded as weather, not as a person.* The one before that: *the Far One is a distance, not a destination; no road shall be built toward it.* The Archive does not rule often. When it does, the town obeys, because the alternative is a disagreement, and in a place where reality is scarce, disagreement is not a difference of opinion; it is a small erosion.

The children play games that would make you uneasy. They play *thinning,* where one child stands at the edge of the roofed lane and looks out at the open plain while the others try to hold them by calling their name; the child who can look longest without flinching wins, and sometimes they see things out there — shapes, second shadows, the provisional geography that the Level is always half-inventing — and they come back changed, with larger eyes and a new note in their voice that the Archivist has to write down. They play *bell-match,* where one child rings a pattern on a stone and the others race to identify it; the best players can hear a pattern once and reproduce it perfectly, and these children are marked early for the Archive. They play *the wolf-call,* which is not a game but a practice: one child stands in a gully and makes the wind's wolf-sound, and the others must not answer. *Once is weather; twice is a person.* The children learn this before they learn to read. It is the first law and the last.

The Held are not unhappy. This is important to understand. They are not grim or stoic or resigned; they are *attentive,* the way a musician is attentive during a performance — not because the music is sad but because the music requires every note to be played, and a missed note is not a tragedy but a silence, and silence here is not an absence but the material from which absences are made. They laugh. They sing walking-tunes in the lanes, wordless melodies that keep time and keep the walker real. They stand in the Long Rain with their faces up and their arms out and they feel what they call *the confirmation* — the agreement of the whole continent at once — and they weep from it, not from grief but from the sheer density of shared existence. They love each other the way you love a rope in a high place: not with desperation but with the complete attention that the situation requires, and the situation always requires complete attention.

They have a saying: *the moss does not know.* It means: the world has finished, and the moss grows anyway, and the moss is right. It means: the old planet has worn down to a memory of itself, and the green runs miles in every direction, and the green is right. It means: you will thin, and you will be archived, and the bells will ring your pattern once a year on the frost-bloom, and you will be right, because being right on the Level does not mean being permanent. It means being attended to while you last. It means being a note in the chord that the town is always sounding, always proving, always keeping real.

The Held do not call themselves survivors. They call themselves *the kept.*
