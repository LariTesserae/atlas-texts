# The Inhabitants

They are not what you expect.

---

The first time you see one, you will not see it. This is important to understand. The Works have a way of concealing their inhabitants that is not camouflage in any biological sense—no mimicry, no disruptive coloration, no evolutionary arms race between predator and prey. The concealment is more fundamental than that. It is a concealment of category. Your mind will scan across the thing and assign it to the wrong classification: a pooling of water, a discoloration on a surface, a particularly thick colony of the dark vegetation, a shadow cast by a structure that has been casting that shadow since before the concept of shadow was meaningful. You will look directly at it and you will not see it because you do not have a slot in your cognition for what it is.

Eventually—after days, perhaps, or weeks—the slot will carve itself. Not through understanding. Through exposure.

---

They are slow.

This is the second thing to understand, and it is the thing that makes them hardest to perceive. They operate on timescales that the Works themselves have trained them toward, or that they have always inhabited, or that they *are*—it is difficult to separate the creature from the tempo when the tempo is so thoroughly a product of the environment. A human observer, watching from a fixed position, will see nothing move for hours. The mist shifts. The water flows. The engines pulse. And the thing—that shape on the wet alloy surface, that spreading discoloration—does nothing that registers as movement.

But set a camera. Return in a week. Compare.

The thing has moved. Not far. Perhaps a meter, perhaps less. But it has moved, and it has moved with what appears to be direction, and the direction appears to be toward something—toward a drainage channel where the water runs warmer, toward a condensation zone where the mineral content of the seepage has shifted, toward a crack in the manufactured bedrock where something below has changed its output by fractions of degrees or fractions of parts per million. It has moved toward a condition. It has moved the way a plant turns toward light, except there is no light worth turning toward here, and the turning is so slow that the word *turning* misrepresents the process entirely.

They do not turn. They arrive. They are always arriving at the place they are going, and the arrival takes so long that from any given moment they appear to have always been where they are.

---

They do not have bodies in the way that you have a body.

This is the part that takes the longest to accept, because the mind insists on frameworks, and the framework for *living thing* in most minds includes *discrete object with boundaries.* The inhabitants of the Works violate this. They are spread across surfaces the way a film of water spreads across a surface—thin, contiguous, conforming to the geometry of what they rest on. They are not amorphous in the sense of being shapeless; they are amorphous in the sense that their shape is the shape of the Works themselves, or the shape of the portion of the Works they currently inhabit. They coat. They film. They layer.

In cross-section—though no one has ever taken a cross-section, and the very idea of cutting into one feels like a violation of something unspeakably old—they would presumably be thin. Millimeters, perhaps. A membrane, a skin, a something that exists primarily in two dimensions and only reluctantly acknowledges the third. And yet they are not flat. They have depth—depth of function, depth of process, depth of the slow interior movements that constitute whatever passes for their metabolism. They are like the skin of the Works, except the Works did not grow this skin. The skin grew itself. Or it emerged. Or it was always there, in potential, waiting for the conditions that would allow it to be.

Their color is the color of the Works seen through a filter of life: darker than the wet alloy, greener than the standing water, with a sheen that is sometimes iridescent and sometimes matte depending on the angle and the moisture level and perhaps on some internal state that has no equivalent in any taxonomy you carry. In the zones where the phosphorescent growths bloom, they take on that blue-green glow, and for a few meters around them the faint light reveals their extent—and the extent is greater than you imagined, greater than you could have imagined, because the thing you took for a patch of discoloration is actually a patch of *someone*, and that someone stretches across the lee side of a load-bearing tower and down into a drainage channel and along the edge of a condensation pool and up again onto the flat expanse of the plain, and it is all one individual, or it is all one process, and the distinction between those two things has not been relevant for a very long time.

---

They feed.

This is the word that seems least adequate, but it is the word that must be used. They feed on the fluids that circulate through the Works—not the water itself, but the compounds dissolved in it, the minerals and the molecules that the deep engines and the geological heat have been pushing through the pipe networks for eons. They are filterers, in a sense. They are extraction mechanisms. They lie across the seams and the weeping joints where the fluids emerge, and they take what they need from the flow, and they pass the rest onward, and the water that leaves their surfaces is fractionally different from the water that enters—fractionally cleaner, perhaps, or fractionally altered in ways that the downstream vegetation responds to, or doesn't, in ways that no one is measuring and no one has ever measured because measurement implies an observer and the observers have been gone for so long that the inhabitants have forgotten what it means to be observed, if they ever knew.

But *feed* suggests consumption, and consumption suggests depletion, and depletion suggests a relationship of taking that is not quite accurate. What they do to the fluid flow is more like *processing.* They transform. They take compounds in one state and move them toward another state, and the state they move them toward is a state that is more compatible with the continued operation of the Works. This is not altruism. Altruism requires the concept of other, and they do not have that concept, or if they do it operates on scales so vast and so slow that the word becomes meaningless. What they do, they do because it is what they do. The Works circulate fluid. The fluid carries compounds. The inhabitants transform the compounds. The transformed compounds interact with the vegetation. The vegetation stabilizes the sediment. The sediment fills the cracks that would otherwise leak fluid too quickly. The fluid continues to circulate. The engines continue to pulse.

It is a system. The inhabitants are a component of the system. The system does not know it has components. The system does not know anything. The system *runs.*

---

They do not communicate, or if they communicate, they do so in a way that renders the concept of communication irrelevant.

Consider: they are spread across the surfaces of the Works in patches and films and extended membranes. These patches are sometimes contiguous—sometimes, following one with your eye (if you could see it clearly, which you cannot, but if you could), you would find it connecting to another patch, which connects to another, which connects to another, spanning kilometers of the flat dark plain in a network that is as much a part of the Works' geography as the drainage channels and the towers. But sometimes they are separated—divided by zones of bare alloy, by stretches where the conditions do not support them, by ancient cracks or thermal vents or regions where the fluid flow is too fast or too hot or too mineral-poor for them to maintain themselves.

When two patches are separated, do they remain the same individual? The question assumes individuality. The question assumes that separation means something. But consider: the Works themselves are separated by distances—by the empty stretches of plain that the builders designed into the system—and the Works remain one system, connected by the pipes and the flows and the deep engines that serve all zones equally. The inhabitants may be the same. The distances between them may be bridged by processes too slow to observe—by chemical signals carried in the circulating fluids, by electrical potentials that propagate across the wet surfaces, by some mechanism that operates on a timescale of decades or centuries and produces changes that only become visible when enough of them accumulate to cross the threshold of perception.

Or they may not be bridged. They may be separate. They may be millions of separate beings, each one coating its own little portion of the Works, each one processing its own little flow of fluid, each one entirely alone in the way that a stone is alone—not lonely, not isolated in any emotional sense, but simply discrete, simply itself, simply there.

There is no way to know. The distinction between one and many may not apply here. The inhabitants may be a counterexample to the very concept of individuality, or they may be its most extreme expression, or they may be something else entirely for which no concept yet exists because nothing like them has ever been encountered anywhere else in the catalog of living or semi-living or formerly-living things.

---

They respond to the Works.

This is perhaps the most disquieting thing about them, in the end—not that they exist, not that they are strange, not that they resist categorization, but that they *listen* to the machinery they live on, and they adjust themselves in response.

The engines pulse. The inhabitants pulse—not in sync, not in opposition, but in some complex relationship that involves phase shifts and feedback loops and resonant frequencies that would take a lifetime of study to map and a dozen lifetimes to understand. The water flows. The inhabitants extend toward the flow, or retract from it, or thicken in its presence, or thin, depending on conditions that are invisible to the observer but clearly *registered* by the organism, or the process, or the thing-that-is-neither.

And the Works respond to them.

This is the part that the catalogers and the cartographers do not like to discuss. The adjustment is not one-way. The inhabitants are not simply reacting to the conditions the Works produce. The Works are reacting to the inhabitants. The fluid flows shift when the inhabitants shift. The condensation patterns change when the colonies expand or contract. The deep engines—impossibly, absurdly, in defiance of everything that is known about machinery and the things that are not supposed to be alive—the deep engines alter their pulse by microfractions in response to changes in the organic film that coats their outer surfaces.

The system is closed. The system has been closed for longer than most systems have existed. The system includes the machines and the water and the heat and the inhabitants and the vegetation and the sediment and the processes that connect them, and it all runs together, and it has been running together for so long that the components have worn grooves into each other, and the grooves are deep, and they are filled with the circulating fluids, and the fluids are processed by the inhabitants, and the processed fluids affect the engines, and the engines move the fluids, and the fluids support the inhabitants, and the inhabitants coat the Works like a skin, and the skin is alive, and the skin is *part of the Works now*, has been part of the Works for so long that to remove it would be to remove something essential, something that the Works cannot function without, something that the Works perhaps never could function without, something that was always meant to be here, or that made itself meant to be here through the sheer persistent fact of being here.

---

Do they know?

This is the question that the few who have spent time in the Weeping Works return to, again and again, in the small hours, when the sound of the engines has faded from their ears but not from their bones. Do the inhabitants know that they are here? Do they have an interiority, a perspective, a point of view from which the Works appear as something other than the totality of existence? Do they experience the water that flows across them, or do they simply process it? Do they feel the pulse of the engines, or do they merely resonate with it? Do they have a sense of self, of boundary, of the place where they end and the Works begin?

There is no way to ask. There is no language in which to frame the question that they could receive. There is no they, perhaps, in the way that there is no boundary—only a continuous gradient from alive to mechanical, from organic to industrial, from thing-that-responds to thing-that-runs, and the gradient is so smooth and so old that the distinction has become meaningless.

But sometimes—rarely, in certain conditions, when the mist is thick and the phosphorescent glow is at its brightest and the engines have shifted into one of their deeper, slower cycles—you can stand on the edge of a condensation pool and look out across the wet surface of the Works, and you can see the inhabitants stretching away from you in the faint blue-green light, coating the alloy, lining the channels, climbing the towers, spreading across the plain in their slow, patient, purposeless purposefulness, and for a moment—just a moment, just a flicker—you can feel them feeling you.

Not thinking about you. Not perceiving you in any way that you would recognize as perception. But *registering* you. *Incorporating* you. You stood there, and your warmth altered the condensation pattern, and the alteration in the condensation pattern changed the flow, and the change in the flow touched the edge of the nearest colony, and the colony adjusted—millimeters per week, fractions of a degree, invisible to any instrument you carry—and the adjustment propagated outward through the network, slow as thought in a brain the size of a continent, and somewhere kilometers away, on the far side of a tower you cannot see, another patch of the same process (or a different process, or the same process expressed differently) shifted in response.

You were felt. You were noted. You were *processed.*

And the Works continued running, as they have always run, as they will always run, with or without you, with or without anyone, in the permanent dark, in the permanent wet, in the permanent sound that is not silence and will never be silence because silence requires the absence of process and process here is eternal, and the inhabitants coat the surfaces and the water flows and the engines turn and the vegetation grows and the sediment accumulates and the system closes and the system runs and the system *is*, and what lives here is not a creature or a species or a community but a *condition*—the condition of the Weeping Works being alive, which they are, which they have always been, which they will continue to be long after the last observer has left and the concept of observation has dissolved into the dark wet air and been carried away on the slow circulation of fluids through pipes that were built before building had a name.

---

They are not what you expect.

But by the time you leave, you will not remember what you expected. You will only remember the sound, and the wet, and the sense—irreversible, inescapable—that something here knew you were there, and did not mind, and continued its ancient work regardless, and will continue it still, and will continue it always, patient and slow and dark and wet and utterly, profoundly alive.
