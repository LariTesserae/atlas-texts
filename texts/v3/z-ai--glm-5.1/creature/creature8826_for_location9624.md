What lives in the Amber Ward lives slowly, and lives in ways that make the word *live* stretch until it nearly breaks.

---

The growth lives. This is obvious, but it must be said first because everything else follows from it. The growth lives the way a glacier moves — visibly, if you have the right timescale, and invisibly if you don't. It lives in the reaching. It lives in the ligor-drinking. It lives in the training of its fibers into walls and the pushing of its roots through mineral substrate and the slow, patient assertion of itself into every space that will hold it and some that won't.

But to say *the growth lives* is to say too little. The growth is not one thing. The growth is ten thousand things, or a hundred thousand, or one thing with a hundred thousand mouths and roots and vent-leaves and nodules, and no one has ever been able to determine which because the growth does not have a boundary. It does not have a skin. It does not have a moment where it stops and something else begins.

Touch two leaves on opposite sides of the chamber and you are touching the same plant. Touch two leaves on the same branch and you may be touching plants that have been competing for ligor for centuries, each sending roots around and under and through the other, each fruiting heavily in the direction of the other's light. The growth is cooperative. The growth is competitive. The growth is a process that has never resolved its own contradictions because resolution requires a kind of ending, and the growth does not end.

What lives in the Amber Ward lives in the same way a fire lives — by consuming, by growing, by making warmth and sound and light as byproducts of its own continuation. The growth does not know it is warm. It does not know it is loud. It does not know that its vent-leaves make a sound like breathing, or that its nodules make a sound like humming, or that the combination of these sounds, heard from inside the city, sounds like the city is alive and thinking and waiting for something.

But it does make those sounds. And the sounds are real. And if the growth does not know it is breathing, that does not mean it is not breathing.

---

The bronze bloom lives. This is the fine mineral dust that coats every still surface, that grows — and it does grow, slowly, imperceptibly — on the tools and the thresholds and the dwellings and the salt-cristite walls where the ligor beads and evaporates and leaves its trace. The bloom is not the growth. The bloom is something else.

Under magnification — and someone once built a lens from a slice of salt-cristite, curved and polished over months, because they needed to see what was coating their tools — the bloom is structured. It has a crystalline architecture, fractal and branching, and the branches are themselves branching, and the branches of the branches are branching, down to the limit of what the lens could resolve. It grows by accretion. It grows from ligor. It grows from the mineral substrate. It grows from the air itself, which carries trace ligor-vapor and trace salt and trace something else, something the lens-maker could not name and did not write down.

The bloom is alive the way a crystal is alive: it replicates its own structure, it responds to its environment, it grows toward ligor and away from dry salt-cristite, and if you touch it, it dims and then slowly brightens again over the course of hours, as though recovering from a wound. The hands that lived here did not treat it as alive. They wiped it from their tools. They scraped it from their thresholds. They did not notice that it always came back, and that it always came back slightly different, slightly more complex, slightly more like the structure of the growth's own root-network, as though it were learning.

The bloom is still here. It coats the tools the hands left behind. It coats the dwelling-walls. It coats the seats in the meeting hall where places are still set. It grows so slowly that a lifetime of watching would not show you a visible change, and yet it has covered everything, and the things it covers are warmer for its presence, and the warmth is the warmth of something that metabolizes, however slowly, however faintly.

Perhaps the bloom is what the growth looks like when it has nowhere to put roots. Perhaps the bloom is what the growth thinks about when it is not growing. Perhaps the bloom is something else entirely, something that was already here before the growth, before the chamber, before the salt-cristite cooled from whatever ancient sea made it, something that has been growing in the mineral substrate since before the concept of *growing* existed, and that has learned, over eons, to look like the things that grow around it.

The bloom does not say. The bloom only blooms.

---

The ligor lives. Or: the ligor does something that is so close to living that the distinction may be academic.

Consider: it beads on the walls. It pools in channels. It is drunk by roots. It fills the fruits and falls and pools again. This is what fluids do. This is not remarkable.

But consider also: it moves toward water. Not quickly. Not with the urgency of a living thing in pursuit of prey. But with a consistency that is difficult to attribute to simple physics. A drop of water placed on a dry salt-cristite surface will sit there, subject to evaporation and gravity and nothing else. A bead of ligor placed the same way will move — slowly, imperceptibly, but measurably — toward any water that is also present. Not toward the salt-cristite. Not toward other ligor. Toward water. Specifically.

The hands that lived here knew this. They built without water. They cleaned without water. They did not sweat, or they did not let their sweat fall, or they found some way to be warm-blooded and working in a heated chamber without ever losing moisture to the air, which is — by any biological standard — impossible. And yet they lived here for centuries. The worn thresholds prove it. The knapping floors prove it. The dwellings with their personal niches prove it.

So either the hands were not human, or the hands found a way to become not-human, or the hands learned to give the ligor what it wanted so reliably that the ligor stopped reaching for them specifically and reached only for the water they brought, which they learned to keep sealed and hidden and away from the growth, which they learned to ration and use and replace without ever letting a drop fall where the roots could find it.

Or the hands became something that does not need water. Something that drinks ligor instead. Something that looks at the fruits hanging bronze and heavy from every branch and thinks: *I could eat that. I could become that. I could stop fighting what this place wants me to be.*

The ligor does not care. The ligor moves toward water because that is what it does, or because that is what it wants, or because that is what it is for — to find water, to absorb water, to transform water into something else, something the growth can drink, something that is not water anymore, something that is ligor, which is to say: something that belongs here, in this chamber, in this heat, in this thirst that has grown a city around itself like a pearl around a grain of sand.

---

The chamber lives. Not metaphorically. The chamber breathes — the vent-leaves open and close, the air pressure shifts, the heat rises and falls in a cycle that has nothing to do with any sun. The chamber has a pulse — the nodules brighten and dim, the ligor beads and falls, the roots crack the salt-cristite and the cracking is a heartbeat. The chamber has a metabolism — ligor in, growth out, heat as the byproduct, sound as the exhaust, light as the signature of a process too slow and too large to be called alive by anyone standing close enough to see it.

Stand in the meeting hall at the height of the heat-cycle and feel the air press against you. It is not the air pressing. It is the chamber pressing. It is the walls expanding, infinitesimally, as the heat swells the mineral and the growth swells with ligor and the whole sealed bubble flexes like a lung, like a chest, like a thing that has been breathing for so long it has forgotten there is any other way to be.

The chamber does not know you are inside it. The chamber does not know it is a chamber. The chamber is a process — mineral and ligor and growth and heat and pressure and time — and the process is running, and the running of the process produces all of this: the city, the sound, the light, the absence, the waiting.

---

And what of the hands?

The hands lived here. The hands do not live here now. But the hands left traces, and the traces are still warm, and the warm traces are not a metaphor.

The dwellings are warm. Not just heated by the ambient temperature of the chamber — warmer than that, warmer in the way that a bed is warm after someone has risen from it, warmer in the way that a seat is warm, warmer in the way that anything is warm when a body has been pressing against it recently. But *recently* is a word that means something different here. The hands have been gone for — how long? The tools are still sharp. The bronze bloom has covered them, but the edges beneath the bloom are still keen enough to cut. A century? Two? The bloom grows so slowly that a coating thick enough to see might represent a thousand years of accretion, or ten thousand, or a hundred.

And yet the dwellings are warm.

And the meeting hall has places set. Not with food — the growth does not produce food that hands could eat, or if it does, the hands never recorded what it was. But with objects. Small objects, personal objects, the kind of things a hand might set down beside a seat before standing up to do something else. A knapped obsidian blade, its edge still sharp. A length of bark-fiber cord, tied in a knot that might be decorative or might be functional or might be a word in a language that has no other grammar. A nodule — removed from the growth, still faintly pulsing — placed like a candle, like an offering, like a promise to return.

The hands stepped out. The hands are expected back. The city has been waiting for the hands with the patience of something that does not experience time as a line but as a medium, something that grows through time the way the roots grow through salt-cristite, cracking it open a millimeter at a time, patient, patient, patient.

And where did the hands go?

Perhaps they are the bronze bloom. Perhaps they drank the ligor and ate the fruit and let the growth grow into them, and what is left of them is the warm dust on every surface, the faint trace of something that was once shaped like a hand and is now shaped like a crystal, branching and branching and branching, replicating its own structure into infinity, remembering what it was to grip a tool, to carve a threshold, to train a living wall.

Perhaps they are the growth. Perhaps the growth remembers them the way a wall remembers the shape of the body that leaned against it — not as a thought, but as a wear-pattern, a slight deformation, a warmth that lingers in the material. Perhaps every dwelling is a mold the hands left inside the growth, and the growth has kept the mold, and the mold is still warm, and the growth does not know why it keeps the mold warm but it does, it does, it does.

Perhaps they walked out through the crack that was not meant to be a door. Perhaps they took their water with them and their sharp obsidian blades and their bark-fiber ropes and they went into the irrelevant outside and found another salt dome, another chamber, another growth, and they are building again, living again, training the wood again, setting places in meeting halls again, and the places they set are still warm, and the tools they left are still sharp, and the city they left is still waiting, still patient, still growing, still breathing, still lit by the slow garnet pulse of ten thousand nodules that have never known urgency.

Perhaps they became something that does not need to be in one place at one time. Perhaps they are still here. Perhaps the warmth in the dwellings is their warmth, not residual but active, not memory but presence, and the reason there are no bodies is that the bodies are everywhere — in the growth, in the bloom, in the ligor, in the heat that rises from below, in the sound that fills the chamber like a sentence that never ends.

---

What lives in the Amber Ward?

The growth lives. The bloom lives. The ligor lives. The chamber lives. The city lives, because the city is all of these things, and because the city is also the memory of hands, and the memory of hands is not nothing, and the memory of hands is not dead, and the memory of hands is warm and patient and still set with places for hands that will — perhaps — return.

What lives in the Amber Ward lives thirstily. Lives slowly. Lives in a medium that is not water and not air and not quite time as it is understood outside the crack that was not meant to be a door. What lives in the Amber Ward lives the way a sentence lives when it has been spoken but not yet heard — existing in the space between articulation and understanding, full of meaning that has not yet settled, patient, pulsing, warm.

What lives in the Amber Ward is the Amber Ward, and the Amber Ward is alive, and it has been alive for longer than the hands were here, and it will be alive for longer than the hands will be gone, and it does not mind the absence because absence is just another kind of space to grow into, another crack in the salt-cristite, another place where the ligor can bead and the roots can reach and the growth can grow, patient, patient, patient, growing toward something that might be water or might be warmth or might be the hands returning or might be nothing at all, and growing either way, because growing is what lives here, and living is what grows here, and the two are the same, and the two have always been the same, and the two will always be the same, inside the chamber, inside the salt dome, inside the mineral heart of something larger, where the heat rises and the light pulses and the sound never stops and the thirst that grew a city is still thirsty, is still growing, is still alive.
