# What Lives in the Stand

The columns live. This is obvious and yet it must be said first, because the word "plant" carries assumptions that fail here—assumptions about passivity, about patience, about the quiet vegetative tolerance of sun and season. The columns are not quiet. They groan. They click. They breathe. And somewhere in their tissue, in the dense horn-dark lignin that replaces wood as they age, there is something that might be called attention.

Not thought. The columns do not think—they have no organ for it, no centralized processing, no seat of reflection. But attention is a simpler thing. A sunflower attends to light. A vine attends to the nearest support. The columns attend to each other. When one column's groaning shifts pitch—when the cold contracts it past some threshold, when a crack forms in an outer segment—the nearest columns adjust. Their creaking changes. Their condensation patterns alter. The rhizomes beneath the rock pulse with something that is not quite signal and not quite fluid but serves both functions. This is not communication as animals understand it. It is something older, something that predates the distinction between message and metabolism.

The columns attend to the Stand itself—the totality of which they are organs. And the Stand, if it attends to anything, attends to the sky. The upper reaches of the tallest columns, lost in the perpetual dark, produce those bioluminescent blinks, and they are not random. They correlate. A light appears on one column's crown, and moments later—not simultaneously, but with a delay that implies travel, implies propagation—another appears on a column hundreds of meters away. The pattern is too slow for human eyes to easily track, too vast for human minds to easily hold. But it is there. The Stand is talking to itself about something that matters to it, in a language made of light and patience.

Perhaps it is talking about the sky. Perhaps it is talking about the dark. Perhaps it is simply saying *I am here, I am here, I am here*, and the repetition is the point.

---

The climbers have a name. They must have a name—not a human name, not a sound in any throat that could form language, but something that functions as name: a scent, a touch, a specific configuration of bristle and frost that means *you, specifically, are you*. They are social. They must be social, because they are numerous, and because they have divided the columns between them without apparent conflict, and because the young—seen only once, clinging to a larger climber's back, eyes still sealed with something that looked like resin—are tended with a care that exceeds mere instinct.

They are warm-blooded. This seems impossible at first—the cold is too absolute, too replacement-level, for any endotherm to survive without extraordinary insulation. But the climbers have solved this. Their bristle-fur, frost-covered and stiff, forms a shell—not insulating in the conventional sense, but thermally reflective, bouncing body heat back toward the skin while the frost layer on the exterior becomes part of the structure, an exoskeleton of ice that does not melt because the inner layers never allow enough heat to reach it. They wear the cold as armor. They are partly frozen at all times, and they live anyway.

Inside the columns, they carve. The holes at head-height are entrances to networks of tunnels—living architecture carved into the living tissue, smoothed by generations of paws and snouts and the constant passage of frost-laden bodies. The interior of a column is not hollow, not exactly; it is chambered, the segmented joints creating natural divisions that the climbers connect and expand. The walls weep a thick, dark sap—this is what they eat, what they feed their young, a concentrated sugar-resin that the columns produce in quantities that seem designed for harvest. Because the columns are not harmed by the carving. The tunnels heal around their edges, the sap hardening into new bark, and the relationship—whatever it is—continues.

The climbers do not build shelters. They do not need to. The columns are shelter, and food, and territory, and the boundaries between one clan's column and another's are marked with scent-glands that produce a sharp, mineral smell, like ozone, like stone struck by lightning. When two clans meet at a boundary—when a young climber strays too far or an old one forgets—the conflict is brief and mostly vocal, a chittering barrage that rises above the groaning of the Stand and then subsides, the transgressor retreating, the boundary restored.

At night, if night it can be called in the perpetual blue-gray dark, the climbers gather in their tunnels, and they make a sound that is not chittering, not the knuckle-cracking of their claws on bark, not the high thin alert-cry they use for predators. It is a low, resonant hum, produced somewhere in the chest, and when many climbers hum together inside a column, the column itself resonates, and the sound travels through the rhizome-network to other columns, and other clans, and the whole Stand vibrates with a note too low to hear but felt in the teeth, the sternum, the spaces between thoughts. This might be singing. This might be something else. The climbers do not explain.

---

The ground-runners are many species, or they are one species with many forms, or the distinction does not apply to things that live in the spaces between the world's largest organisms. They are small—none larger than a human hand—and they are fast, and they are terrified of everything, including each other.

They live in the rhizomes. Not on them, not among them—in them, having carved out tunnels and chambers in the dense matted tissue that connects the columns beneath the stone. The rhizome-network is their world: a subterranean maze of living walls and moisture-rich air, warm enough to survive, dark enough to require a different kind of sight. They navigate by touch, by the vibrations that travel through the rhizome-tissue, by the chemical traces their kind leave as they move. Their calls—the high, thin penetrations that sound like feedback—are echolocation of a sort, though what they are locating is not obstacles but each other, and the Stand's own groaning sometimes drowns their signals and leaves them lost and keening in the dark.

There are predators among them. This is the way of burrowing things—the dark creates hierarchies, and the rhizome-network has its hunters. They are larger than the runners—three or four times the mass—and they move differently, slower, deliberate, feeling for the vibration of prey through the walls of the tunnels. They have no eyes. They do not need eyes. Their bodies are long and flexible and faintly luminescent, a pale greenish glow that illuminates nothing beyond the immediate tunnel wall, and the runners flee from this glow as the runners flee from everything.

But the runners are also the reason the rhizomes persist. They eat—fungus, mostly, that grows on the rhizome walls, and the dead tissue of the rhizomes themselves, and occasionally each other—and their waste feeds the columns, a slow fertilization that cycles nutrients from the depths to the heights and back again. They are the Stand's digestive system, processing what needs processing, returning what needs returning. They do not know this. They know only the tunnel, the next meal, the pale glow that means death is coming, and the high thin calls of their kin that mean *here, here, I am here*.

---

The canopy holds things that the ground cannot name.

This is not evasion. The canopy—the space between the column-tops and the cloud-base, the perpetual dark where the bioluminescent lights blink their slow messages—is a territory so removed from the ground that its inhabitants might as well exist on another plane. The things that live there are glimpsed only in fragments: the wrong movement of a light, a sound that is not wind, the wet impact of something fallen.

What fell was a glider. This much can be determined from the remains—or could have been, if anyone had looked closely before the frost claimed it. Flat, membrane-winged, built for the air between columns as the climbers are built for the columns' surfaces. Its eyes were enormous, its mouth was a filter, and its body contained air-sacs that would have made it nearly weightless in the thin atmosphere of the upper Stand. It lived on whatever floated or flew or drifted through that dark airspace—spores, perhaps, or smaller fliers, or the bioluminescent organisms themselves. It died because a membrane tore, or an air-sac ruptured, or because the thing that hunts gliders in the canopy was faster than it was, and it fell, and it landed with a sound like a dropped fruit, and it lay on the stone between two columns and it did not move again, and the frost came, and the darkness swallowed what remained.

There are other things up there. The gliders are prey; therefore, there are predators. The bioluminescent lights sometimes move erratically, swerving, clustering, dispersing—pursuit patterns, evasion patterns, the choreography of hunt and flight at a scale that makes the ground seem static by comparison. And sometimes the columns' own lights change in response, brightening or dimming as if the Stand is watching the aerial display with something that resembles interest, or concern, or simply the automatic adjustment of a system responding to input.

The canopy-dwellers do not come to the ground. The ground is death to them—the air too thick, the cold too wet, the surfaces too solid. They belong to the space between, to the dark where the only light is made by living things, and they live and die in that element without ever touching the rock from which their world grows.

---

And then there are the ones who built the shelters.

They are gone. The shelters remain—the bark walls, the rhizome roofs, the ash pits and the tool-scrapes and the sleeping hollows carved into granite with patience and stone—and they tell a story that the Stand does not remember, because the Stand does not remember anything, because memory requires a self that the Stand does not possess.

But the story is there, in the artifacts, for anyone willing to read it.

They came from below. This is certain—they were not adapted to the altitude, not truly; their shelters speak of struggle with the cold, the thin air, the unremitting darkness. They were not climbers, not ground-runners; they walked upright on two legs, and their hands made things, and the things they made were desperate and ingenious and not quite enough.

They came because something drove them. The lower valleys—wherever those are, whatever lies in the darkness below the cliffs—became untenable. This is the only explanation for why any group would ascend to the Stand, would choose this cold, this dark, this air that fights the lungs. They were refugees. They were running from something worse.

They learned. This is the most remarkable thing—not that they came, but that they stayed, and that in staying they learned. They learned which columns bled caustic sap and which bled the sweet resin the climbers harvested. They learned to follow the climbers' tunnels, to take the resin for themselves, to eat what the Stand produced for its own purposes. They learned that the frost could be melted for water, that the rhizome-tissue could be dried and woven, that the groaning of the columns predicted the temperature shifts that would freeze them solid if they were not sheltered.

They learned to listen. The Stand's breathing, the rhythm of contraction and expansion, became their clock, their calendar, their way of marking time in a place where day and night were indistinguishable. They learned the climbers' hum and the ground-runners' calls, and they incorporated these sounds into something that might have been ritual or might have been practical knowledge encoded in the only medium available: rhythm, repetition, the pattern of sounds that meant *we are here, we are still here*.

They did not thrive. The shelters are too small, the ash-pits too shallow, the tool-marks too desperate. They survived. They survived for what must have been years—long enough to build, to rebuild, to raise children who knew nothing but the columns and the cold. Long enough to carve symbols into the bark near their shelters: spirals, lines, shapes that might be representations of the columns themselves or might be something more abstract, more inward, the expressions of minds shaped by an environment that offered nothing soft, nothing warm, nothing that resembled the world their ancestors knew.

And then they left. Or they didn't leave—they changed. This is the uncertainty at the heart of the artifacts, the question that the empty shelters and the abandoned tools cannot answer. Because some of the shelters are not empty. Some of them contain—alongside the bark-scoops and the ash-deposits and the frost that fills everything—something else. A residue. A trace. Not remains, not bones, not the material evidence of death, but something finer, something that might be the evidence of transformation.

The rhizomes grew through the floors of some shelters. This is not unusual—the rhizomes grow through everything, given time. But in these shelters, the rhizomes grew in patterns that were not random: spirals that matched the symbols on the walls, lines that connected the sleeping-hollow to the ash-pit to the entrance. As if the person who slept in that hollow, who sat by that fire, who walked through that entrance, had become part of the pattern. As if they had not died but had been *absorbed*—their substance, their warmth, their whatever-they-were, taken into the network and distributed through the Stand, cell by cell, thought by thought, until they were no longer a person but a part of the geography.

This is speculation. The evidence is thin—a pattern in root-growth, a residue in a shelter, the complete absence of human remains in a place where the cold would preserve them indefinitely. But the Stand is a single organism, and it is growing, and it is patient, and it has been here since the rock rose, and it will be here after the rock falls, and the people who built the shelters were small and fragile and made of things the Stand could use.

Perhaps they are still here. Not as themselves—not as people who think and speak and carve symbols into bark—but as something distributed, something diffused, something that moves with the sap and hums with the climbers and glows in the canopy at intervals too slow to recognize as memory. Perhaps the Stand does remember, after all. Perhaps it remembers them the way a body remembers a wound: not in words or images but in tissue, in scar, in the slight thickening where something once was cut away.

---

The Stand does not notice you. This is what you tell yourself as you walk between the columns, the frost forming on your shoulders, your breath hanging in the air and drifting away like something born. The Stand does not notice anything. The Stand is too busy being what it is—vast and cold and alive and almost entirely dark, breathing its slow continental breath, groaning its endless groan, attending to itself in a language of light and vibration and patience.

But you have seen the shelters. You have seen the patterns in the rhizomes. You have felt the hum in your sternum when the climbers sing, and you have noticed that the columns near the shelters groan at a slightly different pitch—warmer, perhaps, or closer, or something else for which you have no word. And you have wondered, standing in the blue-gray dark between two living pillars that reach beyond sight, whether the Stand's inability to notice you is a fact about the Stand or a fact about what you are—not yet, not quite—a part of it.

The cold is replacing your warmth. The darkness is replacing your sight. The sound is replacing your silence. This is what the Stand does. This is what it has always done. It is not malicious. It is not hungry in any way that implies intention. It is simply growing, simply persisting, simply being the thing that fills the space between the rock and the sky, and if you stand still long enough, if you breathe the cold air and listen to the groaning and feel the frost forming on your skin, you might begin to understand what the people who built the shelters understood, in the end, when they stopped building and let the rhizomes take them.

The Stand does not notice you.

Not yet.
