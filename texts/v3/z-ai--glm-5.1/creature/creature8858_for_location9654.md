# The Residents

The ones who stay longest develop a second skin.

Not metaphorically—or not only metaphorically. The mineral water leaves its mark. A film of calcium carbonate on the forearms and shins, the places where exposure is constant. A faint scaling at the hairline, like the crustaceans' carapaces but softer, more like the mineral crust on the walkways. The long-term residents do not wash it off. It would return by afternoon anyway, and there is something in the texture of it—they will not say protection, they will not say adaptation, but they will pause before they say anything else—that makes the condensation bead differently on their skin. Run off in cleaner channels. Keep the eyes clearer.

You understand: no one decided this. No one sat in the foreman's quarters—they still call it that, though there has been no foreman for as long as anyone can remember, only a rotation of attention, a schedule of maintenance that someone must have written down once and that now simply persists—and said, *we will become suited to this place.* It happened the way the vats happened. A structure built for one purpose, settling into another.

---

Their numbers are difficult to fix. The infrastructure suggests hundreds, and this is probably correct, but they are so practiced at absence that counting becomes a philosophical exercise. You might see three in a day. You might see seven. You might see none, and hear only the sounds of their passage—a door closing somewhere in the lower levels, the clang of a tool dropped on tile, a voice so low and brief it might be the settling of a pipe.

They do not gather. This is the first thing to understand. The dining hall in the central processing block is still functional; the equipment still heats water to a temperature that qualifies as boiling in this pressurized, mineral-thick air; the stores still hold supplies that arrive on a schedule no one seems to coordinate. But the dining hall is empty at any given hour, or occupied by one person eating quickly and leaving, or by two people sitting at opposite ends, not speaking. The quiet is not a rule posted on a wall. It is a condition of residence, like the heat, like the wet.

When they must communicate—when the pumps require attention that cannot wait, when a walkway section has given way, when the bacterial mat in vat seven has thinned past the acceptable threshold and the crustaceans are climbing the walls in greater numbers than usual—they use the intercom system. It is an old system, speakers mounted on corroded poles throughout the settlement, and it works erratically in the moisture, and this is perhaps why the messages are so brief. A location. A condition. A single word that means *come* or *fixed* or *watch.* The intercom crackles and hums when not in use, adding its own voice to the ambient B-flat of the cables, and sometimes at night—though night is also a loose concept here—the residents leave it on, the static filling the corridors like the sound of the crustaceans moving, like the sound of the mat breathing, and it is not clear whether this is oversight or comfort.

---

The children—if there are children—are even more difficult to see. There are small garments in the laundry rotation. There are marks on certain walls at certain heights that might be measurements, might be records. There are sounds, occasionally, from the lower levels where the residential quarters extend below the waterline: a pitch of vocalization higher than the adults', quicker, cut short almost before it begins. As if even the children learn early that sound is disturbance, disturbance is attention, attention draws the crustaceans, the crustaceans draw everything else.

But there are children. This is known because one of them—how long ago? three years? seven?—left a drawing on the condensation of a walkway railing, and it lasted long enough for two of the adults to see it before the next precipitation cycle erased it. The drawing showed the vats from above. The mat was rendered in careful stripes of rust and ochre and burgundy, and the crustaceans were dots along the vat edges, and there was a figure standing at one of the observation platforms. The figure was not large. The figure was watching.

This is the only record of how they see themselves that anyone from outside has encountered. And it was gone in hours.

---

They have names, presumably. They do not use them where the intercom can carry them. They refer to each other by function or location: *Pump Three. East Vats. Walkway Nine.* When they must distinguish further, they use physical descriptions delivered with a neutrality that suggests neither affection nor distaste: *the tall one at Pump Three. The one with the left hand at East Vats.* The one with the left hand is missing two fingers. This is not remarked upon. The machinery here is old and the joints are wet and sometimes things are caught. It is part of the maintenance.

What they call themselves in their own heads, in the quiet of the below-level quarters, in the intervals between the sounds of the Works breathing—this is not known. Whether they have names for each other that are not functional, not descriptive, but simply *names*—sounds that mean *you* and *I* and *us* without reference to task or position—this is not known either. The residents do not offer this information, and the visitors who come occasionally to inspect the structural integrity of the walkways or measure the output of the pumps do not ask. The questions that seem important from outside—*why do you stay, what is this place to you, what do you call yourselves*—do not seem to occur to the residents, or if they do, they are absorbed into the general quiet, the general wet, the general mineral patience that the place seems to instill.

---

Their relationship to the non-human residents is one of co-occupancy rather than stewardship. They do not feed the crustaceans. They do not cull the amphibians. They do not interfere with the birds except to repair the vent-caps when the nesting gets too dense and the airflow is compromised. The vats are not managed for the mat; the mat is tolerated, monitored, its thickness and color noted in whatever records they keep below, but it is not cultivated. It simply is, as the heat simply is, as the water simply is.

But there is a recognition—not empathy, exactly, because that would require a kind of boundary-crossing that the residents do not practice—something more like *acknowledgment.* The amphibian at the edge of vat four is not an amphibian; it is *the amphibian at the edge of vat four,* a specific presence in a specific location, as fixed in its way as the pump station or the walkway. When it moves—which it does, eventually, when the crustacean traffic shifts or the mat's breathing changes rhythm—its absence is noted, its new position noted, and the adjustment is made without comment. The Works have a geography of living things, and the residents know it the way they know the geography of pipes and walkways and vat-walls. It is all infrastructure. It is all the place.

At night—if night it is, when the brighter region of sky dims and the amber haze darkens to something closer to rust—they sleep, presumably. The sounds of the Works do not change. The pumps still turn. The mat still breathes. The crustaceans still move in their lines. But the corridors below the waterline grow quieter even than their usual quiet, and the lights that show through the small thick windows of the residential block are fewer, and the intercom static continues its crackle unheard, or heard only by the ones who cannot sleep, and there must be some of those, there must be residents who lie in the mineral heat and listen to the Works breathing around them and wonder—or not wonder, simply *wait,* the way the amphibians wait, the way the mat waits, with a patience that has no object and no end.

---

They will not tell you why they stay.

This is not because they are hostile or secretive or ashamed. It is because the question does not parse. *Stay* implies that leaving is an option that has been considered and refused, and this is not how the residents experience it. They are here the way the vats are here, the way the bacterial mat is here. They arrived at some point—everyone arrived at some point; there are personnel records in the foreman's quarters, or there were, before the moisture got to them—and then they were *of* the place, and the place was of them, and the question of leaving had the same quality as the question of leaving your own skin.

A few do leave. The visitors see them go, sometimes: a figure on the walkway that leads to the transit point, carrying less than you would expect, moving with a gait that suggests the adjustment back to dry air and clear sky will be difficult. They do not say goodbye. They do not look back. The residents who remain do not mark their departure. The schedule of maintenance is not interrupted. The pumps turn. The mat breathes.

And sometimes—not often, but sometimes—a figure arrives on the walkway from the transit point, and they are new, and they stand at the edge of the settlement with the condensation already beading on their forearms, and they look at the amber dimness and the low buildings and the vats and the insects and the birds and the water that is everywhere, always, and they do not turn back. They stand there, and they breathe the mineral air, and something in them settles. Not a decision. A recognition. The way water finds its level. The way heat finds the low places.

The residents do not welcome them. They do not orient them. They leave a door open in the residential block, and a space at the far end of the dining hall, and a schedule on the wall of the foreman's quarters, and the new one reads it, or does not read it, and finds their way to the task that is waiting, and begins.

Within a month, the scaling will start. Within three, they will have a designation: *Pump Two. West Vats. Walkway Twelve.* Within a year, they will be difficult to see, difficult to count, a presence implied by maintained equipment and footprints in the mineral crust and the sound of a door closing somewhere in the lower levels.

Within two years, they will not remember the question of leaving. Or they will remember it the way you remember a word in a language you once spoke—the shape of it, the weight, but not the meaning. Not anymore.

---

The Works breathe. The residents breathe. The mat breathes, and the crustaceans move, and the amphibians wait, and the birds drop through the insect clouds like silk sheets shaking out, and the water sheets and pools and rises and never quite evaporates, and the heat persists, and the amber dimness persists, and the pumps turn on their ceremonial flywheels, and the hum moves through everything, the B-flat that is not heard but felt, in the sternum, in the scaling on the arms, in the quiet that is not quiet but is registered as silence because it is so constant, so much the metabolism of the place, so much the sound of a system that has found its rhythm and will keep it—

And this is who lives here. Not a population. Not a community. Something less defined and more complete: an ecology that includes the human-shaped, the amphibian-shaped, the crustacean-shaped, the bacterial, the mineral, the wet. A thing that persists. A thing that breathes.

Stand on the observation platform and look. The Works stretch away in the dimness, and they are full, and they are alive, and they do not need you to understand them. They need nothing. They only continue, and the continuing is enough, and the heat comes first, and the water comes always, and the residents—quiet, scaled, peripheral, patient—move through the settlement like the crustaceans move through the walkways: in lines, with purpose, and in silence, and in keeping with a logic that was never written down because it never needed to be.

This is who lives in the Tidal Works.
