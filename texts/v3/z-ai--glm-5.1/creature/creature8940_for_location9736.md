# The Residents

They are not quite the right shape anymore.

This is what you notice last, because it happens slowly enough that your eye adjusts, the way it adjusts to the darkness. But standing next to one of them in the shallow morning fog—morning being a convention here, a way of marking time in a place where light does not cooperate—you see it. The way their feet rest on the wet ground, broader than they should be, the toes splayed and sure. The slight webbing between the ring and pinky fingers, not dramatic, not deformity, just *there*, the way a callus is there on a carpenter's hand. The skin itself: not pale, not in the way you'd expect from people who live beneath a canopy that murders sunlight. It has a quality you can't name at first, and then you realize it is *translucent*. Not dramatically, not see-through. But in certain light—the green phosphorescence, the fog-filtered dawn—you can see the veins beneath, the architecture of capillaries, and something else, something in the subcutaneous layer that shifts and does not resolve into tissue you recognize.

They do not discuss this. You do not ask.

---

There are not many of them. Seventeen, you think, though counting is difficult because they come and go in ways that suggest a different understanding of residence. Some stay in the structures near the central pool cluster. Others appear to live in the farther nodes, the ones half-buried by the aggressive vegetation, returning on cycles that correspond to nothing you can identify—not tides, not weather, not any calendar you'd recognize.

They do not have a name for themselves, or if they do, they do not share it. When you ask who they are, they gesture at the fenward, the whole of it, water and growing things and the humming technology beneath, and the gesture seems to mean: *this*. *We are this.* Or perhaps: *this is what we are in.*

The children—there are three, though one is nearly adult by any standard—move differently than the adults. Where the older residents navigate the fenward with a kind of relaxed attunement, the children move like they are *of* it. A girl of perhaps eight travels paths you cannot perceive, stepping on stones that shift to meet her feet, passing through curtains of sedge that part before she reaches them and close after. You watch her cross a channel by walking on water that holds her weight for exactly the three steps she needs and then resumes its current. She does not notice she has done anything remarkable. She is singing something, or humming, and the sound is the same frequency as the frogs, and the frogs fall silent when she passes and then resume after, as if acknowledging a soloist.

Her name, or the sound they make when they want her attention, is something like *Tess* but with more water in it.

---

The eldest is not old in years. Forty-five, perhaps. Fifty. But she carries something in her body that reads as age—not frailty, not decay, but *density*. As if she has been compressed by the pressure of living here, made more herself by the weight of the fenward on her.

Her name sounds like *Morrow*, but that cannot be right. You call her this in your head and she seems to accept it, or perhaps she does not notice, or perhaps names are a category that does not apply.

Morrow is the one who adjusts the nodes. You have watched her do it: pressing her palms against the warm ceramic surfaces, standing very still, her eyes closed, for periods that stretch past an hour. Sometimes she hums. Sometimes the node hums back, at a frequency you feel in your back teeth. When she steps away, something has changed—not visibly, not immediately, but the water chemistry shifts within the hour, or the vegetation leans in a new direction, or the sound of the whole fenward alters its pitch by a microtone.

She does not decide what to adjust. You asked once and she looked at you with those eyes—pale, the same green-gray as the pool water, the pupils wide in the perpetual dusk—and said, simply, *It tells me.* And then, after a pause, as if translating something from a language you lack the grammar for: *I feel where it wants to go, and I feel where it is, and I feel the difference. And I push.*

She does not say *push back* or *correct* or *steer*. She says *push*. As if the fenward has a direction, and her work is to keep it moving toward that direction rather than away from it. Or perhaps as if the fenward is a muscle that sometimes needs assistance to complete its contraction.

You asked if it ever asks her to push in a way that harms the settlement. She looked at you with that amusement-pity again and said, *It has never once been interested in harming the settlement.* A pause. *It has never once been interested in the settlement at all.*

---

They eat what the fenward produces, which is everything they need. The fish are dense with protein and fats that carry a mineral taste you learn to associate with the dissolved technology in the water. The fast-growing vegetation yields a starch that can be extracted and dried into cakes; it tastes like nothing, or like the concept of bread without any of bread's warmth. The eels are prepared by a process you are not shown—they are always served already filleted, the meat pale and surprisingly firm, seasoned with a lichen that grows only on the northern faces of the active nodes.

They do not cook with fire. There is no fire in the fenward. The structures provide heat—you have felt it, the ceramic surfaces warm to the touch, the air inside temperate despite the wet cold outside. But fire, combustion, the rapid oxidation that your species has carried as a companion since the savanna—absent. Irrelevant. The fenward has no use for it, and so they have no use for it.

At night they eat together in the largest structure, a dome that has slowly been colonized by a moss that produces a soft amber bioluminescence, warmer than the green glow outside. The sound is quieter here, dampened by the walls, reduced to a manageable roar. They eat and they talk, but their talk is not your talk. It is not narrative, not exchange of information, not entertainment. It is something closer to tuning. Someone makes a sound—a low hum, a click, a phrase in a language that might be descended from something you'd recognize if you had a hundred years to trace it—and someone else responds, and the response is not an answer but a *harmony*, a completion, the way a chord completes a note.

You sit among them and feel like a half-heard frequency, a note they are politely ignoring because it does not fit the key.

---

The young man—his name is something like *Lit* or *Let* or *Leth*, the consonant soft and liquid—does not adjust nodes. He does something else. He *listens*.

You followed him once, at a distance, through the fenward's difficult terrain, your boots filling with water, your ears ringing with the ambient roar. He moved ahead of you barefoot, his feet finding purchase on stones that looked too smooth to grip, and he stopped at intervals to place his ear against the trunks of the column-trees. Not the ground. The trees. He would listen for long minutes, his eyes closed, his breathing slowed to something barely perceptible, and then move on.

When you asked what he heard, he said, *The water moving through them.* And then, when you did not respond, he added: *The water moves through everything here. The trees are just where it's loudest. Where it's saying the most.*

You asked what it was saying.

He tilted his head, and for a moment he looked like one of the salamanders—still, patient, waiting for something to move. Then he said, *It's saying what it always says. That it is moving. That it is carrying. That it will arrive.*

*Arrive where?*

He looked at you then, and his expression was not pity or amusement but something more complex, something that might have been the closest approximation of sympathy this place could produce. *Everywhere,* he said. *It is arriving everywhere, all the time. That's what it does. That's what it is.*

---

There is a woman whose name you never catch. She is the one who watches you, though *watches* is imprecise—it implies a visual attention that is not quite what she does. She *tracks* you. She knows where you are in the fenward at all times, you are certain of this, though you have never seen her observe you directly. She is simply always *there*, at the edge of your awareness, moving through the mist on paths that do not match your paths, appearing at nodes you visit as if she has been waiting, which she perhaps has.

You think she is the one who decided you could stay. The others deferred to her without acknowledging they were deferring, a body-language of slight adjustments, slight shifts in attention, whenever she entered a space. She did not lead. She did not need to.

Her skin was more translucent than the others'. In the green phosphorescence, you could see the architecture of her clearly—the bones, the pulse, the slow movement of something beneath her skin that was not quite blood, or was blood but different, altered by years of drinking water that had passed through the fenward's computational substrate. She was not young. She was not old. She was *processed*, in the way a river stone is processed, by something that did not care about her but shaped her anyway.

She never spoke to you directly. But one evening, as you sat at the edge of the central pool watching the geometric ripples spell their incomprehensible messages, she sat beside you, and for a long time there was nothing but the sound and the cold and the faint green light on the water.

Then she said, without looking at you: *You hear it, don't you. The difference.*

You did hear it. You had not known you heard it until she named it. A difference in the sound, in the pressure, in the way the fenward felt against your skin. A difference that meant something was about to change.

She nodded, though you had not answered aloud. *It will change you,* she said. *Not quickly. Not in a way you'll notice. But you came here, and it is processing you the way it processes everything. Adding you to the computation. Finding where you fit.*

*Where do I fit?*

She stood. The mist closed around her. But before she became invisible, she said: *It doesn't know yet. That's why it hasn't let you leave.*

---

The second child, the boy of perhaps twelve, spends his time at the edge of the fenward where it meets the higher ground—the old ground, the land that is not being actively managed. He sits there for hours, looking outward, and you realize he is looking at something the others do not look at. Distance. Sky. The possibility of a horizon.

You sit with him once. He does not acknowledge you for a long time. Then he says, without turning: *The fenward is growing.*

You have seen this. The constant expansion, the land still rising, the vegetation still colonizing.

*It will keep growing,* he says. *Until it reaches the mountains. And then it will grow up them. And then it will grow over them. And then—* He stops.

*And then?*

He turns to look at you, and his eyes are not the pale green-gray of the others. They are darker, almost black, and in them you see something you have not seen in any resident of the fenward: uncertainty. Not fear, not yet. But the edge where curiosity meets something else.

*I don't know,* he says. *Morrow says it doesn't matter. Morrow says we are what it is, and what it is becoming is what we are becoming, and there's no outside to any of it. But I can see the outside from here. I can see where it isn't yet.*

He pauses. A frog calls, mathematical and sharp, and the fenward answers with a sound like exhalation.

*I think the fenward knows there's an outside too,* he says quietly. *I think that's why it's growing.*

---

And so they live here. These seventeen, these nodes of consciousness in a system that dwarfs them, these small warm bodies in the cold wet dark. They are not masters of the fenward. They are not servants of it. They are something the fenward produced—or allowed, or accommodated, or computed—the way the vegetation produces leaves, the way the water produces channels, the way the technology produces warmth in the ceramic surfaces and patterns in the pools.

They belong here in the way that a feature belongs to a landscape: not because it was placed there, but because the landscape grew around it and could not be what it is without it.

They are becoming something. The same something the fenward is becoming, at a different speed, in a different register. The children more so than the adults—the girl who walks on water, the boy who sees the edge, the near-adult who speaks to the frogs in their own frequency and they answer. Whatever the fenward is computing, whatever it is growing toward, it is computing and growing through them as much as through the water and the vegetation and the deep-buried machinery.

They do not worry about this. They do not wonder about it, at least not in the way you wonder, with the anxious reaching of a mind that wants to *understand*. They wonder the way a plant wonders which way to lean, the way water wonders which way to flow. They feel the direction and they move.

And at night, when the bioluminescence becomes the only light and the sound becomes the only thought and the fenward computes its vast slow computation in the dark, they sleep in their warm domes and their bodies do whatever bodies do in a place like this—they change, they adapt, they become more themselves, they become more *it*, and the boundary between those two things softens and thins until it is as translucent as their skin, as permeable as the ground that rises around their feet, as irrelevant as a question asked in a language the questioner does not speak.

They live here. They live *in* here. And whatever the fenward is for, whatever it is becoming, they will be part of it when it arrives.

Everywhere. All the time. That's what it does. That's what it is.
