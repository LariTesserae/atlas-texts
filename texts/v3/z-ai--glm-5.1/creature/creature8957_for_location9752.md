# The Inhabitants of the Weeping Plain

---

## The Films

Start with what seems simplest, though nothing here is truly simple.

The films have no name because nothing here names things, but if you watched them long enough — years, lifetimes, the span it takes a film to spread from one pool to the next — you might begin to distinguish them. Not by appearance; they all look the same in isolation, the same pale grey-white slickness on wet stone. But they behave differently. They *are* different.

The slowest film grows in the sheltered hollows where the water barely moves, where the surface is still enough that the dim light reaches the stone beneath. This film is thick — relatively speaking, as thick as a thumbnail in places — and it grows in layers, each new layer building on the residue of the old, so that the oldest patches in the deepest hollows have a stratigraphy of their own, a geological record written in protein and mineral that goes back further than anything alive should remember. When you touch it, it feels like cold jelly, yielding and dense. When you break it, it mends. Not quickly, but with a patient inevitability that suggests it has been mending itself for a very long time and sees no reason to stop now.

This is the film that luminesces most strongly. In the deep hours when the grey sky darkens toward black, the thick patches in the still pools give off a faint blue-white glow — not enough to see by, not really, but enough to make the water shimmer, enough to trace the shapes of the things that move through it. The animals are drawn to this glow. You have watched them orient toward it in the mist, adjusting their paths, seeking the brightest patches with a purpose that predates thought. Perhaps the glow signals nutrition. Perhaps it signals something else. The film did not evolve to glow; nothing here evolved in any direction at all. It simply accumulated, the way sediment accumulates, the way water accumulates, the way sound accumulates, and the glow is part of what it accumulated — some mineral reaction, some chemical inheritance from a time when the stone was different and the water was different and the whole plain was something other than what it has become.

The second film grows where the water moves. Not in the fast channels — nothing grows there, the water strips the stone clean — but in the margins, where the flow slows from a run to a seep. This film is thinner, more translucent, and it does not grow in patches but in strands, fine as spider silk, that trail across the stone in the direction of the current. It anchors at one end and streams at the other, and the moving water stretches it into veils and curtains that catch the debris the channels carry — mineral fragments, shed skin from the animals, the microscopic leavings of a million small lives that pass through the water and leave traces of their passing. The strand-film eats what it catches. Absorbs it. Incorporates it. The film that trails in the channels today contains remnants of everything that has ever flowed past it — a record not of itself but of the plain, written in the language of accumulation.

The third film is the one you notice last, because it is the least like something alive. It grows on the dry stone — or what passes for dry here, the stone that the water has recently left, the exposed surfaces that catch the mist but not the flow. This film is crystalline, a thin crust of mineral and something organic that bonds the mineral together, that builds tiny structures like frozen foam across the stone's surface. It does not grow so much as precipitate — it forms where the conditions are right, accumulates molecule by molecule, and when the water returns it dissolves, going back into solution, becoming part of the flow again until the conditions shift and it precipitates out once more. Is it alive? It reproduces itself, after a fashion — the structures it builds seed new structures nearby, and a crust that begins as a single point can spread across a whole shelf of stone in the space of a season. But it has no boundary, no self, no distinction between the part that is it and the part that is the stone. It is a process, not a creature. An event that happens when stone and water and mist meet in the right proportions and stay that way long enough.

All three films are food. All three films are the foundation of everything that moves.

## The Scrapers

The smallest animals are the ones you hear first, because their sound is the sharpest. Click-click-click-click — a rapid chittering that cuts through the water-roar like a blade, that seems designed to carry, that must carry, because the scrapers use it to find each other in the mist and the grey and the endless wet.

They are not attractive by any standard you bring to the word. Flat bodies the size and shape of your palm, with a carapace that might once have been shell but has been reduced by time and moisture to something softer — a leathery hide, pale grey-white, slick with the water that clings to everything here. Under the carapace: legs. You have counted as many as twelve on a single scraper, though the number varies, and some seem to lose and regrow legs with the same indifferent patience that the films show when mending. The legs are jointed in too many places and end in tiny hooked claws that grip the wet stone, and when a scraper runs — and they can run, alarmingly fast when startled — the sound of those claws on the rock is exactly the sound of fingernails on a slate board, amplified by a dozen legs, repeated in a staccato rhythm that you will never quite get out of your head.

The head, if it is a head, is a bony ridge that runs the width of the body, set low against the ground. Beneath the ridge: a mouth. A broad, rasping mouth lined with hard ridges that work like files, grinding the film from the stone with a methodical scraping motion that gives the creatures their clearest purpose. A scraper feeding is a scraper working — the head moves back and forth across the stone, the file-mouth abrades the film away, and what was pale slickness becomes bare rock, clean and wet and gleaming. The scrapers can strip a pool to its stone in a matter of hours, working in groups, moving in lines across the film like a slow demolition crew.

Their eyes are the unsettling part. Two of them, set wide on the ridge of the head, too large for the body by half — liquid and dark and reflective, like drops of the channel-water given shape and purpose. They see in the dimness. They must. The scrapers move through the mist with a confidence that suggests a detailed map of their surroundings, and they react to movement — your hand, a falling drop, the shadow of a larger animal — with an instant orientation that implies sharp vision in a world where nothing should see well. The eyes have a quality to them that you can only describe as *knowing* — not intelligence, exactly, but an awareness, a sense that the scraper is watching you and filing the information away and deciding, with some small cold part of its ancient brain, whether you matter.

The scrapers live short lives by any measure. They breed constantly — you see them paired in the shallow channels, the smaller mounted on the larger, the process as mechanical and unromantic as their feeding. The eggs are laid in the film itself, buried in the thickest patches where the nutrition is richest, and the young hatch already scraping, already clicking, already orienting those too-large eyes toward the dim world they have inherited. They are eaten by everything larger than themselves. They are washed away in the channels when the flow shifts. They die on the bare stone when they stray too far from the film and lack the energy to find their way back. And they are replaced, constantly, in numbers that suggest a reproductive strategy built entirely around loss — produce enough, and some will survive, and the surviving ones will produce enough, and the sound will continue.

They do not sleep. Or if they sleep, they sleep in motion, their legs still moving, their mouths still scraping, their eyes still watching, as though even rest is a risk in a world that never goes quiet and never goes dark enough to justify closing your eyes.

## The Bellowers

The mid-sized animals are harder to describe because they are harder to look at, not because they are hideous — though they are, by any human aesthetic — but because they are *wet*. Wet in a way that defeats the eye. Their hide is slick and pale and reflective, a membrane of skin over fat and muscle that shines with the water that never stops running across it, and in the dim grey light they seem less like solid creatures than like accumulations of the plain itself — shapes that the mist and water have formed, that might dissolve back into the landscape if you looked away.

They are the size of small deer, but low-slung, their legs short and thick, their bellies nearly touching the stone. The body is barrel-shaped, deep through the chest and belly, designed to hold volume — lungs, gut, the air sacs that give them their voice. The head is broad and flat, the skull barely rising above the line of the spine, and the mouth — the mouth is a device, a tool, an instrument of consumption that dominates the face like a wound. It runs the width of the head and hinges far back, opening to reveal a gullet lined with ridges not unlike the scraper's file-teeth, but coarser, designed for volume rather than precision. A bellower does not scrape film from stone. A bellower *scoops* it — takes the film and the stone beneath it and the water above it and whatever small creatures are feeding in it all at once, a shoveling, glutinous consumption that leaves the rock not just stripped but gouged, new channels carved by the pressure of that mouth and the abrasion of those teeth.

They are territorial. God, they are territorial. You learn this in the first hour, when two males — you assume they are males; the dimorphism is subtle, a thickening of the neck, a swelling of the throat — meet at the boundary of their respective ranges and begin to bellow. The sound that follows is indescribable. The air sacs in the male's throat inflate until the neck is wider than the head, wider than the body, a grotesque balloon of pale wet skin that pulses with the force of the animal's exhalation, and the sound that comes out is not a roar or a cry but a *tone*, a deep resonant note that sustains for as long as the animal can force air through its throat, that vibrates the water on the stone, that makes the pools shiver, that you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears. The males face each other across the boundary and exchange these tones — not simultaneously, but in alternation, a call and response that goes on for hours, each note slightly different in pitch and duration, each animal asserting its claim to the film and the water and the stone beneath both.

You never see them fight. The bellowing seems to resolve things. After hours of this, sometimes days, one of the males will turn and walk — not run, never run, the bellower's gait is a deliberate shuffling that moves only as fast as it needs to — back toward the center of its range, and the other will stand at the boundary with its throat still swollen and its eyes still bright and make one final low sound that might be triumph or might be relief or might be nothing at all, just air leaving a body that has spent too much of it.

The females — if females they are; you are guessing, extrapolating from difference — are quieter, but only by comparison. They move in small groups through the territory of a single male, grazing the film in a pattern that suggests routine, that suggests a route they have followed a thousand times before. They watch you with the same too-large eyes as the scrapers, but with less intensity, less *decision* — as though you are a feature of the landscape, another grey shape in the mist, another sound in the roar. You are not food. You are not threat. You are not mate. You are *weather*, as far as they are concerned, and they treat you with the indifference that weather deserves.

The young are pale replicas of the adults, already grazing, already bellowing in thin piping voices that add their own frequency to the chorus. They stay close to the females for what seems like a short adolescence — weeks, maybe, before they are fully grown and establishing territories of their own. The bellower's life is longer than the scraper's, but not long. A few seasons. Enough time to eat and bellow and mate and eat again, and then the body gives out, and the other bellower's mouths come, and the film grows over the place where the body was, and the channels carry the remainder downstream.

## The Hummers

You do not see them clearly. You are not sure anyone does.

They exist at the edge of the mist, in the deep channels where the water runs dark and the film grows thickest, in the direction the whole plain tilts — south, always south, toward the wall of grey that might be sky or might be something else. You see their shapes: vast, or vast by the standards of this place, low and long and broad, moving with a slowness that makes the bellower's shuffle seem frantic. They do not walk. They *advance*, the way a shadow advances, the way a cloud advances, with a continuous incremental presence that covers ground without seeming to move over it.

Their sound is what you know them by. The hum. A note so low it barely qualifies as sound — a vibration, a pressure, a rhythm in the air and water and stone that you feel in your skeleton, that resonates in your long bones and your spine and the wet chambers of your skull. It is not loud. It is *deep*, in the way that the plain itself is deep, in the way that time is deep, in a register that bypasses the ear and speaks directly to the tissue. When a hummer sounds, the pools shiver in concentric rings. The water in the channels ripples upstream for just an instant, fighting the flow. The stone itself seems to flex, just barely, just enough to make you wonder if you imagined it.

You think they are grazing. The deep channels where they live have the thickest film — the layered film, the ancient film, the film that has been building since before the mountains were ground away — and something that large must eat, must eat constantly, must move through the film and consume it in the quantities that such a body demands. But you never see them feed. You see only the aftermath: channels stripped to bare stone, the film not just scraped or scooped but *excavated*, removed down to a depth that the other grazers cannot reach, the layered residue of ages taken in a single slow pass. And then the hummer moves on, and the film begins to grow back, and the scrapers come to pick at the margins, and the bellower's move in to graze the new growth, and the cycle continues.

How many are there? You cannot say. The mist defeats counting. You have seen three distinct shapes at one time, moving in different directions through the deep channels, but the sound they make is so similar in pitch and timbre that it might be the same voice reflected and refracted by the wet air, the same presence multiplied by the acoustics of a landscape that has been shaped by water and sound for longer than anything has been shaped by anything. There might be dozens. There might be hundreds, out there in the grey, advancing in patterns too large and too slow for you to recognize, following channels you have not found, eating film you will never see.

At night — or what passes for night, when the grey goes dark and the film glows blue-white in the pools — the hum grows louder. Or you become more aware of it. Or the hummers move closer to the surface, drawn by the light, by the thickest patches of glowing film, by some signal in the water that you cannot read. In those hours the sound is everywhere, a continuous bass note that seems to come from the stone itself, that makes the shelter vibrate around you, that presses against your body like a hand. You lie on the wet stone floor and feel the hum in your back and your legs and your skull, and you think: *this is what the plain sounds like when it speaks its own name. This is what the world sounds like when it forgets that anyone is listening.*

You do not sleep. Not really. You drift, and the hum carries you, and the sound becomes a landscape of its own — a territory of vibration where the boundaries between your body and the water and the stone and the hummer's voice dissolve into something that might be rest or might be surrender or might simply be what it feels like to be part of a world that has been finished for a very long time and has no need of you beyond the small contribution your warmth makes to the film.

## The Ones Who Built

They are here still. You learn this on the third day, when you find a shelter that is not empty.

Not occupied — not in the way you mean when you say a place is occupied, with a fire and a cache of food and the signs of daily life. But *maintained*. The gaps between the stones have been cleared of debris. The channel that runs beside the shelter has been deepened, the edges chipped and smoothed to direct the flow. There is fresh cord on the floor, still supple, and a pile of shell-hooks sorted by size, and a scrap of hide stretched on a frame of bone — scraping tools, you realize, scraping tools for the film, for harvesting it the way the animals do, but with hands instead of mouths.

They are here, and they are shy.

You do not see them for another two days after finding the shelter. You see traces — a footprint in the wet silt beside a channel, a shadow at the edge of your sight that resolves into mist when you turn, a pile of film scraped from stone and gathered into a heap that would make no sense unless something had gathered it. They watch you. You know they watch you the way the scrapers watch you — with too-large eyes, with a calculation that weighs threat against interest, with the patience of something that has lived in this landscape long enough to know that most things are not worth running from and almost nothing is worth fighting.

When you finally see them, it is because they allow it.

There are two. A larger one and a smaller one, and you do not know if that means parent and child or mated pair or something else, some pairing that your categories do not accommodate. They are not like you. They are not like anything you would have imagined.

Their skin is grey. Not pale grey, not the white-grey of the animals — a deeper grey, the grey of wet stone, of the plain itself, as though they have absorbed the color of the landscape into their bodies over the generations until they and the rock are the same color and the same wetness and the same patience. They are small — shorter than you, thicker through the torso, with limbs that seem slightly too long for their bodies. Their hands are wide and flat, the fingers broad and calloused, and the nails are thick, almost claw-like, the nails of something that has spent its life working stone. Their feet are broader still, splayed, gripping the wet rock with a sureness that you cannot match no matter how carefully you place your own feet.

Their faces are the hardest part. The eyes are large — of course they are large; everything here has large eyes; the dimness demands it — but set differently than the animals' eyes, closer together, forward-facing, beneath a brow ridge that shadows them and gives them a hooded quality that might be suspicion or might simply be the shape of a skull that has been shaped by the landscape it inhabits. The nose is broad and flat, the nostrils wide, flaring when they breathe, and they breathe through the nose more than the mouth — you realize after watching that they are *tasting* the air, reading the water, smelling the film and the animals and the mineral content of the channels, navigating by scent in a world where vision serves only at close range. The mouth is small. Smaller than you expect. They do not bellow. They do not click. When they speak — and they do speak, you hear them, a low murmuring that is almost beneath the threshold of the water-roar — their voices are quiet, calibrated to carry just far enough, pitched to the frequencies that the plain does not claim.

They are wearing the hide of a bellower. You realize this slowly, the way you realize everything here — by accumulation, by letting the details build until the pattern emerges. The hide has been scraped thin and treated with something — mineral, maybe, or concentrated film — until it is flexible and water-resistant, and it is wrapped around their bodies in simple layers that protect the skin from the constant wet. They have also wrapped their feet in scraps of the same material, and the wrappings make their footprints distinct — you realize you have been seeing those prints for days and mistaking them for the marks the animals leave in the silt.

The smaller one carries a bag made from hide, and the bag is full of film. Harvested film. Scraped from the stone with tools and stored and carried, and you understand suddenly that this is what they eat — not the living film, not the fresh growth, but the film that has been processed somehow, rendered into something more nutritious or more portable or more lasting. They have *agriculture*, after a fashion. They have a relationship with the film that goes beyond grazing. They have learned to take it and treat it and keep it, and this — this small innovation, this single step between eating and farming — is what has allowed them to build shelters and stay in one place and watch you with eyes that hold something more than animal wariness.

They hold *judgment*. They are deciding what you are.

The larger one speaks. A single word, or what you take to be a word — a sound that is not like the click of the scrapers or the bellow of the grazers or the hum of the vast things in the deep channels. It is a sound shaped by a throat that can shape sounds, that has a language of shaped sounds, that lives in a world where communication is possible because the roar of the water has been answered by voices that refuse to be drowned.

You do not understand the word. You shake your head. The smaller one makes a different sound — shorter, sharper, and the larger one responds with something that might be agreement or might be resignation, and they both turn and walk away from you, toward the shelter, toward the channel, toward the life they have made in the wet and the grey and the roar.

They do not invite you. They do not forbid you. They walk, and you can follow or not, and either way they will continue as they have continued, eating the film and building the shelters and speaking their quiet words beneath the sound of the weeping plain, which was here before them and will be here after and does not care one way or the other whether they share their shelters with strange soft things that stumble on the wet stone and flinch at the cold and cannot read the water by smell the way a person should.

You follow. Of course you follow. There is nothing else to do, in a world that has finished becoming itself, except to follow the water and the film and the animals and the people downstream, always downstream, toward the grey wall at the edge of the world, where the sound is loudest and the mist is thickest and the hum of the vast things in the deep channels becomes so constant that it is indistinguishable from silence — which is, you begin to understand, exactly what silence is, in a place that has never known it: not the absence of sound, but the total presence of it, so complete that it becomes the air itself, the water itself, the stone itself, the ground on which you walk and the sky beneath which you walk and the life that watches you with too-large eyes and decides, with the patience of the ancient and the wet, whether you belong here too.
