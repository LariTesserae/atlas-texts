What lives here has never known interruption.

---

The flat swimmers — call them what you will; they have no name for themselves, and the category "swimmer" is itself a kind of lie, since swimming implies a destination, and they have none — the flat swimmers are the chamber's elders. Not as individuals. No single body persists long enough. But the *form* persists. The shape: that broad, undulating surface, eyeless and dense with chemical receptors, has been passing through this water for longer than the water has been warm. It was designed — if design can happen without a designer — for a different chamber, a different chemistry, a different age. It carries its history in its body. The broad surface is a sail first and a feeding apparatus second; the organism rides the convection currents that cycle through the chamber on their slow, hours-long rotation, and it filters as it goes, absorbing the particulate soup through membranes that cover its underside. But the riding is the point. The feeding is secondary. The movement is the thing: the gentle rise as warm water lifts it from the floor, the patient drift across the chamber's dark volume, the slow settling as the current cools and it descends toward the walls again. This circuit — up, across, down, up, across, down — is not merely behavior. It is the organism. Remove the circuit and the flat swimmer does not exist. It does not eat to live; it lives to move. The metabolism serves the motion, not the other way around.

They are not solitary. They do not school. The word is *aggregate*. They accumulate in the thermals, riding the same columns of warm water, their bodies sometimes overlapping at the edges, pressing against each other without acknowledgment or avoidance. When two meet, they exchange chemical information: the state of their internal water, the concentration of particulates they've ingested, the presence or absence of the microorganisms that colonize their surfaces. They do not act on this information in any visible way. They do not change course or adjust their feeding. The exchange simply occurs, and the swimmers continue their circuits, each now carrying a trace of the other — a molecular memory that will fade within hours but that, for a time, makes each slightly less alone in the dark.

Some of them carry passengers. A species of small, many-legged creature — nothing like a shrimp, though the comparison is tempting; the legs are too numerous, the body too soft, the movement too fluid — makes its home on the flat swimmers' upper surfaces. The many-legged things cannot survive in the open water. They require a substrate. The flat swimmers are the only substrate large enough and stable enough in the chamber's central volume. They cling with adhesive pads at the tips of their legs, and they feed on the microorganisms that colonize the swimmers' skin, and when a swimmer settles near the wall to wait for the next thermal, some of them detach and reattach to the stone, joining the layered communities there. Others stay. Some stay for generations — the many-legged things reproduce on their swimmers, giving birth to young who have never touched stone, who will never touch stone, who will live their entire short lives on the back of something that moves through darkness without purpose or direction.

The many-legged things are the chamber's storytellers, though they do not know this. They carry chemical traces from every surface they contact: the mineral signatures of different walls, the biological markers of different filter-feeder colonies, the unique chemical dialect of each flat swimmer they've ridden. A single many-legged body contains a compressed map of the chamber's ecology, written in proteins and lipids, unreadable by anyone but another many-legged thing. When they meet — on a swimmer's back, or on the wall, or in that brief dangerous moment of detachment and reattachment — they exchange these traces, and each walks away carrying a slightly more complete picture of the world they inhabit. They do not use this picture. It does not guide their behavior. But it accumulates, generation after generation, in the chemical structure of their bodies, a library that no one will ever read, a map of a place that has no outside.

---

The filter-feeders on the walls have no selves.

This is not metaphor. The encrusting organisms — the soft, pale, coral-like things that anchor themselves with chemical adhesives and extend feathery feeding structures into the current — they are individuals in the narrowest sense: separate bodies, separate feeding apparatuses, separate deaths. But they do not experience individuation. They do not distinguish between self and neighbor. Their chemical receptors detect food and not-food, warm and cold, the presence of the grazers that crop them. They do not detect the boundary between their own tissue and the tissue of the organism growing beside them. In places where two colonies meet, they merge. The feathery structures intertwine. The feeding becomes cooperative. The chemical signals of one colony flow into the other, and for a time — sometimes hours, sometimes years — the two operate as a single body, sharing resources, coordinating the rhythm of their feeding, pulsing in unison to the slow convection current. Then something shifts — a change in water chemistry, a grazer's bite that severs the connection, a death at the boundary — and they separate again, each continuing as if the merger had never occurred.

They do not remember merging. They do not miss it. They have no mechanism for memory, no structure that could hold it. But the merger changes them nonetheless. The chemical signals they exchange leave traces — new proteins synthesized in response to the contact, new receptor configurations shaped by the neighbor's molecular dialect. After separation, each organism is slightly different than before: more receptive to the other's chemical language, more likely to merge again if they meet, more capable of cooperation. The change is small. It does not accumulate in any direction. But it means that no filter-feeder is ever exactly the same twice. The community on the walls is in constant chemical conversation, and the conversation changes all the participants, slowly, imperceptibly, without purpose or progress.

The grazers know this. The snail-like things that move across the filter-feeder layer, cropping the feathery structures with radulas that have been grinding stone for millennia — they know, in the only way a grazer can know, that some patches of the wall taste different. Some patches are richer, denser, more nutritious. These are the patches where merger has occurred, where the cooperative feeding has produced thicker growth, where the chemical conversation between colonies has created something the grazers can sense but cannot name. The grazers prefer these patches. They return to them, navigating by chemical trails they themselves leave behind, their mucous paths marking the wall like the carvings beneath — deliberate, meaningful, unreadable.

The grazers and the filter-feeders are not enemies. The grazing stimulates growth. A cropped colony regrows denser, more vigorous, more attractive to the next grazer. The relationship has been running for longer than either species has existed in its current form. The ancestors of the grazers cropped the ancestors of the filter-feeders in a different chamber, in a different sea, in a world that might as well be a different planet. The specifics have changed. The relationship has not.

---

There are creatures in the chamber that live too fast to see.

The microorganisms — the thermophilic bacteria and archaea that form the base of the food web, drawing energy from the geothermal warmth that seeps through the carved stone — they reproduce every few hours. A single cell divides, divides again, divides again. By the time a flat swimmer completes one circuit of the chamber, the microbial landscape has turned over completely. What was a thriving colony is now exhausted, depleted, dying; what was bare stone is now thick with new growth. The flat swimmer does not notice. The grazer does not notice. Their timescales do not overlap.

But the chamber notices. Or rather: the chamber *is* the noticing. The microbial turnover drives the chemistry of the water. It produces the particulate soup that feeds the filter-feeders that feed the grazers that feed the many-legged things that colonize the flat swimmers. The entire ecosystem rides on a foundation of life that lives and dies in hours, that turns over so quickly it appears stable, the way a spinning wheel appears solid. The stability is an illusion produced by speed. The chamber is not in equilibrium. The chamber is in constant, invisible, hourly revolution.

And beneath the microbes, in the stone itself, there is something else. Not life — the stone is too hot, too mineral, too dense for anything that could be called alive. But something. A pattern in the crystal structure, a regularity that does not match the surrounding rock. It was there before the carvers came. The carvers found this place because of it — because the stone here was different, because the pattern in the crystal drew their attention, because something about the warmth and the texture and the way the rock held shape spoke to whatever they were when they first began to make. They carved the chamber to match what they found. They did not know they were doing this. They thought they were creating. They were uncovering.

The pattern does not care. It is not alive, not aware, not purposeful. It is a consequence of the geothermal processes that heat the water, the mineral composition of the stone, the pressure of the water above. But it persists. It has persisted since before the carvers, before the water, before the mountain. It will persist after the chamber wears smooth, after the warmth fades, after the last flat swimmer settles to the floor and does not rise again. It is the oldest thing in the chamber, and it does not know it is old.

---

What lives in the Drowned Oratory lives in layers.

There is the microbial layer, where life burns fast and chemical and invisible, where the food web begins in heat and dies in hours, where stability is speed and speed is stability.

There is the wall layer, where filter-feeders merge and separate and merge again, where grazers leave mucous trails that echo the carvings beneath, where the boundary between organisms is suggestion rather than law.

There is the open-water layer, where flat swimmers ride the slow thermals and many-legged things ride the swimmers, where chemical traces map a world no one will ever read, where movement is existence and existence is movement.

And there is the stone layer, where the pattern holds, where the carvings soften, where the warmth rises from something older than life, older than water, older than the concept of a chamber or an oratory or a purpose.

The creatures do not know they live in layers. They do not know they live in a chamber. They do not know that the walls were carved, that the carvings meant something, that the meaning is lost, that the loss is not mourned. They know warmth and food and the pressure of other bodies. They know the pulse of blue-green light in the darkness. They know the slow breath of the water cycling through, the geothermal exhalation, the rhythm that is older than any of them and will outlast all of them.

They do not know they are the end of a history. They do not know they are a completion. They are simply here, in the dark, in the warm, in the thick broth of a drowned oratory that has become exactly what it was always going to become: a room full of life that does not need to understand the room, living inside a monument that does not remember being one.
