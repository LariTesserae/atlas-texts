# The People of the Mesa Apalala

They do not call themselves anything.

Or rather, they did, once—a word that meant *the ones who are here*, which is to say, *the ones who are*, which is to say, *people*. But the word has been spoken so many times across so many generations that it has worn smooth like everything else on the mesa, has become a sound without sharp edges, a murmur that merges with the beetle-drone and the wind, and now when they use it—which is rarely, because there is no one else to distinguish themselves from—it carries the same weight as breathing. A function. Not a name.

---

Their bodies have become the mesa's body.

Not quickly. Not in any single generation. But the way water takes the shape of its container, the way roots take the shape of the cracks they grow through—the people have taken the shape of this place. They are small. Not stunted, not malnourished, simply *compact*, as if the density of life on the mesa has pressed them into a more efficient form. The women average perhaps a hundred and forty centimeters; the men slightly more. Their frames are narrow but dense, their bones thick for their size, heavy with the calciumite that passes through every root and vine and, through these, into every body. When an elder dies and is prepared for the floor—the mesa's floor, the mineral floor, to which they are returned—the bones are notably heavy. The children know this. They heft the clean bones of their grandparents and feel the weight of the mesa inside them.

Their skin has changed. Not dramatically—there has been no sudden mutation, no single gene asserting itself—but across centuries, across the slow press of dim light and mineral diet and the particular quality of air that sits two hundred meters above the eroded world, their skin has become something other than what it was. Pale, yes, but not the pallor of illness or deprivation. A living paleness. The color of the moss-mats in deep shade. The color of new rope-vine before it hardens. A grey-green undertone that deepens in the afternoon light and fades to near-translucence in the dim, and in the very old, becomes almost the color of the hardpan itself—as if the body, having spent its years in contact with the stone, is slowly taking on its qualities.

Their eyes are large. This is the first thing a stranger would notice, if a stranger could ever arrive: the eyes, occupying more of the face than seems right, the irises pale and wide, the pupils dark pools that rarely contract fully even in the brief afternoon sun. They do not blink as often as other people blink. They do not need to. The dim has trained their eyes to gather whatever light exists, to hold it, to make use of it, and in the process the eyes have become something closer to the eyes of the moths that arrive each dusk—soft, dark-adapted, easily overwhelmed by brightness. The elders, who remember more afternoons than the young, sometimes wear strips of fiber-cloth across their brows to block the sun when it comes. Not over their eyes. Over their brows. They have learned that the light enters from above, from the pale sky they rarely look at, and that a barrier there is enough.

Their hands are extraordinary.

This is what they would tell you, if they could tell you anything about themselves—not that their eyes are large, not that their skin has changed, but that their hands *know*. The hands of the mesa-dwellers are broad and flat, the fingers thick, the pads of the fingertips wide and ridged with prints so deep they can be seen from a distance, whorls and loops like the growth-rings of the succulents. A child of twelve can distinguish by touch alone the age of a rope-vine fiber to within a season, can run her fingers along a wall and tell you which blocks were cut by her grandmother's grandmother and which were cut last month, can feel the particular vibration of a drum-beetle through three meters of stone and know whether it is healthy or sick, male or female, ready to mate or near death.

This is how they navigate. Not by sight—the settlement is too dense, too overgrown, too layered for sight to be reliable. Not by sound alone, though sound is a constant companion. But by *touch*, by the feel of each surface, each fiber, each moss-covered edge. Every structure has its texture, its particular combination of rough block and soft pad and worn threshold. Every path has its signature—the give of a certain roof, the slip of a certain stone, the brush of a certain vine at shoulder-height. The people know the settlement the way a weaver knows cloth: not as a picture seen from outside, but as a texture lived from within, a fabric of contacts that the fingers read as fluently as any written language.

The very old—those who have walked these paths for seventy years, eighty—no longer need to touch at all. They feel the settlement through their feet, through the vibrations in the hardpan, through the subtle shifts in air pressure as they move from open space to enclosed. They walk quickly in the dim, unhesitating, their bodies navigating by a proprioception so refined it has become a form of hearing, a way of perceiving the shape of the settlement through the body's own awareness of itself in space.

---

They do not count time the way other people count time.

Or rather, they do—they are not simple, not primitive, not lost in an eternal present—but their counting is different. They count in roofs. A child is born in the year the third roof of the old curing-house finally touched the ground, or in the year the new platform was built above the fungal chambers, or in the year the moss took the southern wall of the gathering-space so thickly that the door had to be cut wider. Events are located not by number but by growth, not by position on a line but by the accumulation of vegetable mass, the slow piling of living material on living material that constitutes the settlement's only reliable calendar.

They know their ages, approximately. An elder will say she has seen nine roofs sag and four new structures raised, which might mean sixty years or seventy—no one can be certain, because the rate of sag depends on the thickness of the vine, the humidity of the seasons, the weight of the goats. But the uncertainty does not trouble them. What does it matter, exactly how many times the fog has risen and the moths have come? What matters is that she was a child when the old loom-house was still standing, and a young woman when it became a hill, and a mother when her daughter built a new room on top of it, and an old woman when her granddaughter spread fiber-cloth on that same roof-hill to catch the evening moisture. The years are not points on a line. They are layers. They are sediment. They are the slow accumulation of a life pressed down into the life of the place.

---

They speak softly.

Not because they are quiet people—some of them are loud, argumentative, given to sudden laughter that startles the goats and scatters the moths. But their voices have adapted to the acoustics of the mesa, where every surface is hard and every wall is close and the drum-beetles provide a constant background against which speech must compete without overwhelming. They have learned to pitch their voices low, to speak from the chest rather than the throat, to let the sound resonate through the dense air and the moss-mats and the fiber-curtains until it reaches its listener not as a sharp signal but as a warm presence, a pressure, a meaning that arrives the way fog arrives: slowly, from all directions at once.

Their language reflects this. The words are long, by the standards of languages shaped by open spaces—multi-syllabled, vowel-heavy, with consonants that soften and blend at the edges. A word for the particular quality of light that comes through fog-gauze in late morning is *ahalanu*, six syllables that take three seconds to speak, that rise and fall in pitch like the beetle-rhythm, that cannot be shouted because shouting would compress the vowels and lose the meaning. A word for the feeling of recognizing a surface by touch that you have not touched since childhood is *yeskalan*, and it is spoken slowly, with the same careful deliberation a weaver uses when returning to a pattern set aside long ago.

They have many such words. Words for gradients of moisture, for textures of stone, for the ages of moss and the moods of goats and the particular sound a roof makes when it is beginning its final sag. Words for the unnamed things in the cracks—never spoken above a whisper, these, not because the things might hear but because the sounds feel wrong at volume, feel like they belong to the deep mineral silence, the pre-voiced silence of the hardpan itself. Words for the dead, which are the same words used for the living but spoken with a different breath-shape, a slight flattening of the vowels that signals absence without stating it, that allows the dead to remain present in the language without occupying the space of the living.

They do not write. There is no material suited to writing—no clay that holds ink, no fiber that takes a mark without disintegrating, no surface smooth enough and dry enough and accessible enough to serve as a record. What they know, they hold. The old women who chip at stones are not writing; they are remembering, working the memory through their hands the way a person might work dough, shaping it, finding its form, letting the rhythm of the chipping bring back what needs to be brought back. Each stone they shape is a specific memory—not a symbol of the memory but the memory itself, encoded in the angle of the cut, the depth of the groove, the particular way the edge flakes away under the tool. No one else can read these stones. They are not meant to be read by anyone else. They are a conversation between the hand and the past, a way of keeping the past warm and present and alive, the way the moss keeps the walls warm, the way the fog keeps the air alive with moisture that is almost but not quite rain.

---

They love each other densely.

There is no other way. Forty meters across, two hundred people, and every surface shared, every wall shared, every breath shared with the person sleeping arm-length away and the person curing fiber below and the person chipping stone above. Privacy on the mesa is a concept without a referent. There are no doors that close—only curtains of fiber and vine that mark transitions without blocking sound or smell or the awareness of another body on the other side. There are no private rooms—only alcoves, shelves, depressions in the moss where a body can curl and be partially hidden, partially alone, which is the most alone anyone can be.

They have adapted to this. Not by becoming indifferent to each other—by becoming *skilled* at each other. They read moods the way they read surfaces: by touch, by subtle shifts in pressure and warmth and the particular rhythm of a person's breathing as they pass in the dim corridor. They know when to speak and when to remain silent. They know when a hand on a shoulder is wanted and when it would be an intrusion. They know the particular silence that means *I am grieving* and the particular silence that means *I am thinking* and the particular silence that means *I am simply here, and that is enough.*

Their loves are not exclusive. This is not a matter of ideology or principle; it is a practical consequence of living so close, for so long, with so few options. A woman may spend her life partnered with one man, sharing his alcove, raising their children in the space between their bodies—but she will also have loved the woman who taught her to weave, and the man who brought her moth-paste when she was sick, and the child who was not hers but who crawled into her lap one evening and fell asleep and became, from that moment, partially hers, in the way that things on the mesa become owned: slowly, through contact, through the accumulation of shared warmth and time.

There is no word for *family* in their language. There is a word—*tessara*—that means *the people whose walls touch yours*, and this is the closest equivalent. Your tessara are the ones you hear through the stone when they shift in their sleep. The ones whose cooking you smell, whose arguments you witness, whose children you catch when they fall from the roof-edges. The ones you will be buried beneath when you die, because the floor of the mesa is the floor of their home too, and your bones will join the heavy calciumite bones of all the tessara who have gone before, pressing down through the moss, through the roots, through the slow sediment of the dead becoming the ground on which the living stand.

---

They do not fear death.

This is not the same as welcoming it. They fight to live—fiercely, stubbornly, with the same patient tenacity with which the roots fight downward through the hardpan. When illness comes—and it comes rarely, because the isolation of the mesa protects them, but when it comes—they gather around the sick one, they bring moth-paste and water caught on leaves, they sit in the dim and wait, and their waiting is a form of fighting, a refusal to let go that is itself a kind of strength.

But when death comes, they do not fight it. They have watched too many things die on the mesa—not just people but goats and moths and the slow dying of roofs and the slower dying of walls, the way a structure settles into its final form by ceasing to be what it was. Death is not an ending here. It is a transition, a shift from one kind of presence to another. The dead are not gone. They are *in the walls now*. They are in the weight of the stone and the density of the moss and the particular vibration of the hardpan when the drum-beetles sync their rhythm. They are in the calciumite that the roots draw up, that the vines bear in their new growth, that the goats eat and the people eat and the children grow from. The dead are not absent. They are *distributed*. They have become the place itself, the way a sound becomes the silence that follows it, the way the fog becomes the moisture in the leaves.

The burial is simple. The body is carried to the mesa's edge—not the outer edge, where the hardpan drops into nothing, but the inner edge, where the settlement meets the mineral pavement, where the cracks go deep and the unnamed things wait in their narrow homes. The body is wrapped in fiber-cloth and laid in a crack that has been prepared, that has been offered to the things inside, that has been accepted—because the things do not always accept, and when they do not, the body must be taken to another crack, and another, until one is found that will receive it. Then the offering is made: fiber-paste, and a stone shaped by an old woman, and the silence that is the sound of respect. Then the body is left. What happens after, no one knows. The things in the cracks are private. They take what is given and return nothing visible, and the people do not ask, because asking would be a violation of the courtesy that has kept the peace between the two peoples—the surface people and the crack people, the ones who walk in dim air and the ones who live in mineral darkness—for as long as anyone can remember.

---

They do not wonder about the world below.

This is perhaps the hardest thing for a stranger to understand—and there are no strangers, have been no strangers for longer than the oldest roof has been sagging, but if there were, this is what would confuse them most. The people of the mesa do not look outward. They do not stand at the edge and wonder what lies beyond the fog, what countries might exist in the eroded lowlands, what other peoples might live and breathe and speak in languages of their own. The scarab-goats do this—stand at the edge and stare outward—and the people watch them do it with a kind of gentle pity, the way one might watch a child reach for something that is not there.

The world below is not unknown to them. They know it exists. They can feel its warmth rising each dusk, can smell its mineral breath, can hear—very faintly, on the quietest afternoons—sounds from below that might be wind or might be water or might be something they have no word for. But they do not go down. They cannot go down. The sides of the mesa are sheer, unclimbable, and even if they were not, there is nowhere to go *to*—the soft stone that once surrounded the mesa has been gone for eons, worn to nothing, and what remains is a gap of two hundred meters between the mesa-top and the lowlands, a gap that cannot be bridged by rope-vine or wing or wish.

And so they do not wonder. Not because they have suppressed the wondering, not because they have made a collective decision to focus inward—but because the wondering has atrophied, has gone the way of the word for *elsewhere*, has worn smooth and then worn away, like a path that no one walks anymore until the moss covers it and it becomes indistinguishable from the ground on either side. They are here. They have always been here. The mesa is not a prison; it is a body, and they are the life inside it, the movement and the warmth and the sound that means something is still alive, still holding on, still pressing inward against the mineral silence that surrounds them.

---

Once, a child asked her grandmother what was beyond the fog.

The grandmother was chipping a stone—a memory-stone, working through some recollection that had come to her hands during the night. She did not look up. She let the rhythm of the chipping fill the space between the question and the answer, and when she spoke, her voice was low and warm and carried no impatience, no sadness, only the gentle certainty of someone who has been asked this before, many times, by many children, and who knows that the answer is not a destination but a direction.

"Beyond the fog is the place where the fog comes from," she said. "It is the place that sends us warmth and moisture and the moths. It is the place the roots are reaching toward."

"Have you ever seen it?" the child asked.

"I have seen the fog," the grandmother said. "I have felt the warmth. I have eaten the moths. That is enough."

The child was not satisfied, but she was patient—children on the mesa learn patience early, the way children elsewhere learn to walk or to talk—and she sat with her grandmother in the dim, and she listened to the beetles, and she felt the hardpan beneath her through the thin soles of her feet, and slowly, without realizing it, the question dissolved. Not answered. Not suppressed. Simply *absorbed*, the way the fog is absorbed by the leaves, the way the sound is absorbed by the moss, the way everything on the mesa is finally absorbed into everything else, until the boundary between question and answer, between self and place, between the living and the dead, becomes as thin and permeable as the membrane of a moth's wing, and the child is no longer someone who lives *on* the mesa but someone who lives *as* the mesa, a small bright pulse in the dense and breathing mass, a note in the chorus that never stops, that has never stopped, that will never stop as long as there is stone to stand on and fog to drink and the patience to wait for whatever the roots are reaching for, deep down, in the mineral silence where the old things live, where the dead go, where the sound of the drum-beetles finally arrives after its long slow journey through the cracks, and is received.
