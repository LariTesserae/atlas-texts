# What Lives on the Assembled Plateau

---

The first and truest answer is: nothing. The plateau told you this itself. No insects. No birds. No worms. The absence is total and deliberate—not a gap in colonization but a refusal. The mat does not welcome guests. It does not support tenants. It is not a habitat. It is the inhabitant.

But this is not the whole answer, because you asked what lives there, and the plateau lives, and nothing that lives does so alone.

---

Start with what you can almost see. The water that runs down every tower-shaft is not clean. It carries color—faint yellows, pale greens, sometimes a milky opacity that swirls and separates as the streams merge and divide. This is not sediment. This is the mat's blood, and it is thick with cells. Single-celled organisms by the billions, by the trillions, flowing through the vascular channels as they flow through you—not passengers but components. They are what make the water rise. They are what break down the old layers into heat. They are what fix the nitrogen and dissolve the minerals from the stone the mat has swallowed. The plateau has a microbiome the way you have one, and it is no more separate from the place than your gut flora is separate from you.

Are they inhabitants? Are your own bacteria inhabitants? The question degrades upon examination. They are the small life that makes the large life possible. They are the plateau's interior.

But there are others that are not so easily categorized.

---

The mat has depth. This is something you cannot see from the surface but must infer, must feel through your feet as the ground shifts and settles. The top layer—the woven rhizomes you walk on—is thin. An inch, perhaps two. Below that, the plant becomes denser, darker, wetter. The living tissue thins and the dead tissue thickens, and the dead tissue is being consumed from within by the same processes that generate the heat, and below that—at a depth of perhaps six feet, perhaps ten—the material becomes something else entirely.

It becomes peat. It becomes a compressed stratum of former plant-matter so dense and so saturated that it behaves like a gel. You cannot dig into it; you would need a blade, and the blade would meet resistance unlike soil or wood—something elastic, something that pushes back. This is the plateau's substrate, its foundation, and it is not inert. The peat-layer is alive in the way that a reef is alive: built by life, composed of former life, occupied by life, generating conditions for more life. Fungal hyphae thread through it—miles of them, continents of them—white and branching and so fine that a cubic inch might contain a mile of filament. They are not the same species as the towers. They are not the same kingdom. They are something older, something that was here before the mat arrived, something that the mat absorbed rather than displaced.

The fungi do not fruit. They have no reason to. There is no wind to carry spores, no animals to carry them, no need to reproduce sexually when the mat provides an endless substrate for vegetative growth. They simply extend—cell by cell, hypha by hypha—through the peat, through the decomposing layers, through the living rhizomes themselves. They are parasites, technically. Commensals, perhaps. Symbionts, possibly. The mat has not evolved a defense against them, which means either it cannot or it does not need to. Perhaps the fungi serve a function. Perhaps they are the reason the dead layers decompose so quickly, the reason the heat is so intense, the reason the mat does not suffocate beneath its own waste.
