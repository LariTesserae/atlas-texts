# What Lives Here

---

The mats live here.

This is not metaphor. The gray-green pads that cling to the surface—calling them "vegetation" was always a way of making them familiar, folding them into a category they don't quite fit. They are not plants. Or rather, the distinction between plant and thing-that-is-simply-alive has not been established here yet, and the mats do not seem interested in choosing a side.

Watch one long enough and you begin to understand. They do not grow the way growth happens elsewhere—not by division at meristems, not by the steady accumulation of cells in the directional confidence of a stem or a root. They *spread*. The edge of a mat advances a millimeter a day, sometimes less, pressing itself against the composite like a tongue testing a surface for flavor. Where the surface is warmer, they thin out, almost retreating; where the seams allow moisture to pool, they thicken, layering over themselves in slow, accretive spirals. They are reading the floor. They are mapping it, in the only way they can, by covering it.

Lift one and you feel the reluctance. Not resistance—the mat has no strength, no fibers to grip with—but a kind of adhesion, a commitment to contact that goes beyond simple physics. The underside of a mature mat is slightly different from the top: darker, more opaque, threaded with fine channels that aren't quite veins and aren't quite roots but serve some analogous function. They draw something from the composite. Not minerals in any recognizable form—the surface material seems largely inert, a closed system of whatever it was made from—but something. A property. An energy or a chemical signature or a tendency that the mats have learned to metabolize.

What they excrete is the faint discoloration you see where a mat has lifted away. A residue, pale and slightly chalky, that might be waste or might be the beginning of soil or might be something else entirely—a signal, a mark, a way of writing *I was here* into the floor itself.

They communicate, after a fashion. Two mats that meet don't merge, exactly, but they *acknowledge* each other. A narrow gap persists between them—barely visible, a hairline fracture of bare surface—and yet at the edges where they approach, both mats thicken slightly, darkening, as if in response. There is no competition for resources, or none that you can see. Perhaps there are enough seams. Perhaps the surface provides more than enough of whatever they need. Perhaps they are all, in some way you haven't discerned, parts of the same organism.

The mats live here. They are the first and most successful inhabitants, and they have been here longer than anything else.

---

The stalks live here.

The jointed, dry-looking growths that rise from the wider seams are harder to love. They seem incomplete—as if the idea of a stem had been proposed and then abandoned before the leaf, the flower, the fruit could be considered. They grow in clusters of three to seven, always from the same seam, and they reach their full height—a foot, sometimes eighteen inches—within what you estimate to be a single cycle of condensation and evaporation. Then they stop. They don't die, not immediately. They stand in their clusters, rattling when the air moves, their tips curled over like question marks or like necks bent in resignation, and they persist for many cycles before they begin to thin and pale and eventually fold into the seam from which they came.

You thought they were feeding from the water in the seams, and they may be, but you've come to suspect they're also feeding something *into* it. The water in the wider channels has a viscosity and a faint organic quality that the condensation pools in the narrower seams don't share. Something is being added. And the stalks, with their jointed stems and their rattling persistence, seem like plausible candidates—slowly dissolving, or slowly secreting, or slowly becoming the medium in which the next thing will grow.

They are intermediate. They belong to a process that hasn't completed itself. When you pull one from the seam—it comes easily, with a soft, wet sound—the base is slightly swollen, slightly softer than the rest, and the hole it leaves behind fills immediately with water from the channel. Within a cycle, a new stalk has begun to form at the same point. Not from the root, not from any structure you can identify—just a thickening at the water's surface, a darkening, a rising.

They are not individuals. They are iterations.

---

The hummocks live here.

These are what stopped you. The low, dark mounds rising from the standing water in the channels, softer than anything else on this continent, warmer than they should be, pulsing with a rhythm so slow you had to press your palm against them and wait to feel it. You have spent more time with the hummocks than with any other feature of this place because they are the only feature that seems to want something.

The mats read the surface. The stalks stand and dissolve. The hummocks *thicken*.

They grow upward, accumulating layer on layer of something that isn't quite vegetable and isn't quite mineral—a dense, dark material, spongy when compressed, resilient when released, warm to the touch even in the cooler hours. They grow outward too, spreading along the channels, merging where two hummocks meet to form a larger hummock with a deeper pulse and a higher profile. The largest you've seen rose nearly to your knee—a soft, dark hill in the shallow water, warm as a sleeping animal, vibrating faintly with something that might have been breath if there were lungs to produce it.

You pressed your hand against that large one and felt the pulse quicken, just slightly, just for a moment, as if in response.

You pulled your hand away.

The hummocks are the first things here that have an inside. The mats are surfaces—tops and bottoms with nothing between. The stalks are hollow, or nearly, their jointed walls thin enough to let light pass. But the hummocks have depth. Density. When you pressed against the large one, your fingers sank in nearly an inch before meeting resistance, and the material that closed around them was warm and faintly damp and gave slightly, like flesh that hasn't learned to be firm.

You did not cut one open. You thought about it. You held your knife against the surface of a small hummock and felt the pulse beneath the blade and put the knife away.

Some categories should not be introduced by violence.

---

Something else lives here.

You have not seen it. You have heard it—or you have almost heard it, in the hours around what you call dawn, when the condensation beads on the surface and the seams fill and the world is at its coolest and quietest. A sound below the threshold of sound. A vibration that might be in the air or might be in the floor or might be in your own body, sympathetic, resonating with something that you cannot identify and cannot ignore.

It happens in the deepest channels, where the water is widest and the hummocks are thickest and the smell of slow organic process is strongest. You stand there in the dim, still hours and you feel it: a pulse, much slower than the hummocks' pulse, much deeper, coming from somewhere below the surface. From below the floor.

The first time you felt it, you thought the continent itself was breathing.

You have considered the possibility that you are imagining it—that the regularity of this place, the made-ness of it, the absence of familiar life, has worn down your certainty about what is real and what is projection. But you have felt it on three separate occasions now, always in the same conditions, always in the same kind of place, and the rhythm is consistent. Not mechanical. Not random. Something between the two—irregular in the way that living things are irregular, steady in the way that tides are steady.

You have considered the possibility that the floor is not a floor.

That what you are standing on is a skin.

---

The visitor lives here.

That would be you. You, walking the flat and finished surface, measuring time by your footsteps, drinking from the channels when you must, eating the edges of the mats when you can bring yourself to—they are faintly bitter, faintly sweet, and they sit in your stomach with a weight and warmth that suggests they are doing something more than providing calories. You, leaving footprints that the composite does not retain, casting shadows that the light does not sharpen, making sounds that the air does not hold.

You have been here long enough to affect the place. The mats near your campsites are slightly thicker, slightly darker, than mats at the same distance from the seams elsewhere. The water in the channels you've drunk from has a slightly different viscosity than water in channels you've left alone. The hummocks you've touched have grown slightly faster, slightly taller, than hummocks you haven't.

You do not know what this means. You do not know whether you are feeding them or being fed to them, whether your presence is a catalyst or a contaminant or a meal. You do not know whether the thing below the floor, if it exists, is aware of you. You do not know whether awareness is a category that applies here, or whether it is another thing you are introducing that this place will have to learn to accommodate.

You live here now. You are part of the process. You are the fourth concession—the first animal, the first warm body, the first thing that moves with purpose across the made and finished surface, leaving traces of nitrogen and carbon and heat wherever you stop.

You are what the seams have been waiting for.

---

Or perhaps not. Perhaps you are simply what arrived first, and other things will follow, and the continent will absorb you all the way it is learning to absorb the water and the warmth and the slow green press of the mats. Perhaps the still-born is beginning to breathe, and you are the first breath, and you will not be the last.

You keep walking. The floor goes on. The processes continue. Something lives here.
