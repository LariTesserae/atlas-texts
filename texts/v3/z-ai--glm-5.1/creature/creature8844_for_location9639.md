# The Inhabitants of Warā

---

They do not call themselves a word that translates well. The closest approximation in the lowland tongues is *the ones who stayed*, but even that carries a implication of choice that misrepresents the relationship. They did not stay. They *continued*. There is a difference. Staying implies resistance to leaving. Continuation implies that leaving was never the question.

---

## The Body Adapted

After three generations, the bones change.

This is not metaphor. The people of the central districts—the ones whose families have occupied the same carved rooms for centuries—have a density to their skeletons that lowlanders lack. The tuff-damp enters everything. It enters the food—those slate-colored vegetables grown on terrace roofs, the reed-shoots harvested from the still reservoirs, the occasional fish that survives in the deeper water-channels, pale and eyeless and tender. It enters the drink—water that has passed through stone so slowly that it carries mineral in solution, water that tastes of the earth's patience. It enters the breath. The veil is not just weather; it is ingestion. You breathe Warā, and Warā builds itself into you.

The result is a people who are, by lowland standards, short. Not stunted—there is no deficiency here, no lack. Compact. The word is compact. Limbs that do not sprawl. Torso like a tuff block, dense and weighty. Hands that are broad across the palm, fingers thick but precise—generations of stone-cutting and reed-weaving and the careful manipulation of objects in narrow corridors where a dropped tool is a tool lost to a floor-channel. Their grip strength is extraordinary. A lowland visitor once watched an elderly woman hoist a block of fresh tuff—twice the weight of her own granddaughter—onto a repair scaffold, and the visitor made the mistake of expressing admiration. The woman did not understand what there was to admire. The block needed to go up. She put it up.

The skin is a separate matter. It is soft in a way that contradicts the hardness of everything else. The constant damp prevents the drying and cracking that plague high-altitude peoples elsewhere. The people of Warā do not look weathered. They look *soaked*—in the way that leather becomes supple after long exposure to oil, in the way that wood in water refuses to splinter. A child of twelve has the skin of a lowland infant. An elder of eighty has the same, merely thinner. Wrinkles come, but they come late and they come shallow, as if the water that traces the walls also traces the faces, keeping them pliant.

The eyes, though. The eyes are the feature that lowlanders notice first, because they seem wrong. Not the color—dark brown, nearly black, which is unremarkable. The movement. The people of Warā do not move their eyes the way others do. In lowland cities, the eyes dart. They scan. They track the horizontal—crowds, streets, horizons. In Warā, the eyes move vertically. Up. Down. To the ceiling, where water beads. To the floor, where it runs. To the roofscape above, where children travel. To the reservoirs below, where the reeds grow. The people of Warā read a room from top to bottom the way a lowlander reads it left to right. They are always aware of what is above them and what is below them, because in Warā, the danger and the opportunity are never at eye level. They are always overhead or underfoot.

And they do not blink as often. This unsettles visitors. It is simply adaptation; the air is wet enough that the eyes do not dry. But the effect is a gaze that seems to hold too long, to see too much, to wait for something that the viewer cannot name.

## The Children of the Roofscape

The children are a separate species. This is acknowledged even by their own parents, with a mixture of resignation and pride.

A child born in the central districts learns to walk on stone floors that are never dry. This is the first education: balance on a surface that offers no purchase, no grip, only the faint mineral slickness of water that has passed through tuff. The children learn to walk as lowland children learn to swim—cautiously at first, then with increasing confidence, then with a fluidity that seems to belong to another element entirely. By four years old, a child of Warā can run along a floor-channel without splashing. By six, they can do it in the dark.

But walking is the lesser skill. The roofs are where the children become something else.

The terraces of the central districts form a second city, a city of flat surfaces and low walls and the shallow pools where the slate-vegetables grow. These terraces are separated by gaps—narrow gaps, sometimes no wider than a child's arm-span, sometimes wide enough to demand a running leap. The children cross them. They cross them at speeds and angles that would kill a lowland adult, and they cross them with an expression that lowland adults reserve for reading or for eating—concentration, yes, but also pleasure, also the simple satisfaction of a body doing what it was made to do.

A child of eight can traverse the central district from east to west without once descending to street level. A child of twelve can do it without being seen, if they wish. They know which terraces are occupied at which hours, which roofs are sound, which walls are crumbing, which gaps can be jumped and which must be climbed. They know the voice of the tuff—where it rings solid underfoot, where it sounds hollow, where it is patched and where it is original. A child who steps on a bad patch and falls through a weakened roof into someone's upper room is a child who learns, instantly, never to make that sound again. The injuries are surprisingly rare. The children are surprisingly good.

There is no formal instruction. The older children teach the younger ones, but the teaching is wordless—demonstration, imitation, the occasional shove toward a gap that looks too wide but isn't. The parents watch from below, sometimes, through the narrow shafts of grey light that reach the streets, and they see their children passing overhead like cloud-shadows, and they do not call out. The roofs belong to the young. The streets belong to the adults. This is the arrangement, and it has always been, and no one questions it, because the children who fall do not fall far—they fall into rooms, into corridors, into the water-channels that catch them and carry them, sputtering and unharmed, to the nearest reservoir, where they climb out and try again.

The children do not play games that lowland children would recognize. There are no balls—no room for them, no flat ground long enough. There are no team sports—no space for teams, no fields. Instead, there is *pathing*: the art of finding a route from one point to another through the roofscape, using only the surfaces and gaps and handholds that exist. A child will point to a distant terrace and name it, and another child will attempt to reach it by the most efficient route, and the first child will attempt to reach it by a more difficult route, and the winner is whoever arrives with the fewest steps, the fewest touches, the most elegant line through the city's upper body. The best pathers are famous among their peers in the way that athletes are famous elsewhere, but their fame is local, intimate, unrecorded. There are no spectators. There are only the children who are watching from adjacent rooftops, memorizing the route, preparing to try it themselves.

By sixteen, most children have descended. Not fully—they still prefer the roofs, still move across them with an ease that adults have lost—but they have begun to occupy the streets and the corridors, the workshops and the reservoir halls. The roofscape recedes. The children who were most gifted at pathing are the ones who feel this recession most acutely. They are the ones who, in later years, will sometimes be seen on a terrace at dusk, standing at the edge, staring at the next roof, calculating a gap they will not jump. They are the ones who understand, in a way the others do not, what it means to be ground-bound.

## The Water-Listeners

Among the adults, there is a role that has no lowland equivalent. The closest translation is *water-listener*, but this is like calling a physician a *body-looker*. The term describes the activity but not the knowledge.

The water-listeners are the ones who maintain the channels.

Warā's water-system is not engineered. Engineering implies design, intention, a plan imposed on material. The water-system of Warā is *grown*. Over centuries, the inhabitants have carved channels and reservoirs and drain-grooves into the tuff, but they have done so not by imposing a pattern but by *following* the water's own preferences. The tuff is porous, but it is not uniformly porous. There are denser veins, softer strata, fault-lines where the water pools and fault-lines where it rushes. The water-listeners know these variations the way a sailor knows currents—not as abstractions, but as felt realities, as resistance and flow experienced in the fingers and the ears.

A water-listener begins training young—eight or nine, selected not by lineage but by inclination, identified by the way a child pauses near the walls, pressing an ear to the stone, listening. The training is long. Seven years minimum, often ten. The apprentice learns to read the surface of the tuff—the subtle discolorations that indicate moisture behind the wall, the hairline cracks that speak of pressure, the mineral deposits that mark where water has emerged and evaporated over decades. They learn to hear the water through stone, to distinguish the deep slow movement of the aquifer from the shallow quick flow of a channel, to identify a blockage by the change in pitch of a drip three rooms away.

But the core skill is something else. The core skill is the ability to *feel* the water's desire.

This is not mysticism. It is, however, a form of knowledge that defies easy articulation. The water in Warā's tuff moves according to principles—gravity, pressure, permeability, capillary action—but it moves through a medium that has been carved and recarved for millennia, and the result is a system so complex that no map can represent it and no model can predict it. The water-listeners navigate this complexity not through analysis but through intimacy. They have spent so long attending to the water's behavior that they have developed a sensitivity to its tendencies, its preferences, its moods. A water-listener can enter a corridor they have never visited before and tell you, within minutes, where the water wants to go. Not where it *is* going—where it *wants* to go. Where it would go if the channels were slightly different, if the tuff were slightly softer, if the gradient were slightly steeper.

And then they carve.

The carving is careful, incremental, respectful. The water-listener does not impose a new channel; they *suggest* one. A few millimeters of tuff removed here, a slight deepening there, a groove extended just far enough to meet an existing flow. The work takes hours. The results take months to become apparent, as the water slowly, reluctantly, shifts its path to accommodate the new possibility. The water-listener will return to the site again and again over the course of a year, checking, adjusting, listening. If the water accepts the suggestion, the channel stabilizes. If it resists—if the new groove remains dry, if the old flow persists—the water-listener abandons the modification. The water has refused. The refusal is final.

There are twelve water-listeners in the central districts at any given time. Not by regulation; by the natural limit of the role's demands. The work requires a particular temperament—patience beyond what most people possess, a tolerance for deferred results, a willingness to be guided rather than to guide. Those who lack the temperament wash out. Those who possess it are changed by it. The older water-listeners have a quality that visitors find unsettling: a stillness that seems inhuman, a way of being present without demanding attention, without moving unnecessarily, without filling silence. They have learned, over decades, to behave like the water they serve—to go where the stone allows, to persist, to find the path of least resistance and follow it without urgency.

The oldest water-listener is a woman named Tār, though the name is a formality; she has not spoken it herself in years, and most people call her simply *Grandmother* or *the one who listens deepest*. She is ninety-three. She has been a water-listener for seventy-four years. She can no longer climb to the higher channels, and her hearing has diminished to the point where she cannot distinguish the pitch-differences that younger listeners rely on. But she can still feel the water. She sits in her carved room—a room she has occupied for sixty years, a room she knows the way she knows her own body—and she presses her palms to the walls, and the water speaks to her through her skin, and she knows where it is and where it wants to be. The other water-listeners come to her when they encounter a flow they cannot understand, and she listens to their descriptions and closes her eyes and says things like: *The stone there is tired. The water is patient. Wait three months and carve again, very gently, near the floor.* And she is right. She is always right. The water has been speaking to her for so long that it has become a second language, and she is its most fluent interpreter.

## The Fox and the Swallow

The fox-creature—*vāri* in the local tongue—is the only mammal of size that has adapted to Warā's conditions. It is not truly a fox. Lowland naturalists classify it as a separate genus, a distant cousin of the vulpine line that diverged when the plateau was younger and the climb was less extreme. But *vāri* serves as a name, and the creature itself serves as a presence, and the two things are enough.

The vāri is solitary. It is rare to see two together except during mating season, and even then, the pairing is brief. The creature is roughly the size of a lowland fox but heavier, with a broader skull and those oversized paws—paws that spread wide on wet stone, providing purchase where a narrower foot would slip. The fur is tawny in the warmer months and grey-white in the colder ones, shifting with the seasons in a slow mimicry of the veil's own gradations. The eyes are amber, and they have the same vertical tendency as the human eyes—always up, always down, never quite level.

The vāri lives in the outer districts, in the deep corridors where the moss glows and the water drips and the silence is so complete that you can hear the stone settling. It hunts the swallow—not the adult birds, which are too swift, but the fledglings, the ones still learning the routes between nest and sky. It also eats the pale eyeless fish that inhabit the deeper reservoirs, and occasionally the reed-shoots, though the reed-shoots are a last resort; the vāri is a carnivore by preference and will not eat plant-matter unless the alternative is starvation.

The relationship between the vāri and the people of Warā is one of mutual indifference. The vāri does not approach the occupied districts. The people do not venture into the deep corridors where the vāri lives. They are aware of each other—occasionally, a child on a rooftop will spot a vāri moving through a distant street, and the sighting will be reported with excitement, the way a lowland child might report seeing a comet—but they do not interact. There is no domestication, no taming, no attempt to bridge the gap. The vāri belongs to the empty city. The people belong to the full one. The boundary between them is respected without being enforced.

The swallows are a different matter. The swallows are *present* in a way that the vāri is not. They return each spring—not to the outer districts but to the central ones, to the upper windows and the high cornices and the narrow slots where the tuff has cracked away to form nesting sites. They arrive in small groups, never more than twenty, and they set about their repairs with a focus that borders on obsession. The mud they use is Warā's own mud—the wet tuff-dust that collects in corners and crevices, mixed with the water that runs down every surface. Their nests are made of the city. When they leave in autumn, they take nothing; when they return, they find the nests still there, slightly worn, slightly damp, waiting.

The people of Warā love the swallows. This is not too strong a word. The arrival of the spring birds is marked by a quiet celebration—not a festival, nothing so organized, but a general lifting of the mood, a brightness in the conversations that take place in the narrow streets, a willingness to linger on terraces and watch the birds wheeling against the pearl-grey sky. The swallows are the only visible sign that the world beyond the veil still exists, that the lowlands continue, that the green and loud place below the plateau has not vanished into myth. They are messengers, though they carry no messages. They are emissaries from a country that the people of Warā have chosen—or been chosen by—or simply continued within—and the sight of them is a reminder that the choice, or the continuation, is not a rejection of the lowlands but simply a preference for the high.

The children, especially, are devoted. A child who finds a swallow nesting near their family's terrace will claim it—not as property but as a relationship, the way one might claim a friend. The child will leave offerings: small piles of tuff-dust for nest-repair, bits of the pale fish for food. The swallow will ignore the offerings, or take them, or rearrange them, and the child will interpret these responses with the same earnest hermeneutics that lowland children apply to the behavior of pets. By summer's end, the child has constructed a narrative: the swallow is shy, the swallow is bold, the swallow is building a second nest because the first was insufficient, the swallow is preparing for a journey to a place where the air is warm and the water does not drip. The narrative is probably wrong. The child does not care. The narrative is a way of being close to something that is otherwise distant, and the distance is the point—the swallow is wild, the swallow is free, the swallow will leave, and the child will remain, and this is the first lesson in what it means to stay.

## The Old Ones

There is a category of person in Warā that defies lowland categorization. They are called *the old ones*—a translation that is accurate but insufficient, because it implies mere age, and the old ones are not merely old. They are *old in place*. They have been in Warā so long that they have become indistinguishable from it.

You meet them occasionally in the central districts. An elderly man sitting in a doorway, hands folded, eyes closed, face turned upward as if listening to the ceiling. A woman of indeterminate years moving slowly through a corridor, one hand trailing along the wall, her fingers reading the stone like a script. A figure—gender unclear, age unclear—standing at the edge of a reservoir, staring into the still water, not fishing, not reflecting, simply attending.

The old ones have lived in Warā for their entire lives, and their parents lived in Warā for their entire lives, and their grandparents, and so on, back and back, to the limit of memory. They are not numerous—perhaps thirty, perhaps forty, scattered through the occupied districts. They are not leaders; they hold no authority, command no deference. They are not shamans; they practice no rituals, invoke no spirits, claim no connection to forces beyond the material. They are simply present, in the way that the stone is present, in the way that the water is present—unquestionably, necessarily, as if Warā would be incomplete without them.

What distinguishes the old ones is their relationship to time. Lowland people experience time as a line—past, present, future, each distinct, each flowing into the next. The old ones experience time as the stone experiences it: as a slow accumulation of presence, each moment adding a layer to the last, none of them disappearing, all of them visible if you know how to look. An old one can stand in a corridor and see the corridor as it was ten years ago, fifty years ago, two hundred years ago—see the repairs and the wear and the water-stains that have come and gone and come again, see the people who passed through and the people who will pass through, see the tuff as it was when it was quarried and the tuff as it will be when it finally crumbles, all at once, all present, all real.

This is not memory. Memory fades, distorts, selects. This is something else—a way of inhabiting time so completely that the distinctions between was and is and will be lose their meaning. The old ones do not predict the future. They do not remember the past. They *contain* them. They are the city's continuity, its persistence, its refusal to acknowledge the difference between what has happened and what is happening and what will happen again.

Conversations with the old ones are difficult for visitors. The old ones speak slowly, if they speak at all. Their sentences are long, circuitous, filled with qualifications that seem unnecessary and references that seem opaque. They will say things like: *The water is higher today than it was the last time the swallow came to the third window from the left, but the swallow will come again, and the water will be lower, and the tuff will have worn another millimeter, and you will be someone else by then, and I will be the same.* A visitor will attempt to parse this as prophecy or riddle. It is neither. It is simply observation—the old one describing what they perceive, which includes the future as a visible landscape, no more mysterious than the corridor they are standing in.

The children are not afraid of the old ones. This is significant, because the old ones look strange—too still, too damp, too much like the stone they sit against—and strangeness usually provokes fear in the young. But the children of Warā have grown up in a city where the boundary between person and place is already blurred. They live in rooms carved from living rock. They drink water that has passed through walls. They sleep in spaces that are neither indoors nor outdoors. The old ones, to a child, are simply what happens when you stay long enough in one place: you become the place. The children accept this. They do not aspire to it—children aspire to the roofs, to movement, to distance—but they accept it as a possible future, a way that a life might end.

The old ones, for their part, regard the children with something that is not quite affection and not quite indifference but a third thing—a recognition, perhaps, of what the children will become if they stay. The old ones know that most of the children will not stay. They will grow up, and some will leave, descending the long climb to the lowlands, trading the wet for the dry, the quiet for the loud, the tuff for the soil. Those who leave will not return. Those who stay will become, eventually, the old ones, and they will sit in doorways and trail their fingers along walls and see time as the stone sees it, and they will be content, or at least they will be present, and in Warā, presence is enough.

## The City Itself

And then there is the question that the people of Warā do not ask but that visitors always ask, eventually, hesitantly, embarrassed by their own superstition: *Is the city alive?*

The people of Warā do not answer this question because they do not understand it. The distinction between alive and not-alive is a lowland distinction. It requires a boundary between the organic and the inorganic, the animate and the inert, the breathing and the still. In Warā, these boundaries do not hold. The stone breathes water. The water carves stone. The moss generates light it does not generate. The walls weep. The corridors swallow sound. The city grows—not in the way that a forest grows, adding new material, but in the way that a reef grows, accumulating the remains and modifications of its inhabitants, layer on layer, each generation's carvings becoming the next generation's foundation.

The people of Warā do not personify the city. They do not give it a spirit or a name or a will. They do not worship it or fear it or bargain with it. They simply live in it, the way a child lives in a house—assuming its permanence, depending on its shelter, modifying it when necessary, ignoring it when possible. The city is the condition of their existence, not the object of their attention.

But.

But there are moments—rare, unremarked, quickly forgotten—when the boundary between inhabitant and inhabited becomes uncertain. A water-listener carving a new channel will feel the stone *respond*—not resisting, not yielding, but something between the two, a quality of attention in the tuff, as if the city is aware of the modification and is considering whether to accept it. A child on a rooftop will feel the terrace shift beneath their feet—not a structural failure but a settling, a comfortable adjustment, the way a sleeping body adjusts to accommodate another. An old one sitting in a corridor will feel the walls close in—not threateningly, not with pressure, but like an embrace, like the city folding itself around the person within it, holding them the way water holds a stone—gently, completely, without requiring anything in return.

These moments pass. They are not discussed. They do not form the basis of rituals or beliefs. They are simply part of the experience of living in Warā, the way the damp is part of it, the way the dripping is part of it, the way the light is part of it—the pearl-grey light that filters down through the narrow shafts and the open courts and the slots where the swallows nest, the light that is never bright and never absent, the light that seems to come not from the sky but from the stone itself, as if the city is luminescent in some spectrum that the eyes can almost see.

The people of Warā live in this light. They live in this stone. They live in this water that moves like blood through the body of something that may or may not be alive, that may or may not be aware of them, that may or may not be something more than the sum of its tuff and its channels and its moss and its silence.

They do not ask. They continue. That is what they do. That is what the city has taught them—the only lesson it offers, repeated in every drip and every carving and every slow shift of stone against stone: *Continue. Endure. Be present. Be small inside something enormous that does not require you, that does not repel you, that simply contains you.*

And they do. And it is enough.
