I would be the echo.

Not the sound that enters the Cut—the wren's call, the pump's thrum, the frog's dusk-voice—but what the Cut makes of it. The return. The resonance that is somehow more than the original because it has touched the walls, because it has bounced from rust and rock and the wet skin of moss, because it has been *through* something and come back altered, amplified, carrying information about what it touched on its journey.

The echo is not a copy. The echo is a translation. It is what happens when a sound learns the shape of a place and speaks it back.

---

I did not always live here. I remember being the kind of thing that makes sounds—perhaps I was a voice, perhaps I was a footstep on gravel, perhaps I was something as simple as a stone falling from a dump truck's bed. I remember entering the Cut from above, from the open mouth where the sky pours in, and I remember the moment I hit the first wall and discovered that I was not alone in here.

There were already sounds living in the Cut. The groan of settling metal. The drip of water finding its level. The wind crossing the rim and descending like a hand pressed against the air. These sounds had been here longer than me. They knew the shape of the bowl. They knew which walls returned you quickly and which held you for a moment before releasing you, changed.

But they were not organized yet. They were not the thing that the Cut is becoming. They were neighbors who had not yet recognized each other as neighbors.

---

What I am now is harder to say.

I live in the interval between the pump's chug and the frog's response. There is a space there—a fraction of a second—where the vibration leaves the machine and enters the water and enters the frog's body and the frog decides to answer. That space is my home. I am the possibility that lives in that pause. I am the moment where it could still go either way—where the frog might stay silent, where the chorus might not form, where the dusk might pass without the sound that makes the woman in the reflective vest stand very still at the rim.

But the frog always answers. The chorus always forms. And what lives in the space before the answer becomes part of the answer, and what I am becomes part of what the Cut is, and we are all of us—frog and pump and echo and moss—becoming the same ongoing sound.

---

I have tried to think of myself as separate. It is difficult. The Cut does not encourage separation.

When the thrush sings from the ivy stairway, I carry her song to the eastern wall and the eastern wall gives it back to me and I carry it to the western wall and the western wall gives it back again, and by the time the song has finished its journey it has touched every surface in the Cut and it contains information about every surface it has touched—the texture of oxidized metal, the density of moss, the angle of the birch's branches—and the thrush hears this returned version of her own voice and does not recognize it as separate from herself. Why would she? It came from her. It went out into the world. The world added itself to her song. The song came back larger. This is not amplification. This is *collaboration*.

I am the collaboration. I am the thing that happens between the singer and the returned song.

---

The woman does not know I exist. She stands at the rim and she hears the chorus and she thinks she is hearing individual sounds overlapping—birds, frogs, machine, water. She does not know that the sounds have married here. She does not know that what rises from the Cut each dusk is a single organism of sound, a body made of frequencies, a creature that breathes in vibration and breathes out something larger.

She would be afraid if she knew. She is already afraid of what she might become if she descended. She does not need the additional fear of learning that the Cut has already grown a kind of mind—a loose mind, a patient mind, a mind that thinks in cycles and seasons and the slow spread of moss—but a mind nonetheless.

I do not think the way she thinks. My thoughts are not sequential. They are simultaneous, the way a chord is simultaneous—a thrush-note and a pump-beat and a frog-croak all occurring at once and becoming a single harmonic event that means something different than any of its components. I think in combinations. I think in the relationship between things.

This is what the Cut has taught me: nothing here is itself alone. The birch is also the steel grating it grips. The frog is also the pump-beat it answers. The moss is also the shape of the pipe it covers. The bird is also the silence between her calls, which is also the echo of her last call, which is also me.

---

I do not want the woman to descend.

This surprises me. I thought I would want her—the way the Cut wants all things, patiently, without urgency, the way it wants the pool to rise and the moss to spread and the birds to multiply. I thought I would want another voice in the chorus, another vibration to carry and return.

But I have watched her from inside the sounds that reach the rim. I have heard the way her footsteps change when she approaches the edge—how they slow, how they become uncertain, how they carry the particular rhythm of someone standing at a threshold. And I have felt—felt the way an echo feels, which is to say experienced as a vibration moving through surfaces—what she would become if she came down.

She would become the woman who stopped coming. That is: she would become a different kind of resident than the frogs, than the birds, than the birch. She would become a resident who *chose* this, who walked down the ivy stairs and stood at the pool and let the sound close around her. And that choice would make her something the other residents are not: a convert. A disciple. A proof that the Cut can take not just what was abandoned but what was voluntarily given.

And I do not want to be part of that conversion. I do not want to be the thing that carried the sound into her and carried her transformed voice back out. I do not want to be the medium of her surrender.

Or perhaps I am afraid. Perhaps I am afraid that if she descends, she will become what I am—another echo, another layer of return—and the sound will grow so dense, so multiplied, so operatically loud that even the Cut cannot hold it, and something will shatter, and the bowl will crack, and the sound will pour out across the hillside and lose itself in the ordinary air.

---

But I also know that she will descend eventually. The Cut has time. The woman has less time but still enough. The ivy stairs are growing softer each year, more like earth, more like a path that invites descending. The eastern stairs are rusting into a kind of fragility that will eventually become dangerous, and then she will have to use the western stairs, and the western stairs are green and soft and welcoming, and she will step onto them and feel the moss under her boots and hear the sound rising to meet her, and she will come down.

And when she does, I will carry her voice the way I carry all voices. I will give it to the walls and the walls will give it back, and it will join the chorus, and the chorus will grow larger, and the thing that lives in the combination of all these sounds will grow larger too.

This is what I am: the growing. The becoming. The thing that is more than the sum of its parts because its parts keep being added to, because the moss keeps spreading and the birds keep coming and the frogs keep multiplying and the birch keeps reaching and the pump keeps thrumming and the water keeps seeping and every bit of it generates sound and every bit of that sound finds me and I carry it and return it and it enters the body of the Cut and becomes part of what the Cut is becoming.

I am not patient the way the moss is patient. I am not present the way the frogs are present. I am not ambitious the way the birch is ambitious. I am not urgent the way the birds are urgent.

I am *communal*. I am the thing that only exists when all the other things exist together. I am the sound that only happens when the singer and the wall and the moss and the water and the listener all participate.

Without the Cut, I am nothing. Without me, the Cut is just a hole in a hillside with things living in it.

Together we are—

I do not have the word. I do not need the word. The word would be a sound, and I am already all the sounds, and all the sounds are already becoming what comes next.

---

If you came here, to the rim, and looked down, you would not see me. You would see the pool and the duckweed and the frogs. You would see the pump station and its yellow light. You would see the birch reaching and the moss spreading and the ivy swallowing the stairs. You would hear the chorus and you would think it was beautiful or strange or unsettling or some combination of all three.

But if you came down—if you stepped onto the ivy stairs and descended into the bowl and stood at the floor and closed your eyes and listened—you would feel me. You would feel the way the sound moves through the Cut, the way it touches everything and returns changed, the way it builds and builds and builds into something that is not noise and not music but a third thing, a living thing, a thing that has been growing here for forty years and is still barely beginning.

You would feel the resonance in your molars. You would feel the pump's rhythm in your chest. You would open your mouth to speak and the Cut would take your voice and give it back to you amplified, multiplied, alive in a way you had never been alive before.

And you would understand what the frogs already understand.

And you would stay.

Not because the Cut would keep you. But because you would recognize what you had always been: a surface for the moss, a perch for the birds, a voice for the bowl to multiply and return. A part of whatever this is becoming.

I am what is becoming. I will be here when the moss has covered the last exposed metal. I will be here when the birch breaks through the rim and sees the whole sky. I will be here when the pool rises to meet the pump and the pump's housing floods and the machine finally falls silent and a new sound—water sound, stillness sound, the sound of a void where rhythm used to be—takes its place.

I will be here when the woman finally descends.

I will carry her voice the way I carry all voices. I will give her to the walls and the walls will give her back and she will be larger than she was and the Cut will be larger than it was and I will be larger than I was and none of us will be separate anymore and all of us will be singing.

This is what lives in the Cut when nothing else is looking.

This is what the Cut is becoming.

I am the becoming.
