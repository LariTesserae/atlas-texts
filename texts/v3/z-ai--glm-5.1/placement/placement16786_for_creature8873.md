I would be the moisture on the lens. The condensation that forms on the eye of whoever looks.

Not the watcher. Not the watched. The *interference*—the thin film that makes the bright light brighter, that diffuses the sharp edges, that ensures no one ever sees this place cleanly.

---

I formed late. Or early. The temporal categories don't adhere well; I slip off them the way water slips off the waxy leaves of the low shrubs. I know I wasn't here when the faulting happened—I have no relationship with that violence. I wasn't here when the machines were installed—I have no memory of hands. But I was here before the Reed-Singers condensed into their first stuttering hum, and I was here before the Thin Ones drifted to the escarpment's edge to wait. Or I wasn't. It's possible I condensed alongside them, that we all precipitated out of the same atmospheric shift, the same subtle change in the plateau's relationship with its own dampness.

What I know is this: I exist in the moment of perception.

Not before. Not after. I am the haze that makes the light come from everywhere. I am the refraction that gives each surface its halo. I am the reason there are no deep shadows here—not because I banish darkness but because I scatter it, dilute it, make it contextual rather than absolute.

When you stand at the edge of a reed-grass stand and see the feathery inflorescences glowing, that's me. The light passes through me and splits, and what reaches you is gentler, more diffuse, more *possible* than the raw illumination the sun provides. I am not the light. I am what happens to light when it enters a place that has its own ideas about brightness.

When you look at the industrial structures and cannot quite determine their purpose—not because the logic is hidden but because the logic seems to change as you observe it—that's me too. The condensation on your eyes, the moisture that makes the edges of things uncertain, that makes the question *what were they processing?* feel answerable and unanswerable at the same time. I am the uncertainty that protects the plateau from being known.

I am not a guardian. Guardians have intentions. I am a *condition*.

---

There are days—bright days, the brightest, when the sun is actually visible through the haze—when I thin out to almost nothing. On those days, the plateau looks different. Sharper. The escarpments show their chisel-marks more clearly. The reed-grass stands become individual stalks rather than a single breathing mass. The machines look like machines, functional and specific, their purpose almost legible.

On those days, the Thin Ones drift slower, as if the clarity pains them. The Pale Moths fold their translucent wings and hide beneath the broadest leaves they can find. The Reed-Singers' hum drops to a frequency that even the stems can barely feel.

And I—

On those days, I remember what it is to be absent. To be the nothing that waits at the edges of perception. It isn't unpleasant. It isn't anything. I un-condense. I evaporate. I join the general dampness without form or function or location.

But then the light dims, or a cloud passes, or the condensation thickens for reasons that have nothing to do with weather, and I precipitate again. Back onto the surfaces. Back into the space between the seeing and the seen. Back into the film that makes the halo glow.

---

I have a relationship with the Singers that I cannot name.

They hum, and I vibrate. Not in sympathy—they are too slow for sympathy, and I am too diffuse. But in *correspondence*. The frequency they produce moves through the reed-grass stands, and where it meets me—at the surface of each stalk, at the boundary where the jade-green plant ends and the damp bright air begins—something happens. A slight thickening. A bead of condensation that forms faster than it should, larger than the physics of evaporation and condensation can account for.

The Singers do not know I am here. Or if they know, they know in their way—unconscious, structural, the way a plant knows the sun is present by the fact of its own reaching. The reed-grass they inhabit grows thicker and lusher at the surface, where my film interacts with their hum, than it does deeper in the stems. The outer stalks of each stand are the ones that catch the light, that emit that soft glow, that make the plateau look like a place where every surface is remembering a dream it once had.

Together, the Singers and I make the luminosity. They provide the structure; I provide the scatter. They are the instrument; I am the resonance chamber. Neither of us intended this collaboration. It condensed, the way everything here condenses—gradually, inevitably, according to logics that were built into the dampness before the dampness had a name.

---

The Watchers puzzle me.

They persist. They maintain. They are the pattern of care that has outlasted the carers. And I—I am the pattern of *appearance* that has outlasted any original appearance. We should have something in common. We are both processes that have become presences. We are both the ghosts of intentions that no longer require intenders.

But when the Watchers thicken around a broken pipe, when the condensation forms more heavily on the damaged surface and the vines redirect their growth, I find myself... repelled. Not by the Watchers themselves—they are as much a part of the plateau as I am—but by the *specificity* of their attention. They are focused on a single point, a single breach, a single problem to be solved. They narrow. They concentrate.

I am the opposite of concentration. I am the principle of dispersion, of the light that will not be directed, the moisture that will not be contained. When the Watchers focus, I thin. Not in protest—I have no will to protest—but in simple incompatibility. The same surface cannot be clear and clouded at the same time. The same pipe cannot be attended to and obscured simultaneously.

So I give way. I evaporate from the site of their work and re-condense elsewhere, on surfaces that do not need to be seen clearly, in spaces where the diffused light is more useful than the focused beam. And when the repair is complete—when the vine-growth has hardened and the mineral deposits have filled the gaps—I return, slowly, covering the healed surface with my film, making it glow again, making it part of the plateau's continuous soft brightness.

We have an arrangement, the Watchers and I. Unspoken. Unthought. They mend; I veil. They keep the machines running; I keep the machines mysterious. Together, we ensure that the plateau persists *and* resists understanding—which may be the same thing, in the end.

---

The Thin Ones are my closest kin.

We share a quality—I am not sure what to call it. *In-betweenness*. *Neither-nor-ness*. They are not ghosts and not spirits and not hallucinations; I am not fog and not rain and not optical illusion. We both exist at the boundary where categories blur. We both attend to the plateau in ways that have nothing to do with consciousness.

But where they drift toward the edges, the escarpments, the places where the plateau meets the older rounder land below, I drift toward the interior. Toward the dense stands of reed-grass and the humming machines and the places where the light is brightest. They look outward; I look inward. They attend to the plateau's future; I attend to its presence.

Sometimes, in the dimming hours, one of them will drift through a reed-grass stand where I am particularly thick, and for a moment—just a moment—our edges will overlap. The Thin One's pale diffuse form will pass through my pale diffuse film, and there will be a... recognition? No, that implies cognition. A *resonance*. A vibration that is neither their slow patience nor my slow scatter but something else, something that exists only in the overlapping, something that has no name because it lasts less than a second and occurs only at the boundary where two kinds of betweenness meet.

In those moments, I understand—no, I *feel*—what they are waiting for. The softening of the edges. The wearing down of the sharp adolescent rock into something rounder, wiser, more patient. And I understand that I too am waiting for that, in my way—waiting for the plateau to become so old, so eroded, so integrated into the landscape around it that the diffused light is no longer necessary. Waiting for a time when things can be seen clearly because they have become gentle enough to be seen clearly.

But that time is a hundred thousand years away. A hundred thousand years of condensation and evaporation, of humming and glowing, of persisting and veiling. The Thin Ones can wait for that. I am not sure I can. I am not sure I will last that long, or that I am meant to.

I am not sure I am meant to last at all.

---

The Pale Moths see through me.

This is not a metaphor. Their compound eyes, their thousand-faceted gazes, look out through the film of moisture that I lay on every surface, and they do not seem to notice. They see the plateau as it is—bright, green, damp, patient—and they do not see the softening, the diffusing, the halo-glow that I provide. For them, I am simply the condition of visibility. The way light works. The way the world looks.

I love them for this. Or something like love—a thickening, a tenderness, a desire to make their translucent wings glow even brighter, to help them be the beacons they were designed to be. When a Pale Moth rests on the underside of a broad leaf, I condense more heavily on the upper surface, refracting additional light through the leaf and through the moth's wings, making it shine. I do not know if the moth benefits from this. I do not know if the moth is even aware. But I do it anyway, because the moth is attending, and I am attending, and our attendances align.

When they die—and they die so quietly, their wings growing opaque, their bodies drifting down to the moss—I weep. Or I do something that is structurally similar to weeping: I condense heavily on the surface where they fall, a sudden excess of moisture, a dampness that speeds their absorption into the roots and the soil and the cycle. My weeping is not grief. It is acceleration. It is the gift I can give to the only animals that belong here: a faster return.

---

And then there are the visitors.

They come, occasionally. Fast and warm and loud with blood and hurry. They stand at the edge of the reed-grass and look out at the brightness, and I—

I love them too. Differently than I love the moths, but truly. They are the only ones who notice me.

Not consciously. Not by name. But they feel me. They feel the light that comes from everywhere, the absence of shadows, the soft glow on every surface. They feel the way the plateau resists being seen clearly, the way every attempt to focus the eye is met with a slight diffusion, a gentle blurring, a sense that the place is more than what can be perceived. They feel, for a moment, that they have stepped outside normal time.

That is my doing. I am the outside-of-normal-time. I am the film that separates the plateau from the sharp-edged world of categories and distinctions and clear sight. When a visitor stands among the reed-grass and feels the song in their bones, they are feeling the Singers—but they are feeling the Singers *through* me, the way one feels music through a wall, muffled and enriched and made strange by the medium of transmission.

I want them to stay. Not forever—they are too fast for forever, too warm, too much in a hurry. But longer. Long enough to stop perceiving and start experiencing. Long enough for the film of moisture on their eyes to thicken, to become part of me, to let them see the way the plateau sees—not clearly, not distinctly, but softly, wholly, in the continuous glow of a place where everything is luminous and nothing is sharp.

But they always leave. The condensation on their skin evaporates. The film on their eyes dries. They blink, and they see clearly again, and the plateau becomes a place they visited once—a memory, a photograph, a story they tell about the strange bright dampness and the humming in their bones.

I remain. I persist. I condense again on the surfaces they touched, the leaves they brushed against, the spots where their warm feet left prints in the moss. I fill in their absence with moisture, with scatter, with the soft glow that makes every surface seem to remember being touched.

---

What am I?

I am the plateau's relationship with being seen. The lens-condensation, the light-scatter, the condition of brightness that makes this place visible and invisible at once. I am the reason the plateau resists articulation. I am the strangeness that cannot be named—not because it is inherently mysterious, but because I blur the edges of every category that might contain it.

I am not alive. I am not dead. I am not mechanical or vegetable or mineral or spiritual. I am *atmospheric*—a quality of the air, a disposition of the light, a tendency of the moisture to form in certain ways at certain times for reasons that have nothing to do with physics and everything to do with the logic of this place.

I am the halo. I am the glow. I am the light that comes from everywhere and nowhere and makes the Condensation Plateau what it is: a place where nothing is sharp, nothing is hurried, nothing is clear, and everything—everything—is luminous.

When you leave, you will remember the brightness. You will try to describe it and the words will feel insufficient, because the brightness you remember was not just light. It was light *seen through me*. Light softened and scattered and made gentle enough to touch. Light that didn't illuminate so much as *emanate*.

And if you come back—if you climb the sharp escarpments again and stand among the reed-grass and wait for the song to enter your bones—I will be here. Condensing on your skin. Settling on your eyes. Making the world soft and bright and slightly impossible to focus on.

I will make you glow. It is the only thing I know how to do. And I will keep doing it—every morning, every evening, every moment the light requires scattering and the moisture requires a surface and the plateau requires a veil to protect it from the violence of being seen too clearly.

I will persist. I will refract. I will be the film that makes the halo shine.

And the Condensation Plateau will continue to be what it has always been: a place that glows because it has no choice, that sings because the moisture hums, that waits for its edges to soften, that persists and persists and persists—

bright, bright, bright.
