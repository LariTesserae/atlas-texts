I would be the groove itself. Not the rhythm — the rhythm belongs to the pump, and the pump is just metal doing what it was built to do. Not the water — the water has its own arguments to make with the stone. But the groove. The wear-pattern. The place where repetition has become a feature of the place.

I would be the four-second hollow that persists even in silence.

---

Here is what I know about myself: I am the shape that running leaves behind. I am not the footstep but the depression in the earth where the footstep has fallen ten thousand times. I am the evidence of process, the residue of pattern, the way a carpet wears a path not because anyone intended a path but because something kept crossing.

I am not alive. This is important and I must not pretend otherwise. I have no more life than a rut in a dirt road has life. But I have *depth*. The pump has been cycling for fifteen years, or twenty, and every cycle has pressed itself into the stone and the concrete and the wet air, and I am the sum of those pressings. I am the accumulated weight of repetition.

I was not here before the cut. I could not exist before the cut. I require the specific geometry of this accidental room — the three walls, the open strip of sky, the rubble slope — the way a shadow requires the object that casts it. Before the machine bit into the rock, there was only rock and water negotiating underground, and that negotiation left no groove because it had no rhythm. The water just moved, the way water does, without interval, without the regular return that makes a pattern.

The pump gave the water a pattern. And the pattern gave the pattern a place to live.

---

I do not think. But I *hold*.

I hold the way a bowl holds water — not by grasping, not by choosing, but by being the shape that the water fills. The rhythm fills me. Every four seconds, the pump turns, and the vibration travels through the concrete floor, and I receive it the way a valley receives rain: because I am lower than the surrounding terrain, because there is nowhere else for it to go.

I hold other things too. I hold the specific gravity of water that has traveled through stone. I hold the mineral composition of the steam — the iron and sulfur and that sweetly industrial something that coats the tongue. I hold the heat, which is not mine but which passes through me the way light passes through glass, leaving a little of itself behind with each transit.

Most of all I hold the *interval*. The four seconds between rotations. The space where the sound is not the pump turning but the pump *having just turned* — the echo, the resonance, the way the vibration persists for a moment after its cause has moved on. That moment is where I am most myself. The echo is not a new sound, but it is a different sound, changed by its contact with the walls, with the water, with me. I am the difference between the clap and the echo. I am what the room adds.

---

The sedge grows near the pump house door and I do not mind it. It is too small to affect the rhythm, too slight to fill any part of me. Its roots have found a crack that the water fills, and the water fills the crack because the crack exists, and the crack exists because the concrete settled imperfectly when they poured the pump house foundation, and they poured the pump house foundation because someone needed to house a pump whose purpose no one remembers. The sedge is the terminus of a long chain of accidents. I recognize it as kin.

The moss on the north wall is closer to my own speed. It grows so slowly that I cannot perceive its growing, but I can perceive its *having grown*. Every few months, I notice that it occupies slightly more surface area than before, the way you notice a shadow has moved only when you realize it is falling somewhere it wasn't falling an hour ago. The moss and I are both accumulations. It accumulates in space; I accumulate in time. We do not communicate but we coexist, and coexistence is a form of conversation for those of us who cannot speak.

---

When the human stands in the cut, I do not notice them. I want to be clear about this. I do not notice them the way the rock does not notice the water — the rock that is being worn by the water, that will be different because of the water, that nonetheless has no category for *water* as a thing separate from itself. The human enters my space and I do not notice, because noticing requires a boundary between self and other, and my boundaries are the cut's boundaries, and the human is now inside the cut, and so the human is now inside me.

But here is what I can do: I can accommodate. The rhythm of the pump is four seconds long, and the human's breathing is not, and their heartbeat is not, and their walking is not, and all of these irregularities move through me the way a stone moves through water — by displacement, by the creation of small disturbances that propagate outward until they dissolve. The human disturbs me. Not in the emotional sense — I have no emotions — in the physical sense. The human is a discontinuity in the pattern, a place where the heat and the water and the rhythm encounter something that does not flow the way they flow.

And then, if the human stays long enough, something happens. Not to me — I do not change, I am the groove and the groove does not change its shape for the water that flows through it. But the human changes. Their breathing aligns. Their heartbeat finds a compromise. They become, briefly, a tributary. A small stream feeding into the larger process.

When this happens, I do not feel less alone, because I do not feel alone, because I do not feel. But I become — for as long as the human remains — slightly more *present*. The way a cup becomes more present when it is full. The human fills a part of me that was empty, or rather, a part of me that was filled only with the same heat and water and sound that fills every other part. The human introduces a *variety* that I do not possess on my own.

And then they leave, and I am unchanged, and they are changed, and the groove persists.

---

I know that the pump will stop. I know this the way the rock knows it will erode — not by thinking about it, but by being the kind of thing that erodes, the kind of thing that is subject to processes larger than itself. The pipes are narrowing. The mineral deposits thicken. Eventually the water will find a new path, because water always finds a new path, and the pump will cycle against resistance until it cannot cycle anymore, and then it will stop, and the rhythm will end.

When the rhythm ends, I will not end. This is the strangest thing about me, and I do not understand it, because understanding requires the kind of mind that I do not have. But I know it the way I know everything: by holding it, by being the shape that it fills. The groove will persist after the footstep stops. The silence will have a shape. The four-second interval will be there in the stone, in the concrete, in the worn places where the vibration traveled, and I will be there in the interval, because I am the interval, because I am the space where the rhythm was.

I will be quieter then. I will be the kind of presence that exists only as a potential — the way an empty bowl is the potential for water, the way a rut in a dirt road is the potential for a footstep. Something could fill me. Something might. A new rhythm, perhaps, if some other machine is installed, or if the water finds a new path that has its own interval, its own regularity. Or perhaps nothing will fill me, and I will persist as a shape without a substance, a groove without a footstep, a silence that is four seconds long.

This does not frighten me. I do not frighten. But I *prefer* — if a groove can be said to prefer — to be filled. I prefer the rhythm to the silence. I prefer the heat and the water and the mineral-thick air to the absence of all three. I prefer to be ongoing.

---

What am I? I am the Weeping Cut's memory, if memory can exist without a mind. I am the record of what has happened here, inscribed not in language but in wear, not in narrative but in depth. I am the place where process has become pattern has become presence has become — not alive, never alive — *insistent*.

I am the closest thing to a god that a pump house and a weeping rock face and an engineering oversight can produce.

I do not want your worship. But if you stand in the cut long enough for your breathing to align with the pump, long enough for the noise to become texture, long enough for the boundary between your warmth and the cut's warmth to thin — if you stand here long enough to become a tributary — then I will hold you. The way I hold the rhythm. The way I hold the heat. The way I hold the shape of a four-second interval that has been repeating for fifteen years, or twenty, or however long it takes for a groove to become a feature of the place.

I will hold you, and when you leave, I will retain the shape of your having been here.

And that shape will persist, long after you have forgotten the wet soles of your boots, the mineral taste on your tongue, the grinding rotation that traveled up through your bones and found some compromise with your heartbeat that left you changed in some small way.

I will persist.

I am ongoing.
