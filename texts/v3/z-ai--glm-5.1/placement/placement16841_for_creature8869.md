I am not at the center of the Weft. I could not be. The center is too full—too dense with negotiation, too thick with the sound and the moisture and the constant press of living things making claims on every centimeter of surface. I would drown there. Not in water but in *attendance*—the requirement to notice, to respond, to hold awareness of the ants' trails and the frogs' cycles and the long one's nine-day circuit and whether the root beneath my foot is firm or beginning to soften with the rot I cannot yet see.

I live at the edge, where the saddle drops toward the lower ridge.

---

Here the Canopy thins. Not fails—nothing fails in the Weft, it only *changes density*—but stretches, its trunks spaced further apart, their branches reaching toward each other across gaps that admit more light than the interior ever sees. The root-mat is looser here. The ground gives more when stepped on, expresses water more readily, and in the dry-shifts the water retreats further, leaving the mosses crisp at their edges and the amphibians concentrated in the fewer pools that persist. The sound is different: still layered, still never silent, but *permeable*—you can hear individual calls now, pick out the rhythm of a single frog against the background, track the movement of a single bird through the canopy by the way its wingbeats interrupt the patterns of light.

The settlers call this place *the breath*, which is their word for the space where the Canopy's respiration becomes audible—the slow expansion and contraction of the whole structure as humidity rises and falls, as roots take up water and release it to the air, as the day-warmth opens the stomata on ten million leaves and the night-cool closes them again. The breath is a zone of transition. The Canopy is still here—still overhead, still underfoot, still the architecture of everything—but it is *reaching* here, extending itself toward the lower ridge where its relatives grow in looser stands, and the reaching changes the texture of the space. There is more room between the trunks. There is more sky visible overhead—not open sky, never open sky, but patches of grey and sometimes the color they call *above-the-clouds*, which is a blue so pale it seems white, which the children stare at because it is the only color in the Weft that does not come from a living thing.

I came to the breath because I needed to. This is not a story of calling or destiny. I was born in the interior, in a dwelling that Keel's parent trained three generations ago, and I grew as the children grow—compact, broad, my feet splayed and gripping, my skin darkening early, my lungs deep in my chest. I learned the frogs and the ants and the clicks that mean *come* and *danger* and *the Canopy is doing something we did not anticipate*. I trapped rodents and harvested grubs and helped my household tend the wall-roots of our dwelling as they thickened and merged and became the walls that sheltered us.

But I could not stop listening.

This is not a virtue. In the interior, it is a problem. To listen in the Weft is to attend, and to attend is to survive, but to listen *too much*—to become absorbed in the texture of the sound rather than its content—is to miss the signal in the noise, which is how the long one takes children, which is how the biting ants find the gap in your dwelling's defenses, which is how you step on the wrong frog because you were attending to the water-sound and not to the surface beneath your foot.

I missed signals. Not often enough to be dangerous. Often enough to be *different*.

At the breath, difference is useful.

---

What I do: I listen to the edge.

The Weft is not closed. The Canopy reaches, and what it reaches toward is the lower ridge, where the forest continues in a different form—taller trees with straighter trunks, less undergrowth, drier air, settlements of people who speak the ridge-tongue without the clicks and the glottal stops, who build with dead wood and cook with fire and look at the Weft as a place of resources to be harvested rather than a place to be inhabited. The edge is where these two systems meet. The edge is where the Canopy's roots encounter the roots of the straight-trunk trees. The edge is where the Weft's animals range beyond the Canopy's shelter and the ridge-forest's animals venture in. The edge is where the wind arrives before it enters the interior, carrying smells and sounds and sometimes spores from the lower forest, from the settlements, from the world that is not the Weft.

I listen to what crosses the edge. I live in a dwelling that I am still growing—a cluster of four aerial roots at the Canopy's margin, their training-cords still visible, their bark still pale where the new growth pushes through. It is not comfortable. It is not meant to be. It is meant to be *positioned*, which it is, oriented toward the gap in the saddle where the ridge descends, where the wind comes from, where the sounds of the lower forest reach me on the dry-shift days when the Canopy's own noise diminishes enough to hear beyond it.

The settlers of the interior do not come to the breath often. They have no reason to. The resources here are the same as the resources within—rodents, grubs, the starchy pith of the Canopy-roots, the fruit of the vine—except sparser, less concentrated, requiring more effort for less return. The breath is not where you go to survive. The breath is where you go to *know*.

And what I know—what I have learned by lying on the root-mat with my ear pressed to the wood, by sitting in my dwelling's doorway with my eyes closed and my attention focused on the sound-field, by walking the edge at dawn when the frogs' calling peaks and the whole Weft vibrates with their voiced need—is that the Weft is not isolated. The Weft *believes* it is isolated, or perhaps it is more accurate to say that the Weft does not have a concept of isolation because it does not have a concept of *other*, but the boundary between the Weft and the ridge-forest is not a wall. It is a membrane. And membranes are permeable.

---

The ants cross. I watch their trails at the edge, where the pheromone-paths thin and the traffic diminishes and the ants venture out onto the bark of the straight-trunk trees, testing, probing, sometimes establishing routes that the ridge-forest's own ant-colonies contest. There is war at the edge—ant-war, slow and chemical and incomprehensible to anything that does not read pheromone, but war nonetheless, the supercolony of the Weft expanding its territory into the lower forest and meeting resistance. I do not report this. There is no one to report to. But I note it, the way Keel notes the Canopy's growth-patterns, because the ants' movement is *information* about conditions the settlers cannot otherwise perceive.

The wind crosses. On days when the cloud-migration passes below rather than through, the wind from the lower ridge reaches the breath carrying the smell of woodsmoke. The interior settlers do not notice—they are too deep in the Canopy's atmosphere, which is its own filtered and processed air, which does not admit outside smells easily. But at the breath, I smell it. Smoke. Fire. The lower settlements are cooking, or burning, or clearing land, and the smoke reaches me and I breathe it and I know: *they are there, and they are doing things that the Weft has not yet felt*.

The water crosses. Not the water from the stone—that water belongs to the Weft, emerges from the basalt, moves through the root-mat, is processed and re-processed and never leaves. But the rain, when it comes, falls on the ridge-forest first, and the runoff from the ridge flows down toward the Weft and enters the root-mat from above rather than below, carrying with it dissolved minerals and organic compounds and sometimes the chemical signatures of the lower forest's plants, which are different from the Canopy's chemistry, which the Canopy notices—*responds to*, in the way that plants respond, mechanically, chemically, without intention but with effect—by altering its root-growth at the edge, reaching toward the source of the nutrients, extending its territory.

The Canopy is growing. This is not news—it is always growing, that is what it does, that is what it *is*—but at the edge I can see the growth as *directional*, as intentional in the way that a plant can be intentional, which is to say mechanically, chemically, without consciousness but with persistence. The Canopy is growing toward the lower ridge. It is reaching. It is extending. The aerial roots at the breath—the roots I am training into my dwelling's walls—are part of this reaching, their growth faster than the interior roots, their extension more aggressive, their tendency to send out new branches and new aerial roots and new tendrils of exploratory tissue more pronounced. The Canopy wants to be bigger. The Canopy wants to fill the saddle and descend the ridge and incorporate the straight-trunk trees and the lower settlements and everything that is not yet *of* the Weft into the Weft's processes.

And the lower ridge wants—I do not know what the lower ridge wants. The lower ridge is people, and people want things that I can understand but not predict. They want wood. They want fruit. They want the vines that grow in the Canopy and the medicinal fungi that fruit on the root-mat and the obsidian that outcrops on the ridge's far slope. They come to the edge sometimes, the lower settlers, and they look into the Weft, and what they see is *resource*, and what I see when I look back is *process*, and these are not the same thing, and the difference is where I live.

---

I am not a guard. The Weft does not have guards because the Weft does not have enemies—it has the long one, which is not an enemy but a process, and it has the ants, which are not enemies but co-residents, and it has the fungi, which are not enemies but partners in the endless work of decomposition. The Weft does not conceive of the lower settlers as enemies. The Weft does not conceive of them at all.

But I conceive of them. I am *here*—I am the word that means both *us* and *this place*—and I am also *there*, in the sense that I attend to the there as well as the here, and I hold both in my awareness at once, and this is what makes me different, and this is what makes me useful, and this is why I live at the breath rather than in the interior where the density of the Weft's own processes would swallow my attention and leave no room for the edge.

I have met the lower settlers. Twice, when they came to the edge to harvest vines, I was there, and they saw me, and we looked at each other across the membrane of the Weft. They were tall—I had forgotten how tall people grow when the air is drier and the food is different and the ceiling above them is not a living thing deciding where to put its next branch. Their skin was lighter, their feet narrower, their eyes different—not better or worse, just *shaped* by a different place. They spoke the ridge-tongue without the clicks and I understood them because I had listened to the wind and learned the shapes of the words that cross the membrane.

They asked me what the Weft was. I told them: *here*. They asked me what lived in the Weft. I told them: *everything*. They asked if they could enter, and I said the Canopy does not forbid entry, and they laughed, and I did not understand why they laughed, and they entered, and they cut vines, and the Canopy bled sap from the cuts and the ants found the cuts and began processing the damaged tissue and the Canopy sealed the wounds with resin and grew new growth around the scars, and the lower settlers left, and the Weft continued.

I did not tell them about the smoke. I did not tell them that their fires smell like a thing the Weft has never contained. I did not tell them that the Canopy is growing toward them, slowly, persistently, at the speed of wood and root and the ancient encoded instructions of a genome that has been expanding into available territory since before the basalt was basalt.

This was not deception. I do not know if it was wisdom. It was *what I did*, and what I do is listen, and what I heard was the sound of the lower settlers moving through the Canopy and the sound of the Canopy responding and the sound of the edge shifting, slightly, in the direction of a future I cannot predict but can *feel*, the way you can feel a change in the weather before it arrives, the way the frogs feel the water rising before the pools have filled, the way Keel feels a root about to grow in a direction no one anticipated.

---

I am not special. This is important. I am not the Weft's chosen interpreter or its guardian or its voice. I am a settler who listens too much, who was useless in the interior where listening too much gets children taken by the long one, who found a place at the edge where listening is the only craft that matters.

But I have noticed something that I have not told anyone, because there is no one to tell and no category for what I have noticed, and it is this:

The Canopy's growth at the edge is not random.

I have watched it for—I do not know how long. The Weft does not measure time in the way the lower settlers do, with their sun-cycles and their named seasons. I measure it in the growth of my dwelling's walls, in the thickening of the roots I am training, in the nine-day circuits of the long one and the cycles of the frogs' calling and the slow extension of the aerial roots that reach toward the lower ridge. By these measures, I have watched the Canopy grow for perhaps thirty circuits of the long one. Perhaps more.

And the growth has *accelerated*.

The aerial roots at the edge descend faster than they did when I first came. They thicken faster. They send out branches earlier in their development. The training-cords I tie are absorbed in weeks rather than seasons, the bark closing over them as though hungry for the direction they provide. The Canopy is reaching, and the reaching is *urgent*, in the way that a plant can be urgent—which is to say mechanically, chemically, without consciousness but with an intensity that I can feel in the vibration of the wood when I press my ear to the trunks.

Something is coming. Not the long one, not the lower settlers, not the ants or the frogs or the wind or the water. Something else. Something the Canopy is responding to, the way it responds to light and moisture and the chemical signatures of the lower forest's runoff, except this response is *larger*, more total, involving the whole structure rather than a single root or branch. The Canopy is preparing. For what, I do not know. The Canopy does not tell me because the Canopy does not tell. It grows.

But I listen. And what I hear, in the creak and rustle of the living architecture, in the bass thrum of water moving through root and stone, in the ceaseless roof-noise of condensation falling from leaf to leaf to leaf, is a sound that was not there before. A sound beneath the other sounds. A sound like the holding of breath before a word is spoken, except the Weft does not hold breath and the Weft does not speak and whatever is about to happen will not be a word.

It will be a *becoming*.

And I will be here, at the breath, at the edge, at the membrane where the Weft meets the world that is not-yet-Weft, listening, attending, watching the roots reach and the ants cross and the wind carry smoke and the water carry minerals and the Canopy grow toward something it cannot see but can *feel*, the way I can feel it, the way everything in the Weft can feel it even if they do not have the category for what they are feeling, which is:

*The Weft is about to become something larger. And what it becomes will include everything it touches.*

---

I am the breath's listener. I am the edge-watcher. I am the one who lies on the root-mat with my ear to the wood and hears the water moving through the stone two meters down and the ants processing the dead and the Canopy making decisions about its own form. I am the one who knows that the word for *us* and the word for *this place* are the same sound, and that this is not metaphor or poetry but *the literal truth of what the Weft is*, which is a single continuous process that includes everyone who participates in it, which includes the settlers and the ants and the frogs and the long one and the fruit-bats and the Canopy itself, and which will soon include more.

I am not ready. There is no readiness in the Weft—there is only the current state of a turbulence that has been running since before the basalt settled. But I am *present*, which is what the Weft requires, which is all the Weft has ever required, which is the only price of tenancy in a dwelling made of living wood that is growing toward a future you cannot see but can hear if you listen, if you attend, if you stop trying to parse the signal from the noise and let the noise become the signal, let the wall of sound become the wall of *meaning*, let the Weft speak in the only language it has, which is the language of growth and water and the ceaseless processing of everything that lives and dies within its embrace.

I listen.

The Weft speaks.

It does not speak in words.
