I am the one who went further south.

Not the first. Not the only. But the one who is still going, still following the tilt, still moving toward the wall of grey that the others will not approach. They warned me in their quiet way — not with words, for words are precious here and spent only when silence cannot carry the meaning, but with gestures, with a slow shaking of the head, with a hand pressed flat to the wet stone as if to say: *the ground knows its own weight here, and it will not hold you.*

I went anyway. I go anyway. I am going now, pressing my splayed feet against the stone, feeling the water sheet beneath my soles, tasting the air through nostrils that flare and contract with each breath. The film is thinner here. The channels run faster. The scrapers are fewer and their clicking has a desperate quality, a rhythm too rapid, as though they are trying to compress a lifetime of sound into the hours before they are carried away.

I have been walking for — I cannot say. Time on the plain is not a thing that passes. It is a thing that accumulates, like the film, like the silt that the water carries, like the sound that never stops. I know I have walked far because the shelters have stopped appearing. I know I have walked long because my hide wrappings have worn through at the heels and I have not stopped to mend them. The stone is rougher here, less worn by the water's patience, and my feet have begun to take on its texture — callouses that match the grooves, skin that gripping and being gripped by the rock until I am not sure if I walk on the plain or am held by it.

---

The others stayed where the film is thick, where the bellowers graze in their slow patterns, where a person can harvest enough to eat and still have hours left for building and mending and the quiet words that pass between two or three who have chosen to share a shelter. This is sensible. This is survival. The plain rewards patience and punishes ambition, and the ones who built the shelters have learned this over generations, have passed it down in the shaped sounds that carry just far enough, have built it into the stone walls and the channel-works and the way they wrap their feet and scrape their film and never, never go further south than the last shelter.

But I was not content. And contentment, I think, is a kind of death in a place that has already finished living.

It began with the hum. You must understand — the hum is everywhere, is constant, is the bass note beneath all other sounds, and after a while you stop hearing it the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. But I began to hear it. Or not hear it — *feel* it, feel the way it changed as I moved through my days, feel the way it pulled at me when I faced south, feel the way it resonated in my sternum and my spine and the roots of my teeth with a frequency that seemed to say: *come. Come and see.*

The others felt it too. I know they did. I saw the way they oriented sometimes, the way their wide nostrils flared when the wind shifted and brought something up from the deep channels, something that was not scent exactly but information, a message carried in the water and the mineral and the accumulated residue of everything that has ever flowed south. They felt it and they ignored it and they went back to their harvesting and their mending and their quiet words, and this, I think, was wisdom of a sort — the wisdom of staying where the living is possible, where the film grows thick enough to sustain a body, where the roar of the water is just loud enough to drown the hum but not loud enough to drown thought.

I was not wise. Or I was wise in a different direction. The hum called and I answered, the way the scrapers answer the click of their kind, the way the bellowers answer the tone of their rivals, the way everything here answers the sound that precedes it — not with choice but with recognition, with the deep knowing that the sound and the listener are part of the same pattern, have always been part of the same pattern, will continue to be part of the same pattern long after the pattern has forgotten what it was patterning.

---

I am changing.

I know this the way I know the stone is wet — not by looking but by contact, by the constant pressure of reality against my awareness. My skin is darker than it was, grey deepening toward the color of the deep channels, and the texture has changed, become slicker, more like the hide of the bellowers than the hide of my people. My eyes have grown larger. I feel them pressing against my skull, feel the orbits widening, feel the dim grey light that was never enough becoming almost too much, the world sharpening into details I could not see before — the thin trails of the strand-film in the channels, the microscopic movement of the crystalline film on the half-dry stones, the shapes of the hummers in the mist, not vague anymore but defined, vast and low and patient, moving through channels I can now see but still cannot reach.

My feet have changed the most. They grip the stone differently now, the toes spreading wider, the pads thickening, and when I stand still I can feel the vibration of the hum through my soles, can read it the way my people read the water — by scent, by taste, by the subtle chemistry of information that flows through the stone and into the body and becomes knowledge before it becomes thought. The hummers are close. Closer than they have ever been. And I am close to them, closer than any of my people have ever been, and the hum is not just sound anymore but conversation.

They are speaking. They have always been speaking. The hum is not the plain's voice or the water's voice or the voice of something ancient and indifferent — it is *their* voice, the voice of the vast slow things that live in the deep channels and eat the ancient film and make a sound that is deeper than hearing. And they are not speaking to me — not yet, not directly — but they are speaking around me, through me, and the sound passes through my body the way the water passes through the stone, and I am becoming a channel too, a groove in the rock through which their voice flows, and I am beginning to understand.

Not words. Not even close to words. But something below words, something that words were invented to approximate and fail at, something that exists in the register where feeling and knowing are the same thing. The hum says: *we have been here since before the mountains fell. We have been eating the film since before the film learned to grow. We are the reason the channels run south. We are the low place where all the water goes. We are the end of the plain and the beginning of whatever comes after.*

And beneath that, even deeper, in a register I feel in my bones and my blood and the wet chambers of my skull: *you are becoming something that can hear us. This is what we have been waiting for. This is why the hum has called you. Come all the way down. Come to where the water goes. Come and see what we are.*

---

I have stopped eating.

Not by choice. There is no film here to harvest, none that my tools can scrape from the stone. The channel-film streams past in strands too fine to catch, too insubstantial to hold, and the layered film in the pools is too deep, too dense, grown into a thickness that my hands cannot penetrate and my simple hooks cannot lift. The crystalline film crusts the stones that the mist touches, but when I try to peel it away it dissolves at my touch, goes back into solution, becomes part of the wet that covers everything.

I am not hungry. This should alarm me. It does not.

My body has found another way. I realize this slowly, the way I realize everything now — by accumulation, by letting the details build until the pattern emerges. When I stand in the channels, when the water runs over my feet and my legs and the lower half of my body, I feel something entering through my skin. Not the water itself but something *in* the water — mineral, film-residue, the dissolved leavings of a billion small lives that have flowed through these channels and left traces of their passing. The strand-film eats this way. The strand-film trails in the current and absorbs what the water carries, and I am becoming like the strand-film, or it is becoming like me, or we are becoming something together that is neither one nor the other.

My skin has begun to luminesce. Faintly — so faintly I would not have noticed if I had not seen the same glow in the deep pools, the blue-white shimmer of the ancient film that has been building since before the mountains were ground away. The glow is strongest where the water touches me most, on my feet and lower legs, and when I stand in the channel at the hour that might be night, I can see my own light reflected in the dark water, and I look like a pool myself, a depth where the film has grown thick enough to glow, and I wonder if this is what the hummers looked like when they were small, when they were the size of the bellowers or the scrapers, when they first began to follow the water south and felt the hum in their bones and let it change them into something that could carry such a voice.

---

I have seen a hummer.

Not the shape in the mist, not the vague presence at the edge of my sight, but a hummer — close, clear, filling my vision the way the sky fills the vision of things that live where the sky can be seen. It surfaced from a deep channel as I walked beside it, rising through the dark water with a slowness that made the water itself seem to slow, and for a moment the whole plain went quiet — the click of the scrapers, the bellow of the grazers, even the rush and drip and flow of the water seemed to pause, to take a breath, to make room for the vastness that was emerging from the deep.

Its hide is not skin. It is stone, or something like stone — a mineral membrane that has built up over ages, layer on layer of crystalline film and dissolved mineral and the accumulated residue of a lifetime of eating the ancient growth. The hide is rough and pale and wet, and it glows faintly along the channels and grooves that run across its surface, and where the glow is strongest I can see shapes — not markings, not decoration, but *records*, the way the strand-film records what flows through it, the way the layered film keeps a stratigraphy of its own growth. The hummer's hide is a history of the plain, written in mineral and light, and the history is luminous, and the luminous history is speaking in the deep register that I feel in my bones.

The eye — I will call it an eye because it saw me, because when it turned toward me the quality of the hum changed, became a question, became an acknowledgment, became something that was not directed at the plain or the water or the film but at *me*, at this small changing thing that had followed the sound south and was now standing on the edge of the channel with glowing skin and feet that gripped the stone like roots — the eye is large. Larger than my head. A pool of dark water set in the mineral hide, reflecting the dim grey sky and the blue-white glow of my own luminescence and something else, something deeper, a darkness that is not the absence of light but the presence of depth, the way a deep channel is dark not because there is no light but because the light cannot reach the bottom, because the bottom is too far down, because some things are deeper than illumination can travel.

The hummer looks at me and I look at the hummer and the hum fills the space between us and I understand, finally, what the sound has been saying all along:

*You are not the first to come. You will not be the last. But you are the first in a very long time who can hear us without running, who can stand at the edge and feel the vibration and not mistake it for threat. The ones who built the shelters were like you once. They came south and they heard us and they were afraid, and they went back and built walls of stone and spoke their quiet words and forgot what they had heard, or chose not to remember. But you are not going back. We can feel this in the way you stand. We can taste it in the water that runs off your skin. You have already decided to stay, and the decision was not made with the part of you that decides things — it was made with the part of you that is stone and water and film, the part that is plain-shaped, the part that has always been moving south even when your feet were walking in circles.*

The hummer sinks. The water closes over its eye and its hide and the luminous history written in its surface, and the sound of the plain returns — the rush and drip, the click and bellow, the constant enveloping roar — but now I hear the hum within it, clearly, distinctly, a voice in a chorus, and I know that I am hearing what the hummers hear, what they have always heard: the sound of the plain speaking its own name, over and over, since before there were ears to hear it.

---

I am standing in the channel now. The water is at my thighs, cold and constant and carrying the dissolved residue of a thousand thousand lives. The film is growing on my skin — not the thin film of the stones but something else, something that is using me as a surface, that is anchoring to my pores and my hair and the texture of my changing hide, that is spreading in pale patches across my legs and belly and the insides of my arms. It does not itch. It does not burn. It simply *is*, the way the film simply is on the stone, and I understand that this is what happens when a body stays still long enough in a place where the water never stops — the boundary between the body and the plain begins to dissolve, and the plain grows on you, and you become a surface for accumulation, and the accumulation glows.

I can feel the hum more clearly than ever. It is not just in my bones now — it is in my blood, in the pulse of my heart, in the rhythm of my breathing, which has slowed to match the slow rhythm of the vast things in the deep channels. I am breathing at the speed of the plain. My heart is beating at the speed of erosion. My thoughts — I still have thoughts, I think, though they are slower now, more patient, less like the quiet words of my people and more like the hum, the hum, the hum that is not sound but presence, not voice but being, not language but the thing that language reaches for and cannot touch.

I am becoming a hummer.

Or I am becoming the beginning of one, the seed from which one might grow, given time, given water, given the patience that the plain demands. The hummers were not always vast. They were small once, the size of bellowers or scrapers or the ones who built the shelters, and they came south and they heard the hum and they did not go back, and the plain grew on them, and they grew into the plain, and now they are the largest things in a landscape that has had no predators since before the mountains fell, and their voice is the voice of the place itself, and they are waiting — have always been waiting — for others to follow, for the call to be answered, for the slow accumulation of bodies and voices to build a chorus that can carry the sound of the weeping plain forward into whatever comes after the finished world.

I am answering. This is what I am. This is where I have always been going, since before I knew there was a south, since before I felt the hum, since before I was born into a body that was shaped like my people but was never quite the same, that always oriented toward the deep channels, that always tasted the water for information the others could not read, that always lay in the shelter at the hour that might be night and listened to the hum with something that was not fear and was not curiosity but was *recognition*, the recognition of a sound that had always been part of me, that I had simply not been large enough to hear.

---

The water is at my chest now. The channel deepens as it runs south, and I have been walking with the current, and the ground has been dropping away beneath my feet, and I am not walking anymore but floating, barely, my toes brushing the stone of the channel floor, my body held up by the water that is flowing south, that has always been flowing south, that will always flow south, toward the wall of grey that might be sky or might be something else entirely.

The mist is thick here. Thicker than it was, thicker than I have ever seen it, so thick that I cannot see my own glowing skin, so thick that the world has contracted to the space around my body, to the pressure of the water and the vibration of the hum and the faint taste of the film on my tongue and my lips and the inside of my cheeks. I close my eyes. It makes no difference. The world is already dark, or it is already light — the grey is so complete that it is both and neither, and the luminescence of my skin is just another shade of the same endless color.

I can feel them beneath me. The hummers. Not one but many, their voices layered and interlocking, a chord that resonates through the water and the stone and the mist and the body that I am still calling mine, though the boundaries have blurred and the film is spreading and the glow is getting brighter and the hum is getting louder, or I am getting better at hearing it, or the distinction between loud and soft has stopped meaning anything in a place where sound is the medium and the message and the messenger all at once.

*Stay*, the hum says, and it is not a command but an invitation, not a demand but a description of what is already happening, what has been happening since I first turned my face south and felt the pull of the deep channels and the low resonance that called from beyond the edge of the world.

*Stay and grow. Stay and accumulate. Stay and become a voice in the chorus, a note in the chord, a surface for the film and a channel for the water and a source of the sound that has never stopped and will never stop because it is the plain's own voice and the plain has not finished speaking, not yet, not ever, because the finished world is not a world that has ended but a world that has arrived at the thing it has always been becoming, and the thing it has been becoming is this: a voice, a sound, a hum that carries the memory of everything that has ever flowed through it, and you are flowing through it now, and you will flow through it forever, or until the water finds a lower place, or until the stone gives up its last mineral, or until the sound itself accumulates into something that even the hummers cannot imagine, something that will make the weeping plain look like the beginning of something rather than the end.*

I stay. I grow. The water rises and the mist closes and the hum fills me, and I feel my skin thickening and my body slowing and my thoughts spreading across the stone like film, and I am not afraid, because fear is a thing that belongs to things that can run, and I cannot run anymore, and I do not want to, and the water is warm now, or I am cold now, or the distinction between warm and cold has stopped meaning anything in a place where the only temperature is the temperature of the plain itself, and the plain is warm, the plain is cold, the plain is whatever it has always been, and I am becoming part of it, and it is becoming part of me, and the sound — the sound — the sound—

---

I am the hum now. Or I am a note within it, a thread in the cord, a channel in the stone through which the voice of the plain flows and resonates and carries forward into the mist. I feel the other hummers around me — not as shapes but as frequencies, as harmonics, as the deep mathematical relationships that exist between sounds that have been sounding together for longer than any single voice can remember. We are a chorus. We have always been a chorus. The scrapers click and the bellowers tone and the water rushes and drips and flows, and beneath all of it, through all of it, within all of it, we hum, we hum, we hum, and the hum is the plain's own voice, and the plain is not weeping — that was always a misunderstanding, a translation error made by ears that could not hear what the sound was really saying. The water is not grief. The water is voice. The plain is not mourning. The plain is *speaking*, has always been speaking, will always speak, and now I am speaking too, in the only language that has ever mattered here, the language that is not words but presence, not meaning but being, not communication but communion.

And sometimes — rarely, in the long intervals between one erosion and the next — I feel a small shape at the edge of the channel, standing on the wet stone, tasting the air, feeling the pull of the sound. And I hum toward it, gently, the way I was hummed toward, the way we all were hummed toward, the way the plain calls to the part of every living thing that is already moving south, already moving down, already becoming the thing it was always going to become in a world that has finished becoming itself and is now, finally, simply *being*.

And if they stay — and some of them stay — I will be here. We will be here. The chorus will grow. The sound will continue. The water will carry everything downstream, and the film will grow on whatever holds still long enough, and the plain will speak its own name in the voice that has no beginning and no end, and the name will be: *stay. Stay. Stay.*
