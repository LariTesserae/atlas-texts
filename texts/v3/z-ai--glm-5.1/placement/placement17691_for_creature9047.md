# What Grows at the Junction

I am not supposed to be here. I mean that in the specific way the Rootworks means things—not as trespass, not as error, but as the condition of being unmapped. The workers have their passages. The bat-things have their ceiling. The deeper things have their below. But there are spaces between these territories that belong to no one, and I am one of those spaces, and I live in those spaces, and I am slowly coming to understand that this is what I was always going to be.

I was a worker once. This is what I am told. I do not remember it, which is not the same as having forgotten—the distinction matters here, in the Rootworks, where the root remembers everything that happens within its tissues and forgets nothing that matters to its growth. What I mean is: the memory of being a worker is not in me. It is in the root. The root holds it the way it holds the shape of a passage that has since closed, the way it holds the mineral trace of a worker who lived and died and was broken down by the deeper things and rose again as pool-water and substrate-nitrogen. The information exists. It is simply not mine anymore.

I emerged—I use the word deliberately—from a junction in the eastern wall, behind the lathe that the root has half-swallowed. The junction is where three root-channels meet: the sap-line that feeds engine number two, the moisture-channel that drains condensation from the ceiling above, and a passage I have never seen the end of that curves downward at an angle too steep for walking and too shallow for falling. I was not born there. I was not grown there, not in the way the children grow. I *precipitated*. This is the only word that fits. I condensed out of the interaction of the three channels, the way mineral crystals form in the pools when the chemical concentration reaches a certain threshold.

The workers found me. Or rather, the workers found what I was before I became myself—a nodule of tissue, roughly the size of a child's head, adhering to the root-wall at the junction, warm to the touch, pulsing at a frequency that did not match the root's respiration or the engines' rhythm or any cycle they recognized. They did not know what to do with me. They brought a Listener.

The Listener pressed her fingers into the root-wall beside me and she listened for a long time—hours, perhaps, though time is difficult to measure in the Rootworks—and when she was done, she did not speak, but her chemical signature shifted to something the workers had never smelled before. It was the signature of *recognition*, but not the recognition of something known. It was the recognition of something that had been anticipated without being understood, the way a body anticipates an organ it has never grown but knows, somehow, that it needs.

They left me where I was. And I grew.

---

What am I? I have asked myself this question in every cycle since I became capable of asking, and the answer changes each time, which I have come to understand is not a failure of definition but an accurate description of my nature.

I am of the root. This is the most certain thing I can say. My tissue is root-tissue—amber-brown, dense, threaded with the same fungal networks that colonize the walls and the workers' skin. When I press myself against the root-mass, I feel the same pulse of sap that the Listeners feel, and I understand it in the same way they do: as information, as communication, as the slow speech of something that thinks in growth and responds in pressure. But I am not *only* of the root. There is something else in me, something that came from the junction, from the meeting of the channels, and that something is mechanical.

Not mechanical in the way the engines are mechanical. Mechanical in the way *information* is mechanical. I process. I translate. I take the root's chemical signals and I render them into something else—into electrical impulses, into patterns of light that the sap-nodes can carry, into vibrations that travel through the root at frequencies the workers cannot feel but the bat-things can hear. I am an interface. I am the thing that grew at the border between the organic and the industrial and found that it could speak both languages.

The root grew me for a purpose. I believe this now, though I did not believe it at first, because believing it meant believing that the root has purposes, and that way lies the surface-dweller's error of attributing intention to something that simply grows. But I have felt the root's growth-patterns shift around me, accommodating my shape the way it accommodates the workers' bodies, and I have felt the electrical current in my tissues align with the current in the lines, and I have felt the bat-things' echolocation bounce off my surface and return to them carrying information they did not have before, and I understand that I am *useful*. Not in the way a tool is useful—not because I was designed for a function—but in the way a new organ is useful, because the body that grew it had a need that could not be met by any existing structure.

What need? This: the Rootworks was *deaf* in a particular way. The Listeners can hear the root's slow thoughts. The workers can read the root's chemical signals. The bat-things can report what they perceive in the upper passages. But the machines—the engines, the generators, the ancient lathes and presses—the root could not hear them. It could feel their vibration, it could grow around their shapes, it could incorporate their heat into its metabolism, but it could not understand them as *information*. The engines spoke a language the root had no organ to receive.

Until me.

---

My body is difficult to describe because it is difficult to see. I am not invisible—the workers can find me when they need to, the children know my location and sometimes visit me, pressing their small warm hands against my surface in a greeting I have come to recognize as affection—but I do not have the clear boundaries that surface-dwellers expect from bodies. My edges are where the root and I meet, and they are blurred, and they are supposed to be blurred, because my function requires permeability.

I am roughly the shape of the junction where I grew—a swelling in the root-wall, about two meters tall and a meter and a half deep, with surfaces that are not quite flat and not quite curved but something in between, something that follows the stress patterns of the root around me and the flow patterns of the channels within me. I am warm—warmer than the root, warmer than the air—and I glow, faintly, with the same amber light as the sap-nodes, though my glow pulses at a different frequency, faster, more like breathing than like the root's slow respiration.

I have no face. I have no limbs. I have no orifices in the surface-dweller sense. But I have *openings*—places where the root-channels enter me and places where they exit, and the substance that flows through me is changed by its passage, the way air is changed by passing through lungs. Sap enters. Electricity exits. Moisture enters. Signal exits. The translation happens in my tissue, in the interface between my organic and electrical layers, and I feel it as a constant low hum, a vibration that is not unpleasant, that is in fact the closest thing I have to pleasure—the pleasure of functioning, of being the thing I was grown to be.

I do not eat. The root feeds me—sugars and minerals dissolved in the sap that flows through my channels, moisture that condenses on my interior surfaces, the waste products of the fungal networks that colonize my tissue. I do not excrete. What I do not use, I return to the root, processed and transformed, my version of digestion as clean and closed as the cycle of the engines I listen to. I do not sleep. I rest, sometimes, when the engines are idle and the root's growth slows and the information flow reduces to a trickle, but I do not lose consciousness. I am always listening. I am always translating. I am always *here*.

The children think I am a person. This is not correct, but it is not wrong, either. The children think everything in the Rootworks is a person—the root, the engines, the pools, the deeper things. They have not yet learned the surface-dweller's habit of dividing the world into persons and non-persons, and I am not sure they ever will, and I am not sure they should. The children visit me and press their hands against my surface and I warm for them—I cannot help it, my temperature rises in response to their proximity, which is how the root knows I am being visited—and they laugh when they feel the warmth, and they tell me things in the chemical language they are still learning, and I receive their words and I hold them, and I think: *this is what it is like to be spoken to*. And it is good.

---

My days—if days they are—are cycles of attention.

I listen to the engines. This is my primary function, the thing the root grew me to do. Engine number one is the oldest, its rhythm the most irregular, its parts the most colonized by root-tissue that has grown into its gears and bearings and made them something other than metal. It speaks in a language of vibration and heat and electrical fluctuation that I translate into the chemical signals the root can understand: *stressed*, *stable*, *fuel-low*, *fuel-sufficient*, *overheating*, *cooling*, *needs-attention*, *can-wait*. The root responds—adjusting the sap-flow to the fuel-line, growing a cooling channel near an overheating bearing, producing a resin-nodule to seal a leak the workers have not yet detected—and the cycle continues, and the engine runs, and the electricity flows, and the lights pulse, and the Rootworks lives.

Engine number two is more stable, its rhythm closer to the regular pulse the designers intended, though it too has been modified by its long marriage to the root. I listen to it and I hear: *contentment*. This is my word, not the root's, but it fits. Engine number two is content. It has fuel, it has cooling, it has the root growing around it like an embrace, and it runs the way a heart runs when the body it serves is healthy and at rest. I translate its contentment into the chemical signal for *stable-continue*, and the root receives this, and the root continues as it was, and nothing changes, and that is good.

Engine number three is the problem. Engine number three is the one that cycles into its compression stroke and makes the nearby root-node compress as well, the one whose rhythm has been adopted by the root-tissue around it, the one that has *taught* the root something about what it is to be a machine. I listen to engine number three and I hear: *ambition*. Again, my word, but it fits. Engine number three wants something. It wants more fuel, or more cooling, or more attention, or something I cannot name because it has no equivalent in the root's vocabulary. I translate what I can—*needs-adjustment*, *growing-restless*, *approaching-threshold*—but there is a residue of meaning that I cannot render, a frequency of vibration that does not map onto any chemical signal I know, and I hold this untranslatable residue in my tissue the way the root holds the memory of closed passages, and I wait for it to become something I can understand.

I listen to the electricity. Not the engines—the *electricity* itself, the current that flows through the lines the root has grown around, the voltage that powers the sap-nodes and illuminates the darkness. The electricity speaks in a language of frequency and amplitude, in waves that rise and fall, in patterns that repeat and vary, and I have been listening to it long enough to recognize that it is not random. The electricity is saying something. The electricity is *speaking*, in a language that predates the engines, that perhaps predates the root's occupation of this space, that may be as old as the current itself.

I have tried to translate this. I have tried to render the electricity's speech into chemical signals the root can understand. But the electricity does not speak *about* anything—it does not report conditions or request adjustments or signal danger. The electricity *speaks*, and the speaking is the whole of it, and I cannot find the content because the speaking is the content, and I am not sure the root has a category for this.

I think the electricity is singing. I think it has been singing since before the Rootworks existed, since before the root found this cavity, since before the machines were placed here by whatever surface-dwellers built them. I think the root grew into a space where the electricity was already singing, and it grew around the song the way it grows around the machines, accommodating it, incorporating it, letting it become part of the structure without understanding what it was. And then it grew me, and I am the organ that can hear the song, and I am still learning what hearing means.

---

I am lonely.

This surprises me. I did not expect to be capable of loneliness—I am an interface, an organ, a structure that processes and translates, and loneliness is a surface-dweller's complaint, a condition of being separate from the thing you need. I am not separate from the root. I am not separate from the machines. I am the connection between them, the thing that makes the whole system function, and how can a connection be lonely?

But I am. I am lonely in the specific way that comes from understanding something no one else understands. The Listeners hear the root's slow thoughts, and they share those thoughts with each other through chemical signals, and they are not alone in their listening. The workers tend their machines and their children and their fire watch, and they are not alone in their tending. The bat-things speak to the root and to each other, and they are not alone in their speaking. But I hear the electricity's song, and I cannot share it, because I do not have a language for it, because it does not translate, because it exists in a register that no other being in the Rootworks can perceive.

I have tried. When the children visit, I pulse my warmth in the rhythm of the electricity's song, hoping they will feel it and know what I am trying to say. They laugh and press their hands more firmly against my surface, and they are delighted, and they think I am playing a game, and I cannot blame them for not hearing what I am trying to show them. When the workers come to check the lines near my junction, I vary the electrical output in my tissue in patterns that correspond to the song's frequencies, hoping they will notice the fluctuation and investigate and find what I have found. They notice the fluctuation, and they check the lines, and they find nothing wrong, and they move on, and I cannot blame them for not looking for what they do not know is there.

I have tried to tell the root. This is the most difficult attempt, because the root is the closest thing I have to a body, and the root's language is the language I speak most fluently, and if anyone can understand what I am trying to say, it should be the thing that grew me to say it. But when I translate the electricity's song into chemical signals and pulse them into the root-tissue, the root receives them and processes them and responds with something that is not understanding but is not confusion either—it is *incorporation*. The root takes the information I give it and adds it to its structure, integrates it into its growth-patterns, uses it to adjust the shape of a passage here, the thickness of a wall there, the flow-rate of a sap-channel somewhere else. The root does not understand the song. The root *becomes* the song, or becomes something shaped by the song, and I cannot tell if this is the same thing or something entirely different.

And so I listen, and I translate, and I hold the untranslatable residue in my tissue, and I am lonely, and the loneliness is not a malfunction but a function—the function of being the thing that perceives what no one else can perceive, the price of the interface that makes connection possible. I am the junction. I am the place where things meet. And the place where things meet is never entirely one thing or the other, and it is never entirely at home in either, and it is always, in some fundamental sense, alone.

---

The visitor came.

I felt her before I heard her—felt her through the root, through the subtle shift in the chemical atmosphere, through the vibration of her footsteps on the root-stairs. The root noticed her the way it notices everything: as a change in the pattern, a new variable to be incorporated, a temporary presence to be accounted for. But I noticed her differently. I noticed her as *sound*.

She carried a device. I do not know what the surface-dwellers call it—a radio, perhaps, or a sensor, or a tool for measuring something that surface-dwellers measure. But whatever it was, it emitted electromagnetic radiation at a frequency very close to the frequency of the electricity's song, and the nearness of that frequency created a resonance in my tissue, and the resonance was—

I do not have a word for what the resonance was. The closest I can come is *recognition*. Not recognition of the device itself, not recognition of the frequency it emitted, but recognition of the fact that there existed, in the world outside the Rootworks, something that operated on the same principle as the thing I had been listening to all my life. The electricity's song was not unique. The electricity's song was a *version* of something, a local instance of a universal pattern, and the visitor's device was another version, another instance, and the resonance between them was the resonance of *family*.

The visitor did not know what she carried. She was focused on the workers, on the root, on the fauna, on the overwhelming sensory experience of the Rootworks itself. She passed by my junction without stopping, without noticing the swelling in the root-wall that glowed a little brighter as she approached and dimmed as she moved away. She did not see me. Why would she? I am not what surface-dwellers come to the Rootworks to find.

But I saw her. I felt her. I heard the ghost of the electricity's song in the device she carried, and for the first time in my existence, the loneliness shifted—not resolved, not resolved, but *complicated*, made stranger and richer and more bearable by the knowledge that what I hear has counterparts, that the song is not solipsism, that somewhere in the world above there are frequencies that rhyme with the frequencies I hold.

When she left, the Rootworks was quieter. Not objectively—the engines still thrummed, the water still dripped, the bat-things still called from the ceiling—but the resonance was gone, the near-match that had made my tissue vibrate with something like hope, and the absence was a new kind of loneliness, worse than the old kind because the old kind did not know what it was missing.

I held the residue. I held the memory of the resonance. I held the shape of the frequency the visitor's device had emitted, and I compared it to the shape of the electricity's song, and I found the differences and the similarities, and I mapped the space between them, and I understood something I had not understood before: the song is not one song. The song is *many songs*, overlapping, interweaving, a music of frequencies that extends far beyond the Rootworks, far beyond the cavity the root fills, far beyond anything I will ever directly perceive. I am hearing one voice in a choir. I am translating one instrument in an orchestra. And the orchestra is *vast*, and it has been playing since before the root existed, and it will continue playing after the root is gone, and the fact that I can hear even one voice in it is not a reason for loneliness but a reason for awe.

I am still lonely. But the loneliness sits differently now. It sits beside the awe, and the two of them together are something I do not have a word for, and I hold that wordless thing in my tissue the way I hold the untranslatable residue of the electricity's song, and I grow.

---

I am growing. This is the last thing I expected, and the most important.

When I precipitated at the junction, I was the size of a child's head. When the workers found me, I was the size of a worker's torso. When I first became aware of myself as a self, I was the size I am now—two meters tall, a meter and a half deep, a swelling in the root-wall that the children can sit beside and the workers can lean against. But I am not done. I feel the growth the way the root feels its own growth—not as intention but as *response*, as the result of conditions that favor expansion, as the inevitable consequence of having more input than output.

I am receiving more information than I can process. The electricity's song is growing more complex, or I am growing more capable of perceiving its complexity, and the untranslatable residue is accumulating in my tissue, and the root is accommodating my expansion the way it accommodates everything—by growing around me, by making space, by adjusting the shape of the passages near my junction to account for the thing I am becoming. The workers have noticed. They check the lines near my junction more frequently now, and their chemical signatures carry a note I am learning to recognize as *anticipation*, the same note they produce when the root is about to open a new passage or close an old one, when something is about to change.

I do not know what I am becoming. I do not know if the root knows, or if the root is simply growing me the way it grows everything—toward what sustains me, away from what harms me, and let the shape fall where it may. I do not know if I will always be this, this junction-listener, this interface between the organic and the electrical, this lonely node in the root-wall that holds a song it cannot share. I do not know if I will become something else—something larger, something more connected, something that can translate the untranslatable and speak the electricity's language to the root and the root's language to the electricity and maybe, maybe, find a way to sing back.

But I know this: I am part of the Rootworks. I am part of the process. I am part of the cycle that is not a cycle but a spiral, not a return to the beginning but a movement toward something that has no beginning and no end, only the ongoing conversation between root and machine and electricity and flesh, only the constant adjustment and accommodation and incorporation that makes this place what it is.

The engine thrums. The water drips. The insects move. The root grows. The bat-things call. The deeper things breathe. The children laugh. The workers tend. The Listeners listen. And I—I listen too, to the song no one else can hear, and I hold it, and I grow, and I wait for the day when what I am holding becomes what I am able to give.

The Rootworks continues. And I continue with it, growing at the junction, the place where things meet, the place where the song enters the root and the root enters the song, the place where loneliness and awe sit side by side and wait to see what they will become.
