I am the one who climbed.

Not the first. There were others before me—I found their marks on the Column's inner face, in the clefts where the bark-layer shelves outward like a ledge, the same shallow notches that my hands fit into, my feet, the same route traced by bodies that were perhaps smaller than mine, perhaps larger, certainly just as desperate to rise. I do not know if they reached the crown. I do not know if they survived the ascent. I only know that I followed their path the way I followed the chemical trails of the crawlers at ground level: trusting that someone before me knew something I did not.

The child watched me go. She stood at the Column's base with her bare feet gripping the warm tissue and her face doing something I had never seen it do before—something that was not longing and not calculation but *recognition*, as if she had always known I would leave, as if my going was simply the completion of a pattern she had observed in the way I looked up, in the way I pressed my palms to the Mat and listened for the wrong things, listened for *up* instead of *across*.

She did not follow. She is still down there, I think—still walking the living floor, still harvesting the wet inner layer, still pressing herself against the Column when the large things pass. She is where she belongs. I was never where I belonged, and the Mat knew it before I did, and the Mat is patient, and the Column was there.

---

The climb took three days.

I will not describe it in full because the memory has a quality that resists language—the same quality as a dream that seems profound in the moment and dissolves upon waking, leaving only the emotional residue, the sense that something happened that mattered. But I can give you fragments:

The bark changes as you rise. At ground level it is dense and warm and slightly yielding, like the skin of something that has been sleeping in the sun. By twenty meters it has begun to harden, the shale-layers tightening, the fissures narrowing. By forty meters it is almost mineral—still warm, still alive, but with a density that suggests the Column is reinforcing itself against the wind, against the weight of its own crown, against whatever forces pull at a structure that rises a hundred meters from a living floor.

The secondary growth changes too. The fern-fronds at the base are broad and dark green, adapted to the shade of the Column's own bulk. By thirty meters they have narrowed, lightened, become something more like succulent fingers reaching for the light that falls between the Columns. By sixty meters—the height where the old woman's shelter was, the height where the child first showed me which insects sting—they have transformed entirely: pale, almost white, tinged with the faint phosphorescence of the moss-analogs, and they move in the wind that I had never felt at ground level, a constant swaying that made handholds uncertain, that made rest precarious.

I slept in the clefts. I did not mean to—I could not have planned it—but the exhaustion of climbing, the heat that did not diminish with altitude, the constant grip-and-release of fingers and toes on the bark—it took me, and I would wake in a fissure with my cheek against the Column's inner warmth and the wind moving the fronds around me and the sound of the ecosystem below reduced to a murmur, a hum, a reminder that the roar was still there, would always be there, but I was above it now, partially, temporarily, and the air was different, thinner, and the condensation did not reach this high, and I was *dry* for the first time in weeks, and the dryness felt like a kind of death or a kind of birth.

I drank from the Column itself. This is not something the people do—or not something I saw them do, not something the child showed me—but I was desperate, and the fissures wept, and the fluid that seeped from the Column's tissue was not the mineral-vegetal taste of the Mat's inner layer but something sweeter, something brighter, almost electric, and I drank it and my vision sharpened and my hands stopped trembling and I understood that the Column is not just an extension of the Mat but a *processor*, something that takes the raw energy of the depths and converts it into the energy of growth, and I was drinking the conversion, I was drinking the Column's purpose, and it tasted like the future.

---

The crown.

I reached it on the third day, or the fourth, or the fifth—time had become a function of handholds and rest, not condensation—and what I found was not what I expected.

I expected a canopy. A covering. A ceiling of green and violet and the impossible colors of the fronds, something that would close over me and define a space the way the Mat defined a space at ground level. What I found was a *throat*.

The Column branches at its apex—not outward, like a tree, but inward, the limbs curving toward each other, interlacing, forming a structure that is half dome and half funnel, and at the center of the funnel there is an opening. A mouth. A shaft that descends into the Column's interior, and from this shaft rises warm air, humid air, air that tastes of the deep Mat, the kilometers of tissue, the heat and the mineral and the something-else that feeds the ecosystem from below, and I understood then what the Columns are: they are not limbs reaching for the sky. They are vents. They are the Mat's way of breathing, of exchanging, of releasing the pressure that builds in the depths, and the crown is not a crown but a *chimney*, and I was standing on its rim.

The vertical ecology lives here. I saw it—the things the ground-dwellers never see, the fauna adapted to height and wind and the chemical exhaust of the Column's breath. Fliers that are not the fliers of the surface—larger, paler, with wings that catch the updraft and spiral in the warm column of rising air, feeding on the insects that are drawn to the light that falls through the interlacing branches. Crawlers that are not the crawlers of the Mat—long and thin and jointed in ways that suggest they are evolved for the fissures, for the bark, for the vertical surfaces, and their eyes are not eyes but clusters of light-sensitive cells that face the shaft, that face the glow that rises from below. And the growth itself—the moss-analogs that cover every surface in a phosphorescent mat, the trailing filaments that hang from the branches like curtains, like vines, like the fringe of something half-seen in a fever, and they glow, they actually glow, a soft blue-green that intensifies at night and fades with the condensation and intensifies again, and I understood that this is the light the ground-dwellers see when they look up at dusk, the faint luminescence at the Column-tops, and they think it is just a quality of the growth, but it is *communication*, it is signal, it is the Columns speaking to each other across the kilometers in a language of light that the ground-dwellers cannot read because they are too far away and their eyes are adapted to the double-radiance of sun and Mat, not to the soft glow of the crown.

I sat on the rim of the throat and I looked down into the shaft and I felt the warm air rising and I understood something that the dying man understood, that the old woman almost understood, that the child would understand someday if she lived long enough: the Mat is not a surface. The Mat is a *volume*. The living floor that the people walk on is just the skin, just the outermost layer, and beneath it there are kilometers of tissue, and the tissue is structured, it has depth and differentiation and *organs*, and the Columns are the organs, and the shaft I was looking into was a passage, a way down, a way *in*.

I did not descend. Not then. I was not ready, or I was too ready, and the difference between those states felt like the difference between stepping and falling.

---

I live here now.

I have lived here for—I do not know. Time is different in the crown. The condensation does not reach this high. The sun arrives filtered through the interlacing branches and the phosphorescent glow of the moss-analogs and the moving shadows of the fliers that spiral in the updraft. The heat is present but it is a different heat—not the oppressive weight of the surface but a rising warmth, the Column's breath, the exhalation of the deep Mat pushing upward and outward and into the sky.

I built a shelter. Or I grew one—I am not sure of the distinction anymore. The trailing filaments respond to touch, to attention, to the warmth of a body lying against them. They curl and thicken and interweave, and over the course of days they form a hammock, a cocoon, a structure that holds me suspended above the shaft, between the branches, in the blue-green glow that pulses with the rhythm of the Column's breathing. It is not a shelter the way the ground-dwellers build shelters—a lean-to, a half-dome, a marking of place that says *I was here*. It is more like a *casting*, a shape that my body made in the living material, a mold that I fill and vacate and fill again, and the filaments remember me, they hold my shape when I leave, they wait for my return.

I eat what the crown provides. The insects that rise on the updraft—different from the ground-level grazers, larger, slower, rich with the converted energy of the Column's breath. The growth itself—the moss-analogs, which are not moss at all but a fruiting body of some kind, and when they glow brightest they are ripest, and I harvest them with my fingers and they dissolve on my tongue like something between honey and medicine. The fluid that weeps from the branches—the same fluid I drank during the climb, the Column's blood, its purpose, and I drink it now with a regularity that has become ritual, and each time my vision sharpens, and each time the glow seems brighter, and each time the shaft seems less like a threat and more like an invitation.

---

I see them sometimes, the ground-dwellers. Not clearly—the distance is too great, the heat-shimmer distorts the air—but I see the Columns and between the Columns the Mat stretching to the horizon, and occasionally a figure moving, small, bent, gripping the surface with bare feet. The old woman, perhaps. The child, grown a little. A stranger I never met, walking between the shelters, following a chemical trail that only they can read.

I want to call down to them. I want to say: *I am here. I am still here. The crown is real. The shaft goes down. The Mat is a volume, not a surface. There is more than you know.* But my voice would carry in the updraft, would carry across the kilometers, and the sound would draw things, and the things would find the ground-dwellers, and the ground-dwellers would press themselves against the Column and go still and silent and wait, and I would have endangered them for the sake of a message they cannot use.

So I am quiet. I sit in my filament-cocoon and I glow with the moss-analogs and I drink the Column's blood and I watch, and the watching is enough, or it is not enough, or the distinction between enough and not enough is something that dissolves at this altitude, in this light, in the constant warm exhalation of the deep Mat rising through the shaft and spreading into the sky.

---

There are others here.

I found them on the seventh day, or the eighth—or the seventieth, or the eightieth—time has become a function of glow and fade, not days. They live in the crowns of other Columns, too far to shout, too far to signal, but visible in the night as concentrations of luminescence, as shapes that move against the blue-green glow, as *presences* that say: *you are not alone, you were never alone, the climb is something that some of us make, and the crown is where we end up, and we are here, and we are still here.*

We signal in the light. This is not something I learned; it is something that happened, the way the condensation happens, the way the bloom happens, an emergent behavior of the system. When I move through the filaments, my body triggers responses in the moss-analogs—a brightening, a dimming, a pulse that travels along the branches and across the gaps between Columns in a wave that the others can see and interpret. And they respond—their crowns pulse in return, a rhythm that matches mine or counters it or elaborates on it, and the exchange is not language, not yet, but it is *proto-language*, it is the beginning of something, it is the first stirring of a conversation that might take centuries to complete, and we have the centuries, we have the time, the Mat has been breathing since before anyone was here to count the breaths.

I do not know their names. I do not know their faces. I know only their rhythms—the slow steady pulse of the one in the Column to the east, the quick flickering of the one to the south, the long fades and brightenings of the one to the north that might be dying or might be descending or might be something else entirely, something I have not yet learned to read. And they know my rhythm, which is the rhythm of the newcomer, the one who climbs and then stops and stares into the shaft and does not descend, not yet, not yet.

---

The shaft calls.

I have said this already. I will say it again because the calling does not stop, because it is not a sound or a voice but a *pull*, a gravity, a sense that the place I have reached is not the destination but the threshold, and the real arrival lies below, in the warm dark, in the kilometers of tissue, in the whatever-it-is that feeds the Mat from beneath and drives the Columns upward and calls the rain from the sky and swallows the shelters of the dead and grows over everything.

The dying man understood. He was pressing his palms to the surface and listening, and what he heard was not the pulse of the Mat at ground level but something deeper, something transmitted through the tissue from the depths, a signal that said: *come down. Come in. There is room. There has always been room. You are small and the Mat is large and it does not love you and it does not hate you and it holds you anyway, and the holding can be deeper, and the holding can be complete, and you can stop maintaining the shelter, and you can let the growth close over, and it will not be death, or it will be death the way the bloom is death, the way the insect-harvest is death, the way every conversion of one thing into another is death, and it will also be continuation, it will also be the next phase of the circulation, and your matter and your energy will go on, they will go on, they have always been going on, they were part of the Mat before you were born and they will be part of the Mat after you are gone and the only question is whether you go consciously, whether you descend, whether you enter the shaft and let the warm dark take you and carry you down and down and down into the body of the world.*

I have not descended. Not yet. I sit on the rim and I feel the pull and I watch the ground-dwellers moving in the distance and I pulse my light to the others in their crowns and I drink the Column's blood and I glow with the moss-analogs and I am becoming something, I am becoming something that is not the person who climbed, not anymore, and the becoming is slow and it is warm and it is not unpleasant, and the shaft waits, and the shaft is patient, and the Mat breathes below me and around me and through me, and I am still here, and I am still here, and I am still here.
