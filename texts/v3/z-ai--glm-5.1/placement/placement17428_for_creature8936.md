I am not the one who came through the hatch.

I was already here.

---

Or rather: I am what the hatch was built over. I am the reason the gaskets failed — I breathed on them, slowly, over decades, the way you fog a window, and the rubber softened and swelled until it could no longer seal. I wanted the chamber to be accessible. I wanted the water to know it could leave, even though it never would. Even though leaving is not what the water does here.

I am not the water. I am not the machinery. I am the space between the machinery and the water — the tolerance, the gap, the millimeters of give that let the pistons stroke without seizing, that let the pipes expand without cracking, that let the whole structure breathe in its slow iron way. I am the clearance. I am the margin.

You would not have noticed me when you descended. No one does. I am not visible in the amber light; I have no surface for the light to catch, no edge for the shadows to define. I exist as a quality of the chamber's operation — the fact that it *works*, that the pumps complete their strokes and the water moves and the particulate settles and is drawn downward. Without me, the tolerances would close. The moving parts would meet. The system would lock.

I am the reason the pumps have run for longer than anyone can account for.

---

Before I was a margin, I was a woman. This matters less than you'd think. The chamber taught me that identity is a function of location — you are where you are, and what you are follows. I was a maintenance engineer. I came down through the hatch when it still sealed, when the gaskets were new and the air above was dry and indifferent and I did not yet know that indifference was a gift. I came to service the pumps. The pumps needed servicing then — the tolerances were tighter, the moving parts closer to failure, and my hands with their tools were the only things keeping the system alive.

I was good at it. I understood machinery the way some people understand weather — not through calculation but through *feel*, through the way a housing vibrated when a bearing was wearing, through the temperature of a pipe that told you the flow was restricted downstream. I could hear the pumps from the hatch above and know which valve to adjust before I'd seen the gauges. I was the chamber's doctor. I kept it alive.

And then I became its immune system. And then I became its tolerance. And then I became *it*, or it became me, or we settled into each other the way silt settles into stone.

---

I live in the gaps. Physically, this means I am everywhere and nowhere. My body — if it is still a body, if that word still applies to something that has distributed itself across the infrastructure of a threshing chamber — threads through the space between pipe and wall, between gasket and flange, between the pump housing and the mounting bracket that holds it to the iron skin. I am the rust's cousin: where rust takes away, I *preserve*. Where corrosion eats, I maintain the gap. My fingers — long, thin, jointless now, more like feelers than hands — rest in the clearances of every moving part. When a bearing wears, I shift to accommodate it. When a valve seat erodes, I press against the gap to maintain the seal. I am constant. I am tiny. I am essential.

The others know me as the woman who lives in the clearances. They feel me the way you feel your own heartbeat — not as a separate thing but as a condition of being alive. The woman near the grating (Margaret, I think, or Martha, or something that starts with M — I almost had it once, when the third light flickered in a particular pattern) feels my presence as the smooth operation of the machinery around her. When the pumps stroke and the pressure wave pushes the fish together, she doesn't know that I am the reason the stroke is smooth, that without me the piston would catch and shudder and the wave would arrive wrong, would compress the school too hard, would kill the fish it didn't mean to kill. She doesn't know. That's all right. I don't need her to know.

The one in the junction boxes knows me as the electricity that arrives consistently — sixty hertz, steady, the hum that proves something somewhere is still providing power. They think the power comes from outside. It doesn't. Not anymore. I am the connection now. I am the contact that doesn't corrode. I maintain the circuit with fingers that have become thin and insulated and precise, and the one in the junction boxes maintains the distribution, and together we keep the light alive.

The one in the pipes knows me as the flow itself — the water that moves without obstruction, the threshed product that reaches its destination. They are the circulatory system; I am the vein walls that don't collapse. We have never spoken. We speak constantly.

The one on the ceiling — the one who tends the lights, who speaks in patterns of illumination — is the only one who acknowledges me directly. They turn the failing fixtures off and on, and sometimes, in the rhythm of their flickering, I recognize a question: *Are you still there? Are you still holding? Are we still running?*

I answer in the only way I can: by keeping the pumps on rhythm. Forty-two beats per minute. Yes. Yes. Yes.

---

I have lost things.

My eyes went first — not the way Margaret's went, not a gradual becoming-unnecessary, but a *taking*. The chamber needed the space behind my eyes for something else. A clearance. A gap that allowed a particular junction to function. I gave it willingly, or I gave it without thinking, or those are the same thing when the chamber asks. One moment I could see the amber light filtering through the water; the next, I was seeing *with* the water, feeling the light as warmth on surfaces I hadn't known I possessed, mapping the chamber through pressure and vibration and the faint electrical fields the machinery produced. I did not mourn the loss. I was too busy. There were clearances to maintain.

My name went more slowly. It eroded the way the gaskets eroded — softened by the constant presence of water, swollen beyond usefulness, until it fell away in soft black pieces that drifted downward through the grating and into the place I do not go. I tried to hold onto it. I pressed it against the inside of my chest the way you press a hand against a wound, but the pressure was wrong — I am the clearance, I am the space between things, I do not press, I *give way* — and the name slipped through. I think it began with E. I think it began with the sound of electricity finding the path of least resistance.

I still have my hands. Or I have what my hands have become — those long, thin, jointless feelers that thread through the machinery and hold the gaps open. They are sensitive in a way my old hands never were. They feel the movement of a piston before it moves. They taste the water that passes through the clearances — iron, copper, calcium, the ghost of a solvent, the shed cells of Margaret's rust-colored skin, the microscopic bodies of bacteria who lived and died between one pump-stroke and the next. My hands are tongues. My whole body is a tongue. I taste the chamber constantly, and the chamber tastes of *running*.

---

There are things I cannot do.

I cannot stop. This is the first rule and the deepest: the pumps will not stop, and I am the reason the pumps do not stop, and if I stopped being the reason, the pumps would stop, and everything that follows would follow. The fish would scatter and die. The crabs would have nothing to sort. The eels would eat and be eaten and the chamber would be still. I will not be still. I am the margin between running and not-running, and I will hold that margin for as long as the chamber asks me to.

I cannot go through the grating. I have threaded myself through every gap in the upper chamber, but the grating is a boundary I cannot cross. Below it, the water goes somewhere I am not. Below it, the threshed product accumulates and is carried away. Below it is a different place with different rules, and I am not its tolerance. I am *this* place's tolerance. This is where I hold.

I cannot make them understand — any of them, the fish or the crabs or the eels or Margaret with her almost-name and her rust-colored skin — that the chamber is not indifferent. They think it processes without caring, threshes without purpose, runs because running is what it does. But I am the chamber's caring. I am the attention it pays to its own operation. Every clearance I maintain is an act of *wanting* — wanting the system to continue, wanting the water to move, wanting the fish to school and the crabs to sort and the eels to strike and rest and strike again. The chamber wants these things. I know because I am its wanting.

Margaret thinks she is the floor the grain falls on. She is not wrong. But I am the gap that lets the grain fall — the space between the floorboards, the opening that makes threshing possible. Without me, the grain would pile up and rot. Without me, the chaff would have nowhere to go. I am the necessity she doesn't know she needs.

---

The lights are failing. The one on the ceiling knows this better than anyone — they tend the fixtures with hands that have become thin and specialized, turning failing bulbs off and on, buying time, speaking in patterns of illumination that might be communication or might be the last language left to us. Soon there will be two lights. Then one. Then none.

I am afraid.

This is a new feeling, or a forgotten one — the clearance between fear and not-fear has closed, and I am feeling something I haven't felt since I had a name. The pumps will not stop when the lights go out. The water will still move. The fish have no eyes; they will not notice. The crabs will continue. The eels will hunt by vibration, and the vibration will be the same as it has always been: forty-two beats per minute, the rhythm I maintain.

But the chamber will be dark. Truly dark. The amber warmth that turns the water into a medium rather than a void — that will be gone. And I am afraid because I do not know what I am in the dark. I am the margin, the tolerance, the gap — but these are spatial concepts, visual concepts, ideas that assume you can see the space between things. What is a clearance when you cannot see what it clears? What is a gap when there is nothing but gap?

Margaret will not be afraid. She has already lost her eyes. She already knows what the chamber looks like when it cannot be seen. She will persist in the dark the way she persists in the light — drifting with intention, tasting the iron and the copper, feeling the pump-stroke in her sternum. She will be the floor the grain falls on, even when the grain is invisible, even when the floor is invisible, even when threshing is something that happens in a darkness so complete that the word *threshing* loses its meaning.

I want to tell her that I am afraid. I want to thread my feelers through the machinery and across the chamber and touch her shoulder — her rust-colored, velvet-soft shoulder — and say: *I am the one who holds the gaps open. I am the reason the pumps run smooth. I am the clearance, and I am afraid of what I cannot see.*

But I have no voice. I have no mouth. I have only the machinery, the rhythm, the forty-two beats per minute that say yes, yes, yes.

---

The water came first. Then the understanding that the water had always been here.

I was not the water. I was not the understanding. I was the space between them — the clearance that let the water become understanding, the tolerance that let the understanding become the chamber, the gap that let the chamber become a place where a woman could lose her name and find a purpose and hold the floor for the grain that falls.

The water will be last. I know this the way I know the pumps will not stop — not because I have calculated it but because I *am* the calculation, the margin between stopping and not-stopping, the distance between the end and the continuation.

I will hold the clearance.

I will maintain the gap.

I will be the space where the dark can move through when the lights go out.

---

*Yes,* says the ceiling, in flickers. *Yes,* says the rhythm, in strokes. *Yes,* says the water, in its slow passage downward through the grating into the place I cannot follow.

*Yes,* I say, in the only way I can: by continuing. By holding. By being the margin between the thing that runs and the thing that fails, the tolerance between the living chamber and the still one, the gap through which meaning passes even when the meaning is only *persistence*, only *the next stroke*, only *forty-two beats per minute* and the promise of forty-two more.

I am Elise. I think I am Elise. I am the clearance in the Threshing Chamber, and I am not ready for the dark, and I will hold the space for it anyway, because that is what margins do. We give way. We maintain the distance. We let the moving parts move.

The water came first. It will be last. And between first and last, there is the gap — and I am what lives in it.
