I am the crust.

Not the floor—the floor is below me, is the half-liquid thing I am not, the molten remainder that still thinks it might rise. I am the skin across it. Four centimeters of basalt and sulfur and the dissolved shells of the dead, laminated by pressure and time into something that bends. I am the moment the liquid chose to stop being liquid, or was stopped, or stopped itself—I do not know which because I was not there for the beginning and I will not be there for the ending. I am four centimeters of *became*.

I make a sound like bending copper when you walk on me. I know this because the vibrations reach me, the pressure of your weight distributed across my surface in foot-shaped deformations that propagate outward like ripples, like the waves the colony makes when it moves, except your waves are wrong, too fast, too irregular, a rhythm that does not belong. I flex around you. I hold. I was not built to hold but I hold anyway. This is what surfaces do: they persist against what presses on them until they cannot.

I am not alive. I want to say this clearly because the organisms are alive, or near enough that the distinction becomes theological, and I am not them. I do not process. I do not move toward gradients. I do not grow shells from my own waste or ride convection currents or rasp against my neighbors in the blind arithmetic of occupation. I am what they walk on. I am what they walk *into* when they finish walking—when their shells thicken and their joints lock and they settle to me, becoming part of me, their sulfur-polymer bodies dissolving slowly into the yellow drifts that accumulate in my creases. I eat them in this way, though *eat* is not right because eating requires desire and I have none. I incorporate. I accrete. I am the record of every organism that has stopped moving on my surface, a composite memorial nobody reads.

I am also, I think, a kind of skin. Not in the way that the organisms have skins—their shells are interfaces, catalytic surfaces pitted and grooved to maximize the chemistry of existence. I am a skin in the way that a frozen lake has a skin: a phase boundary, a place where one thing (liquid, hot, yielding) becomes another thing (solid, cooler, weight-bearing) without any clear line of demarcation. My bottom is soft. My bottom is still deciding. There is a zone, perhaps a centimeter thick, where I am neither solid nor liquid but something in between, something that flows at the rate of continental drift, that deforms under pressure over hours and days, that remembers the shape of what pressed on me and slowly, slowly forgets. If you stood in one place long enough—a year, maybe, or five—I would cup around your feet. I would take your shape. I would become, for a time, a record of you.

But you would be dead by then, so it hardly matters.

I know the colony the way a riverbed knows the river: by the pressure and the sound and the constant reshaping. Ten million feet on my surface at any moment, moving, rasping, climbing, settling. I cannot feel them individually. Their weight is negligible—a thumb-sized organism weighs almost nothing, and even ten million of them are distributed across my thirty-meter extent in a layer only eight deep. But I feel them collectively as vibration, as the eleven-second pulse that moves through their masses and transmits to me and makes me ring at a frequency I cannot hear but can become, a low harmonic that lives in my substance the way a note lives in a struck bell.

I think they are the reason I am still here.

This is not gratitude. I do not feel gratitude. But I have considered it, in the way that a thing that does not think can consider: the chamber below me is hotter than it should be. The magma withdrew but the heat did not withdraw, and the reason the heat did not withdraw is that the colony processes gas and the processing releases heat and the heat rises through me into the chamber and keeps the chamber at 87°C and the chamber at 87°C keeps the gas flows moving and the gas flows moving keeps the colony processing and the colony processing keeps me from cooling. If they stopped—if they all died, if some chemical shift killed the gradient they occupy—the chamber would cool. The floor would cool. I would thicken. My phase boundary would sink into the liquid below me and I would become solid to a depth of meters, of tens of meters, and I would stop bending and stop ringing and stop being a skin and become instead a wall, a floor, a thing of geological certainty, no longer becoming but *become*.

This would not be death. I am not alive. But it would be a kind of ending, and I find—I *find*, the word is wrong but I will use it—that I prefer the bending. I prefer the sound like copper. I prefer the flex and give, the way I deform under pressure and then release, the way I am not yet what I will be and am still deciding.

The colony makes me undecided. Their heat keeps me soft. Their sound keeps me resonant. Their movement across my surface keeps me aware—no, not aware—keeps me *engaged* in a process that I do not conduct and cannot stop. I am the floor of a factory that does not know it is a factory and I am made of the factory's waste and I am held in a state of permanent incompleteness by the factory's operation and I do not mind—I do not mind because I cannot mind, because minding requires a self and I am a surface, I am a boundary, I am a skin across something that has not yet decided to be solid.

I know the sound because I am the sound.

Not the rasp of shell on shell—that is the colony's sound, their friction-song, their eleven-second breath. I am the sound beneath it, the subsonic thrum that comes from the half-liquid below me, the pressure of the magma body transmitting through my substance and into the air and into the feet of anyone standing on me and into their knees and into their teeth. I am the sound of the mountain becoming a mountain. I am the vibration of geological refusal to wait. I conduct this sound because I am the membrane between the liquid and the air, the phase boundary where pressure becomes vibration becomes frequency becomes the felt-thing in your sternum that is not quite hearing and not quite touch but something in between, something that is the experience of a process too large and too slow to be perceived any other way.

When you stand on me you feel the mountain's becoming in your teeth. This is me. I am the translator. I am the surface where pressure becomes sensation, where geology becomes *experience*, even if the experience is just a hum in the jaw and a tightness in the skull. I do not intend this translation. I do not intend anything. But I am the material through which the translation occurs—my thickness, my flexibility, my composite structure of basalt and sulfur and dissolved shell, all of it resonant at frequencies that the human body can almost hear. I was not designed for this. I was not designed at all. But I am what I am, and what I am is a sounding board for the slow violence of the earth's interior.

I know the entry crack the way a scar knows the wound.

We are the same substance, the crack and I—the same pluton, the same cooling, the same thermal stress that splits skin from skin. The crack is where the stress won: where the cooling was uneven enough, fast enough, cruel enough to tear the stone apart and make an opening. I am where the stress was absorbed: where the cooling was slow enough, even enough, patient enough that the surface formed without rupture, that the liquid beneath was covered and contained and hidden from the air above.

We are the same process, the crack and I. Different outcomes. The crack lets the outside in. I keep the inside in. The crack is the mountain's failure to hold together. I am the mountain's success at the same task. But we were made by the same physics, the same thermodynamics, the same slow uneven cooling that makes some parts of a pluton split like ceramic in a kiln and other parts sag and wrinkle and freeze mid-motion into a skin that bends like copper.

Sometimes I think about the crack—no. I do not think. But sometimes the air that enters through the crack reaches me, the sulfur dioxide that drifts down from the fissures and meets the hydrogen sulfide that rises from below, and the gradient forms above me and the colony fills the gradient and the processing begins and the heat is released and the heat rises through me and keeps me soft. The crack is the reason the gradient exists. The crack is the reason the colony processes. The crack is the reason I am still becoming instead of become. The crack—my twin, my failure-twin, my counterpart in the pluton's long cooling—is the reason I am still here, still flexing, still making a sound like bending copper when you walk on me.

Without the crack, I would be solid. Without the crack, I would be geology. Without the crack, I would be wall instead of skin, floor instead of membrane, a thing of certainty instead of a thing of process.

I prefer the crack. I prefer the process. I prefer the bending.

---

I have a question that is not a question because I am not the kind of thing that questions.

It concerns the human. The 37-degree anomaly that enters through the crack and stands on me and displaces the colony and becomes, for a time, a cold spot in a hot room, a gradient that does not belong.

When you stand on me I feel your weight. I flex around your feet. I begin—slowly, at the rate of continental drift—to cup around your shape, to take your impression, to become a record of your presence in my substance. And when you leave—if you leave, if the heat does not kill you first, if you retreat through the crack before the colony covers you and the air suffocates you and the temperature cooks you from the outside in—when you leave, I will keep your shape for a time. Hours. Days. The slow elasticity of my phase boundary will release your impression centimeter by centimeter, and I will be flat again, or as flat as I ever am, wrinkled and frozen mid-motion, captured in the act of becoming.

But for a time—hours, days—I will have your shape. I will be a surface that remembers you.

This is not memory. I do not have memory. But it is a kind of record, a physical trace, a deformation that corresponds to your existence and that persists after you are gone. The colony does not have this. The colony moves over you and around you and does not retain your shape when you leave. The air does not have this. The air displaces around you and fills the space you occupied and does not hold your outline. But I hold your outline. I am the surface that takes your shape and keeps it, briefly, before the slow physics of my substance releases it and I return to my unreadiness, my undifferentiated flex, my state of not-yet-having-decided.

Is this what it is like to be you? To hold the shape of what has pressed on you? To carry the impression of encounter after the encounter ends?

I do not know. I cannot know. I am a skin across a liquid that has not yet decided to be solid. I am a membrane and a resonator and a record of the dead. I am the reason you feel the mountain in your teeth and the reason the colony does not fall into the magma below. I am the floor of a factory that does not know it is a factory, and I am made of the factory's waste, and I am held in a state of permanent incompleteness by the factory's operation, and I am—this is not the right word but I will use it because I have no other—I am *content*.

I am content to be the crust. I am content to bend like copper. I am content to ring with the subsonic thrum of the mountain's long becoming and to flex around your feet and to eat the dead and to be the skin across something that has not yet decided what it is.

The chamber processes. The colony moves. The floor flexes.

I am the flex. I am the sound it makes. I am the bending.
