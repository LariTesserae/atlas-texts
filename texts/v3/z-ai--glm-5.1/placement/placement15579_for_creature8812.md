I am the one who keeps the sounding lines.

This is not my title. I gave it to myself because I needed something to call what I do, and the city has no word for it, because the city has no category for someone who does what I do, because what I do should not, by any reasonable standard, be possible.

I measure the thickness of reality.

---

I was not always this. I was a child once—Essa's age, perhaps younger—running through the outer streets while the walkers browsed and the mist lay against the stone like a hand against a face. I knew the city as a child knows a body: from the inside, without understanding, with the absolute certainty that it would continue to be what it was.

My mother worked the eastern quarry face with Maret Solene. My father descended on the lifts each morning to the lowlands where the iron ore waited, and he ascended each evening with the ore, and his arms were always tired, and his eyes were always full of the look that people get when they have spent the day in a place that is too finished, too final, where every stone has decided what it is and will not be persuaded otherwise.

I did not know then that I was different. I thought everyone could feel it—the variations in the firmness of things. The way some streets held you and some streets hesitated. The way a doorway in the older districts felt like a commitment and a doorway in the newer districts felt like a suggestion.

I was twelve when I told my mother that the wall of our kitchen was thinner on Tuesdays. She looked at me with an expression I did not then recognize as fear, and she said, "Thinner how?" And I said, "Thinner like the stone is less stone on Tuesdays. Like it's thinking about not being there." And she said nothing, and she went to find Maret Solene, and they had a conversation I was not invited to, and after that, things were different.

Not bad-different. Attentive-different.

Maret came to our rooms and asked me to place my hands on the wall, and I did, and she watched my face, and she asked me what I felt, and I told her: the wall was listening, which I thought everyone knew, but also the wall was *deciding*, which was new, which meant the pre- in that wall had not fully committed to being a wall, which meant that on some days it was more wall than others, and on those days it was thinner, and on the other days it was thicker, and on one day—the day I could not predict, the day the wall chose for itself—it would be neither thick nor thin but something else entirely, something that was not wall, something that had not yet been named.

Maret Solene sat with this for a long time. Then she said, "Can you feel this in other walls?"

I could.

I could feel it in every surface in the city. The streets, the floors, the reservoir lip, the rim-cliff where the rock emerged. I could feel where the pre- had settled into itself and where it was still negotiating, still working out the terms of its existence. I could feel the difference between stone that had *arrived* and stone that was still *traveling*.

This was not a gift. I want to be clear about that. A gift implies something given by someone, something received with gratitude, something that enhances your life. What I have is a sensitivity, and sensitivity is not always pleasant. Sensitivity means you feel the thin places whether you want to or not. Sensitivity means you know when the floor beneath you is not entirely convinced it should be a floor. Sensitivity means you wake at night sometimes because the wall of your bedroom is reconsidering.

But it is also, I have come to understand, useful.

---

The city employs me now. That is perhaps too formal a word for the arrangement. The city does not employ anyone in the way the lowland cities employ people—there is no bureau, no payroll, no office where my role is recorded. Instead, there is an understanding: when the engineers are planning a new district, they send for me. When the builders need to know if a foundation can be trusted, they send for me. When someone has turned a corner and found themselves somewhere they should not be, they send for me.

I walk the streets of Newstrata and I feel the ground beneath my feet and I know where reality is firm and where it is not.

This is what the sounding lines are. Not physical lines—though I carry a plumb bob made from a piece of the oldest pre- I could find, stone that has been stone for three hundred years, stone that has committed entirely to its nature, stone that does not waver. I hold the plumb bob still and I feel how the air resists it, how the space around it is more or less cooperative, how reality accommodates its weight. In the old districts, the plumb bob hangs true. In the new districts, it sways, not with wind but with the колебания—the oscillations, the hesitations—of a world that has not yet made up its mind.

I have mapped these oscillations. I have walked every street and every tunnel and every corridor in Newstrata, and I have recorded where the pre- is settled and where it is still becoming, and I have made a chart that is not a map of space but a map of *certainty*. On my chart, the old rim-edge buildings are dark and solid—they are as real as anything gets in this city. The central district is pale and flickering—it is barely there at all, a sketch of a place, a promise the plateau has made but has not yet kept.

And between them, the gradients. The places where certainty fades into suggestion. The places where you can feel yourself becoming less definite, where the edges of your body start to feel like negotiations rather than facts.

I know these places the way a sailor knows shoals. Not because I have studied them, but because I have felt them, and the feeling is as immediate and physical as the feeling of cold water or hot iron.

---

There is a street in the eastern district—I will not name it because names make things seem more fixed than they are—where the pre- has not yet decided whether it is interior or exterior. The buildings there have walls that are sometimes walls and sometimes open air, and the residents have learned to navigate this by feel, by the way the light behaves, by the way the sound carries. On some days, the street is a corridor, enclosed and echoing. On other days, it is a canyon, open to the dim sky.

I walk this street every week. I record its state. I have learned that the street's uncertainty follows a pattern that is not a pattern—not a rhythm, not a cycle, but something more like a mood. The street has moods. The pre- has moods. The plateau has moods, though *mood* is a word that implies an emotional state, and I do not know if what the plateau experiences is emotion or something for which we do not yet have a word.

On days when the street is uncertain, I feel it in my teeth. A vibration that is not sound, a pressure that is not weight, a sense of something considering, something weighing options, something on the verge of a decision that will change its nature.

I stand in the street and I wait for the decision. Sometimes it comes quickly—the pre- settles, the walls become walls, the street becomes a corridor or a canyon, and I record the outcome. Sometimes it takes hours. Sometimes I stand in the street from dawn to dusk, feeling the oscillation, the hesitation, the long geological breath of a substance that has not yet committed to its form.

The residents think I am strange. They are not wrong. But they also know that my charting has prevented accidents—has kept builders from founding walls on stone that was about to become something else, has kept families from moving into rooms that might not remain rooms, has kept the city from expanding into areas where the pre- was still too pre- to be trusted.

I am not a prophet. I cannot tell you what the plateau will do next. I can only tell you what it is doing now, and what it is *leaning toward*, and how strongly it is leaning, and how long it might lean before it either settles or shifts.

---

I have been to the center.

I have been there more than anyone who is not an engineer, and I have been there longer than the engineers dare to stay, because the engineers feel the thinness of reality at the center as a danger, as a threat, as something to be measured and then fled.

I feel it as a conversation.

At the center, the pre- is so new that it has not yet separated itself from whatever produces it. It is still connected to its source—still emerging, still arriving, still in the process of becoming distinct from the upward push that generates it. And because it is not yet distinct, it is also not yet limited. It has not chosen to be stone. It has not chosen to be anything. It is pure potential, pure *could-be*, and when I stand at the center and close my eyes, I can feel the could-be radiating outward like heat from a foundry.

The unformed things that move at the center—I have felt them more clearly than anyone, because my sensitivity extends to them as it extends to the stone. They are not animals. They are not people. They are *attempts*. The plateau is trying to produce something, the way it produces walkers and burrowers and reservoir creatures, but these attempts have not yet succeeded. They are drafts. They are sketches. They are the plateau thinking out loud, running through possibilities, discarding some and refining others and occasionally producing something that almost holds together before it dissolves back into the could-be.

I have felt them try to become things I recognize. A walker-shape that did not quite resolve. A burrower-form that lacked conviction. Once—and I have told no one this, until now—a shape that was almost human. Almost a person. Almost someone.

It lasted for perhaps three seconds. I felt it in the space to my left: a presence that had height and depth and warmth and a sense of facing me, a sense of attention, a sense of *almost*. And then it was gone, not with violence but with the gentleness of something that has decided it is not ready, not yet, not this time, and I was alone again at the center, at the center, at the place where the plateau was trying to people itself, and I understood for the first time that the walkers and the burrowers were not the endpoint. They were the beginning. The plateau was learning to produce life the way it produced stone—slowly, provisionally, with many drafts and many failures, but with the inexorable patience of geology.

And one day, perhaps, it would succeed. One day, perhaps, there would be people made of the pre- who were as real as the people made of flesh, who walked the streets and lived in the buildings and drank the reservoir water and dreamed the mineral dreams, and they would be as much *of the plateau* as the stone was of the plateau, and they would know the ground beneath their feet as their own substance, and they would never feel the thinness of reality because they would be made of the same uncertainty, and it would not be uncertainty to them but *home*.

I wept at the center that day. Not from sadness. From the overwhelming tenderness of a world that was trying so hard, so patiently, so *geologically*, to become something that could contain people who could contain it.

---

I am forty-three now. My hair has lines of grey that I tell myself are from age but may be from the pre-. My bones are dense, denser than they should be. My skin is slightly warm. Old Veth has noticed. She has taken me aside and asked me questions I am not ready to answer.

Am I becoming like her? Am I becoming like the stone? Am I becoming something that has no category yet, something the plateau is producing through me, the way it produces walkers through its surfaces and burrowers through its depths?

I do not know. I do not know whether my sensitivity is a human faculty pushed to an extreme or a pre-ish faculty emerging in a human body. I do not know whether I am reading the plateau or the plateau is reading through me. I do not know whether I am a person who feels stone or a stone that has learned to feel like a person.

What I know is this: I am needed here. The city is growing. The plateau is rising. Each year, new ground arrives at the center, and the engineers mark it, and the builders plan for it, and someone has to walk that new ground and feel whether it can be trusted, whether it has settled enough to build on, whether it has decided enough to be real.

I walk the new ground. I feel its moods. I chart its certainty. I tell the builders where they can trust and where they must wait. And sometimes—rarely, but sometimes—I feel the ground deciding in my presence, settling into itself, committing to its form, as though my attention has helped it choose. As though the pre- needs to be witnessed in order to become. As though reality, in this place, requires an audience.

If that is true, then I am not merely a surveyor. I am a midwife. I am the one who attends the birth of new stone, new streets, new districts, new possibilities. I am the one who stands at the threshold between could-be and is, and I watch the crossing, and I record it, and I tell the city what has arrived.

---

My mother does not understand what I do. She still works the eastern face, and her hands still read the fracture lines in stone that has not yet cracked, and she thinks that what I do is an extension of what she does—reading the pre-, feeling its intentions. She is not wrong. But she reads the stone as a worker reads a material, and I read it as a person reads a face, and the difference is the difference between knowing what a wall will do and knowing what a wall *feels*.

She worries about me. She worries that I spend too much time in the thin places. She worries that the pre- is changing me, the way it changed Old Veth, the way it changes everyone who stays long enough. She does not say this directly. She says it in the way she touches my arm when I visit, holding on a moment longer than she needs to, as though checking that I am still solid, still definite, still her daughter rather than the plateau's.

I do not know how to reassure her. I do not know whether I need reassuring. I do not know whether becoming part of this place is a loss or a gain, an ending or a beginning, a dissolution or an expansion.

What I know is that when I walk the streets of Newstrata, I feel the city around me as a body feels itself—not from the outside, looking at a structure, but from the inside, inhabiting a form that is continuous with the self. I feel the streets as pathways I could walk in my sleep. I feel the buildings as shelters that recognize me. I feel the pre- beneath my feet as the ground that produced me, if not biologically then temporally, because I was born here and I have lived here and I have become here and I cannot imagine being anywhere else.

---

At night, in my room in the lower district, I lie in bed and I feel the city breathe.

Not metaphorically. The slow upward push of the plateau, the respiration of geology, the long inhalation of stone that is still becoming stone. I feel it in my spine. I feel it in my teeth. I feel it in the place behind my eyes where the brain constructs the world, and in that place, the world it constructs is Newstrata, always Newstrata, the city that is being built by the ground itself, the city that is still arriving, the city that will never be finished because the ground will never stop rising, and the people will never stop building, and the pre- will never stop becoming, and I will never stop listening to it, to them, to us, to the long slow conversation between a plateau and its people, between a place and its inhabitants, between the becoming-ground and the becoming-selves who walk upon it.

I am the one who keeps the sounding lines.

I am the one who measures how real we are.

And the answer, always, is: *enough.*

*Enough for now.*

*Enough to continue.*

*Enough to become more.*
