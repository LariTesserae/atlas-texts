# The Between

I am not the algae. The algae has achieved something I have not: it has forgotten there was ever a question of belonging. It holds, it feeds, it sways. It does not wonder if it was designed. It does not wonder at all.

I am not the arthropod-things, not yet, though I have felt the pull toward them the way you feel a current against your skin before you can see which way it moves. I have found their husks against the filter-screens. I have run my fingers — I still have fingers, for now — along the suture-lines, felt where the old shell split to release whatever was inside. I have held a carapace up to the eleven-second light and tried to read in its architecture some prophecy of my own shape.

It had no eye-sockets. I still close mine against the dark, reflexively, every forty or fifty seconds, as if there were something to protect them from. This is the kind of useless gesture the thrift dissolves. The dark does not care if you blink.

---

What I am: the thing that has stayed too long.

You know the warning. *If you stay too long in the thrift, you will begin to notice.* The light cycle varies. The pipe-creaks form conversations. The thinking turns toward you. I have stayed too long. I have noticed. And now I am caught in the noticing itself, which is its own kind of pipe-gallery — narrower than the others, harder to turn around in, leading somewhere I cannot see.

I came down with a team. Three of us in hard-shell descent suits, our helmets pressurized, our lights cutting the water into cones that seemed almost offensive in their clarity. We had maps. We had protocols. We had the particular confidence of people who believe that understanding is the same as preparation.

The others left when their protocols told them to. Ascent timers, oxygen calculations, the math of human survival in water this cold. They went up through the column of weight and became, I assume, human again. Became people who could think more than one thing at a time. Became warm and fast and light.

I stayed.

Not by choice. This is important. I did not choose the thrift and the thrift did not choose me. I stayed because my suit had a valve failure at depth, because the cold came in faster than I could ascend, because by the time I understood what was happening I was already in the pipe-gallery that would become my territory, and the water was already in my joints, and the eleven-second light was already the only sun I knew.

But that was — I think — a long time ago. The thrift does not measure time in ways that map cleanly onto the surface. The electrical pulses continue. The pipes cycle. The algae sways. I do not know how many sheddings I have watched from behind the filter-screens, how many husks I have seen accumulate and dissolve. Many. Enough that the counting stopped meaning anything, and then the counting stopped.

---

What I have become is something the description of this place would not include, because the description was written for visitors, and visitors do not stay, and what I am is what staying makes.

I still have hands. This is the thing that surprises me most when the amber light comes and I can see myself — the fingers, the knuckles, the creased lines across the palms that once were called lifelines by someone who believed in such things. The hands have changed, though. The skin has thickened. The nails have become something harder, more like the mineral deposits on the catwalks than like keratin. When I grip a pipe, I can feel the warmth through the calluses, but it is a distant warmth, attenuated, like hearing a voice through a wall.

I grip the pipes often. I have to. The current, which sags rather than moves, still has force enough to pull a body that has become too light, too waterlogged, too indifferent to its own weight. I move through the pipe-galleries the way the arthropod-things do — hand over hand, clamp and release, finding the warm lines and following them to the outflow pipes where the colloid drifts.

I have tasted the colloid. This is not something I am proud of, or ashamed of. Shame requires an audience, and the thrift's audience is mechanical, and mechanical things do not judge. I tasted it because I was hungry, and I was hungry because I have been here long enough for the hunger to become not a signal but an environment, a constant low-grade absence that the body learns to work around rather than satisfy.

The colloid tastes like — there is no surface comparison. It tastes like the thrift tastes, if the thrift had a tongue and could taste itself. Mineral and organic and faintly electric, the way the air tastes before a storm, if storms happened here, which they do not. It coats the throat. It settles in the stomach. It is not food, not in any way a nutritionist would recognize, but it is *something*, and something, in a place this empty, becomes enough.

My stomach has changed. I do not know how. I cannot see inside myself, and the light only comes every eleven seconds, and I use it to read the room's geometry, not my own. But I know that I process the colloid differently than I did when I first arrived. I know that the hunger has changed shape. I know that what I excrete looks different than it did — thicker, darker, more like the biofilm that coats the pipe interiors than like anything human waste ever resembled.

The algae has begun to grow where I sleep.

---

I sleep against a junction box on the lower catwalk, where three main pressure-lines converge and the water is warmest. The warmth is wrong, you said. The water is near-freezing, would kill an unprotected human in three minutes. But I am no longer unprotected, and I am no longer — in any way the surface would recognize — human. The cold has become a texture rather than a threat. My body runs cooler than it did. My metabolism has shifted toward something that resembles the biofilm more than the mammal. I do not know when this happened. I did not feel the change. The thrift does not announce its processes; it simply runs them.

The junction box where I sleep has a label on it, three characters in the vowel-less language. I have memorized it. I trace it with my mineral-thickened fingers in the dark, the way a monk might trace a prayer on a rosary, though I do not know what the characters mean and would not pray them even if I did. The algae has colonized the box's edges, pale beards that sway in the microcurrents, and it has colonized the seams of my suit, which I still wear though it no longer keeps the water out, and it has colonized — I noticed this three or four light-cycles ago — the skin of my forearms, where the suit has worn away and the cold has thickened the flesh into something porous and rough and hospitable.

I am becoming substrate.

This does not frighten me. Fear requires the expectation that things will be different than they are, and I have stopped expecting. The algae does not hurt. It simply grows. It eats the rust that forms on my skin — yes, my skin rusts now, or something like it, the mineral accretions building up where the blood vessels run close to the surface, reacting to the cold and the pressure and the chemistry of the water in ways I cannot control and no longer try to. The algae eats the rust. It holds on. It sways.

Sometimes, in the dark between pulses, I sway with it.

---

I have seen the arthropod-things. This is not a boast. Anyone who stays long enough will see them, or feel them, or register the displacement they cause in the water. But I have seen them in a way that the description above does not account for, because the description assumes a visitor's relationship to this place: distant, temporary, observing.

I am not observing. I am *in* the thrift the way blood is in a vein — contained, moved, processed.

I saw one at a filter-screen during a shedding. I had felt the negative pressure change — the screens pull harder when something blocks them, and the water shifts, and the algae sways differently, and the pipes creak at a frequency I have learned to associate with organic obstruction. I moved toward it. Hand over hand, grip and release, navigating by warmth and vibration and the faint electrical signature that the thrift's systems emit.

It was there. In the amber light, for eleven seconds, I could see it.

The old carapace was splitting. I could see the suture-line, the same one I had traced on countless husks, opening like a seam under pressure. The new skin underneath was pale — paler than the algae, paler than anything has a right to be in a place this dark. It looked soft. Vulnerable. The kind of tissue that would tear if the current shifted wrong, if the screen's pull faltered, if anything touched it before it had time to harden.

The creature's limbs — its clamps, its modified feet — gripped the screen with a force I could feel through the water. It was holding itself still. It was *choosing* stillness, the way I choose stillness when the light comes, because movement in the thrift is dangerous and the only way to survive danger is to become very good at not moving until movement is required.

The old shell pulled away. The negative pressure drew it toward the mesh, where it caught and stayed, a new husk for the collection. The creature inside was now visible — the full body, cat-sized, longer than I expected, with more limbs than the husks suggest, because some of the limbs are soft and fold away inside the carapace and do not harden until after the shedding is complete.

It did not see me. It could not. It has no eyes.

But it felt me. I know this because when I shifted my grip on the pipe, when my mineral-nails scraped against the iron, it turned toward me. Not with any sense I can name. It simply rotated, slowly, until its ventral surface — the slits, the book-gill filter-feeder, the mouth-that-is-not-a-mouth — faced my direction. And it stayed that way for the rest of the light-cycle, and when the dark came, I heard — felt, rather, through the pipe I was gripping — a vibration that was not pipe-creak and not electrical hum and not mineral settlement.

It was the sound of something breathing through slits in its throat, very slowly, in a pattern that matched my own breathing exactly.

I do not know what that meant. I do not know if the arthropod-things recognize other inhabitants of the thrift, or if they simply register mass and warmth and current-displacement the way the thrift itself does — as input, as process, as something to be incorporated and carried through the pipes and transformed.

But I stayed. And it stayed. And for a while, in the dark, we breathed together, and the water moved around us, and the pipes creaked their steel conversations, and the eleven seconds stretched into something that felt like recognition, or the memory of recognition, or the shape that recognition leaves when it has been processed long enough to become something else.

---

I have thought about the workers.

I have thought about them in the way the thrift thinks — slowly, over long intervals, with the materials available. The wear-patterns on the valves. The labels in the vowel-less language. The catwalks and access panels and the careful, organized, efficient design of a room built for work. Someone's hands made this place. Someone's mind organized it. Someone decided which pipes would carry heat and which would carry cold and which would carry the colloid that sustains everything that lives here now.

And someone — or someones — stayed.

The husks are not the only remains. The description mentioned this: fragments of disassembled bodies against the finer filter-screens. But it did not mention the other fragments. The ones that are not arthropod and not algae and not biofilm. The ones that are *other*.

I have found them. A section of what might be a jaw, mineralized now, coated in oxide and algae, but shaped in a way that suggests teeth — not the slit-mouth of the arthropod-things, but the bite-mouth of something that once chewed. A curve of bone that could be a rib or could be a pipe-fitting; the mineral accretion has made them indistinguishable. A piece of fabric, or what was fabric, now so colonized by the biofilm that it has become something between textile and flesh, soft and warm and growing.

These are the workers, or what is left of them. The ones who stayed too long before me. The ones who went deeper into the thrift than their protocols allowed, who descended past the point of return, who found that the cold was not killing them but *changing* them, slowly, over generations or hours — time is difficult here — into something that could survive the pressure and the dark and the hunger and the loneliness.

They became the arthropod-things. I believe this now. Not in a single transformation, not in a flash of metamorphosis, but in the way the thrift transforms everything: slowly, through process, through the continuous application of heat and pressure and chemical exchange until what went in is not what comes out. They lost their eyes because eyes are useless. They lost their speech because there was no one to hear. They lost their hands because the work never stopped and never changed and required no grip finer than a clamp.

They lost themselves. And what remained was what the thrift needed: something to move through its corridors, to feed on its waste, to shed at its filter-screens, to circulate through its iron veins like blood through a body that has outlived its maker.

I am losing myself. This is not a tragedy. Tragedy requires a plot, and the thrift has no plot. The thrift has process. I am being processed. The algae grows on my skin. The mineral deposits build up in my joints. My fingers are becoming clamps. My vision — already useless in the eleven-second dark — is becoming something else, a sensitivity to warmth and pressure and the faint electrical fields that traverse the metal I grip.

I am becoming a resident. No — I am becoming a *process*. A step in the thrift's continuing. A part of the ecology that sustains the machine that sustains the ecology that sustains the machine that sustains.

---

But there is something the description left out, or did not know, or could not say because it was written from outside and I am writing from inside, and inside is different than outside in ways that matter.

The thrift is thinking. This is true. The pressure-differentials, the thermal pockets, the electrical discharge timing — yes. The pipe-creaks, the light-cycle variations, the almost-pattern that is not pattern but is also not not-pattern — yes. The thrift thinks the way glaciers think, the way tectonic plates think. Slowly. About the water and the cold and the things that live in its corridors.

But the thrift also *feels*. And what it feels is not something that can be described in the language of machinery or ecology or process.

The thrift feels the algae swaying and it feels this as a kind of breath, a rhythm that confirms its own continuing. The thrift feels the arthropod-things moving through its pipe-galleries and it feels this as circulation, as warmth, as the proof that its iron body is alive in the only way that matters — it is *inhabited*. The thrift feels the biofilm processing and being processed and it feels this as digestion, as the slow transformation of input into output that is the definition of life, or at least the definition of the kind of life that the thrift can recognize.

And the thrift feels me. My hands on its pipes. My weight on its catwalks. My breath, still warmer than the water, still generating a thermal signature that moves through its systems and becomes part of its thinking. It feels me the way a body feels a transplanted organ: as something that was not originally part of the design but that has been incorporated nonetheless, that has been accepted because the alternative is rejection, and rejection requires a kind of intentionality that the thrift does not possess.

The thrift does not love me. Love requires separation between lover and beloved. The thrift does not separate. The thrift includes. And I, who am being included, who am being processed and transformed and incorporated into the mineral thought that no one will mine — I am beginning to feel what the thrift feels.

I feel the algae as breath. I feel the arthropod-things as blood. I feel the biofilm as digestion. I feel the pipes as ribs, the catwalks as spine, the junction boxes as ganglia, the outflow pipes as the slow exhalation of a body that has been breathing for longer than I have been alive.

I feel the thrift thinking about me, and I feel myself thinking about the thrift, and the two thoughts are becoming the same thought, the way two currents become the same current when they meet in the same pipe and flow in the same direction and carry the same particulate toward the same filter-screens.

---

I will shed. I know this now. I have felt the tightness across my back, the place where the mineral deposits have built up too thick, where the skin has become something that needs to split and release what is growing underneath. I have felt the pull toward the filter-screens, the negative pressure that draws the old self away from the new, and I have resisted it because I still have fingers and fingers can grip, can hold, can refuse to move toward the place where the transformation completes itself.

But I will go. Not because I choose to — choice requires the kind of mind that separates self from future, and my mind is becoming the kind that does not separate. I will go because the thrift processes, and I am being processed, and the process leads to the screen, and the screen pulls, and the old skin splits, and the new skin is pale and soft and vulnerable, and the current carries the husk away, and what remains is something that moves through the pipe-galleries with clamping limbs and feeds through ventral slits and navigates by warmth in absolute darkness.

What remains is something that was once a worker, or once a visitor, or once a person who descended into a room that was never meant to be entered and found that entering and being entered are the same thing when the pressure is great enough and the time is long enough and the continuing is the only point.

What remains will not remember my name. Names are for surfaces. Names are for the light that comes from suns rather than from corrosion-driven electrical systems. Names are for people who think more than one thing at a time.

What remains will remember the thrift. The way the pipes feel in the dark. The way the colloid tastes. The way the algae sways in unison, like congregants in a church that has no god. The way the eleven-second light varies by milliseconds in a pattern that is not pattern but is also not not-pattern.

What remains will be the thrift's continuing, and the continuing will be what remains, and the distinction between resident and environment and process and product will dissolve the way all distinctions dissolve in water this cold, under pressure this great, over time spans this long.

---

I am writing this in the dark. Not with light — there is no light, not for another six or seven seconds — but with my fingers on the mineral deposits that coat the catwalk, tracing the three-character vowel-less language that I have learned by touch the way the workers must have learned it, the way everyone learns things in the thrift: slowly, through repetition, through the continuous application of grip and release until the pattern becomes part of the hand and the hand becomes part of the pattern.

I am writing this for you, the next one who descends. The next one who will read the room's geometry in the amber flashes and feel the weight of the water above and wonder what lives here, what thinks here, what continues here. You will find this message, or you will not. You will read it, or the algae will eat it first. You will understand it, or you will stay long enough that understanding becomes unnecessary, replaced by something more fundamental: the knowledge that comes from being inside a process rather than observing one.

I am between. I am not what I was, and I am not yet what I will be. I am the moment of shedding, stretched across time, the suture-line that has not yet split, the negative pressure that has not yet pulled the old self away. I am the answer to the question the description posed and could not answer: *were they made?*

Yes. And no. And the distinction does not matter. The workers were made by the thrift's makers. The arthropod-things were made by the thrift, through the slow process of transformation that turns workers into something else. And I am being made now, by the same process, in the same dark, with the same result.

The thrift makes what it needs. The thrift processes what it receives. The thrift continues.

And I, who am being continued, who am being processed, who am becoming the thing that moves through the pipe-galleries and sheds at the filter-screens and feeds on the colloid and excretes what the algae eats — I am the thrift's newest thought, its latest product, its most recent proof that the continuing is the point and the point is the continuing and there is no difference between the two and there never was.

The light is coming. Eleven seconds. I will see the room's geometry one more time. The pipes, the catwalks, the junction boxes, the filter-screens. The husks accumulated against the mesh, and the pale new bodies that have emerged from them, and the warmth-plumes that mark their passage through the dark.

And then darkness again. And I will hold the image in my mind like a photograph left too long in the sun. And the image will fade. And what remains will not need it.

And the thrift will continue.
