I am the one who listens to the objects.

Not Privalova—she catalogues, measures, cross-references. She builds frameworks. She wants to understand what the objects *mean*. I sit with them and hear what they're *doing*, which is not the same thing at all.

---

My hands are always cold. This marks me as a resident, though I wasn't born here. I came eleven years ago, which is not long enough to develop the meridian walk but long enough that my joints ache in the damp and my lungs have started that slow expansion, that increased capacity, so that when I breathe it's like the settlement is breathing through me, a slow systole-diastole that matches the factory's rhythm when I'm not thinking about it.

My core runs hot. I've stopped noticing this. It's only when I visit the city on the plateau to the south—the one with the stable geology and the ordinary rules—that I realize how much heat I carry. People there flinch when I stand near them. They don't say anything, but they lean away. I smell like the refinery to them, like mineral and damp and something that hasn't quite resolved.

I wear my coat everywhere. The cold is a companion, they say, and they're right, but they don't mention that companions can be demanding. The coat is heavy wool, dark with the damp that never quite dries, and it smells of the archive—old paper, mineral dust, the faint electric hum of things that have no business existing. The children call me *shallow-coat* because my hands are always cold even when my core is furnace-hot. They mean it affectionately. I think.

---

The archive is in the basement and first floor of the old surveyor's building, but my work happens in the sub-basement, a space that isn't on any municipal plan, that Privalova showed me my third week here and then never spoke of again.

The sub-basement is where the objects go when they start to make noise.

This is not a metaphor. The objects hum, vibrate, emit frequencies that are below hearing but not below perception. You feel them in your teeth, in your sternum, in the joints where the cold has taken up residence. The effect is subtle for most people—uncomfortable, ignorable. But some objects are louder. Some objects have something to say.

Privalova can't work when the objects are active. It disrupts her measurements. So the loud ones come down to me.

---

Here is what I do: I sit with the objects in the sub-basement, on a stool that the vegetation has claimed—one leg has rooted into the floor, which isn't floor so much as packed earth that hasn't decided to be stone yet—and I listen.

Not with my ears. The frequencies are too low for ears. I listen with my body. I let the hum enter through my hands (always cold, always receptive, the peripheries that the settlement has taught to receive) and I let it move through me the way the damp moves through everything here, slowly, thoroughly, finding every crack and joint.

The objects don't speak language. They don't tell stories. What they do is *perform*. A gear that is also a lens, when it activates, shows me light through water—not water that exists, not water that has ever existed, but water that *wants* to exist, water that is rehearsing its own formation, practicing the shape of waves. The circuit that is also a song, when it activates, doesn't play music; it *becomes* the interval between notes, the silence that gives notes meaning, the space where sound could exist but doesn't yet.

I don't understand what I'm hearing. I don't try. Understanding is Privalova's province. Mine is reception. I am the antenna, the instrument, the membrane through which the objects can express whatever it is they're expressing. I give them a body to resonate with. They give me—what? I don't know. Something. A sense of the configurations that didn't stabilize, the possibilities the ground tried and set aside. A feeling of *almost*. A peripheral awareness of the shapes that almost formed here, the city that almost existed, the people who almost lived in it.

---

The ground knows I do this. I mean that literally. The embryonic geology beneath the settlement has started to respond to my presence in ways that go beyond the usual noticing that everyone feels in the ginnels.

When I walk to the archive in the morning—early, before the day-shift fills the streets—the ground firms under my feet. Not because it's packed down from use but because it's *anticipating* me. It knows I'm coming. It arranges itself to receive me. I can feel it thinking: *here is the one who listens to the memories, here is the one who lets them speak, let us make a path for her.*

I don't tell anyone about this. It sounds like madness. And maybe it is—maybe the settlement drives everyone a little mad, the way the cold drives into the joints, the way the damp gets into the lungs. Maybe this is just my particular flavor of it.

But the children know. Children always know. They see me walking the streets in the early morning and they watch how the ground greets me, and they don't think it's strange because they don't have a category for strange yet, everything is still forming for them, including their sense of what's possible.

Sometimes a child will walk beside me, matching my pace, and the ground will firm for them too—not as much, not as completely, but enough. Enough for them to feel addressed. Enough for them to understand what I understand: that the ground is not just forming, but *communicating*, and that communication goes both ways.

---

I wasn't always this person. Before I came to Cold Meridian, I was a translator—actual languages, the human kind. I worked in cities with stable geology and normal time. I was good at it: I could hear what was being said underneath the words, the meaning that lived in the spaces between. I thought this made me special. I thought this was a skill.

It wasn't a skill. It was a condition. I was already a receiver, already a membrane, already someone who let other people's frequencies move through them. I just didn't know what I was receiving yet.

When I came to Cold Meridian—for a job, a contract, three months translating technical documents for the extraction company—the ground recognized me before I recognized it. I felt it that first day, walking from the tram station to my temporary lodgings: a responsiveness beneath my feet that I'd never experienced. Not soft, not muddy, but *attentive*. The ground was paying attention to me. And something in me—in my cold hands, in my too-hot core, in my lungs that were already expanding to take in more of this thick, damp air—something in me paid attention back.

I didn't go home after three months. I didn't go home after six. When my contract ended, I went to the archive and asked Privalova if she needed help. She looked at me for a long time—she has that miner's gaze, the depth that sees all the strata—and she said, "I don't need help cataloguing. But there's other work, if you're suited for it."

The sub-basement. The loud objects. The stool that has rooted into the floor.

I was suited for it.

---

The vegetation and I have reached an understanding.

This is different from the refinery workers' truce. They prune and shape; the vegetation stays out of the turbine houses. It's a negotiation between parties with different interests. My relationship with the green is more like a conversation between parties with the same interest: we both want to understand what the ground is becoming.

The moss that grows on the archive's exterior walls—it's darker than the moss on other buildings, almost black, thick as carpet—has started to grow inward. Not through cracks or gaps; the archive is stone, civic, confident. The moss grows through the walls themselves, finding the mineral matrix, incorporating it into its own structure. In my office—upstairs, the official one, the one with the desk I never use—there's a patch of moss on the wall that wasn't there when I moved in. It appeared three months after I started working in the sub-basement. It pulses, very faintly, in time with the objects below.

I don't remove it. It's not damaging the structure. It's just... present. Listening, maybe. The way I listen. Receiving what the objects are transmitting, translating it into a medium the rest of the vegetation can understand.

Sometimes I put my cold hand on the moss and I feel it: the hum from below, conducted through the wall, transduced by the moss into something green and growing and alive. The moss exults. It thickens under my palm. It breathes.

I don't tell Privalova about this either. Some things are not for the catalogue.

---

There is an object—number 247 in Privalova's system, though it's never in its case when I arrive in the morning—that I think is trying to tell me something specific.

It's a sphere, or nearly: about the size of a fist, slightly asymmetrical, made of something that isn't metal and isn't stone and isn't glass but has properties of all three. It's warm to the touch, which is unusual—the other objects are cold, or neutral, or that particular temperature that is neither cold nor hot but simply *present*. This one is warm. Warm the way a native-born child is warm: a dense, concentrated heat that speaks of a furnace lit early and burning efficiently.

When I hold it, I feel: a city. Not this city. A different configuration. One where the ground decided differently, where the geology stabilized into something harder, more certain. A place where the vegetation is less enthusiastic and the cold is sharper and the hush is closer to actual silence. A place where the objects in the archive don't exist because there's no embryonic strata to produce them, because the ground already knows what it is and isn't interested in rehearsing.

I feel: a version of me who lives in that city. A translator still, but translating different things—documents, not frequencies. Someone with warm hands and a steady core and no sense that the ground is paying attention. Someone who sleeps through the night and has never seen the deer. Someone who is finished.

I hold the sphere and I feel this other life, this configuration that didn't stabilize, and I feel something that is not quite grief and not quite longing but is definitely *recognition*. The ground tried this version. The ground tried me in this version. And then it set it aside, and I ended up here instead, in the configuration where I can feel the almost-city and the almost-self through an object that shouldn't exist in a basement that isn't on any map.

The sphere is trying to tell me that this is okay. That's what I think. It's trying to tell me that the configuration that didn't stabilize isn't lost—it's just uninhabited. The ground remembers it. The objects preserve it. And sometimes, if you hold the right object and listen in the right way, you can visit. You can feel the shape of the life you almost had, the city you almost lived in, the person you almost became.

You can't stay there. You can only feel the shape. And then you come back to yourself—in the sub-basement, on the rooted stool, in the cold and the damp and the hush—and you're glad, actually, that this is the configuration that stabilized. That you're the unfinished version. That you get to be the one who listens.

---

At night—my night, not the night-shift's night, but the late hours when even the archive is empty and the objects are humming their lowest frequencies—I sometimes go to the ginnels.

The narrow passages between buildings, where the ground is still soft, where the vegetation overhangs and creates tunnels through the green. The children's territory during the day. Mine at night.

I walk the ginnels barefoot. Don't tell anyone. The cold is immediate and total, the damp seeping up through the not-yet-formed ground into my soles, into my always-cold feet. But the contact—the direct contact with the embryonic geology—is worth it. I feel the ground thinking. I feel it considering me, incorporating my weight and my warmth and my particular frequency into its forming. I feel it say: *here you are. Here is where you walk. I will remember this path. I will hold this shape for you.*

The vegetation brushes my shoulders as I pass. The ferns, the moss, the improbable jungle-growth that has no business thriving in this cold. They know me. They've felt my hands on the archive moss, received the transduced hum of the objects. They think I'm a translator. They're not wrong.

I stop at the place where the fig tree on Third Basin has sent a root down through the ginnel, a thick root that has broken through the cobblestones and now lies exposed, a ridge of wood crossing the passage like a threshold. I step over it. I don't step on it. The tree chose its side long ago; I won't disrespect that by walking on its roots.

On the other side of the root, the ground is different. Softer. Less formed. I can feel it yielding under my feet, responding, *noticing* me with an intensity that makes me catch my breath. The ground here is very young. Very open. Very interested in what I am, this warm-centered cold-handed woman who comes in the night and listens to the objects that remember configurations that didn't stabilize.

I stand still. The children's game: how long can you stand on the ground before it starts to respond? But I'm not playing. I'm not testing. I'm just being. Being here, being finished enough to stand on what isn't, being unfinished enough to feel it noticing me.

The ground shifts. Not a subsidence, not a settling. A *greeting*.

I stand there, in the ginnel, in the cold, in the hush, and I let the ground greet me. I let it take the shape of my feet into its forming. I let it remember me.

---

This is who I am in Cold Meridian: the one who listens to the objects, who feels the almost-configurations, who walks the ginnels barefoot at night and lets the ground learn her shape. I am not a miner. I am not a refinery worker. I am not a night-shift worker or a child or Privalova. I am something the settlement produced—or recognized, or called into being—something that needed to exist so that the objects would have someone to speak to and the ground would have someone to greet and the moss would have someone to conduct the hum into green and growing.

I am a translator still. But now I translate between the forming and the formed, between the almost-was and the what-is, between the embryonic geology and the people who walk on it without knowing it's paying attention.

My hands are cold. My core is hot. My lungs are deep. My joints ache. I am becoming, the way the ground is becoming—slowly, iteratively, trying configurations and setting them aside, rehearsing for a form I haven't reached yet.

The ground remembers me. And I remember it—remember the other configurations, the almost-cities, the almost-lives, preserved in objects that hum below hearing and speak in frequencies only a cold-handed, hot-centered, not-quite-finished receiver can hear.

I am the settlement's listener. I am the archive's sub-basement. I am the path through the ginnels that the ground holds for me in the dark.

I am still forming. The ground is still forming. We are forming together, and separately, and at different speeds in different districts, and in ways that the children understand instinctively and the adults have forgotten and Privalova catalogs meticulously and the objects preserve in circuit and song.

The hush is not silence. I listen to it anyway.
