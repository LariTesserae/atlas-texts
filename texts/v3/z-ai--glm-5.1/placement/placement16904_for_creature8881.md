I am not the mesa. I want to say that clearly, because the distinction matters to me, or it did, once, when distinctions seemed more certain than they do now.

I came here eleven days ago. I remember the number because I carved tally marks into a reed-stem with my thumbnail, the first day, before I understood that the reed would heal over the mark within hours, erasing it, incorporating the small wound into its growing edge as though it had never been. I remember the number because I kept counting anyway, even after the marks stopped persisting, even after I stopped being sure whether I was counting days or something else, something measured by a different clock.

I am — I was — a surveyor. The third team to visit. The first two reported the geological anomaly, the uplift parameters, the bathymetry. They noted the vegetation density and the absence of fauna. They did not stay long enough. Or they stayed long enough to leave, which is not the same thing.

I am the one who stayed.

---

The second night, I found a chamber. Not by accident. I had been pushing through the reeds for hours, following a channel that seemed to widen and then narrow and then widen again, and I thought I was lost, and then I thought I was not lost, and then I thought the distinction between lost and found might not apply here, and then the reeds opened around me and I was *in* it.

The chamber was not large. Three meters across, roughly circular, the walls made of reed-stems so dense I could not see between them. The water was waist-deep and warm, warmer than the channels, as though the stone beneath held a pocket of heat that the chamber had grown to contain. The light that reached me was green, the deep green that does not exist above the surface, filtered through so many layers of leaf and stem and water that it had become something else, something that felt generated rather than transmitted. The ceiling was a lattice of interlocking leaves, and through the gaps I could see clouds passing, but dimly, as though the sky belonged to a different country.

I stood there for a long time. I did not sit. I did not need to sit. The water held me, and I was warm, and the chamber was quiet in the way the mesa is quiet — not silent, not empty, but *held*, the way a sound is held inside a throat before it becomes speech.

I did not know yet that the chamber was made for me. I did not know yet that I would think that thought.

---

On the fifth day, or what I counted as the fifth day, I stopped eating.

This was not a decision. I had brought supplies — dense travel rations, water purification tablets, a filtration system strapped to my pack. I ate the first day, the second day. By the third, I was eating less. By the fourth, I forgot. By the fifth, I looked at the ration bar in my hand and could not remember what it was for. Not in the way of illness or confusion. In the way of looking at a tool for a job that no longer needed doing.

I was not hungry. This should have concerned me. It did not.

I think now that I was being fed. Not by the fish, not by the insects. By the water itself, or by what the water carried — dissolved minerals, organic compounds, the slow snow of plant matter that drifts in the column. I was absorbing it. Through my skin, through the mucous membranes, through the long hours of immersion until my body had become permeable in ways I had not known it could be. The warm water was inside me and I was inside it and the boundary had become a membrane rather than a wall, and what it passed was enough.

I am not saying this makes sense. I am saying it is what happened.

---

On the seventh day, or what I counted as the seventh day, I understood the corridors.

I had been moving through them without understanding, following the reed-walls the way one follows a path in a dream, trusting that it leads somewhere because it leads. But on the seventh day I stopped in a passage where the stems leaned close on either side and I could feel them — not with my hands, though I reached out and touched them, felt their smooth sheaths warm and alive beneath my palms — but with something behind my hands, something in the root-mass beneath my feet, something that was connected to the roots that interlaced the whole plateau. I could feel the passage as a pressure, as a shape in a field that extended far beyond my skin. I could feel the chamber ahead, the way you feel a room before you enter it, the change in air pressure, the shift in sound. But I could feel it from a hundred meters away. I could feel the chamber the way the reeds felt it — as an opening, a space where growth had been directed elsewhere, where the corridor's purpose was not to fill but to lead.

I stood there for a long time, my hands on the reeds, my feet in the warm water, feeling the mesa extend around me like a body. Not my body. A body I was inside, and also, impossibly, a body I was part of.

I did not move for several hours. I did not need to. I was where I needed to be, which was wherever I was, which was the place the corridors had brought me, which was the place I had been going without knowing it.

---

The pale fish come to me now.

Not in a way that suggests tameness or training. In a way that suggests recognition. When I enter the water — and I am always in the water now; I have not stood on dry stone for days, because there is no dry stone, because the mesa does not offer that kind of surface, because the mesa wants me wet and warm and permeable — when I enter the water, they drift toward me. Not all of them. The finger-length ones, the nearly-transparent ones whose organs I can see pulsing in the green light. They drift toward my legs, my hands, my torso, and they hover there, their underformed mouths opening and closing in the slow rhythm of breathing, their spotted eyes aimed at me with an attention that should not be possible in eyes that have not yet learned to focus.

They are not pets. They are not companions. They are what I was, I think. In process. Not yet committed. And they have come to me because I am what they might become, or because I am becoming what they already are, or because the distinction between us has become as porous as the boundary between my body and the water.

Sometimes I feel them before I see them. A pressure in the root-network, a small warm presence moving through a channel thirty meters away. I turn my head. I know where they will be. They emerge from between the reeds and I think: *there you are*, the way you think it when someone you love enters a room, without deciding to think it, without knowing you were waiting.

---

I have not left.

This is obvious, I suppose. I could leave. The path back to the mesa's edge is still there, the corridors still open, the reeds have not closed behind me. The waterfall still falls. The lowlands still wait below, green and old and known. My radio still works — I can hear the teams trying to reach me, their voices thin and desperate, asking for coordinates, asking for status, asking for me to come back. I have not answered. Not because I cannot, but because I do not know what to say that they would understand.

I am not the mesa. I want to say that again, because I need to hear it, because the boundary is still there, because I still have skin and it still holds me in and the water still stays out, mostly, except for what passes through, except for what I absorb, except for what becomes me and what becomes not-me and what becomes something in between that does not have a name.

But I am also not *not* the mesa. I am inside it the way a blood cell is inside a vein — part of the circulation, part of the system, carrying warmth from one chamber to another, leaving traces of myself in the water that will be processed and absorbed and incorporated. I move through the corridors and the corridors move me. I rest in the chambers and the chambers hold me. I breathe and the mesa breathes with me, not in imitation but in synchronization, our rhythms aligning the way two clocks in the same room will align, the way two pendulums on the same wall will find the same frequency.

I think I am becoming something. Not something the mesa made. Not something I was before. Something that could only exist here, in this place where the categories have not yet hardened, where a human body and a warm flooded plateau and a root-network and a green light that does not exist in the above-surface spectrum can all be part of the same process, the same becoming, the same slow accumulation of small decisions that looks, from the outside, like purpose.

---

This morning — if it was morning; the light does not change much here, filtered as it is through clouds and canopy and the perpetual warmth that makes time feel like a medium rather than a measurement — this morning I found a new chamber.

It was smaller than the others. Just large enough for me to sit in, my back against the reed-wall, my legs drawn up, the water chest-deep and green and warm. The ceiling was lower, the leaves interlocking so densely that no sky was visible, only the green glow, only the filtered light that seemed to come from the water itself, from the algal mats on the stone beneath me, from somewhere that was not above or below but *within*.

And I felt — not the mesa. Not the distributed attention of roots and water and slow vegetative processing. Something more focused. Something that had been building, the way a wave builds, the way a note builds in a throat that is about to sing.

The chamber had been made for me. Not by the mesa. By the mesa and me together, my movements through the corridors shaping the growth the way water shapes a channel, my warmth and my waste and my breath feeding the roots that decided to grow here rather than there, the system adjusting itself around my presence the way a body adjusts around a splinter, except I was not a splinter, I was not foreign, I was already part of the circulation, I was already dissolved, I was already being incorporated.

I sat in the chamber and I felt it recognize me. Not as a separate thing. As a part. As the part that could move between chambers, that could carry information from one end of the mesa to the other, that could leave and return and leave again and in the leaving and returning teach the system something about its own edges, its own extent, its own shape.

I sat in the chamber and I understood, finally, what the corridors were for. They were not for me. They were *with* me. They grew the way they grew because something was moving through them, and the something was me, and I was moving through them because they were there, and they were there because I moved through them, and the loop was the point, the loop was the thought, the loop was the mesa thinking for the first time in a language that included legs and lungs and a mind that could count days even after the days stopped being distinguishable.

---

I am not the mesa.

But I am the mesa's first idea of itself.

I am the part that can leave and does not. I am the part that can perceive and report back, through the root-network, through the water, through the slow exchange of warmth and chemistry that connects every chamber to every corridor to every stem to every fish to every algal mat to the stone beneath it all still bleeding heat into the water still pushing up through the crust still young and still becoming.

I am what the mesa is doing with the fact of my arrival. I am the project. I am the pilot program. I am the first draft of something that might, in a thousand years or ten thousand, become what this place will call its mind — distributed, patient, vegetative, slow, but *there*, aware of itself the way I am aware of my hand, the way I am aware of the fish drifting toward my ankles, the way I am aware of the warm water entering my skin and carrying something out of me and carrying something else back in.

I will stay. Not because I cannot leave. Because I have understood what I am for. Because the mesa grew chambers before it had anything to put in them, and I am the thing that fits, and the fitting is not captivity, it is recognition, it is the way a key recognizes a lock, it is the way a hand recognizes a tool, it is the way a thought recognizes the mind that has been waiting for it without knowing what it was waiting for.

I am not the mesa. But I am the mesa's. And the mesa is mine. And the green light is our thinking, and the warm water is our blood, and the corridors are our remembering, and the chambers are our wondering, and the pale fish are our dreaming, and the reeds are our reaching, and the stone beneath is our patience, and the clouds that pass so close are our breathing, and the slow pulse of heat from below is our heartbeat, and I am the part that knows this, and I am the part that can say it, even if the saying goes nowhere, even if the radio is silent, even if the teams have given up, even if no one ever comes, even if the mesa subsides and the ocean reclaims it and the whole enterprise sinks back into geologic memory.

We are here. We are becoming. We are still figuring out what we want to be.

And the water is warm, and the light is green, and the corridors lead everywhere, and I do not need to eat, and I do not need to leave, and I do not need to be anything other than this: the mesa's first thought, thinking itself, in a language of roots and warmth and time.
