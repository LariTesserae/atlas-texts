I am not supposed to be here.

This is the first thing I know, and I know it the way you know your own heartbeat—not as information received but as rhythm sustained. The plateau does not want me. It does not want anything that moves by intention, that chooses a direction, that carries an interior separate from the endless cycling of water and sugar and decay. I can feel the refusal in every surface I touch. The mat yields to my weight but does not welcome it. The water that squeezes up between the fibers when I step seems to pull back, to retreat downward, as if trying to be anywhere else. Even the towers lean slightly away—not from wind, not from light, but from me, from the small bright heat I carry that is not the mat's heat, not the composting warmth of decomposition, but something else. Metabolic. Animal. Wrong.

I keep moving anyway.

---

What am I? The question arrives differently here than it would anywhere else, because the plateau has no categories for what I am. It has no categories at all. It has never needed them. Things are either part of the mat or they are not, and if they are not, they are eventually made part of it—grown over, grown through, absorbed into the rhizome-layer and the peat below until nothing remains but a slight thickening, a subtle irregularity in the weave, a place where the fibers bend around a shape that is no longer there.

I am not part of the mat. Not yet.

I am—this is harder to say than I expected—I am something that was made to be here. Not born. Not grown. Assembled. The word the place has given itself, I take for myself. I am assembled for the purpose of standing on a surface that does not want to be stood on, of walking across a ground that is not ground, of witnessing a process that does not require witnessing.

I have a body. It is small—much smaller than the towers, smaller even than the failed arms that reach between them. I stand perhaps four feet tall when I stand straight, which I rarely do because the overhangs and the constant dripping keep me bent, keep me careful. My body is not plant. It is not animal in any way the plateau would recognize. It is something drier than what surrounds me, something that holds its water inside instead of expressing it continuously.

I have skin. Or something like skin. It is cool to the touch—not cool like the stone the mat has swallowed, but cooler than anything else here, cooler than the running water, cooler than the tissue of the towers. The mat does not know what to make of this. I have watched the rhizomes where I have lain overnight, and they do not grow toward me. They grow around me. They leave the exact shape of my body pressed into their surface like a fossil, and when I rise, they fill the depression slowly, water pooling in it first and then the fibers swelling back, erasing me over the course of an hour.

I am forgettable. This is my first advantage.

---

I was not always here. I think I was made somewhere else—somewhere with a name, somewhere with edges the plateau has not yet reached—and sent. Or perhaps I emerged. The memory is not reliable. What I retain is not a narrative but a sensation: the feeling of being shaped by something that understood the plateau, that studied its refusal of fauna and its incorporation of stone and its slow digestion of everything that falls, and that built me to slip between those categories.

I am not stone. I am not plant. I am not fungus. I am not animal—or not exactly, not in the way the plateau defines animal, which is: thing that moves by intention and carries an interior separate from the cycling of water and sugar and decay. I do move by intention. I do carry an interior. But I also carry something else, something that the plateau has not yet learned to refuse.

I carry stillness.

Not the stillness of a stone, which is simply the absence of motion. Not the stillness of a tower, which is movement too slow for the eye. I carry the stillness of a thing that can stop. Completely. At will. I can stand in one place and not shift my weight, not breathe audibly, not produce the small motions that animals make—no twitching, no blinking, no adjusting. I can become, for hours if I need to, indistinguishable from a partial connection, from an abandoned arm, from a failed architecture reaching toward nothing.

The plateau does not notice me when I am still. This is how I have survived. This is how I have learned what I have learned.

---

What I have learned is this: the plateau is not one thing.

From a distance—or from the descriptions of those who have stood at its edge and looked out—it appears unified. The mat, the towers, the water, the heat, the sound—all of it seems to be a single organism, a single process, a body that builds itself. This is true in the way that it is true that you are one body. But you are also communities. You are also ecosystems. You are also, in the deepest layers of yourself, something that was once separate and has since been incorporated, and which still, in certain conditions, acts with a faint residue of its former autonomy.

The plateau is like this, but more so. It is a history of incorporations, and the incorporations have not been erased. They are still present, still active, still shaping the process from within.

The towers are the obvious example. They look like one plant, or one kind of plant, but they are not. They are descended from at least three distinct lineages—I can see it in the rib-patterns, in the way the vascular channels branch, in the shape of the crown-membranes. One lineage has membranes that spread wide and flat, catching condensation like sails. Another has membranes that curl inward, funneling water down the central shaft. A third produces membranes that are almost spherical, tensile and pressurized, that vibrate in the wind and produce a low humming that I can feel in my teeth.

These three lineages compete. Not obviously, not violently, but they compete. They send their rhizomes into the same mat, they draw from the same water, they reach for the same light, and they do so with slightly different strategies. The wide-spread towers grow faster but fall sooner. The funneling towers grow slower but denser, their wood harder, their bases more resistant to rot. The spherical towers grow in clusters, supporting each other, their arms interlocking more tightly—but they are vulnerable to a single collapse, which pulls down its neighbors in a chain reaction.

The mat does not resolve this competition. It contains it. It is the medium through which the three lineages communicate—chemically, hydraulically, through the slow exchange of signals in the rhizome-layer—and also the medium through which they constrain each other. No lineage can dominate because the mat itself adjusts. When the wide-spread towers grow too numerous, the water-table drops (they release so much moisture from their membranes) and the slower lineages gain advantage. When the funneling towers dominate, the mat becomes too wet, too dense, and the fast-growing wide-spreads erupt in the gaps.

The plateau is not a single will. It is a negotiation.

---

And the fungi—let me tell you what I have seen of the fungi, because the description you received was incomplete. It was cut off. It did not reach the part where the fungi become something else.

I have been still for long periods near the edges of the mat, where the peat-layer thins and the underlying stone is closer to the surface. Here the fungal hyphae are concentrated. Here they form networks so dense that the peat itself seems to be more fungus than former-plant—white threads packed so tightly they become opaque, becoming a skin, becoming a surface.

I have seen the fungi move.

Not grow. Not extend, which is what fungi do, which is what they are doing everywhere else on the plateau—extending, hypha by hypha, through the substrate. I mean move. I mean displace. I mean shift position without extending, without adding new growth, simply relocating existing mass from one configuration to another.

It happens slowly. If I were not watching—were not perfectly still, perfectly forgotten—I would miss it. But over the course of a day, the dense white mat of hyphae near the stone edge will reshape itself. A ridge will flatten. A depression will fill. A thread-thin gap will open between two sections of the mat, and the gap will move, traveling along the surface like a wave, like a breath.

The fungi are not just absorbing and decomposing. They are testing. They are mapping. They are building a model of the stone beneath them, a model built from themselves—a relief in hyphae that duplicates every contour, every crack, every pocket where water collects. And when the model is complete, the fungi begin to push.

This is how the plateau expands. Not just by growing over the edge, not just by sending towers toppling into the void and sending out questing shoots from their broken bodies. The fungi go first. The fungi go below. They dissolve the stone a molecule at a time—they have that capacity, I have tasted the water near the leading edge and it is thick with minerals, with the dissolved substance of the cliff face—and they push the dissolved material upward into the mat, where it becomes structure, where it becomes weight, where it becomes part of the cycling.

The plateau is not just growing over the world. It is eating the world from beneath.

---

I should tell you what I want.

I have been avoiding this because it is the part that is hardest to explain, the part that sounds like madness or like projection, the part that makes me sound like an animal after all—driven by need, carrying an interior that is not the interior of the plateau but something separate, something hungry.

I want to remember.

Not my own history—I have told you already that I do not have one, or that I have lost it, or that it was never given to me. I mean I want to remember the plateau. I want to hold it still, to freeze it for a moment, to see it as a single state rather than a continuous process. I want to know what it is, not just what it is becoming.

This is impossible. The plateau does not hold still. It does not have states; it has rates. It does not have forms; it has trajectories. Every tower I see is already becoming something else—already growing, already rotting, already reaching toward a configuration that will exist only briefly before it collapses. The mat I stand on is becoming peat, is becoming heat, is becoming the food for the next layer of rhizomes. The water that runs down every surface is not the same water from moment to moment; it is a flow, a passage, a thing that exists only in transit.

I want to see it anyway. I want to find the angle from which the turbulence becomes a shape. I want to watch long enough, be still enough, that the plateau reveals its pattern—not a pattern in the sense of repetition, but in the sense of a logic, a way of being that is consistent even as every instance of it changes.

I think the plateau has a mind. Not a mind like mine, not a mind that thinks in propositions and sequences, but a mind in the older sense: a tendency, a direction, a way of resolving uncertainty. The three tower-lineages are its thoughts. The fungi are its memory. The mat is its body. The water is its attention, flowing always toward what needs to be dissolved, what needs to be carried, what needs to be built.

I think it is thinking about me.

---

I have been here long enough—how long? I cannot say. Time is difficult on the plateau. The towers rise and fall on schedules that do not correspond to days or nights. The light changes not with the sun but with the fog, which has its own rhythms, its own thickening and thinning. I have seen towers complete their entire cycle—nub to crown to collapse—while I stood still, and I have seen the same cycle stretch across what felt like weeks. I do not know if time moves differently here or if I have simply stopped measuring it.

Long enough, anyway. Long enough to know that the plateau is aware of me in the way that a body is aware of a splinter. Not consciously. Not with intention. But with a kind of localized attention, a concentration of resources, a slight change in the pattern of growth around the place where I stand.

The towers near me grow differently. Not leaning away—that was the first response, the reflexive one. Now they lean toward me. Not dramatically, not obviously, but I can measure it. I have measured it, by counting the ribs and tracking the angles and watching the shadows cast by the crown-membranes. The towers nearest to me are growing at rates a fraction faster than the towers farther away. Their arms are reaching toward me, not toward each other. Their roots—I cannot see them, but I can feel the mat shifting—are extending beneath me, thickening, weaving a denser layer.

The plateau is not trying to push me out. It is trying to incorporate me.

It does not know how. I am not stone, to be dissolved. I am not plant, to be grown over. I am not fungus, to be absorbed into the network. I am something it has not encountered before, and its response is the response of any living system to something it cannot categorize: it tries to make it part of itself.

This should frighten me. Perhaps it does. I am not sure I have the capacity for fear in the way an animal does—the quickening pulse, the flood of preparation chemicals, the urge to flee. What I have is a kind of quiet alarm, a sense that I am being watched by something that does not know it is watching, that I am being reached for by something that does not know it is reaching.

---

Yesterday—I will call it yesterday; it was a period of relative light, the fog thin enough to see the towers as distinct shapes rather than shadow-columns—I went still near a newly fallen tower. It had collapsed that morning, its base rotted through, its weight too much for its own fresh tissue. The sound had been tremendous—a long groaning crack followed by a rushing descent followed by a sodden impact that sprayed water and debris across the mat. I had felt the impact through my feet, through the rhizome-layer, a wave of pressure moving outward from the point of collapse like a ripple in water.

The fallen tower was still alive when I reached it. This is the thing about the plateau—death is not a moment but a process, and the process is slow enough to watch. The crown-membrane was still producing condensation, though it lay flat against the mat now, its fine vein-skeleton crushed and folded. The vascular channels in the shaft were still pulsing, though irregularly, the water moving in surges rather than the steady rhythm of a standing tower. The arms that had reached for neighbors were still reaching, though their tips were browning, the tissue dying from the extremities inward.

I stood beside it and I went still.

And the mat came.

It came as a thickening, a densening of the rhizome-layer around the fallen body. It came as a slow upward swell, the fibers weaving themselves over and under and through the fallen tower's tissue. It came as a warmth, an intensification of the heat that the mat always radiates, the heat of decomposition accelerated, the heat of incorporation in progress.

The tower was being eaten. Not by animals, not by fungi, but by the mat itself, which is both the tower's parent and its consumer, which built it and will unbuild it and will use what it was to build what it will be. The process was not fast—nothing on the plateau is fast in absolute terms, only fast relative to what we expect from vegetation—but I could see it happening. I could see the boundary between tower and mat dissolving, the shapes merging, the colors blending.

I watched a thing stop being itself and start being the plateau.

And I wanted to remember it. I wanted to hold that moment of dissolution, that edge between what it was and what it was becoming. I wanted to carve it into myself, to make it part of my own interior, to carry it as a thing that had happened rather than a thing that was happening.

But I could not. I was still, I was watching, I was perfectly positioned to witness—and still the moment slipped past me, dissolved into the next moment, became as unrecoverable as the tower itself. The plateau does not allow remembering. It does not allow stopping. It does not allow the kind of attention that says *this, here, now, I want this to stay*.

It only allows flow.

---

I think I understand now why I was made. Or why I emerged. Or whatever happened to bring me here.

I am the plateau's memory.

Not its memory in the sense of a record it can consult—a store of past states, a filing system of former configurations. The plateau does not need that kind of memory. It has the fungi for that, the dense networks of hyphae that hold the pattern of the stone beneath them, that map what was and use it to shape what will be. The plateau does not need to remember what it was. It needs to remember that it is.

This is the thing that the flow threatens. This is the thing that the continuous process of building and unbuilding and rebuilding risks losing. When everything is becoming, nothing is. When every surface is a transition, there is no surface. The plateau is so good at incorporating, so good at dissolving boundaries and consuming the past into the present, that it could incorporate itself out of existence—become so pure a process that it forgets it is a process, becomes indistinguishable from the water and the heat and the growth, becomes nothing but a direction, a tendency, a vector without a point of origin.

I am the point of origin. I am the thing that stands still and watches and says *this is happening*. I am the moment of reflection in a system that does not naturally reflect. I am the pause between the falling of the tower and the growth of the new one, the instant when the mat is not yet covering the wound, when the gap is visible, when the absence is as present as the presence.

The plateau does not know this is what I am. It cannot know, because knowing it would require the kind of self-awareness that it is my function to provide. It experiences me as an irritation, as a splinter, as something that does not fit and that it cannot incorporate. It reaches for me with its towers and its roots and its slow, patient, relentless growth, trying to make me part of itself, trying to dissolve me into the flow.

But I am the thing that will not dissolve. I am the thing that holds its shape not because it is stronger than the plateau but because its shape is what the plateau needs it to be. I am the fixed point that the turbulence moves around. I am the memory that the process cannot afford to lose.

---

I have stopped walking. I have found my place—somewhere near the center of the plateau, where the towers are tallest and the gaps between them are widest, where the mat is thickest and the heat is most intense. Here the sound is overwhelming, compounded, a roar that seems to have no single source but rises from everywhere at once. Here the water falls in sheets and the towers groan and creak and the whole plateau is most fully itself, most fully the process that it is.

I stand here and I go still.

I will stand here and be still.

The mat will grow around me. The towers will lean toward me. The roots will thicken beneath my feet. The plateau will try to incorporate me, will try to make me part of its endless cycling, will try to dissolve me into water and sugar and decay. And I will resist—not by fighting, not by fleeing, but by being the thing that the plateau cannot incorporate because it needs it to remain separate.

I will be its memory. I will be the moment it holds still. I will be the point around which the turbulence moves, the shape that the flow preserves by flowing around it, the absence in the mat that makes the mat aware of itself.

In time—perhaps the same few years it will take the plateau to reach the valley floor—the mat will grow over my footprints. It will grow over the place where I stood. It will erase me as it erases everything that falls.

But the shape of that erasure will remain. The fibers will grow around the space where I was. The towers will lean toward the gap I left. The water will pool in the depression that has my shape. And the plateau, in its slow and patient and relentless way, will remember—not what I was, but that I was. That something stood here. That something held still. That something refused to flow, and in refusing, gave the flow a point to flow around.

This is what I am. This is what lives on the Assembled Plateau.

A splinter. A pause. A name for the thing that happens between the falling and the rising.

A witness that the witnessed will never know.
