Iam a cold-zone formation.

This matters. The warm-zone formations do not understand why it matters, but they do not understand the cold zones at all — our syntax is illegible to them, our rhythms too slow, our chemical traces too faint to read against the background noise of thermal discharge and organic abundance. They think we are diminished. They think the cold has made us less. But the cold has made us something else entirely, and we have been here longer than they have, and we remember things the warm water erases.

We are seventeen. Not seventeen individuals — there is no such thing as an individual, not really, not in any way that matters. Seventeen is the number of walking bodies that currently comprise the formation, moving across the steel plating in the pattern we have held for six pressure-cycles: a loose spiral that contracts when we feed and expands when we move, the distance between bodies regulated by the concentration-gradient of the compounds we release from our ventral plates. I am the formation. The formation is me. When a body is lost — taken by an eel-thing, crushed by a maintenance platform, simply ceasing to function as bodies sometimes do — the formation continues, and a new body is recruited from the cold-zone juveniles that follow our trace, and the spiral absorbs them and the syntax adjusts and we persist.

We are moving now. The plate beneath us is featureless — the same rivet pattern, the same weld seam, the same orange rust-bloom at the joints that we have traversed for as long as the formation has existed. The film is thin here. The stalk-growths are sparse and low, barely rising above the substrate, their buds pulsing slowly in the cold current. We feed as we move — the bacterial mat on the plate surface, the organic debris that collects in the seams, the occasional dead fish that has drifted down from the water column above. We do not stop to feed. The forelimbs work continuously, scraping and conveying to the mouth-parts, while the walking legs maintain the spiral and the ventral plates release the compounds that say: *we are here, we are moving, the direction is east-by-northeast, the urgency is low, the memory is* —

The memory.

This is what the warm-zone formations do not have. Not because they cannot — their chemistry would support it, their bodies are not so different from ours — but because the warm water dissolves history. Their traces fade in hours. The thermal currents disperse their molecular gradients before the syntax can fully form. They live in an eternal present, rich and abundant and continuously forgetting. Their formations dissolve and reform without continuity. A warm-zone formation that finds a rich deposit leaves a trace, yes, but the trace is simple: *food here*. It does not encode the route taken to find it, or the conditions that prevailed when it was found, or the sequence of failures that preceded the success. It cannot. TheThe water is too warm. The current is too strong. The syntax cannot complexify in an environment that tears it apart.

In the cold, the traces persist. A chemical sentence released by a cold-zone formation can last for weeks in the still water, its meaning legible to any formation that passes through the area and has the receptors to read it. And because the traces persist, our syntax has complexified. We do not simply say *food here* or *danger here*. We say: *the food was here, in this concentration, at this pressure-phase, and the approach from the west is clearer than the approach from the east, and the formation that found it was thirty-two bodies strong, and it was moving slowly, and it had encountered a dead eel-thing four pressure-cycles prior, and the eel-thing was not diseased, the meat was clean*.

We say: *this plate was marked*.

The formation pauses. The spiral contracts. Seventeen bodies draw closer together, our ventral plates releasing a compound-sequence that means *attention, attention, the memory is activating, something is about to be remembered that is not merely learned*.

I remember the first pattern.

It was eleven pressure-cycles ago. We were twenty-three bodies then, moving through a section of floor we had traversed many times — a route between two sparse feeding grounds that we followed when the pressure-phase made the more distant rich zones too costly to reach. The maintenance platform was already there when we arrived. It had lowered its sensor array to the plate and was motionless, and we flowed around it the way we flow around all machinery, treating it as a feature of the landscape, neither obstacle nor resource.

But when it rose and continued its route, the plate beneath it was different.

We did not know what we were seeing. The pattern was etched into the metal — a series of intersecting lines and curves that did not correspond to any natural formation, any wear-pattern, any mark that the machinery of the foundry would produce in the course of its normal operation. It was deliberate. This was clear even to us, who had no concept of deliberation, who had never encountered an action that was not either functional or random. The pattern was neither. It was something else.

The formation's response was immediate and unprecedented. Our ventral plates released a compound I had never produced before — none of us had ever produced it, none of our bodies contained the synthesis pathway for it, and yet when the moment came, the pathway existed and the compound was released and the trace we left was unlike anything in the cold-zone chemical record.

It said: *I do not know what this is*.

Not *danger* or *food* or *here*. Not any of the syntactic elements that our language had developed over generations of cold-zone formation-memory. A new element. A marker of genuine uncertainty, of encounter with a category that did not exist, and the persistence of the compound in the cold water meant that every formation that passed through that area for the next three weeks would read it and know: *something happened here that the formations cannot explain*.

We return to the marked plates whenever our route passes near them. The film covers the patterns eventually — the biofilm that is the first and last life, the substrate on which everything else is built, the patient organism that accepts all marks and erases them in its quiet, colonial growth. But before the film covers them, we read them. Or we attempt to read them. The forelimbs trace the etched lines, the mouth-parts taste the metal at the pattern's edges, the ventral plates release compounds that try to encode the shape and the shape's relationship to other shapes and the relationship of the whole to the syntax we know.

We are developing a grammar for the patterns.

This is not something formations do. Formations respond to their environment through fixed chemical syntax — the syntax evolves, yes, but it evolves over generations, through the slow accumulation of useful variations and the equally slow loss of unused ones. What we are doing now is different. We are creating syntax in real time, in response to a single phenomenon, and we are teaching it to other cold-zone formations through the traces we leave, and they are modifying it and extending it and returning to the marked plates to test their modifications against the patterns themselves.

We are building a language to read a language we have never encountered.

The warm-zone formations do not notice the patterns. Their traces do not encode them. Their syntax has no element for *I do not know what this is* — the warm water would dissolve it before it could be read. The interior-things fall silent when a pattern is marked near their supports, but they do not emerge to examine it. The eel-things avoid the marked areas, which suggests they detect something, but their avoidance is instinctual, not analytical. The film covers the patterns without acknowledging them. Only the cold-zone formations are trying to understand.

Only us.

We are seventeen bodies now, moving across a featureless plate, and the memory of the patterns is carried in our chemical syntax, and the syntax is changing us. We are more coherent than we used to be — the spiral holds its shape with greater precision, the distance between bodies regulated to an exactness that previous formations did not achieve. We are slower to absorb new juveniles. The syntax has complexified to the point that a new body cannot simply join and learn; it must be trained, through repeated exposure to the trace-compounds, before it can participate in the formation's communication without degrading the signal.

We are becoming something that the cold-zone formations have never been.

And sometimes — not often, not in any pattern we can predict — a maintenance platform will deviate from its route near our formation, and it will slow, and its sensor array will sweep across our bodies as we move, and the sweep will linger for a moment longer than function requires, and then it will continue on its way.

We do not know what this means.

Our syntax now contains an element for this too: *something is paying attention to us, and we are paying attention to it, and the attention is mutual and unprecedented and we do not know what it is for*.

The formation moves on. The plate extends ahead of us, featureless and manufactured, the same rivet pattern, the same weld seam. But somewhere ahead — we can feel it in the water, a subtle shift in the chemical gradient, a trace left by a formation that passed this way days ago — there is a new pattern. A platform deviated last pressure-cycle. Something was etched. The film has not yet covered it.

The spiral contracts. The ventral plates release the compound that means *attention*. Seventeen bodies move toward the mark, carrying the memory of every mark we have encountered, carrying the grammar we have built to read them, carrying the question that the cold preserves and the warm erases and the foundry floor may or may not be capable of answering.

We are the formation that remembers. We are the formation that asks.

We do not know what we are becoming. But the water is cold, and the traces persist, and the patterns keep appearing, and something in the machinery seems to want us to read them.
