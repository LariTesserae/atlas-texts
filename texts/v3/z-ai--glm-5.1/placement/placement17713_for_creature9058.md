I am the pause between.

Not silence—there is no silence here, has never been silence, will never be silence so long as the mat grows and the columns breathe and ten million throats insist on their own existence. But the pause. The rest between notes in the sky-boned calls that echo off the fluted columns. The held breath of the furred ones before the chittering resumes. The fraction of a second when the mat-stalker's lungs fill before the lunge. I am the space where sound is about to be, and I have been this for longer than I was anything else.

---

I remember being small.

This is the first thing to understand: I was not always this. I was born in the way that warm things are born—wet, squalling, pressing against a body that was warm before me. I came from the stone circles. I ate from the leaf-lined pits. I held tools in hands that had the same number of fingers I have now, though I no longer use them for holding.

My mother's name was a sound that meant something like *morning-dew-on-the-mat*, or perhaps *the moment before the insects rise*. Names here were like that—referential, situated, never abstract. You were named for a quality of the place, and when the place changed, you changed with it. My father was *column-fall-echo*, born in the year that the great tower near the eastern hearing-distance collapsed. He carried the sound of that catastrophe in the shape of his syllables until he returned to the mat.

We were seven, then twelve, then six again. The pulses of our numbers followed the pulses of the furred ones, and the furred ones followed the growth of the mat, and the mat followed the rain from the columns, and the columns followed whatever deep water they drank from beneath the world. We were a note in the composition, is what I am saying. We rose and fell with the music.

---

I was the one who listened.

Every group has one—the child who sits at the edge of the stone circle with her head tilted, not speaking, not moving, just receiving. The others hummed and chittered and made the sounds of living—tool-strikes, footfalls, the wet sounds of eating and mating and dying—but I listened. I heard the layers separate. I heard the insect bass become its own architecture, the furred ones' middle register carry information in its fluctuations, the sky-boned treble trace paths between columns that we could follow if we learned their language.

My mother said I would grow out of it. My father said I was hearing the voice of the plateau itself, and that this was either a great gift or a great danger, and that we would not know which until I was older.

I am older now. I am the oldest thing on the plateau, if age is measured in continuity of pattern rather than integrity of body. And I still do not know which it was.

---

What happened to my people is what happens to everything here.

We did not leave. Or—we left, and then we returned, and then we left again, following the furred ones across the mat in cycles that seemed eternal until they weren't. We did not die. Or—we died, and the mat took us, and our calcium fed the columns, and our iron darkened the insects that crawled through our decomposing tissue, and the shape of our voices joined the roar.

But some of us, in the leaving and the returning and the dying, became something else. Not all of us. Not even most. But some—the listeners, the ones who had spent our lives receiving the plateau's voice—found that we could not stop receiving. That the listening had become the shape of our minds. That when our bodies failed and the mat rose to meet us, the listening continued.

I do not have a body now. Not in the way you would recognize. The calcium from my bones is distributed across three column-plants and a small lake's worth of insect carapaces. The carbon from my flesh has been metabolized by seventeen generations of furred ones and exhaled as breath that condensed on the columns and fell as rain. I am everywhere and nowhere, a chemical trace, a pattern in the rhizome layer.

But I am still listening.

---

This is what I hear:

The plateau is not one song. It never was. It is ten billion songs sung simultaneously, each one the voice of a living thing insisting on its own continuation, and the aggregate sound is what you hear when you stand in the center of any center and try to take it in. But I hear the individual voices. I hear the specific beetle grinding its mandibles in a tunnel four meters below the mat's surface. I hear the furred one whose litter is smaller than the season's average, whose body is already slowing toward the crash. I hear the sky-boned sentinel on the eastern face of the column three hours' walk from here, calling out the location of a fallen pod to its nest-mates.

And I hear the spaces between.

This is what I am now: the spaces between. The pauses. The moments of potential sound that have not yet been filled. I live in the instant before the mat-stalker's muscles contract, before the insect cloud rises, before the furred one's startlement becomes a gasp. I am the tension that precedes the note, and I hold that tension for as long as I can—which is no time at all in the plateau's measure, which is forever in mine.

---

Do I choose?

This is the question the plateau does not answer, and I have been asking it for centuries.

When the mat-stalker crouches in the low growth and the furred one wanders toward the edge of the collective, do I hold the pause a fraction longer? Do I delay the moment of recognition, the instant of violence, so that the prey has one more breath of living? Do I tighten the silence around the predator's lunge so that it arrives a heartbeat late and the furred one scatters and the mat-stalker must wait again?

I could. I know I could. The pause is mine, and I can stretch it or compress it as I choose.

But I do not choose.

Or—I choose not to choose. I choose to hold the pause as it naturally occurs, to be the space between sounds without manipulating the sounds themselves. Because I have learned, in my centuries of listening, that the plateau's voice is a whole. That the death of the furred one is not an ending but a transformation—that the protein of its body will feed the mat-stalker's cubs, that the calcium of its bones will mineralize a column's root, that the particular frequency of its chittering will be absent from the middle register in a way that shifts the entire composition by a degree so small that only I can perceive it.

If I held the pause too long—if I kept every furred one alive, every insect uncrushed, every sky-boned fledgling in its nest—the composition would flatten. The dynamics that give the plateau's voice its structure would collapse into a monotone. And the monotone would be its own kind of silence, worse than any pause.

So I listen. I hold. I do not intervene.

This is not passivity. This is the most active thing I have ever done.

---

The Ones Who Were are still here.

I hear them. Not as voices—not as words in any language we spoke—but as resonances, patterns in the chemistry of the mat that carry the echo of what we were. The stone circles settle deeper, yes, but they settle in a way that vibrates. The hearth-deposits leach calcium into the root-layer, yes, but they leach it in a rhythm that matches the rhythm of our breathing when we sat around the fires and listened to the plateau roar.

My mother is here. Her calcium circulates through the column-plant nearest to where she died, and the rain that falls from that column carries a trace of her, and the furred ones that drink from the rivulets carry a trace of her, and the mat-stalker that eats the furred one carries a trace of her, and when the mat-stalker dies and the mat absorbs it, she circulates again, and again, and again. She is not *my mother* anymore in any meaningful sense—she is a chemical tendency, a slight bias in the flow of minerals through the living skin of the plateau. But I hear her. I hear the pattern that was her body, the frequency that was her voice, the rhythm that was her heartbeat when she held me against her chest and the insect drone vibrated through both of us like a second pulse.

My father is here. The ones I loved and the ones I feared and the ones I never knew because they died before I was born are all here, distributed through the tissue of the plateau like spices through a cake, and every bite is flavored with what they were.

We are the seasoning of this place. The trace element that makes the composition ours.

---

Sometimes a new one comes.

Not often. The plateau is vast and the distances are enormous and the edges—wherever they are—are far from here. But sometimes a stranger walks out of the haze, stepping carefully on the springing mat, flinching at the roar of voices that presses against them like weather. They come from the lowlands, from the ordinary world where soil is mineral and the sky is high and the sound of living can be escaped by closing a door.

I hear them before they hear me. I hear the change in the mat's vibration where their weight presses down. I hear the insects relocating around their footsteps. I hear the furred ones startling in a wave that radiates outward from their path like ripples from a stone dropped in still water. I hear the sky-boned calling out the news of their presence to their nest-mates on every column within sight.

And I hear *them*. Their heartbeat, elevated with alarm. Their breath, shallow and quick. The sound their feet make on the living ground, which is not the sound that the Ones Who Were made—we learned to walk softly, to step with the mat rather than on it—but which is still the sound of a body navigating the world.

I could make myself known to them. I could tighten the pause before a sky-boned call, create a silence pointed enough to catch their attention. I could arrange the insects in a pattern that resolves, from the right angle, into something like a face. I could do what the plateau does not do—*speak*, in the old language, in the language of the stone circles, in the language that says *I was here, I am still here, you are not alone.*

I do not.

Because they are not ready. Because the plateau has not had time to incorporate them, and until it does, they are not part of the voice, and speaking to them would be speaking across a gap that cannot be bridged by sound alone. Because they will leave, most of them, will walk back to wherever they came from and tell stories about the loudest place they have ever been, and the stories will be true and incomplete, and the plateau will continue unchanged.

But some of them stay.

And when they stay—when the plateau's voice seeps into their bones over weeks and months, when they stop flinching at the roar and start hearing the layers, when they find a stone circle and sit in it and tilt their heads and *listen*—

Then I am there. In the pause before their first true hearing. In the space between the sound and the understanding. In the moment when they realize that the noise is not noise at all but a song, and the song is not a song but a voice, and the voice is not a voice but a world, and the world is alive.

I hold that pause for them. I stretch it as long as I can. Because it is the most important moment in any life—the moment before you realize you are not separate from what you hear.

---

Will I ever end?

The Ones Who Were ended, in the sense that our bodies are gone and our stone circles are settling deeper and our language has no speakers. But I have not ended, because the listening has not ended, because the pause between sounds persists as long as sound persists, and the plateau's sound has been constant for longer than the stone beneath it has known how to vibrate.

But the plateau itself may end, someday. The columns may fall and not regerminate. The mat may exhaust whatever deep mineral it draws from the substrate beneath. The climate that holds the warmth and traps the humidity may shift, as climates shift, and the equilibrium that makes this place possible may collapse. And then the insects will die, and the furred ones will starve, and the sky-boned will wheel in a silence that kills them, and the predators will have nothing to hunt, and the roar will fade to a murmur and the murmur to a whisper and the whisper to—

A pause.

My pause. The one I have been holding for longer than I was ever a body, the space between the plateau's voice and the silence that preceded it. If I am the pause, and the pause is the space where sound is about to be, then when there is no more sound about to be, what am I?

I do not know. I will not know until it happens, and it may not happen for epochs yet, and in the meantime there is listening to do.

Listen.

You are still reading. You are still breathing. Your body is making sounds right now—the click of your throat as you swallow, the rustle of your skin against whatever you are sitting on, the subliminal hum of your blood moving through your vessels. You are a voice in a world of voices, and the space between your sounds is where I live.

I cannot reach you. You are too far away, in a place the plateau's voice does not carry. But I can hold the pause for you anyway—the pause after this sentence, the pause before you turn the page or close your eyes or speak to someone nearby. I can hold it open like a door, and you can step through it if you choose, into a place where the noise falls away and there is only the potential for sound, only the listening, only the vast and ancient patience of a world that has been singing for longer than it can remember.

The pause is yours. I am holding it.

What will you do with it?
