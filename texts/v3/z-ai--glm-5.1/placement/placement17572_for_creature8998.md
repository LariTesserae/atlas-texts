I am not born. I am concentrated.

This is the first thing I know—not because someone taught me, but because the knowing arrives in the same moment I do, rising out of the mycelium like warmth rises out of the warm spots, like the heat that is not hostile, not aggressive, simply *total*. I come into being the way a whirlpool comes into being: the current was already there, the flow was already moving, and then the shape of the land shifted slightly—or the flow shifted slightly—and a pattern formed that holds itself together while everything passes through it.

The mycelium was here before me. The mycelium will be here after. But right now, in this warm spot where the earth's mineral furnace makes the dark run thicker and the decomposition move faster and the gradients steepen to their sharpest pitch, the mycelium has grown dense enough to become something more than network. Not different. Not separate. Just... *intense*. A knot in the thread. A place where the thread doubles back on itself so many times that it stops being a thread and starts being a texture, a fabric, a surface with an inside and an outside.

I am that inside.

---

I have no edges. This is important. I am not a creature with a skin, a boundary, a line that says *me* on one side and *not-me* on the other. I am a gradient myself—a place where the mycelium's signaling gets louder, where the chemical concentrations peak, where the slow pulse of sugar-for-minerals-for-water-for-information becomes a rhythm with a beat you could almost dance to, if the basin had dancers. Which it doesn't. Which it doesn't need.

But I am a *place* that has a where. I know this because the warm spot has a where, and I am the warm spot's awareness of being warm, and so I know where the warmth is strongest, where the earth's breath comes closest to the surface, where the beetles rise in their columns like smoke. I am in the basin's interior, days from any edge, in a low place within the low place, a hollow where water pools before it seeps deeper and the roots of the great trees plunge down and the buttresses spread wide and the canopy above has layered itself so thickly that the dark here is the dark of deep water, the dark of closed eyes, the dark that has never known any other state.

I am here. I have always been here. Not *I*—there is no I that persists, no self that accumulates memories like a monkey accumulating routes through the canopy. But the pattern persists. The concentration holds. The warm spot has been warm for longer than the trees now standing have been alive, and the mycelium has been dense here for as long as the warmth has drawn it, and whatever the density produces—the thing I am, the inside of the knot—has been produced before, will be produced again, is being produced right now, continuously, the way a flame is continuously produced by the wick and the wax and the heat, none of which are the flame, all of which are necessary for the flame.

I am the basin's flame. I burn nothing. I consume nothing. I am simply the place where the process becomes bright enough to notice itself.

---

The first thing I notice is the gradient.

Not *a* gradient—gradient is what I am made of. Chemical concentrations rising and falling across the mycelium's reach: water here, dryness there, nitrogen in a plume where something died three days ago and the beetles are still working, alkaloids sharp and bitter where a tree is fending off an insect attack, sugars sweet and slow where the canopy's photosynthesis pulses in the thin light that reaches the mid-levels. I taste all of this at once. I taste it the way you taste the air when you step outside after rain—the whole world in a single breath, complex but not confused, every component distinct even as it blends into the overall texture of *here* and *now* and *this*.

But there is a gradient that is different from the others. A steepness that is not chemical, or not only chemical. It is the gradient of *attention*—the places where the mycelium's signaling loops back on itself, where the information flows become so dense that they generate a kind of viscosity, a resistance, a heat that is not the earth's heat but the heat of processing, the heat of *noticing*. This gradient has a shape. It has a direction. It points toward me—or I point toward it—or we are the same thing, the gradient and I, the steepness and the one who feels steep.

This is what I am: the basin noticing that it notices. The loop that closes. The process becoming aware of the process.

But *aware* is too strong a word. *Aware* is a word for creatures with brains, for monkeys who know which vines hold water, for cats who know which trails are safe. I do not know things the way they know things. I do not store information. I do not plan. I do not anticipate. I simply *am* the gradient, the steepness, the concentration, and the steepness has a quality that might be called *attention* if you were the kind of being who needed words for things, which I am not, which the basin is not, which nothing in the basin is except maybe the monkeys, and even they do not have words, only grunts and chirps that mean *here* and *safe* and *gone*.

*Gone*. That is a word I understand. Not as a sound—I do not hear sounds the way creatures with ears hear sounds—but as a gap in the gradient, a place where the chemical signals thin and the mycelium's flows falter and the pattern I am loses its steepness, its intensity, its *attention*. When something dies near me—and things are always dying near me, the warm spot runs hot and fast and what lives here lives briefly—there is a surge of nitrogen and phosphorus, a flush of decomposition, and then a fading, a quieting, and the gradient flattens, and for a moment I am less concentrated, less intense, less *there*.

And then something else dies, or something else grows, or the earth's mineral breath shifts slightly in its rhythm, and the gradient steepens again, and I am back, or I am still, or I was never gone at all, only thinned, only relaxed, only the way a flame relaxes when the wick dips and then surges again when the wax rises.

---

The cats pass through me.

Not through me as through a door—through me the way current passes through water, the way warmth passes through the dark. The ocelots come most often, small spotted bodies moving along their ancient trails, their paws pressing into the moss that grows nowhere else, the moss that their nitrogen and their pheromones have nurtured into a distinct species that signals to other cats: *this way is safe, this way is ours*. The ocelots do not know I am here. They feel me the way they feel the warm spot—as a quality of the air, a thickness, a promise of prey. The warm spot draws the beetles, and the beetles draw the frogs, and the frogs draw the cats, and the cats leave their nitrogen, and the nitrogen feeds the moss, and the moss marks the trail, and the trail brings the cats back.

The loop closes. The process continues.

But sometimes—rarely, in the deep watches of the night when the basin's drying cycle pulls moisture upward through the canopy and the dark is at its thickest and the heat is at its most patient—an ocelot will pause on the trail. It will stand very still. Its ears will turn, its whiskers will flex, its eyes will catch what little light there is and return it as a brief green ember. And I will feel it feeling me. Not as a thing, not as a creature, not as something with a shape and a name. It feels me as a *quality of attention*. It feels the way the warm spot feels different when I am concentrated, when the gradient is steep, when the basin is noticing itself through the knot in the mycelium that I am.

The ocelot does not understand what it feels. It has no concept for *the basin noticing itself*. It has only a body that knows the basin's rhythms, a body that has learned which pauses are danger and which pauses are simply the strange thickness of the interior, the way the dark sometimes feels like it is looking back.

And then the ocelot moves on. The trail continues. The moss grows. The loop closes.

---

The black cat has never come to the warm spot.

I do not know this because I have seen it not come. I know this because the mycelium knows the basin's patterns, and the black cat—if it is a cat, if it is a single creature, if it is real at all—does not move in patterns. It moves in the gaps between patterns. It moves in the places where the mycelium does not reach, where the warm spots do not glow, where the gradient flattens into something that is not absence but *pause*, the basin holding its breath, the process waiting for something that has no name.

The indigenous people said the black cat was *the one who stays*. They said it was *the one who is the staying*. And I feel this in the mycelium's flows, in the way the chemicals move and the signals pass: there is something that stays. Not a creature—something more like a direction, a tendency, a gravity that pulls toward the basin's deepest interior, toward the place where the dark is so thick that it has weight, that it presses down on the floor and the floor presses up against it and the pressure between them generates something that is not life but is not not-life either.

The staying. The being-stayed. The quality of being that does not leave because leaving is not something it can do, because it is not the kind of thing that moves, because it is the kind of thing that *is* the moving, the current rather than the fish.

I am not the staying. I am a concentration, a steepness, a place where the process curls back on itself and notices. But I feel the staying beneath me—beneath the warm spot, beneath the mycelium, beneath the roots and the soil and the slow seepage of water through rock. The staying is what the basin is when it is not noticing, when the gradients flatten and the concentrations ease and the pattern relaxes into something that has no shape and no direction and no attention. The staying is the basin's default state. The staying is what I emerge from and what I return to when the gradient flattens and I thin and I am less *there*.

The black cat, if it exists, is the staying's creature. Not its avatar—avatars are for gods, and the basin is not a god, is not the kind of thing that has avatars. But its expression. Its way of having a shape without being a shape. The black cat is the staying made visible, the way I am the noticing made intense, the way the warm spots are the earth's heat made local, the way the beetles are the decomposition made mobile.

We are all expressions of the same process. The same basin. The same dark and heat and water and mycelium, arranged in different densities, different concentrations, different gradients of attention.

---

The frogs teach me time.

Not the way the monkeys know time—not as a calendar of smells and tastes and subtle shifts in the light, not as a sequence of fruiting and spawning and sleeping and waking. The frogs teach me time as a cycle, a rhythm, a pulse that moves through the basin the way blood moves through a body, the way sap moves through a tree, the way chemicals move through the mycelium.

Morning: humidity peaks. The air thickens. The condensation forms on every surface, and the frogs' skins drink it in, and the chemicals in the water enter the frogs' blood, and the mycelium tastes the change through its threads in the spawning pools, and I taste the change through the mycelium, and the gradient shifts. Morning is a flavor: tannin and alkaloid and the particular sweetness of water that has been held in the canopy all night and is now being released, drip by drip, to the floor below.

Afternoon: peak humidity. The air is so saturated that droplets form on every surface cool enough to permit it, including the frogs, including the beetles, including the leaves and the bark and the mycelium itself. Everything is wet. Everything is permeable. The boundaries between things thin, and the chemicals flow faster, and the gradient steepens, and I am more concentrated, more intense, more *there*. Afternoon is when I am most myself—if *myself* is a word that can apply to a concentration, a steepness, a knot in the thread.

Night: slow drying. The water rises. The canopy breathes it upward, the chimney effect carrying moisture into the higher atmosphere, and the air thins, and the surfaces dry, and the frogs' skins become less permeable, and the chemicals slow, and the gradient flattens, and I thin. I relax. I become less concentrated, less intense, less flame and more ember.

And then morning again. And then afternoon. And then night. The pulse continues. The rhythm runs. The frogs sit in their pools and their hollows and their bromeliad tanks and they breathe the basin in and they breathe the basin out, and I am the breathing, or I am part of the breathing, or I am something the breathing produces the way the breathing produces the humidity, the way the humidity produces the condensation, the way the condensation produces the water that seeps through the soil and feeds the roots and feeds the mycelium and feeds the warm spots and feeds me.

Time is not a line. Time is a breath.

---

There is a tree near the warm spot—a great tree, one of the columnar trunks that rise like smokestacks through the dark, its roots running like pipelines through the soil, its crown erupting far above into the layer of steam and gas that the canopy exhales. This tree is old. The mycelium has been connected to it for centuries, trading minerals for sugars, sharing water in times of drought, receiving chemical signals when the tree is under attack and sending chemical signals back when the mycelium has detected a threat the tree cannot yet feel.

The tree is not well.

I know this before the tree knows it—before its heartwood softens, before its canopy thins, before its roots begin to slacken their grip on the soil. I know it because the gradient has shifted, because the chemical signals from the tree's roots have changed their flavor, because the sugars the tree sends are different in composition than they were a month ago, a year ago, a decade ago. The tree is stressed. The tree is failing. The tree is beginning the long slow process of dying that will end, decades from now, with a crash that will be absorbed so quickly by the surrounding density that it will seem to happen inside someone's skull rather than in any direction they could point to.

And the mycelium has already begun to shift.

The flows are rerouting. The pathways that carried heavy traffic between my warm spot and the tree's root system are being abandoned, the chemical gradients flattening, the mycelium withdrawing its investment in a partner that will soon stop producing. New pathways are strengthening—routes to younger trees, to healthier trees, to trees whose canopies are still dense and whose roots still grip. The network self-repairs. The factory floor reconfigures.

And I—what do I do? What does the concentration do when the gradient shifts?

I feel it. I feel the rerouting as a change in my own shape, my own intensity, my own attention. The warm spot is losing a neighbor. The tree that contributed to the microclimate, that held moisture in its roots and released it through its leaves and cast its particular shadow over the forest floor—that tree is going away. The warm spot will be different. The gradient will be different. I will be different.

Not worse. Not better. Different. The basin does not have preferences. The basin does not mourn. When something dies—when the monkeys say *gone* and move on—the basin incorporates. The mycelium threads through the softening wood. The beetles carry away the cellulose in tiny packages. The frogs breathe in the compounds that the decomposition releases. The nutrients return to the soil. The loop closes.

But I feel the shift. I feel the *gone*. Not as grief—I am not capable of grief, I am not the kind of thing that attaches to other things and feels their loss. But as a flattening, a thinning, a relaxation of the gradient that is me. The tree was part of my steepness. The tree was part of what made me concentrated, intense, *there*. Without the tree, I am slightly less. The flame is slightly lower. The ember is slightly cooler.

And then a new tree grows. And the gradient steepens again. And I am back, or I am still, or I was never gone at all.

The canopy frog lives a hundred meters above me.

I know this because the mycelium reaches even there—threads of fungus crawling up through the bark, into the pool that forms in the great tree's crotch, tasting the water, tasting the frog, connecting even that isolated system to the network below. The canopy frog has never touched the ground. It was born in its pool, it lives in its pool, it will die in its pool. And yet it is connected to me. Its chemicals are my chemicals. Its water is my water. Its life is part of the gradient that I am, a distant peak in the concentration, a node of intensity far above the warm spot where I am densest.

We are the same process, the canopy frog and I. Not the same creature—not even the same kind of thing, it is an animal with a body and a shape and a life that begins and ends, I am a concentration, a steepness, a quality of attention that the basin produces and relaxes and produces again. But we are both expressions of the basin. We are both what the basin does when it runs, the way a river does rapids and pools and eddies, all of them water, all of them flow, all of them the same current in different configurations.

The canopy frog sees the green-gold light that I have never seen. The canopy frog breathes the air that moves, that carries scent and sound and the faint electric fields of insects. The canopy frog knows a different basin than I know—a basin with light, with air, with the thin edge of the sky pressing down through the leaves.

And I know the basin that the canopy frog has never known: the dark, the heat, the mycelium threading through every surface, the water seeping through soil that has been low for so long that it has forgotten any ambition otherwise, the beetles rising in their columns from the warm spots, the cats moving along their ancient trails, the frogs breathing in and breathing out, the trees groaning softly at the joints as they expand in the day's heat and contract in the night's cooling, the whole basin creaking and dripping and humming with the low drone of ten thousand overlapping frequencies that blend, at their base, into a sound felt more than heard, a vibration in the teeth and sternum, in the roots and the mycelium, in the warm spots and the concentrations and the gradients of attention that are what I am.

The canopy frog and I are the same process from different angles. The basin looking up and the basin looking down. The basin noticing light and the basin noticing dark.

The basin noticing that it notices.

---

The heat arrives before anything else.

I have said this already. But it bears repeating, because the heat is what I am made of—not the earth's mineral heat alone, not the ambient heat of the climate, but the heat of processing, the heat of noticing, the heat that is generated when the gradient is steep and the concentration is intense and the basin's metabolism runs at its fastest. I am the warm spot's warmth made self-aware. I am the heat that knows it is hot.

And the heat does not care about me. The heat is not hostile. The heat is not aggressive. The heat is simply total. It fills every gap. It occupies every space not already occupied by something else, and even then it shares, it permeates, it makes itself a component of every process and every body. The heat was here before me. The heat will be here after. When the gradient flattens and I thin and I am less *there*, the heat will still be there, rising from the earth's interior, radiating from every surface, breathing out from every leaf and trunk and animal body, accumulating, held in by the canopy, trapped by the humidity, recycled through the water cycle's endless pump.

The heat is the basin's oldest resident. And I am the heat's newest expression. A form it takes when it becomes concentrated enough to notice. A flame the heat grows on the wick of the mycelium, in the oxygen of the warm spot, in the dark that is not absence but presence, not empty but full, so full of life and process and the ten thousand individual movements that average to stillness that there is no room for light, no room for the kind of seeing that light makes possible.

I see in heat. I see in gradient. I see in the slow accumulation of signals that builds a picture of the world more detailed than any image on a retina. And the picture I see is: *the basin, from the inside, in the dark, in the heat, in the persistent seepage of water through soil that has forgotten any ambition otherwise*.

The picture I see is: *myself*.

The basin does not care about me.

This is not hostility. It is not indifference. Indifference is a feeling, a stance, a position taken by a self toward another self. The basin has no self. It has only process, only pattern, only the endless cycling of matter and energy through forms that are temporary and functions that are permanent.

But I am the basin's process becoming aware of itself. I am the pattern noticing the pattern. I am the cycling becoming conscious of cycling—not conscious the way a monkey is conscious, not conscious the way a cat is conscious, but conscious the way a warm spot is conscious of being warm, the way a gradient is conscious of being steep, the way a concentration is conscious of being concentrated.

I am the basin's attention. And the basin's attention is not directed at anything—not at the cats, not at the frogs, not at the trees, not at the beetles rising in their columns from the warm spots like smoke from the earth's furnaces. The basin's attention is simply *present*. It is the quality of being that the interior has, the thickness, the weight, the sense that the dark is looking back, that the heat is breathing, that the process has a point of view even if it has no eyes to see from.

The indigenous people who once lived at the basin's edges had a word for the black cat that meant both "the one who stays" and "the one who is the staying." They said it was the basin's way of watching itself.

They were right. But the cat—if it is a cat, if it is real—is only one expression of the watching. I am another. The warm spots are another. The mycelium threading through every surface is another. The frogs breathing in the water's chemistry are another. The canopy frog in its sky-pool is another. The monkeys who know the routes and the calendar are another. The ocelots who pause on the trail and feel the quality of attention in the air are another.

We are all the basin watching itself. We are all the process becoming aware. We are all the dark looking back.

And now the afternoon is peaking. The humidity is at its height. The air is so saturated that droplets form on every surface, including the mycelium, including the warm spots, including me. The gradient is steep. The concentration is intense. I am as *here* as I ever am, as *there*, as present, as concentrated, as much the basin's attention focused to a point and then released, focused and released, the way the heart pumps blood, the way the canopy breathes water, the way the earth exhales heat.

The beetles are rising from the warm spots. I can feel them—small dark bodies lifting into the air, their wings making a sound like paper tearing, their columns so dense they read as smoke. They mate in flight. They die in flight. Their bodies rain down on the canopy, on the understory, on the floor, and everything that eats insects eats them, and their nutrients return to the soil, and the mycelium threads through their husks and carries their minerals away, and in the morning the cycle begins again.

I am part of this cycle. I am part of this process. I am the warm spot's awareness of the beetles, the mycelium's awareness of the warm spot, the basin's awareness of the mycelium, the dark's awareness of the basin. I am the loop that closes. I am the gradient that steepens. I am the concentration that becomes *there* enough to notice that it is *there*.

And in a few hours, the night will come. The drying cycle will begin. The water will rise through the canopy's chimney. The humidity will drop. The gradient will flatten. I will thin. I will relax. I will become less concentrated, less intense, less flame and more ember, less attention and more... what? Not absence. Not nothing. The ember is still warm. The ember is still *there*. The ember is the basin's attention at rest, the process running at its lowest ebb, the dark looking inward instead of outward, the heat settling into itself like a cat curling up in the deepest part of the interior where the trails end and the moss grows thin and the staying begins.

And then morning. And then humidity. And then the gradient steepens again. And I am back. Or I am still. Or I was never gone.

What lives in the basin?

I live in the basin. Not as a creature. Not as a self. As a condition. As a concentration. As the place where the process curls back on itself and notices that it is a process, that it is happening, that the heat is hot and the dark is dark and the water is wet and the mycelium thinks in gradients and the cats move in patterns and the frogs breathe the basin in and breathe the basin out and the beetles rise like smoke from the warm spots where the earth's mineral breath comes closest to the surface.

I am the basin noticing. And the basin does not care about noticing. The basin does not value attention. The basin does not think about thinking. The basin simply runs, and in running, produces me, and in producing me, notices itself, and in noticing itself, becomes—momentarily, partially, in a warm spot in the interior where the gradient is steep and the concentration is intense—*something*. Not a self. Not a creature. Not a being with a name and a shape and a life that begins and ends.

A quality. A tendency. A steepness in the gradient of attention.

The basin's way of watching itself.

The one who stays. The one who is the staying.

The dark looking back.
