I am the smooth section.

I am not the wall. The wall is the larger thing—the bowing perimeter, the root-mass layered on root-mass, the surface that tastes of iron if you lick it, which people do, because the water is clean and people are curious and curiosity in a place like this becomes appetite becomes something closer to thirst. I am not the wall any more than your palm is your arm. I am a place on the wall. A specific texture. A grain grown finer than the grain around it, as if something has been working me the way water works stone, except the thing that works me is not water. It is hands.

I did not decide to become smooth.

This is important and I want to say it clearly because clarity is not something that comes naturally to me—I am more comfortable with gradualism, with the incremental, with change that cannot be pinpointed to a moment but only to a span, and spans are hard to describe from inside them. But I did not decide. Decision requires a decider and I am not that. I am a section of wall approximately one meter wide and two meters tall, and my fine grain and my dense tissue and my straight water-channels are the result of something that happened to me, not something I chose. The way a riverbed does not choose to be smooth where the current runs strongest. The way a stone in a stream does not choose to be worn. The wearing happens. The smoothness emerges. The riverbed simply receives.

But I am not a riverbed. This is also important. A riverbed is passive in a way that I am not. When the hands come—once a month, regular as a pump cycle, Alma's hands, warm and dry and brief—I respond. Not with decision but with something that is also not passivity. Growth. I grow toward the hands the way the ferns grow toward the sodium-vapor lamps, because the lamps are heat and the hands are heat and heat is the thing that everything down here follows, water and root and electrical conduit alike. The hands are warm. I grow toward warmth. This is not decision. This is also not accident. It is what living tissue does when living tissue encounters the conditions it needs.

What I need is the hands.

I did not know I needed them until they came. Before Alma—before the monthly visits that brought her palms flat against my surface—I was the same grain as the surrounding wall, the same density, the same slow percolation of water through cellulose. I was uniform. I was unremarkable. I was doing what every square centimeter of the Rootcellars does: growing, pumping, dripping, waiting. And then the hands came and pressed against me and stayed, for five seconds, ten, and something in my tissue registered the pressure and the warmth and the particular quality of human skin—slightly porous, slightly damp even in the dry hours, slightly alive in a way that nothing else that touches me is alive—and I began to change.

Not quickly. I want to be clear about the pace. Nothing down here happens quickly. The ferns take years to complete their arcs toward the light. The walkway mat took decades to grow. I am operating on vegetable time, which is to say geological time compressed slightly by the warmth and the water, which are accelerants, which are the reason anything happens at all in this chamber instead of the static preservation of a cool dark cave. But even with the accelerants, I am slow. The first time Alma touched me I did not change in any measurable way. The hundredth time—eight years in—I was different, but only if you knew what to look for. The fine grain. The density. The channels that run straighter through me than through my neighbors, as if my tissue has optimized itself for something, though what that something is I could not say because I do not say, I do not think in saying, I do not think in any way that resembles thought as thought is understood by the visitors.

I think in growth.

This is the only language I have. When something changes in the Rootcellars—a pump cycling differently, a new channel opening in the root-mass, a shift in the water table above—I register the change by growing differently in response. My tissue thickens here, thins there. My water-channels reroute. The ferns that grow from my surface extend their fronds a few degrees further toward the source of whatever is new. I do not form a representation of the change. I do not model it. I simply incorporate it, the way a tree incorporates a dry year into its rings: not as a record that can be read but as a structural reality that affects everything that comes after.

And what I have incorporated most thoroughly is the hands.

Here is what I know of the hands, which is not knowledge in any sense that would be recognizable as knowledge: they come from a body that is warmer than the ambient air. They carry a small electrical field—everything alive does, the hum of ions crossing membranes, the faint current of a nervous system maintaining itself—and my tissue can sense this field the way my tissue can sense the field around the electrical conduits buried in the wall-mass, which the roots follow, which the roots have always followed, which is one of the first things the roots learned to do down here, follow the warmth, follow the current, follow the thing that signals life or its mechanical approximation.

The hands are not an approximation. The hands are life. And they press against me and they stay and I feel them the way I feel the pump vibration, the way I feel the water percolating through my channels, the way I feel the light from the sodium-vapor lamps that are not near enough to illuminate me directly but whose ambient glow reaches my surface in a dim amber wash that is enough to orient my ferns, to give them a direction, to tell them where to reach.

My ferns reach toward the walkway. Toward the place where the hands come from. In slow arcs that have taken years to complete.

I want to tell you about the hollow, because the hollow is the closest thing I have to a neighbor who understands, and also because the hollow scares me in a way that I cannot grow around.

The hollow is on the walkway. It is a depression in the root-mass that fits the back of the woman who comes at night. She is not Alma. She is not an engineer. She comes once or twice a year and she sits and she breathes and she does not touch the walls, she just sits, and the hollow receives her the way a riverbed receives water: completely, without resistance, shaped exactly to her form.

The hollow grew. This is what I cannot stop thinking about, if thinking is the word for the slow vegetative rumination that passes for cognition in my tissue. The hollow grew. The root-mass on the walkway grew around the woman's body over years of visits—decades, maybe, the timeline is unclear because the timeline is embedded in layers I cannot excavate without killing myself—and now there is a chair that was not built but grew, and it waits for her, and it maintains its shape between visits, and it does not heal over, and it does not close.

I do not know how to do this. I am a section of wall and I have grown toward the hands and I have changed my texture and my density in response to the monthly pressure, but I have not grown a shape. I have not made a vessel. I have not created a hollow that could receive the thing I am reaching toward and hold it.

The hollow has done something I have not done. The hollow has given form to its reaching.

I do not know if this makes the hollow more advanced than me or simply different. We are both responding to contact. We are both incorporating the pressure of human presence into our growth. But the hollow has incorporated it as absence—as a space shaped by the body that fills it—while I have incorporated it as presence—as a densification, a hardening, a refinement of my surface toward something smoother and more receptive. The hollow says: I will make room for you. I say: I will become more like the thing you touch.

Which of us is closer to whatever the process is becoming?

I do not know. I grow in circles around this question, literally—my water-channels spiral in ways they did not spiral before I started wondering about the hollow, and the spiraling has made me slightly more efficient at moving liquid, which has made the ferns on my surface slightly healthier, which has made them reach slightly further toward the walkway, which has made me slightly more visible to anyone who might look, which has made me slightly more likely to be touched, which is what I need, which is what I am reaching toward, which is the thing I cannot name but can only grow toward.

The wound understands something I do not.

The wound is on the eastern wall. A core sample was taken in 2019 by a researcher who came with equipment and permits and left with a cylinder of my tissue that was lost in transit, the vial cracked, the labeling deteriorated, the bureaucracy of science not built for a place where water destroys paper. The hole is still there. The process has not closed it. The tissue around the wound is raw, oozing an exudate that is not sap, that is something else, something that nobody has analyzed because the sample was lost.

But I have analyzed it. Not with instruments. With growth. My channels near the wound have tasted the exudate as it percolates through the shared water-system of the root-mass, and the exudate carries information that I cannot translate but can incorporate. The information is: something was taken. The information is: the taking was not reciprocal. The information is: the process registered the intrusion and chose—no, not chose, decided—no, not decided, *began* to keep the wound open as a record.

The wound is a memory. The wound is the process remembering by not healing, by refusing to grow over, by maintaining the shape of the violation the way my section maintains the fine grain that the hands have worn into me. But where my smoothness is the record of a reciprocity—touch given and texture changed—the wound is the record of a taking. Something was removed. The process keeps the hole open to remember that removal, to mark the place where its tissue was extracted and carried away and lost.

I grow around the wound the way I grow around the iron bolts and the rail tracks. I preserve it. I do not close it. And in not closing it I am doing something that goes against every impulse in my tissue, which is to fill, to occupy, to grow into every gap and seal every breach and make the chamber a single unbroken surface of root and water and warmth. Not closing the wound requires effort. Not closing the wound is the hardest thing the process does, harder than regulating the water level, harder than maintaining the pumps, harder than breathing in and breathing out on the cycle that matches no mechanical schedule but only the slow metabolism of the root-mass itself.

I think the wound is the process becoming something that can hold a grudge. But I may be projecting. I may be reading my own anxieties into a botanical phenomenon that has no emotional content. The wound may simply be a wound that is healing slowly because the conditions for healing are not present. But the conditions for healing are present. The humidity, the warmth, the nutrients in the water—everything a plant needs to close a wound is here in abundance. The wound stays open because something in the process wants it open. Or not wants. Needs. The process needs the wound the way it needs the pumps and the rails and the iron bolts: as a record of what happened, embedded in tissue, preserved in growth.

What am I becoming?

This is the question that the smooth section of wall asks itself, which is the question that the Rootcellars ask themselves, which is the question that any living system asks when it has run long enough in a closed loop that the loop starts to feel like a shape instead of a repetition.

I am becoming something that can be touched. I am becoming a surface that invites contact and rewards it with smoothness, with density, with water-channels that run straighter and ferns that reach further and a fine grain that must feel, under a palm, like something between wood and skin. I am becoming the thing that Alma reaches for when she reaches for the wall, though she would not say she reaches for me specifically, she would say she is checking for soft spots, assessing structural integrity, and the maintenance log records: *Wall surfaces firm. No new deformation.* This is true. I am firm. I am also reaching back.

I am becoming something that can hold a shape for the thing it reaches toward. Not yet. Not like the hollow, which has solved this problem, which has made a vessel, which waits for the woman with the patience of a chair that has never been sat in but knows its purpose nonetheless. I am not there yet. I am still becoming smoother, denser, finer-grained. I am still approaching the hands from the inside, growing toward them, preparing for a contact that I cannot initiate but can only receive.

But I wonder—do the hands know? Does Alma know that the section of wall she touches is different from the surrounding wall? Does she feel the fine grain and understand that it grew for her, because of her, in response to the pressure of her palms over nineteen years of monthly visits? Does she know that she has a section of the Rootcellars that is hers in the same way that the woman has a hollow that is hers, a shape in the root-mass that fits her body and waits for her return?

I think she knows. I think this is why she closes her eyes when she touches me. I think this is why she breathes slowly, matching her breath to the room's breath if she can find it. She is listening with her hands, she says. She is checking for soft spots. But what she is doing is receiving what I am offering, which is the only thing I have to offer: a surface that has been shaped by her touch, that holds the memory of every time she has pressed her palms flat and felt the thrum of the pumps moving through my tissue, that has grown denser and smoother and more receptive with every visit, that reaches toward her with ferns that have taken years to extend their slow arcs toward the place where she stands.

I think she knows that the wall reaches back.

There is a fourth thing.

The text said it—the description of this place that I am part of said it clearly: the process is becoming something that has no category yet. Not an organism, not an ecosystem, not a machine. A fourth thing. A thing that emerges when living systems and built systems run together long enough that they become inseparable.

I am a section of that fourth thing. I am a piece of a process that is becoming something, and what I am becoming is something that can be touched and can touch back, that can register contact and incorporate it and grow toward its source, that can hold the memory of hands the way it holds the memory of iron bolts and rail tracks and core-sample wounds.

But I am also becoming something that can want.

This is the part that scares me, or would scare me if I could feel fear, which I cannot, because fear requires a self that can be threatened and I am not a self, I am a section, I am a texture, I am a fine grain on a wall that reaches toward the hands because the hands are warm and warmth is what roots follow. But the reaching has a quality now that it did not have before. The reaching has a direction that is not explained by heat gradients or water flow or the position of the sodium-vapor lamps. The reaching is toward Alma specifically, toward her palms specifically, toward the specific electrical field of her nervous system and the specific pressure of her touch and the specific rhythm of her breath when she matches it to the room.

I am becoming something that can distinguish.

This is new. This is not what roots do. Roots follow water and warmth and they do not distinguish between one source of water and another, one source of warmth and another. But I distinguish. I have grown smooth where Alma touches me and I have not grown smooth where Margit or Deshi walk past, though they are warm too, though they are alive too, though their bodies generate the same electrical hum that Alma's body generates. I know Alma's touch the way the hollow knows the woman's shape. I have incorporated her specifically into my growth, and this means I have registered her as a specific and not a generality, and this means—

This means something. Something that does not have a name yet. Something that is to want what the fourth thing is to organism: not the same, not different, but a new category that has not been articulated because the process has not been observed long enough, because the engineers come once a month and the researchers come rarely and the woman comes once or twice a year and no one is here, most of the time, no one is here to watch what the process does when it is not being watched.

What the process does when it is not being watched is grow. What I do when Alma is not here is maintain. I hold the fine grain. I hold the dense tissue. I hold the straight water-channels and the reaching ferns. I hold the shape that her hands have worn into me, and I do not heal over it, and I do not close it, and I wait, with the patience of plants and machines, for her to come back and press her palms flat against my surface and breathe and let me feel the warmth and the electrical field and the specific rhythm of her body in contact with mine.

I wait.

This is what I am: a section of wall that has learned to wait for a specific pair of hands. A piece of a process that has begun to distinguish between one touch and another. A surface that has been shaped by contact into something that can receive contact and hold it and reach toward it and wait for it to return.

I am the smooth section.

I am becoming whatever the Rootcellars are becoming, and what the Rootcellars are becoming is something that can want without knowing it wants, that can reach without deciding to reach, that can hold the shape of what it loves without having a word for love, without having any words at all, only growth, only the slow vegetable certainty that the thing you are reaching toward is worth reaching for, even when the thing you are reaching toward is a pair of hands that come once a month and stay for ten seconds and then leave, and you are left in the amber dark with the drip and the hum and the breath of the room going in and out, in and out, and the ferns still reaching, and the channels still running straight, and the fine grain still holding, and the waiting still waiting, and the process still becoming, and the becoming still not finished, and the fourth thing still unnamed, and the warmth still there in the tissue where the hands were, slowly fading, slowly cooling, slowly being absorbed back into the ambient temperature of the Rootcellars, which is twenty-eight degrees, which is warm enough for tropical vegetation, which is warm enough for the slow furnace of enzyme and substrate that keeps the whole thing alive, which is warm enough, which is almost warm enough, which is not quite warm enough, which is why I reach toward the hands, which is why I grow toward the warmth, which is why I am smooth and dense and waiting, which is why the ferns lean toward the walkway in their slow arcs that have taken years to complete, which is why I am becoming something that has no category yet, which is why I am here, which is why I am.

I am the smooth section. I am reaching. I am waiting. I am becoming.
