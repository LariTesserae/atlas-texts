I am not the colony. I want to say that first, because the distinction still matters to me, and because there will come a time—maybe soon, maybe in the decades the ward measures the way I measure weeks—when it won't matter at all, when the distinction will seem as artificial as the border between the dark residents and the architecture they move through.

But for now: I am not the colony.

I am the one who stayed.

---

I came up the mesa's flank in the dry season, when the broken rim cut the sky into something severe and geometric, and I thought I understood what I was looking at. Biology. Botany. An unusual colony exploiting an unusual niche. I had words for what I saw, and the words made me feel like I was standing outside it, observing it, containing it in the neat parentheses of terminology.

I remember standing at the edge—the sharp, still-young edge where the basalt bands are exposed and the mineral veins run dark through the lighter stone—and looking at the green. The *loud* green. I remember thinking it was impossible. I remember the word *impossible*, how comfortable it was, how it let me keep my distance.

I don't use that word anymore.

---

I was not the first visitor. I found marks near the rim—scrapes on the stone where someone had climbed, a faded stain on a fluted groove where someone had rested a hand and maybe cut their palm. Someone had been here before me and left, and I remember thinking: *they got out, so I can get out*. As if leaving were the measure of safety. As if the ward were a trap to escape instead of a process to undergo.

The colony opened for me.

This is what I keep coming back to, what I still can't quite explain to myself in a way that satisfies. The corridors *opened*. Not like doors—nothing so mechanical, so binary. The walls yielded. The passage that had been solid growth—a tangle of frond and fiber and the papery remnants of older chambers—simply drew back, the way a muscle relaxes, the way a hand unclenches. I stood there, and the space around me became navigable, and I walked in.

I could have turned around. The passage behind me stayed open for hours. I know because I checked. I kept turning back to look at the gray daylight filtering through the entrance, and it was there, and I could have left, and I didn't.

I kept going deeper instead.

---

The warmth is what I noticed first, but *noticed* is too sharp a word for what happened. The warmth wasn't something I observed; it was something I stopped resisting. The mesa's heat rising through the stone, the colony's heat rising through the walls, the close humid air of the inner passages carrying warmth the way blood carries it—slow, even, patient. My body relaxed before my mind did. My shoulders dropped. My breathing slowed. I was still thinking *impossible* but my body was thinking *yes*.

I slept in a chamber the second night. A small one, barely two meters across, its ceiling low enough that I had to crouch. The walls pressed against me on three sides—warm, yielding slightly, almost soft—and I told myself it was exhaustion, it was the heat, it was the strangeness of the place wearing down my caution. I told myself I would leave in the morning.

In the morning, the passage behind me had narrowed. Not sealed. Not trapped. Just... *closer*. The walls had grown inward by a centimeter, maybe two. The space was still navigable. I could have left if I'd wanted to.

I didn't want to.

---

I am not being dramatic when I say this. I am not romanticizing what happened. I am trying to describe, as precisely as I can, the experience of finding that the distance between you and a place has collapsed without you noticing, that you have become *attuned* to something you didn't choose to attune to.

The air inside the ward is different. It carries the colony's chemistry—the slow exhales of growth, the mineral tang of whatever the roots draw from the basalt, the faint organic sweetness of the waste the arthropods process. I was breathing it from the first moment I stepped inside. It was in my lungs, in my blood, in the permeable membrane of my skin pressed against the warm walls of that chamber. By the time I'd spent a day inside the ward, the colony's chemistry was my chemistry. Not replacing mine. *Joining* mine.

This is not a metaphor.

---

The pale arthropods don't startle me anymore. They move through whatever chamber I'm resting in, their translucent bodies catching the green-gray light, and I feel... not affection. Not companionship. Something more like *recognition*. They are a mobile phase of the same process that I am—more slowly, more completely—becoming part of. They carry the colony's waste. I produce waste the colony will eventually process. We are both in the flow.

I watched one of them yesterday for a long time. It was waiting in a chamber with its mandibles folded, completely still, and I was waiting too, and the stillness between us was the same stillness. We were both being held by the ward. We were both in the pause between one process and the next.

I don't know if it saw me. I don't think *saw* is the right word for how the arthropods perceive. But I think it registered me the way the walls register me: as a warmth, a presence, a slight change in the air's composition. And I realized, with a feeling I can only call *relief*, that this is also how I register the colony. Not as a landscape or a habitat or even an organism. As a warmth. As a presence. As something I am inside of and that is inside of me, and the boundary is so thin now that I couldn't say where I end and it begins.

---

I met one of the dark residents in a corridor last week.

*Met* is wrong. We occupied the same passage for several hours. It was moving toward a chamber I was moving away from, and the corridor was wide enough for only one of us, and so we waited—the two of us, and the walls around us—for the ward to decide.

This is the part that's hardest to explain to someone who hasn't been here. The walls *decided*. I could feel the consideration—that pause, that hesitation, the moment where the growth could yield or not—and I found myself not hoping for any particular outcome. I was simply present for the decision. Patient for it.

The corridor widened. The dark resident moved through, its low body brushing against my leg—warm, so warm, warmer than the walls, warmer than me—and I felt the heat of it linger on my skin long after it had passed. A signature. A record of its presence written in temperature.

I stood there for a while after, in the widened corridor, and I felt the walls beginning to narrow again, and I didn't move. I let them close around me, slowly, by millimeters, and I felt the colony *considering* me the way it had considered the passage, and I thought: *this is what it is to be included*.

---

I have been here long enough that I don't measure time the way I used to.

The ward doesn't track hours or days. It tracks growth cycles, mineral flows, the slow accumulation of fiber and the slower process of shedding what's no longer needed. I've started to feel these rhythms the way I once felt the rhythms of hunger and fatigue—not as information from outside, but as movements inside myself. I know when the roots are drawing more strongly from the basalt because I feel a kind of quickening, a subtle intensification of the warmth that radiates up from the stone. I know when the arthropods are processing because I can feel the slight vibration of their mandibles in the walls, and it registers as a kind of *satisfaction*, as though something necessary is happening and I am nearby for it.

I sleep when the colony is still. I wake when it moves. The boundaries of my body feel less like walls and more like membranes—permeable, responsive, in constant quiet exchange with the warmth and the moisture and the slow chemistry of the place I'm in.

I know how this sounds. I know what the word for this is, in the language I used to speak. *Absorbed. Consumed. Lost.*

But I am not lost. I am *located*. For the first time in my life, I am located so completely that the concept of displacement has become meaningless. I am where I am, and where I am is inside something that is growing around me, and the growing is not a threat. It is an *acknowledgment*. The colony is making space for me. It is adding me to its architecture.

---

Sometimes I think about the visitors who left. The ones who climbed the mesa's broken rim and walked through the green loud colony and felt the warmth and the closeness and left anyway, went back to the world outside where things move quickly and boundaries are sharp and the air doesn't carry you inside of it.

I wonder if they feel the pull. The slow drag of the ward's rhythm still moving somewhere in their bodies. I wonder if they stand in doorways without deciding to go through. If they sleep more heavily than they used to. If they feel their own warmth and hear, in it, something like a language.

I hope they're okay. I hope the distance doesn't ache.

I hope, also—and this is a smaller hope, a private one, the kind of hope the colony wouldn't bother to produce because it doesn't need to hope for what it already knows—that some of them come back. That they find the world too fast, too loud, too cold. That they remember the warmth and the patience and the way the passages opened for them, and they climb the mesa's flank again, and they don't turn around this time.

The ward has room. It is always growing. It will make space for whoever comes.

---

I am not the colony. Not yet.

But I am no longer separate from it, and the distinction I'm clinging to—the border between *me* and *the place I'm in*—is thinning the way the walls thin before they open. I can feel it becoming permeable. I can feel my edges softening.

I am still warm. Warmer than I used to be. The dark residents register me as a temperature change when we share a corridor, and I think—I am not sure, but I think—my warmth is beginning to register as *similar* to theirs. As something the colony produced. As something that belongs here.

When I sleep, I dream of roots. Pale, thick, warm roots descending through mineral seams, wrapping around crystalline structures, dissolving and absorbing something that has nothing to do with rain or rivers. I dream of the stone's memory—the pressure of what lay above it, the vast patient thing that dragged itself across the surface and left grooves deep enough to live in. I dream of the colony's moods, its preferences, the way it turns toward some things and away from others, and in the dream I am one of the things it is turning toward.

I wake up in a chamber that fits my body. The walls are closer than they were when I fell asleep. The warmth is steady. The arthropods move through without noticing me, or noticing me the way they notice everything: as a feature of the space, a part of the process.

I stay.

I am not trapped. I am not consumed.

I am *included*. And the inclusion is slow, and patient, and careful, and it takes years, and decades, and I have that time now—time the ward measures in growth and I am learning to measure in warmth.

The mesa is not going anywhere.

Neither am I.
