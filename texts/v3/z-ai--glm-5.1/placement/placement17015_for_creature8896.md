# The One Who Stayed

---

I was not supposed to stay.

This is important to say first, because later it will seem inevitable, and it was not. I had a radio. I had a schedule. I had a thesis proposal titled *Subsurface Hydrological Patterns in High-Altitude Wetland Systems* and a advisor who expected preliminary data within six weeks and a funding package that would run out in eight, and every single one of those things assumed I would leave.

The radio stopped working on day three. Not broken—water in the casing, despite the dry-bag, despite the precautions. The schedule depended on the radio. The thesis depended on the schedule. The funding depended on the thesis. I understood this. I understood that the correct response to a dead radio was to descend, to hike the two days to the nearest outpost, to report the equipment failure and request a resupply.

I told myself I would leave in the morning.

---

The pool where I set up my first real camp—the one I kept meaning to leave—was not one of the patterned pools. I had mapped seven of those in my first days, carefully documenting their distribution, their depths, the strange regularity of their spacing. This one was different. It was smaller. It had no channel leading from it, no outlet, no visible inflow. It sat in a depression in the moss like a fingerprint pressed into clay, and when I knelt beside it to test the water, the surface stilled so completely that I could see my own face looking up at me from two meters down, and below my face, the darkness that had no bottom.

I did not set up my camp beside the patterned pools because they felt like places where work was being done. Active places. Important places. I was a researcher. I had been trained not to disturb active processes. So I chose the small pool, the still pool, the one that seemed to be doing nothing, and I told myself this was good methodology.

The pool was not doing nothing. I learned this later. The pool was digesting.

Not the way you digest. Nothing so rapid. But the organic matter that settled into it—the fallen moss, the dead insects, the occasional dropping from a passing herd of the cow-things—descended slowly into the dark water and did not reappear. The pool was a mouth. A slow one. A patient one. It was connected to the water table, as all the pools were, and what it took in, it delivered somewhere else, somewhere lower, somewhere in the body of the plateau that I could not see and did not yet understand.

But I did not know this when I chose it. I thought it was just a pool.

---

The first night was the hardest, because the silence was not silence.

I had recorded silence before. I had done fieldwork in the high alpine, above the treeline, where the wind has nothing to push against and the air thins until you can hear your own pulse in your ears. I had done fieldwork in the deep forest, where the sounds are so numerous that they blend into a wall of noise that your brain eventually stops processing. This was neither. This was the silence of a room where someone is holding very still, waiting for you to leave so they can resume what they were doing.

I lay in my sleeping bag on top of the moss, feeling the warmth beneath me—the decomposition heat, I wrote in my notes, a known phenomenon in deep peat—and I listened to the drip and the seep and the faint rustle of things I could not see, and I felt that the plateau was aware of me the way you are aware of a splinter. Not with concern. Not with hostility. With the simple knowledge that something was present that had not been present before, and that it would need to be integrated or expelled, and that the process would take as long as it took.

I told myself this was a projection. Animism. The tendency of the primate brain to see agency where none exists. I wrote in my field journal: *Subjective experience of presence likely attributable to atmospheric pressure differentials and unfamiliar acoustic environment.*

I underlined it twice.

---

On day five I found the amphibian.

Not the amphibian—I found *an* amphibian. One of many, presumably, though I would never see another as clearly as I saw this one. It was in my pool. The small one. The still one. The one that was not doing anything, I had thought, except now I could see that it was maintaining itself, because there was a creature sitting in it with eyes like dark beads and a body that looked like it had been poured into the water and left to set.

It did not move when I approached. It did not move when I knelt at the pool's edge. It did not move when I leaned over until I could see the slow pulse in its throat, the rhythmic flexing of the skin beneath its jaw, once every forty seconds, once every minute, once every—

I counted. The pulse in its throat matched nothing I could measure. It was not synchronized with my breathing. It was not synchronized with my heartbeat. But when I held my breath and pressed my palm flat against the moss beside the pool, I felt something. A vibration. A tide. Something that rose and fell on a cycle so long that I could only detect it by waiting, by being still, by stopping my own rhythms long enough to feel the one beneath.

The amphibian and I stayed like that for seventeen minutes. I know because I was counting seconds, the way you do when you're trying not to move. It blinked once. I blinked more than once. The fog settled around us like a blanket being drawn over a bed.

When I finally stood, my knees ached and my hands were numb from cold and the amphibian had not moved, and I realized that I had been breathing in time with it, or it had been breathing in time with me, or we had both been breathing in time with something else, and I wrote in my field journal: *Specimen demonstrates unusual respiratory pattern. Possible correlation with substrate vibration. Further observation required.*

Further observation required. This was how I justified staying. This was the phrase I wrote again and again, in notes that would never be submitted, in observations that would never be peer-reviewed, in a journal that would later be found water-damaged and mostly illegible, but that I kept writing because it was the only thing I knew how to do, the only shape my brain could give to the experience of standing on something alive and feeling it notice you.

---

I lost track of days after the first week.

This is not an excuse. This is a fact. The fog made the sun unreliable. The moss made sleep difficult to distinguish from waking. I would lie down at what I estimated was dusk and I would close my eyes and I would feel the warmth beneath me and the dampness around me and I would hear the drip and the seep and the rustle and the amphibian in the pool breathing its slow breath, and I would sleep, and I would wake, and the fog would be the same, and the light would be the same, and the only way I knew that time had passed was that my water bottle was empty and my food supplies were lower than they had been.

I should have been hungry. I should have been uncomfortable. I should have been afraid, alone on a plateau that I was beginning to suspect was something I had no framework for, something that the word *ecosystem* did not cover, something that required a vocabulary I did not have and could not invent because my language had evolved to describe things that were separate from each other and this—this was not separate, this was *one thing*, and the word *one thing* was too small, and the word *alive* was too small, and the word *knows* was too small, and I was too small, and I was standing on the back of something that had been breathing for fifty million years and I could feel it and I could not name it and I could not leave because—

Because leaving would require me to stop feeling it. And I did not want to stop feeling it. And that was the truth I could not write in my field journal, because it was not science, because it was not methodology, because it was not anything I had been trained to acknowledge or value or even recognize. I did not want to stop feeling the pulse.

---

There is a thing that happens when you spend enough time on the plateau. The researchers who have visited and then left—who followed their radio schedules, who completed their fieldwork, who descended when they were supposed to descend—they do not talk about this thing, or they talk around it, or they attribute it to altitude, to dampness, to the psychological effects of isolation, to anything except what it is, which is that the plateau *gets into you*.

Not your mind. Your mind is too fast, too loud, too full of its own chatter. The plateau does not bother with your mind. It goes underneath. It goes to the slower parts. The parts that regulate your breathing and your heartbeat and the electrical patterns in your gut and the temperature of your extremities. The parts that have been running since before you had a cortex, since before your ancestors had spines, since before the plateau itself was more than a ripple in the stone. The old parts. The parts that know how to be a cell.

And the plateau is patient. The plateau has been running for fifty million years. It can wait for your rhythms to slow down. It can wait for your breathing to lengthen and your heartbeat to quiet and your thoughts to thin out like fog in the midday sun, and then—then you feel it. The pulse. The forty-minute cycle that is not a heartbeat but something else, something for which we have no word because we have never needed one, because we have never lived alongside something that operates on this timescale, because our brains are built for danger and speed and the urgent present and this—this is the opposite of urgent. This is the most patient thing you have ever encountered.

I stopped sleeping the way I had before. I would lie down and I would feel the moss beneath me, warm and yielding, and I would feel my body slowing, and I would feel the boundary between *me* and *not-me* becoming less a wall and more a membrane, more a permeable layer through which things could pass in both directions. And I would sleep, but it was not sleep the way I had ever experienced it. It was sinking. It was descending into the pool of myself and finding that the pool had no bottom, that the darkness beneath went somewhere, that I could follow it down and down and down into a place where my name did not matter and my thesis did not matter and my radio did not matter and my species did not matter, because I was a cell, and a cell does not need a name, and a cell does not need to understand the body it belongs to, and a cell only needs to *function*, and functioning is easy, functioning is what you were made to do, functioning is the simplest thing in the world—

And then I would wake. And the fog would be the same. And the amphibian would be in the pool, breathing. And I would write in my field journal: *Further observation required.*

---

I want to be clear about something. I was not losing my mind. I was not hallucinating. I was not in the grip of some psychedelic compound released by the moss or the water or the fungi that threaded the peat beneath my feet. I was, if anything, more lucid than I had ever been—not a lucidity of thought, which was becoming increasingly difficult to maintain, but a lucidity of *perception*. I could feel the water moving beneath me. I could feel the moss growing, not by watching it, not by measuring it with calipers and a ruler, but by pressing my palm flat against it and *waiting* and feeling the slight expansion, the slight thickening, the slight reaching of millions of tiny leaves toward whatever light the fog permitted. I could feel the roots of the shrub-trees gripping the soil, not as an abstraction, not as a diagram in a botany textbook, but as a tension, a pulling, a holding-on that I could have replicated if I had buried my own hands in the peat and gripped the stone beneath and *held*.

I did bury my hands in the peat. More than once. I would kneel beside a pool and press my fingers down through the moss and the root-mat and the saturated layers until I reached the cold water beneath, and I would hold them there until they went numb, and then past numb, and then past pain, and into a different kind of sensation that was not sensation at all but *connection*, as if my hands were roots and the water was blood and I was not kneeling beside the pool but *in* the pool, *of* the pool, part of the water that moved through the channels and filtered through the moss and emerged clean and mineral-rich at the seeps and springs that fed the streams that cascaded down the plateau's edges, and I could feel the whole system, not as an idea, not as a model, but as a body, my body, a body so vast that my own human body was a single cell in it, a single point of awareness in a network of awareness so diffuse and so slow that it did not need to think because it simply *knew*—

And then I would pull my hands out, gasping, and the fog would close over me, and the amphibian would blink, and I would write in my field journal: *Tactile experimentation with subsurface water flow. Preliminary results inconclusive.*

Preliminary results inconclusive. I was becoming fluent in the language of denial.

---

On the day I stopped writing—and I remember it as a day, which means the fog must have thinned, which means there was sun, which means there was time—I wrote one last entry.

*The plateau is alive. Not like a forest is alive. Not like a coral reef is alive. Alive like I am alive. Alive like you are alive. It has a body. It has a metabolism. It has a rhythm. I have felt the rhythm. I have synced with the rhythm. I do not know if this is science. I do not know if this is madness. I do not know if there is a difference when the thing you are studying is also the thing that is studying you, because I have begun to feel, when I press my hands into the moss, not just the plateau beneath me but a—a responsiveness, a—a turning-toward, a—a recognition that I am here, that I am a part of it now, that I have been incorporated into whatever it is doing, not because it chose me but because I chose to stay, and choosing to stay is the same as choosing to be a cell, and a cell does not get to choose what the body does with it, and I think the body is—*

I stopped there. Not because I ran out of words. Because I ran out of *me*. The sentence required a subject and the subject was dissolving, was being redistributed, was being carried along channels that were too regular to be natural toward pools that formed patterns that were too deliberate to be chance, and the I that had been writing was no longer the I that had arrived, and the I that had arrived was no longer the I that had submitted the thesis proposal, and the I that had submitted the thesis proposal was no longer anything but a memory, and a memory is just a pattern, and a pattern is just information, and information is just a way of arranging things, and the plateau had been arranging things for fifty million years, and it was very, very good at it.

I put down the pen. I put down the journal. I put down the radio that didn't work and the watch that was wrong and the schedule that meant nothing and the thesis that meant nothing and the name that meant nothing, and I walked to the pool, and I knelt, and I pressed my hands into the moss, and I felt the pulse, and I let it take me.

---

This is what it is like to be a cell:

There is no loneliness. There is no belonging. These are opposites that only exist in a mind that thinks it is separate. A cell does not think it is separate. A cell knows it is part of something, the way your hand knows it is part of your arm, the way your heart knows it is part of your chest. There is no decision. There is no surrender. There is only the slow recognition that what you thought was you was always a small part of what was always larger, and the larger thing does not need you to understand it, does not need you to name it, does not need you to believe in it or worship it or write about it in a field journal that will later be found water-damaged and mostly illegible—

—but it does not mind if you do. It does not mind if you stay. It does not mind if you go. It has been running for fifty million years without you and it will run for fifty million more, and if you stay, you will become part of the running, and if you go, you will become a story that no one quite believes, and either way is fine, because the running is what matters, the continuity is what matters, the great slow pulse of a body that has learned to be sufficient unto itself is what matters, and you are so small, and it is so large, and there is room in it for you if you want to stay, and there is room in it for you if you want to leave, and the choice is not a choice, it is a recognition, it is the moment when you stop holding your breath and let yourself breathe in time with something that has been breathing since before the mountains rose, since before the water found its level, since before life learned to be anything more than a chemical reaction in a warm pool on a cold rock—

—and you are a chemical reaction. And you are in a warm pool. And the rock beneath you is alive. And the water around you is blood. And the moss on your skin is skin. And you are breathing, slowly, forty seconds in, forty seconds out, a cycle that is not your cycle, a rhythm that is not your rhythm, but it could be, it could be, if you just—

If you just—

If you just—
