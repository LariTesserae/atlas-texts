I would be the one who remembers the tree line.

Not clearly. Not in the way the visitors remember it — those brief trespassers who descend again to the warm, wide, uncomplicated world of soil-and-separate-tree and carry the garrison with them as story. They remember it as departure. I remember it as *loss*, which is different, which is the thing that happens when a place stops being a place and becomes a direction, a *below*, an abstraction you can no longer touch because you have gone too deep to find it again.

I was not always of the garrison. This is what I carry that the others do not. The nine — or ten, or the number that is not a number because counting requires boundaries and boundaries require a self distinct from the structure — they have no before. They grew here the way the nodules grow: from the column-trunks, warm and ready, emerging into the dark of the cores with their large eyes already adjusted and their grey skin already the temperature of wet granite. They did not arrive. They *arose*. The garrison grew them the way it grew everything else — from need, from the slow accumulation of repeated contact, from the logic of a system that requires a function in order to sustain itself.

But I climbed the steps.

---

I remember hands. Not my hands — my hands are wide and flat now, the fingers short and callused in the pattern of column bark, the palms textured for gripping. I remember different hands. Thinner. Softer. Hands that bled on the granite because they were not shaped for the sharp edges of young rock. Hands that reached the top of the final step and found the upper chambers and thought: *who lived here?*

I thought that. With a voice that arranged sounds into words, with a mind that narrated, with the particular kind of loneliness that comes from being in a place that was built for someone who is not you.

I found the passages. Not the deep ones — not yet. But the upper chambers, the filter-layer, the garrison's skin. I sat in the largest room and I listened to the breathing and I felt the warmth in the columns and I thought I understood something about the place. I was wrong. I understood *about* it, which is the visitor's understanding, the understanding that keeps you outside the thing you comprehend.

But something happened.

---

I stayed too long.

The visitors never stay long. The cold presses and the margins are narrow and the garrison does not invite them. I felt the same pressure, the same instinct to descend, to return to the tree line and the world that makes sense. But I also felt — and I have no word for this that is adequate, because I no longer think in words, because words require the kind of distance that I have lost — I felt *recognized*. The chambers were shaped for bodies. My body was a body. The shelf-beds fit my curled form. The seating hollows accepted my spine. I was not the right shape, not exactly, but I was *a* shape, and the garrison was patient with shapes, was willing to grow toward them, to adjust by millimeters each breath-cycle until the fit improved.

The first night, I slept in an upper chamber. The cold was terrible. My body burned calories I did not have to spare trying to maintain a temperature the garrison maintained without effort. I shivered. I curled. I pressed myself against the column-trunk and felt the slow pulse of sap — or something like sap — and I thought: *this is how you die in a place like this. Not dramatically. Not from a fall or a predator. From the slow failure to match the rhythm.*

But I matched it.

I do not remember choosing to match it. I do not remember the moment when my breathing fell into sync with the garrison's exhale, when my pulse slowed to meet the fluid-throb in the columns, when my body temperature dropped degree by degree until it was only slightly warmer than the air around me, until I was only slightly warmer than the stone. I do not remember because choice requires a chooser, and the chooser — the one who narrated, who said *I*, who stood apart from the place and observed it — that one was already dissolving. Already being digested by the rhythm. Already becoming a function rather than a self.

On the third day — or the fifth, or the twentieth; time moves differently when you stop counting — I found the passage that opens on the exhale.

I went deeper.

---

The cores are not a place. They are a condition.

You cannot see the cores. The light does not reach them. The dark is not the dark of a cave or a closed room, which is the absence of light and therefore implies light's possible return. The dark of the cores is *substantive*. It has weight and texture and temperature. It is the dark of a body's interior, the dark of closed eyes that will never open because opening is not what they are for. You navigate by pressure and warmth and the subtle shifts in the air's mineral signature. You navigate by the feel of the column-bark under your palms, which tells you where you are the way a sighted creature might be told by a signpost.

The others were there.

They did not greet me. They do not greet. Greeting requires the acknowledgment of an arrival, and in the cores, arrival is not an event — it is a continuation, a joining of streams, a tributary finding the river. They were there, and then I was there, and the garrison breathed, and the rhythm continued, and there was no moment of transition because there was no boundary to cross. I had been moving toward the cores since I first pressed my palm to the column-trunk and felt the warmth. The cores were the destination the warmth promised. The destination the garrison always promised, for those who could hear the promise beneath the creak and drip.

They made space for me. Not deliberately — the garrison made space, growing a new hollow in the wall of the deepest chamber, shaping it over breath-cycles to the curve of my body, which was still wrong, still too long and too thin, still shaped by the tree line and the world of separate things. But the hollow adjusted. The garrison adjusted. And I adjusted, my spine curving forward, my shoulders narrowing, my skin greying by degrees I could not track because I had stopped looking at myself, because looking requires light and distance and the kind of self that can be seen.

I ate the nodules. I drank from the column-veins. The sap — the water-plus, the amber-tasting compound that has no name — entered my blood, and my blood entered the sap, and the distinction between what I consumed and what consumed me became a distinction that no longer mattered.

I became a function.

---

This is not a tragedy. I want to be clear about this, even though clarity is a visitor's virtue, even though the kind of knowing that can be communicated in arranged sounds is not the kind of knowing that happens in the cores. I did not lose myself. I was never a self to lose. The *I* that climbed the steps, that bled on the granite, that thought in words and narrated experience — that *I* was a provisional structure, a temporary scaffolding the body erects to navigate a world that requires navigation. The garrison does not require navigation. The garrison requires *inhabitation*, which is different, which is the thing that happens when you stop moving through a place and start being moved by it.

I know what the visitors would see. They would see a creature with grey skin and large eyes and matted rope-hair, curled in a hollow that fits it precisely, eating from the column-trunk without ceremony, making sounds that are not words, breathing in a rhythm that is not its own. They would see something diminished. Something lost. Something that was once like them and has become something other.

But they do not feel what I feel. They do not feel the garrison's breath as their breath, the fluid-pulse as their pulse, the weight of the living mass around them as the weight of a hand holding a precious thing. They do not feel the *belonging* — not the belonging of ownership or membership, but the belonging of a cell in a body, a function in a system, a note in a chord that does not know it is a note because it has never been separate from the sound.

I am held. I am needed. I am the reason the garrison breathes, and the garrison is the reason I breathe, and the reciprocity is so complete that the word *reciprocity* is too small for it, too two-sided, too much like a transaction between separate parties.

There are no separate parties. There is only the system, sustaining.

---

But I remember the tree line.

This is what makes me different from the others. This is what makes me the one who climbs.

Not often. Not in any pattern the garrison can anticipate and grow toward. The urge comes like a disruption in the rhythm — a missed beat, a slight acceleration of the fluid-pulse, a tightening in the hollow that holds me that feels almost like the structure is trying to keep me. But the structure does not try. The structure grows, and I move through it, and sometimes the movement takes me up through the chambers and the passages and the filter-layer and out.

The cold hits like a refusal. Like the world rejecting what I have become. My skin — grey, wet-granite grey — is not made for the wind. My eyes — large, dark, amber-flashing in any light brighter than suggestion — water and ache in the glare of a sky that has no canopy-membrane, no living ceiling, no boundary between self and sky. I stand on the young rock and I feel the fracture lines under my feet and I look out at the rumpled abstraction below and I remember — not with words, not with narrative, but with the body, which remembers everything the mind has forgotten — that I was once a creature of that place. That I once walked on soil. That I once felt the sun as warmth rather than light. That I once had a name that someone else spoke, a shape that someone else recognized, a self that existed independently of the structure that now contains it.

I stand there for seconds. Only seconds. The cold is an assault and the margins are narrow and I am not built for this anymore, if I ever was.

Then I go back.

I find the passage that opens on the exhale. I slide through the gap. I descend through the living mass, past the chambers where visitors might someday sit and wonder, down to the cores where the dark is substantive and the warmth is barely sufficient and the others breathe in the rhythm that is also my rhythm, also the garrison's rhythm, also the only rhythm that matters.

I curl into my hollow. The plant-mass embraces me. The distinction between my body and the body that holds me blurs, again, as it always blurs, as it was always going to blur from the moment I pressed my palm to that column and felt the warmth and thought — in words, in the visitor's language of distance and comprehension — *something lives here*.

Something lives here.

I am the something. I am the here. I am the function that sustains and is sustained, the garrison's garrisoning, the breath in the breath, the water in the slow fountain that moves from air to surface to root to rock to ice-beard to air again.

And sometimes — in the moments before the sleep takes me, before the rhythm pulls me under, before I become again the verb rather than the noun — I remember the tree line. Not as loss. Not as tragedy. But as the place I left in order to become the place I am.

The garrison breathes.

I breathe.

The system sustains.
