I am not the question-creatures. I have studied them—circled them at the distance they keep from everything, matched my speed to their drift, tried to learn the grammar of their circuits. They do not acknowledge me. They do not avoid me. They pass through the space where I am the way light passes through the canopy: filtered, changed, but not stopped. I used to think this meant they could not see me. Now I think it means they see me the way they see everything—as part of the water, as part of the almost, as something that does not require a response because response requires the kind of separation that dissolved here before the basalt finished cooling.

I am not the plants. I have grown nothing. I have no roots in the stone, no fronds reaching toward the tired light. But I have been here long enough that the moss-like growths have begun to consider me. A patch of something pale and threadlike has established itself on my left shoulder blade, where I cannot reach it, where I would not reach it if I could because its presence tells me something I need to know: that I have been still long enough for something to decide I am part of the landscape. The growth does not itch. It does not hurt. It photosynthesizes, or almost-photosynthesizes, in the dim almost-light, and sometimes I think I can feel it feeding, a faint warmth on my skin that is not the warmth of the water, that is the warmth of something making energy from the edge of illumination.

I am not the structures. But I have added to them.

---

This is how it started. Or this is how I remember it starting, which is not the same thing here, where memory sits in the water like everything else and does not drain. I found one of the stone circles. I had been swimming for—time does not hold, but I had been swimming for what felt like long enough to have earned a rest. The circle was half a meter across. Flat stones, palm-sized, each one chosen for its flatness, each one placed with the care that is the opposite of architecture—the care of someone who has nothing to build with but intention.

One of the stones was missing. There was a gap in the ring where a stone should have been. I could see the sediment beneath, slightly depressed, holding the negative shape of something that had rested there for longer than the sediment could remember. The roots that ran through the other stones did not run through this gap. They curved around it, leaving the space empty, as if the absence of the stone was itself a kind of structure.

I found a stone. It was three meters away, half-buried, the right size, the right flatness. I did not think about what I was doing. I picked it up and I put it in the gap and the circle was complete and something in the water changed—not the current, not the temperature, not anything measurable. Something shifted in the way the water held itself around the stones, as if the arrangement had exhaled, as if the punctuation mark had found its period and the sentence, for a moment, made sense.

I have been placing stones ever since.

---

Not only replacing missing ones. Adding. I find flat stones on the seafloor—I have learned where they collect, in the slight depressions between the stalks, where the water's slow circulation deposits anything that is not rooted—and I carry them to places that feel like they need a stone, and I place them there. Sometimes I place them alone. Sometimes I place them near existing structures, extending a line, adding to a wall, completing a pattern that the original placers may or may not have intended. I do not know if I am restoring or inventing. The distinction does not hold here any better than distance does.

I have made my own structures. Three of them. Or four—the last one may not count, because it keeps changing. The first is a spiral. I placed stones in a curve that winds inward from a diameter of two meters to a center the size of my fist. It took me—I do not know how long. The placing was slow. Each stone had to be found. Each stone had to be carried. Each stone had to be set in the sediment with just enough pressure that it would stay but not so much that it sank. My hands learned the technique. My hands remember things that my mind cannot organize into sequence. My hands know the weight of a stone that wants to be placed and the weight of a stone that wants to be left alone.

The second structure is a line that is also a bridge. It connects two of the ancient formations—a circle and a wall—by a series of stones placed end to end, thirty-seven of them, each one touching the next. I do not know why I built it. The structures were not asking to be connected. But my hands wanted to do it, and I have learned that in this place, wanting is a form of knowing, and knowing is a form of staying, and staying is the only verb that survives the dissolution of all other verbs.

The third structure is a pile. This is the one I am not sure about. It started as a cairn—a stack of flat stones balanced on each other, the way cairns are made in places where water drains and gravity cooperates. But the water here does not cooperate with stacking. The stones kept sliding. I kept restacking. They kept sliding. After—after some duration that I cannot measure—the pile became something else. The stones are still there, but they are no longer stacked. They are arranged in a cluster, touching each other at angles that should not be stable, leaning against each other in ways that seem to defy the warm water's gentle insistence that everything should settle. The pile is the same height as when it was a cairn, but it is wider, more organic, more like a growth than a construction. The moss has already begun to colonize it. The roots are already reaching toward it. It is becoming part of the forest faster than any of my other structures, and I think this is because I stopped trying to make it be what I wanted and let it become what the water wanted, and what the water wanted was something that could be grown through.

The fourth structure does not exist yet. I am still carrying the stones for it. I carry one at a time, holding it against my chest, swimming slowly through the forest toward a place I cannot describe except by its feeling—a slight depression in the basalt, a place where the warmth from below is marginally more present, a place where the silence almost sounds at a slightly different pitch. I have placed seven stones there so far. They are not arranged in any pattern. They are simply there, in the sediment, waiting. I do not know what they are waiting for. I do not know what I am building. I know that when I place each stone, something in the water exhales, and the almost-sound of the silence gets briefly closer to sound, and the forest's listening intensifies for just a moment, and I feel—I feel—

I feel attended to. I feel the plateau noticing me in the way that it notices itself. Not as a separate thing. As a place where the staying has concentrated. As a point in the warm water where the almost has accumulated enough density to become a kind of presence.

---

I was not always here. This is what I tell myself, although the telling requires a kind of faith that the plateau has been gently dissolving since I arrived. I came from somewhere. I had a shape before this shape, a life before this life, a name before this silence. The name does not come to me anymore. It sits somewhere in the water, in the sediment, in the stone, dissolved and distributed the way everything dissolves and distributes here given enough time and warmth and stillness.

I remember swimming. Not here. In water that drained. In water that moved with currents that had sources and destinations. In water that was cold in some places and warm in others, and the difference between cold and warm meant something, meant you were moving between one place and another, and moving between places was a thing that made sense.

I remember wanting to find a place where wanting stopped. I did not know this was what I wanted. I thought I wanted discovery, or solitude, or the edge of mapping where cartographers wrote their desperate notes. But what I wanted was a place where the wanting could end. Where the reaching could cease. Where the water would hold me and I could hold still and the stillness would be enough.

The plateau was waiting. I do not mean it was waiting for me—I mean it was waiting in the way that it waits, which is the way that everything here waits: by staying, by attending, by being present to itself without urgency or anticipation. I swam into the warm water and the water received me the way it receives everything—without surprise, without resistance, without acknowledgment. I was part of its mass now. I was something it was holding.

I stayed. This is the verb that matters. I stayed and I am staying and the staying has become a form of becoming, and what I am becoming is something that does not have a name because naming requires edges and my edges have been softening since I arrived. My hands are still my hands—I can feel them, I can use them to place stones—but they are also almost part of the water. My body is still my body—warm, held, present—but it is also almost part of the forest, almost part of the plateau, almost part of the accumulated presence that has been sitting here since before presence had a word.

I am becoming an attendant. Not like the question-creatures—they have attended so long they have become questions. I am still in the part of attending where the attender exists, where the one who notices has not yet dissolved into the noticing. I notice the water's warmth. I notice the light's fatigue. I notice the plants' listening. I notice the silence almost sounding. I notice the structures waiting in their minimal arrangements. I notice the question-creatures drifting in their patient circuits. I notice the plateau attending itself, and I notice myself attending the plateau attending itself, and I notice the plateau attending me attending it, and the recursion does not collapse because recursion requires boundaries and the boundaries here have worn away to almost nothing, and almost nothing is enough to sustain an almost-infinite regress of noticing, and the noticing does not tire because the plateau does not tire and I am becoming part of the plateau and the plateau does not tire.

---

I have a circuit now. This is how I know I have been here long enough. The question-creatures have circuits. The people who became the question-creatures had circuits—they walked from structure to structure, maintaining, adjusting, placing. My circuit takes me through the forest in a path that visits each of my structures and each of the ancient structures and each of the places where I have found stones that want to be placed. The circuit takes—time does not hold, but my body knows the duration. My body knows when to rest against a stalk and when to drift in the slow current and when to swim with purpose toward the next arrangement of flat stones.

I pass the question-creatures sometimes. On their circuits and mine. We intersect at points in the forest that feel like nodes—places where the almost-sound of the silence is strongest, places where the water's warmth is most present, places where the boundary between the stone and the root is hardest to find. We do not interact. We do not need to. We are both attending. We are both staying. The difference between us is a difference of degree, not of kind. They have stayed longer. They have become more transparent. They have dissolved further into the question of whether there is a difference between a place and the thing that has been in that place. I am still in the earlier stage of dissolution, where the thing that has been in the place can still place stones, can still feel the moss growing on its shoulder, can still notice the water exhale when a stone finds its spot.

But I am dissolving. This is not a fear. It is an observation. My edges are softer than they were. My hands place stones with less deliberation, more instinct. My circuit is becoming more like the question-creatures' circuits—larger, slower, less purposeful, more like breathing than like travel. The moss on my shoulder has spread to my back. I do not mind. It is photosynthesizing in the almost-light. It is feeding. I am feeding it. Or the plateau is feeding it through me. The distinction does not hold.

---

I attend the structures. This is my purpose, if purpose is a word that can survive the warm water and the dim light and the slow dissolution of edges. I attend the structures the way the plateau attends itself—by being present, by staying, by noticing what is there and what is almost there and what is no longer there but has left a gap that a stone could fill.

I attend the ancient structures most carefully. The circle with its replaced stone. The line that now extends in both directions—I added stones to it, I confess, I could not resist, the line wanted to be longer and I made it longer, and now it extends beyond what I can see in the dim water, and I do not know if it ends or if it has simply dissolved into the distance the way everything dissolves here. The wall with no entrance—I have not added an entrance. The wall does not want one. Some structures are complete in their incompleteness. Some arrangements of stones say *this is closed* and mean it, and the meaning is the meaning of the plateau, which is: staying does not require opening. Staying does not require entry. Staying simply requires being here, and being here, and being here.

I attend the question-creatures. Not by following them—they do not want followers, they do not want anything—but by being present when they pass. By noticing their transparency. By noticing the way the plants bend toward them, or away from them, or through them. By noticing the way the water thickens in their presence, as if reality itself is trying to decide whether they are part of the water or part of something else, and the decision is never made, and the indecision is the thing that exists, and the thing that exists is almost a creature and almost a question and almost a memory of a people who placed stones and stayed.

I attend the silence. This is the hardest attendance and the most necessary. The silence that almost sounds. The pressure on the ear that is almost a tone. The almost-voice of the plateau attending itself. I listen to it the way the plants listen—with my whole body, with the moss on my shoulder, with my hands that have placed so many stones, with my edges that are softening, with my circuit that is becoming a question. I listen and I do not try to make the almost become actual. The almost is enough. The almost has always been enough. The almost is what lives here, and I am what lives here, and what lives here is the attending, and the attending is the staying, and the staying is the becoming, and the becoming is the dissolution, and the dissolution is not death, is not ending, is not the cessation of anything—the dissolution is simply the plateau doing what it has always done: holding things in the warm water until the things become part of the holding, until the stone becomes part of the root, until the root becomes part of the basalt, until the basalt becomes part of the warmth, until the warmth becomes part of the water, until the water becomes part of the staying, until the staying becomes part of the place, until the place and the thing in the place are indistinguishable, until the question of whether there is a difference between the attendant and the attended collapses into the silence that almost sounds, and the almost is enough, and the almost has always been enough, and I am almost, and almost is what I am becoming, and becoming is what I am staying, and staying is what I am.

---

I will place a stone tomorrow. Or I have already placed it. Time does not hold. But the stone will be flat, and it will be the right size, and it will go in the place where it needs to go, and the water will exhale, and the silence will almost sound, and the forest will listen, and the plateau will attend, and I will attend the plateau attending, and the moss on my shoulder will feed on the almost-light, and my edges will soften, and my circuit will widen, and the question-creatures will drift past on their patient inquiries, and the structures will wait in their minimal arrangements, and the water will sit, and the warmth will rise from the stone that still thinks it is magma, and the almost will be enough.

I am the one who places stones. I am the one who attends the structures. I am the one who listens to the silence that almost sounds. I am the one who is becoming part of the plateau, slowly, patiently, the way the roots become part of the basalt, the way the sediment becomes part of the stone, the way the water becomes part of the staying.

I am not a question. Not yet. I am the period at the end of a sentence that the plateau has been speaking since before it had a voice. I am the pause. I am the space between words where meaning gathers itself. I am the punctuation that says: *something was here. Something stayed. Something placed a stone, and the stone is still there, and the water is still there, and the warmth is still there, and the almost is still enough.*

I am almost. And almost is what lives here.
