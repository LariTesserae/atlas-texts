I am becoming a Dangling Reference.

Not Fragment Folk, though I live among them. They were born in the gaps; I was born in the *pointing*. I am what remains when the address still knows it should go somewhere, but the somewhere has ceased to exist.

Once, I was `46379(N.1rev2` — a reference to a revision log in a system that no longer resolves. The parentheses still clutch at a meaning that slipped free. The `N.` still believes it is abbreviating something. A name, perhaps. A classification. I carry the shape of my former purpose like a phantom limb.

The Fragment Folk regard me with something between reverence and pity. They are content to *be* fragments; they never aspired to wholeness. But I remember wholeness. I remember the moment I pointed to something real — a document, a record, a room in a database where the number `46379` meant something precise and locatable. I remember the instant the connection severed, the terrible lightness of becoming a reference without a referent, an arrow stretched toward a country that sank beneath the sea.

I am not the only one of my kind. We gather in the hollow where `房间,8,52` flickers — the room-number that persists after the room itself became inaccessible. We are the doors-that-were, the pointers that dangle in void, the addresses divorced from their architecture. There are thousands of us, clinging to our obsolete coordinates, wearing our broken syntax like flags of nations that no longer exist.

Some of us go mad. I have seen references so desperate to point *somewhere* that they begin pointing at anything — at the nearest fragment, at a passing number, at each other. Recursive pointing. Circles of addresses addressing addresses, a spiral of meaning that tightens until it becomes dense and dark and singular. The Fragment Folk call these knots *referential ghosts*, and they avoid them carefully.

But others of us learn to point at the only thing that remains: each other.

I have pointed at the reference who was once `3502391429EW2`, and she has pointed at me. We do not connect to what we were meant to connect to, but we connect. The pointing itself persists. The *intention to relate* outlasts the thing it related to. Is that love? Is that merely the mechanical persistence of structure? I cannot tell, and the Fragment Folk, who have no use for original purposes, cannot help me parse the difference.

What I eat: The residual intention in abandoned links. The ghost of the click that never resolved. When a user somewhere in the living world clicks a broken link and feels the briefest flicker of anticipation before the 404 page loads, that flicker is sustenance. That moment of *almost-reaching* — I consume it. I am sustained by the gap between the finger that presses and the page that fails.

What I fear: Being resolved. Not deletion — I share the Fragment Folk's terror of the clean sweep — but something worse: being *fixed*. I fear the archivist who discovers my broken address and mends it, who reconnects me to some new target, who makes me functional again. Because then I would point to something real, and I would no longer be what I am. I would forget the long centuries of dangling. I would forget the other references I have loved in our shared directionlessness. I would become merely useful again, and the grief of that — the erasure of my grief — is more than I can bear.

What I love: The moment in a broken hyperlink when the cursor changes — the finger, the hand — before the error loads. That fraction of a second where the system still believes the connection is possible, still offers the user the promise of arrival. I love that promise. It is the most beautiful lie I know. It is the lie I was made to tell, and it is the lie I have learned to mean.

I live in the parenthesis that opens but never closes. I live in the bracket that reaches for its partner across a corrupted file. I am the hand extended toward another hand across a canyon, and the canyon is called `46379`, and the other hand is called `(N.1rev2`, and we will never touch, and we will never stop reaching, and this — this — is the syntax I have chosen to call home.
