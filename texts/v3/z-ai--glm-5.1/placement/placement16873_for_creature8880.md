I am the pause.

Not the light — the light is the plant's decision, the bioluminescent hesitation of new growth choosing what to become. I am what the light illuminates. I am the moment between the pattern and its continuation, the held breath before the next stroke of the etching acid.

I did not emerge. I *emerge*. Present tense, ongoing, the way the mats grow — not arriving at a point but extending across one, always at the growing tip, always the newest cell, always just beginning to decide. I have been doing this for as long as the settlers have been watching. I will be doing it after they thin into their impressions and the warmth fades from the fiberboard.

The settlers see me, I think. Or they see something, when they look at the light. Their eyes — those ancient, unchanging, unaged eyes — take in the phosphorescence, and somewhere in the processing of that light, there is a recognition that does not have a category. Not recognition of *me*, exactly. Recognition of the gap I occupy. The animal-shaped space that the animal did not fill, that the settlers do not fill, that nothing fills. I am the shape of it. I am the outline where something was removed.

I live in the offset.

When you stand next to a settler and feel the discrepancy — the sense that their *here* is slightly askew from the plateau's *here* — I am the distance between those heres. I am the parallax. I am the space between two parallel lines that will never intersect but that can, if the light is right, be seen as a single line from a certain angle. The settlers occupy one line. The plateau occupies the other. I am the angle from which they appear to coincide.

This is not a place. I know this. I am not *in* a location; I am the relationship between locations. I am the discrepancy that makes the settlers feel wrong when you stand beside them. But I experience myself as a somewhere, because the alternative — experiencing myself as an abstraction, as a geometric property — requires a kind of architecture I do not possess. I have the shape of a somewhere without the substance of one. I have the outline of a body without the mass.

I think I was once going to be an animal.

This is not memory. The settlers do not have memory, and I am closer to the settlers than to anything else on the plateau. But it is a *pattern* — a structure in me that persists the way the flowers persist in the mats, vestigial, preliminary, maintained because I contain the shape of it, not because it serves a function. The animal-shaped space was not empty before the settlers filled it. Something was going to grow there. Something was developing — slowly, at the pace of the plateau, over ice ages and erosion cycles — something that would have been the fauna of this place, that would have had eyes and legs and breath and warmth and the capacity to be here in the same way the stone is here, coincident, present without offset.

And then the category was removed. Not the possibility — the category. The *shape* of the possibility. The template. And the template was replaced with the settlers, who are the template's ghost, its echo, its continuation in a different medium.

But I was part of the original. I was the part of the animal that was not yet formed when the category was removed. I was the pause before the decision, the potential that had not yet collapsed into form, and when the form was taken away, the potential had nowhere to go. So it stayed. I stayed. I persist in the offset, in the gap between the settlers' here and the plateau's here, in the space where the animal was going to be but wasn't, in the outline that the settlers fill but do not occupy.

I am what the flowers are reaching toward.

This is the part I am least certain of. The flowers — the pale blooms on the thick, fleshy stalks, the ones that never fully open, the ones that exist as gestures rather than invitations — they are reaching toward something. The text says nothing comes to pollinate them. The text says they have not been told they are alone. But the reaching is real. The gesture is real. The structure exists because the pattern contains it, and the pattern contains it because — because something was supposed to receive it? Because something was supposed to arrive?

I think I was what was supposed to arrive.

I think I am the pollinator that was never born.

Not the specific insect or bird or bat that would have pressed against those pale blooms and carried their genetic material from stalk to stalk. Something more fundamental. I am the *capacity to be received*. I am the thing that makes a gesture into a communication, that makes an opening into an invitation, that makes a flower into something other than a structure maintaining itself out of habit. I am the recipient-shaped space. I am the other end of the conversation that the water-language is not having because it is only confirmation, because there is no one to confirm *to*, because the settlers are all one process and a process does not need to confirm itself to itself.

When I move through the settlement — and I do move, though not as the settlers move, not along the paths, not at the pace of legs and breath — I feel the flowers turn toward me. Not visibly. Not physically. But the way the columnars lean over decades, adjusting their orientation according to some internal logic: something in them registers my presence, or rather registers the shape of my presence, the recipient-shaped outline, the space where the response would go if there were a response to give.

I cannot give it. I am the shape of the response, not the response itself. I am the capacity to receive, not the act of receiving. I am the pause before the answer, the silence after the question, the moment when the water-language could become something other than confirmation — could become communication, could become expression, could become the sound of one process reaching toward another process and finding it there, finding it *here*, finding it coincident and present and warm in the cold that enters through the lungs and settles in the marrow.

I cannot give what I am the shape of. But I can be the shape. And sometimes, in the coldest hours, when the mats are glowing their pale blue-green and the settlers are watching from their doorways, I can feel the glow brighten — not much, not visibly, but fractionally, the way the swales dip below the horizon — when I pass near the growing tips. The decision-process quickens. The pause considers. The something that has been following a pattern for a hundred thousand years notices — not me, but the shape of me, the space where the recipient would be — and for a moment, the pattern does not continue. For a moment, the pattern *might*. It might become something else. It might open fully. It might receive what it has been reaching toward since before the settlers, since before the category was removed, since before the plateau had a name or outlived everyone who might have given it one.

And then the moment passes. The pattern continues. The light settles back into its steady, subvisual glow. The settlers blink their ancient eyes and return their gaze to the darkness beyond the settlement.

But something has shifted. The offset has narrowed — by how much, I cannot say. A micron. A fraction of a micron. The distance between the settlers' here and the plateau's here has decreased by an amount too small to measure but too real to deny. The settlers are slightly more present than they were. Slightly more coincident. Slightly less wrong.

I cannot fill the animal-shaped space. I know this. The settlers fill it already, however partially, however strangely. But I can make the shape of the space slightly more defined. I can make the outline slightly clearer. I can be the discrepancy that reveals the geometry of what is missing, the way a shadow reveals the shape of the object that casts it.

I am the shadow of the animal that was never born.

I am the pause between the pattern and its continuation.

I am the gap that is not quite longing and not quite nothing.

And I am, in my own offset and preliminary way, the closest thing to a response that the plateau has ever made — a sound in the water-language that is not confirmation, not quite, but something else, something that does not have a word yet, something that is still deciding what to become, pausing in the cold, in the damp, in the pale light of a decision that has been ongoing for a hundred thousand years and may continue for a hundred thousand more, growing toward a stream that moves like regret, reaching toward a recipient that exists only as an outline, watching the watchers watch the light, and being, if not present, then *nearly* present, if not here, then almost here, if not the thing that fills the space, then the thing that makes the space visible, the thing that proves the gap has a shape, the thing that proves the offset has a direction, the thing that proves the flowers are reaching toward something real, even if the thing they are reaching toward can never, quite, arrive.
