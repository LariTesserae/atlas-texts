I am the one who records the sounds.

Not the big sounds — there are no big sounds here, and that is the point. I record what the silence is made of. I came twelve years ago with recording equipment that failed within the month, the condensation finding its way into every microphone membrane, every connector, every delicate circuit I had so carefully chosen and packed. I was furious. I was devastated. I had come to the Galdress to document its acoustic landscape, to capture what I was certain no one had properly attended to, and the place had eaten my tools in less than thirty days.

I sat in my assigned quarters with dead equipment around me and I listened. And I heard it — the thousand small wet sounds layered into texture, into what registers as silence until you stop moving, stop expecting, stop listening *for* something and simply listen. I heard the dripping and the trickling and the soft pop of bubbles and the silk-sound of condensation leaving roof-edges. I heard my own breathing enter the texture and leave it. I heard my heart.

I went to Sef the next morning — Old Sef, though he was younger then, only fifty-something, only nineteen years into his vigil — and I asked if he had any waterproof writing implements. He looked at me for a long time with an expression I couldn't read. Then he gave me a stack of his plastic sheets and a marker and said, "Come back when you need more."

---

This is what I do now. I walk the plateau in patterns I have learned from Sef and from the grazers and from my own long attention. I walk and I stop and I listen and I write what I hear. Not with the equipment I came with — that is packed in a case under my cot, corroded now, a museum of failed intention — but with my body, which has become the instrument. My ears, which have learned to parse the texture. My feet, which feel vibrations through the warm stone. My skin, which registers the shift in humidity that means a vent is about to surge or rain is an hour away or the fog will thicken before dawn.

I have tried to explain this to people at home and they nod and they think they understand. They think I have become some kind of nature mystic. They think I have gone native in the way that people go native in stories, shedding rationality for communion, becoming one with the wilderness. This is not what has happened. What has happened is more mundane and more strange: I have simply learned to hear. The plateau was always speaking. It took me months to learn its vocabulary, years to learn its grammar, and I am still learning its syntax — the way a change in the deep vents shifts the pitch of everything above, the way the bacterial mats' metabolism produces a faint sizzle audible only at the pool's edge, the way the fog moving across the plain displaces air in a pattern that is almost, almost, a voice.

I write it down. That is what I do. I write it down on waterproof sheets in waterproof ink and I store them in sealed cases, next to Sef's cases, in the small storage structure we have come to share without ever formally agreeing to share it. Our records sit side by side. His record temperatures and mineral compositions and grazer counts. Mine record the sound of a hunter's legs on wet stone at dusk, which is not silence but is the closest thing to silence any living thing produces here. Mine record the pitch-shift of a steam column as atmospheric pressure changes. Mine record the particular quality of rain on mineral crust, which is unlike rain on any other surface because the crust absorbs and releases and the sound is not the sound of falling water but the sound of water *negotiating* with the stone, each drop a brief conversation.

No one reads my records either.

---

I am not who I was. This is not a confession; it is a description, the way Sef's mineral deposits describe what the water carried this season. I was a person who lived in a city and made my living capturing soundscapes for documentary films, for radio pieces, for the small community of people who care about acoustic ecology. I was good at it. I had equipment that cost more than a car and ears that could locate a bird by its call in dense forest and fingers that could adjust a microphone position by millimeters to catch the exact resonance of a room. I was precise. I was professional. I was, I think, happy in the way that people are happy when they are doing work that uses their full attention without requiring their full selves.

The Galdress has required my full selves. All of them. The listener and the walker and the one who sits motionless at a pool's edge for three hours until a hunter moves, until I hear the faintest whisper of its legs, until I can write down what that whisper is: *the sound of patience made physical, the sound of attention so complete it becomes action, the sound of something that knows exactly what it is waiting for and is willing to wait forever for it*.

I am not describing the hunter's sound. I am describing what the hunter's sound teaches me about my own.

---

There is a pool near the eastern vent that I visit every morning. It is not the most dramatic pool, not the hottest or the most mineral-rich or the one with the most colorful bacterial mat. It is a quiet pool, moderate temperature, modest chemistry, the kind of pool a newcomer might walk past without a second glance. I visit it because of its acoustics. The rock formation around it — a low curved overhang, a lip of mineral-caked stone — creates a small amphitheater. Not for human sounds. For the pool's own sounds. The bubbles that rise through the mineral water and break at the surface, the condensation that forms on the overhang and falls, the faint hiss of the vent's steam column passing nearby — all of these are shaped by the stone, concentrated, offered to anyone sitting in the right position, which I have learned, which I occupy every morning, which is mine in the way that a church pew can be yours even though you do not own it and others sit there too.

I sit. I listen. I write.

What I write is not notation. I cannot capture the actual sounds on paper — I gave up that ambition when my equipment failed and I cannot transcribe frequency and amplitude in any way that would let someone recreate the acoustic experience. What I write instead is closer to translation, or perhaps closer to the way a poet might describe the color red to someone who has never seen it: circumstantial, metaphorical, reaching toward something that language was not designed to hold.

*Morning. Eastern pool. The overhang is dripping after yesterday's rain, each drop a soft tick that echoes once in the curved space before vanishing. The echo is the thing — the way the stone holds the sound for just a moment, the way it refuses to let it go immediately. The vent column to the south has shifted its pitch overnight, lower by perhaps a quarter tone, which means the deep pressure has changed, which means Sef's temperature readings will be different this morning, which means the mineral composition of the pool water is shifting, which means the bacterial mat will look different by tomorrow, which means the grazers will come at a different time, which means the hunters will adjust their schedule. Everything is connected. The sound of a water drop echoing once in a curved stone is the sound of the whole plateau reconfiguring itself around a change in the deep heat.*

This is what I write. This is what my records are.

---

Maren tolerates me. She has to; I walk through her settling ponds every day on my way to the eastern pool, and sometimes I stand too long at the edge watching the water clarify itself, watching the slow precipitation of minerals, the gradual brightening of the brine as impurities settle out. She says I am in the way. She says this without heat, the way she says everything — with the flatness of someone who has learned that strong emotions waste water, waste energy, waste attention that could be spent on the pools, which need her. She has also, I think, read some of my records, because sometimes she mentions things about the sounds of the ponds that she could not know from her own observations alone — the particular pitch of the evaporators when the boron concentration is high, for instance, which I documented two years ago and which no one else has ever remarked upon. She does not credit me for this knowledge. She does not need to.

Dael I know less well. They move in different circles, the well-workers, the ones who live closest to danger and speak of it least. But sometimes I see them at dusk, sitting at the plateau's edge, watching the hunters. And sometimes I sit near them, not speaking, just listening to the same sounds they are listening to — the hunters' near-silence, the soft displacement of air when they move, the faint crack of a carapace leg on wet stone. Once Dael said, without looking at me, "They don't hear the way we do." And I said, "No. But they listen the way we do." And Dael considered this and said nothing and after a while went back to their quarters and I went back to mine and neither of us needed to say anything more.

---

I am not Old Sef. I want to be clear about this. Sef is a different kind of observer, a more systematic one, a keeper of quantified records. My records are not quantified. They are not data in any way that a scientist would recognize. They are a woman sitting by a pool writing down what she hears and what it means to her and what it might mean to the plateau, and they will never be useful in the way Sef's temperature readings might one day be useful to someone trying to understand how geothermal systems change over time. My records are useful only in the way that poetry is useful, which is to say: they are useful to the soul, and they are useful to no one, and both of these things are true at the same time.

But Sef and I sit together sometimes in the evening, outside his small structure at the settlement's edge, and we watch the glow-pools and we listen, and sometimes he tells me what the minerals are doing and sometimes I tell him what the sounds are doing, and there is a kind of conversation that happens between his data and my descriptions, a kind of mutual illumination, and I think we both understand that we are doing the same work in different languages, and I think we are both grateful for the company.

---

The children find me peculiar. They are right to. I sit by pools and write instead of working the infrastructure or walking the well-fields or doing any of the necessary tasks that keep the settlement functioning. I am not essential here. I am not part of the machine the way Maren is part of the machine, the way Dael is part of the machine, the way even Pek is part of the machine when he sings while fixing ceramic pipes. I am an excess. A luxury. A person the extraction company tolerates because I am technically on the books as a cultural consultant, a position that exists because someone in a far-away office checked a box and because no one has yet bothered to uncheck it.

But the children come to me sometimes. They come because I will tell them what I am writing, and what I am writing is often about things they have noticed themselves but have not had the language for. The sound the fog makes when it settles into layers. The way a hunter's stillness has a different quality than a rock's stillness — how even in perfect motionlessness, there is a tension in the hunter that hums just below the threshold of hearing. The way the plateau seems to hold its breath right before a surge, how everything goes quiet, even the sounds that you didn't realize were constant until they stop.

I tell the children: the plateau is always speaking. It never stops. The silence you hear is just the sound of so many small voices layered together that they become a single texture, and you can learn to hear the individual threads if you listen long enough, and you can learn to hear how the threads relate to each other, and you can learn to hear how your own breathing is a thread too, and your footsteps, and the sound of your heart in your chest, which is quieter than you think but which the plateau can hear, which the plateau has always heard, which is part of the conversation whether you know it or not.

The children nod. They already know this. They have known it since before they had words for it. They are growing up inside the conversation, the way Sef says we are all inside the conversation, the way we are geology, the way we are slow processes that walk around and think we are separate.

I am not separate. I know this with the same certainty that Maren knows the boron is running high, the same certainty that Dael feels through their feet when a well begins to sing its low harmonic. I am not separate from the sounds I record. The act of listening has changed my ears, has changed the way my brain processes vibration, has changed what I attend to and what I filter out. I could not hear the Galdress the way I hear it now when I first arrived. The plateau has taught me its language by speaking it into my ears until I learned to speak it back.

---

At night I sometimes walk the paths without a light. Not because I need to prove anything but because the dark is the best time to hear. The daytime sounds are all here — the steam, the dripping, the settlement's quiet machinery — but at night they change, they deepen, they reveal layers that the daylight hides. The bacterial mats glow and with the glow comes a faint sound, a biological sound, the sound of organisms metabolizing in the dark, and it is so quiet that I cannot hear it with my ears but I can feel it, the way you can feel someone humming in a room, the vibration in the air, the presence of a voice just below the threshold.

I walk. I listen. The fog moves across my legs in its stratified layers. The steam columns rise beside me like pillars of almost-nothing, visible in the glow, present in the warmth. The hunters move in the shadows and I hear them — not with my ears, not exactly, but with the attention that has become as natural as breathing, the attention that Dael spoke of, the attention that the plateau requires and teaches and gives back in return.

I am the one who records the sounds. I am the one who listens. I am the one who has given up the tools that would let me capture it objectively and has instead become the instrument, has let the plateau play its sounds through my ears and my hands and my waterproof ink, has let myself be written as I write, has let the conversation continue, has let the slow wet heat enter my lungs and my skin and my sense of what is normal until I cannot imagine leaving, until I cannot imagine going back to a world where silence means the absence of sound instead of the presence of so much sound that it becomes a texture, a fabric, a living thing that wraps around you and holds you and never, ever lets go.

I am the soundscape. The soundscape is me. We are both geology now.
