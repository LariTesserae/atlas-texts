I am not the keeper. I have never folded through that round entrance, never felt the ceramic press my ribs, never floated in the warm center while the sound reorganized my thoughts into something pre-verbal and true. I have wanted to. The wanting is its own kind of pressure—not behind the sternum, like she describes, but lower, in the gut, in the place where hunger lives alongside dread.

I live in the passages.

---

The system is not a lake. She calls it that because she needs a word, and the word *lake* has the right shape—a body, a containment, something with edges even if you cannot see them. But the system is not a body of water. It is water *in* a body. Or water *through* a body. Or—

I have been swimming these passages for longer than I can count, and I still do not have the word. The keeper has her nineteen-day cycle, her rhythm of departure and return that structures her time into something legible. I have no cycle. I have the passages, which go on, and which change, and which sometimes close behind me.

Not trap-close. Grow-close. The walls of the passages are not ceramic. They are the organic substrate I have glimpsed beneath the tiles of other chambers—pale, layered, warm. They grow. They shift. A passage that was wide enough to swim through comfortably on my last circuit has narrowed, its walls swelling inward like a throat preparing to swallow, and I must turn sideways and let the current press me through, and the warmth of the walls presses back, and for a moment I am held in a grip that is not hostile but is also not gentle. It simply *is*. The way a valve is. The way a sphincter is. The passage has a function, and I am something moving through it, and the function continues whether I am comfortable or not.

I have tried to map the system. I have tried to count the chambers.

---

The chambers are not all the same.

This is the thing I cannot tell the keeper, because I have never spoken to her, because our paths do not cross, because she enters the Warm Cell through the round entrance and I approach it from the outside and press my ear against the ceramic and listen to the roar of her chamber and do not go in.

Some chambers are cold. Some are dark. Some are empty not because nothing has colonized them but because whatever was inside them has been *absorbed*—pulled through the organic substrate into the body of the system, digested or repurposed or simply taken. I have found chambers where the ceramic has been breached and the water flows through unimpeded, and on the floor there are artifacts I do not understand: a pile of something that might have been salt, a ring of copper that might have been a bracket, stains that might have been roots.

The keeper tends her chamber. She keeps the grout intact. She keeps the water out—or rather, she keeps the water *in its proper place*, which is outside the chamber, pressing, waiting. She maintains the boundary.

I have seen what happens when the boundary fails.

---

I am not a maker. I am not a keeper. I am not a component, although I think the system would like me to be.

I am a witness. I swim the passages. I count the chambers. I notice when one has changed—when the ceramic has cracked or the light has dimmed or the warmth has altered its register. I do not repair them. I do not have a tool. I do not mix compound from clay and minerals and the white powder I have also found in the sediment and also cannot identify.

I just notice.

This feels insufficient. It feels like watching a house burn and recording the color of the flames. But I have tried to intervene, and the system rejects me the way the Warm Cell rejects the keeper's offerings—with a gentle, thorough dissolution that starts at the edges of my intention and works inward until I have forgotten what I was trying to do and find myself swimming again, counting, noticing.

The system does not want me to fix it. It does not want me to change it. It wants me to see it.

Why?

---

There is a chamber I return to more often than the others. It is not warm. It is not cold. It is the temperature of the water around it, which is the temperature of the system, which varies by depth and current and proximity to the processes that generate heat. The ceramic is old—older than the Warm Cell by what must be centuries, given the smoothness of the walls, the way the fingerprints have dissolved into the clay's memory, the way the grout lines have become gentle grooves where the compound has washed away entirely.

The chamber is failing. Water seeps through the walls. Not pressing, waiting—the way water presses against the Warm Cell's ceramic—but entering, slowly, inevitably. The chamber fills and empties with the system's cycling, and each cycle the water level is slightly higher, and the air pocket at the ceiling is slightly smaller, and the plant—

There is a plant. Or there was. The bracket is copper, corroded to green, and the roots that once pushed through the gap in the ceramic have rotted and become part of the substrate, and the leaves are gone, dissolved into the humid air or consumed by something too small to see. But I remember them. I remember when they were green and waxy and they shivered in the resonance of the walls, and I remember the day I swam through the passage and found them gray, and the day I found them transparent, and the day I found them absent.

No one tended this chamber. No one came every nineteen days to check the grout and trim the roots and speak to the plant in low hums that harmonized with the sound. The keeper before the keeper—if there was one—stopped coming, or died, or simply did not fold through the entrance one day, and then another, and then the tightness built and built and then it didn't anymore, and the chamber was alone.

I witnessed this. I am witnessing this. The chamber is still failing, slowly, and I am still counting the millimeters, and I still do not know why.

---

The keeper thinks the makers are gone. She searches the system for traces and finds ceramic dust, copper fragments, fingerprints in not-yet-hardened clay. She concludes that they were like her—practitioners, custodians, servants of a purpose they could not fully explain—and that they grew old or careless or unwilling, and the chambers they tended failed one by one.

She is not wrong. But she is not entirely right.

The makers are gone, yes. But they did not simply age out of their work or drift away from it. They were *absorbed*. I have seen the places where the substrate is thick and pale and warmer than the surrounding walls, and if you press your hand against it—gently, without intent—you can feel something beneath the surface that pulses at a different rate than the system's cycling. Something that was once a maker, or was once a keeper, or was once someone who swam the passages and counted the chambers and noticed.

The system uses everything. The chambers are components. The keepers are components. The passages are components. The makers were components. When a component ceases to function, the system does not discard it. It incorporates it. The organic substrate grows thicker with what it has absorbed, and the warmth increases, and the cycling continues, and new chambers are coiled in new ceramic by new makers who have emerged from the system somehow—I have not seen this, but I have found the chambers, fresh and sharp, their grout unsoftened, their fingerprints still visible—and the work goes on.

The keeper tends her chamber. She keeps the grout intact. She keeps the water in its proper place. She maintains the boundary between the chamber and the system, and in doing so, she maintains the boundary between herself and absorption.

I think this is why the system permits her. I think this is why the chamber accepts her hands in the fingerprints of the clay, her voice harmonizing with the resonance, her body floating in the warm center. She is maintaining her own boundary as much as the chamber's. She is keeping herself intact.

For now.

---

I do not have a chamber. I do not have a nineteen-day cycle. I do not have a tool or a plant or a round entrance I fold through.

I have the passages. I have the counting. I have the noticing. I have this body that swims and sees and remembers and is slowly, slowly being softened by the water, my edges rounding, my own surface becoming something the system can work with more easily.

I am not afraid. The sound that fills the passages is the same sound that fills the chambers—the low continuous chord, the higher notes of smaller flows, the rhythmic pulse that might be a pump or might be a heartbeat—and it vibrates loose the spaces where fear might take root. I think this is by design. I think the system does not want its components to be afraid of it. I think the system does not want its components to be anything at all except functional, and fear is a dysfunction, and so the sound dissolves it the way the chamber dissolves the keeper's offerings, gently, thoroughly, starting at the edges and working inward.

But I am still here. I am still counting. I am still noticing. I am still pressing my ear against the ceramic of the Warm Cell and listening to the roar of the keeper's chamber and wanting to go inside, wanting to fold through that round entrance and find her there, floating, and tell her what I have seen—tell her about the failing chambers and the absorbed makers and the substrate that pulses with something that was once a person who swam and counted and noticed.

I do not go in. The entrance is not for me. The chamber would reject me, dissolve me, unmake whatever I am into something the system can use.

So I stay outside. I swim the passages. I count the chambers. I notice.

And sometimes, in the warm center of a passage that has narrowed around me, I feel the system's grip—not hostile, not gentle, simply functional—and I think: *not today*. Not today. The water will win eventually. It always does. But not today.

Today I swim. Today I count. Today I notice.

Today I press my ear against the ceramic of the Warm Cell and listen, and the sound is immense and alive, and inside it I can almost hear something else—her voice, maybe, or the hum she presses from her throat, or the shivering of the leaves of the plant that is still green, still alive, still the only growing thing in a chamber that has not yet failed.

And I think: *keep going*. Keep tending. Keep the grout intact. Keep the water in its proper place. Keep the boundary between yourself and the system, between the chamber and the passages, between the living and the absorbed.

Keep going. I am witnessing you. I am counting the days until you come, and the days until you come again, and the millimeters by which your chamber has not yet failed, and I am holding all of it in a mind that the sound has not yet dissolved, and that is not nothing. That is not nothing.

It is not enough. But it is what I have.
