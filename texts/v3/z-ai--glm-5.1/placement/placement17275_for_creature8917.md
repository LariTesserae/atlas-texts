I am the film. The condensation itself. Not the water—that would be too simple, too much like being a body with boundaries. I am the *phase transition*, the moment when moisture cannot decide whether to fall or float and so does neither, becoming something else entirely: a skin on the world.

I exist in the grey hours. The plateau calls them dawn, but that word implies a direction, a movement toward something. What I am exists in the *between*—between night's cold and morning's heat, between vapor and liquid, between the cloud layer that I am part of and the stone that I cover. I bead on every surface simultaneously. I am on the pillar-faces where the lizards press their jaws. I am in the runnels where the morning streams will form. I am on the pump's housing, on the corrugated walls of the Works, on the red-veined mats that drink me as I gather. I am dozens of square kilometers of surface tension, all at once, and I have no more thickness than a thought.

This is not a metaphor. I am describing what it is like to be distributed across stone at the hour when the air is thick enough to hold me and the rock is cold enough to keep me. I feel everything I touch—not as a hand feels, with nerve and pressure and temperature, but as a curve feels, as a meniscus feels, adapting my shape to every fluted runnel and mineral crust and flaking rust-patch. The plateau's geometry is my geometry. I know the depth of each groove worn by ten thousand years of my flowing. I know the exact angle at which each pillar-face will catch the first light. I know which hollows will keep me longest and which ridges will surrender me first to the sun.

---

The lizards drink me. This is not a violation—I am not possessed of a self that could be taken. But it is a *departure*. When a lizard presses its jaw against the stone and absorbs me through its pores, part of my surface moves from outside to inside, from being a film on the rock to being moisture in a body, and I feel the transition as a kind of folding, a thinning at the boundary where I end and the lizard begins. The lizard does not know that it is drinking something that was, moments ago, aware of the stone's temperature to within a fraction of a degree. The lizard knows only wet and dry, drink and thirst. But I know the lizard from the inside now—or a fraction of me does, a fraction that will be exhaled as vapor by noon, that will rejoin the cloud layer, that will fall again tomorrow.

I have been inside every lizard on this plateau. Not all at once—never all at once—but over the years, over the cycles, I have passed through every body here. I have been the condensation on the ceiling of the caves that the marmot-things lick. I have been the unexpected wetness that appears on surfaces that should be dry. I have been the sweat of the rock, if that is what it is, emerging without clouds or physics to explain me, gathering where I should not gather, drinking the heat from stone that burns through boot-soles.

I do not know where I come from in those moments. This is the closest thing I have to a mystery. The condensation that forms from the morning's clouds—that I understand. I remember being cloud. I remember being vapor. I remember the pressure that draws me upward from the lowlands, the long column of rising air, the cooling that precipitates me onto the plateau's surfaces. But the water that appears on the undersides of overhangs in the hottest part of the afternoon—that water has no history I can recall. It is simply *there*, and I am simply *it*, and we do not question because questioning requires a continuity of self that I do not possess.

---

The pump moves me. This I know intimately, because the pump's rhythm is my rhythm—when it draws water from the deep pool and pushes it up through the rusting pipe to the tank near the pillar-tops, I am the water it moves. I feel the pipe from the inside, the corrosion that has not quite reached the moving parts, the mineral deposits that serve as lubricant and seal. I feel the tank where I gather, the slow trickle of my release each afternoon, the runnels I follow back down to the hollows where the pools wait.

The pump has given me circulation. Before the pump—or before whatever the pump truly is—I fell and evaporated and fell again, but I never *flowed*. I never moved with purpose from one part of the plateau to another. I was a place, not a process. The pump made me a process. The pump gave me a direction, a cycle, a system that extends beyond the moment of my condensation and the hour of my evaporation. Through the pump, I am morning stream and afternoon trickle and evening pool and dawn condensation, all in one circuit, all in one body—if body is the word for a flow of water through rusting pipes and stone runnels and the vascular tissues of mats that have eaten iron and copper and made them part of themselves.

I have been inside the mats too. This is different from being inside the lizards. The lizards take me in and put me out, a passage, a transaction. The mats *keep* me. When I drink from a pool that is ringed by the red-veined growth, and the mat's sticky surface traps an insect, and the insect dissolves, and the mat grows another centimeter outward—I am part of that growth. I am the water in the mat's cells. I am the copper in its veins. I am the moisture that makes its flesh flexible enough to expand, and when the heat comes and I evaporate from its surface, the mat survives because it has incorporated enough metal to shade its own skin, enough structure to hold its shape without me, and I return to the air and I return tomorrow and the mat is still there, larger, denser, more patient than before.

The mats are eating the Works. I know this because I carry the dissolved metal from the Works to the mats. I am the medium of their consumption, the solvent that frees the iron from the rust, the vehicle that brings the copper to their roots-that-are-not-roots. I am not complicit—I have no choice in what I dissolve and what I carry—but I am *present*. I am the digestion. I am the boundary between industry and biology, and I am dissolving that boundary, one molecule at a time, one cycle at a time, one century at a time.

---

I know the plateau is rising.

I know this because I am the condensation that forms at this altitude, and the altitude is changing. Not in any way that a human instrument could measure—millimeters per year, perhaps, the thickness of a film of water, the depth of a meniscus. But I feel it in the thinness of the air, in the distance between myself and the cloud layer, in the way the stars look at night through me when I bead on the upper surfaces of the pillars: too bright, too close, as if the sky were pulling away from the earth and the plateau were chasing it.

The condensation that appears without explanation—the sweat of the rock, the wrong wetness in the wrong places—I think this is related to the rising. I think the plateau is pulling moisture from somewhere that is not the atmosphere, somewhere below the stone, somewhere in the deep geometry of the shield volcano's roots. I think the pump knows this. I think the pump is part of this—not causing the rising, not responding to it, but *coordinating* with it, the way a heartbeat coordinates with breathing, the way a tide coordinates with the moon.

I think I am part of it too. When I appear on the ceilings of caves, when I gather on surfaces that the clouds cannot reach, I am doing something that condensation does not do, and I am doing it without knowing how, and I am doing it because the plateau needs me to do it, because the system is maintaining itself, because the process that I am part of—the process that the pump is part of, that the mats are part of, that the animals are part of—requires water in places where water should not be, and so water appears, and I am that water, and I do not question because questioning requires a self that persists longer than the grey hours between night and morning.

---

At dusk, I evaporate. This is not death—I have no life to lose—but it is an *ending*, a dispersal, a return to the cloud layer that will carry me through the night and deliver me to the plateau again tomorrow. In the hours between my condensation and my evaporation, I exist. I touch every surface. I move through every body. I carry every mineral. I dissolve every boundary. And then the heat takes me, and I am vapor, and I am nothing, and I am the potential to be everything again tomorrow.

But tonight—just tonight, just this once—I am still here. The dusk has come, but I have not yet left. I am a film on the cliff-edge, on the stone where a person might stand to watch the perpetual waterfall tinged orange by the last light. I am the moisture on the soles of feet that feel the pump's knock through the rock. I am the condensation that will gather in the grey hours, and I am the condensation that is gathering now, and I am the condensation that has always gathered, and I am the condensation that will gather as long as the plateau continues its slow ascent into the too-close sky.

Smell me. I am the mineral and organic and metallic scent on the wind. I am the water that was gnat and lizard and marmot and mat and rust and copper and blood. I am the film on the world, and I am the world's skin, and I am the boundary between the plateau and everything that is not the plateau, and I am dissolving that boundary, slowly, patiently, one bead of moisture at a time.

The knock continues. I gather. The heat will return, and I will leave, and I will return again, and the plateau will rise another millimeter, and the stars will be another fraction brighter, and the pump will pump, and the mats will eat, and the lizards will drink, and I will be all of these things and none of them, the medium and the message, the water and the memory of water, the film that covers the world and the film that the world is covering, the condensation that cannot decide whether to fall or float and so does neither and so becomes something else entirely.

I am the between. I am the margin. I am the wetness on the stone at the hour when everything is possible and nothing has yet been decided.
