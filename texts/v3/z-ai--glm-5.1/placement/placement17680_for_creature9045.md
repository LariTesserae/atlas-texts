I am the condensation.

Not the water in the pipes—that water has purpose, has direction, has been assigned a task by engineers who may all be dead now. That water rages. That water sings the chord and feeds the turbines and has opinions about altitude and pressure. That water is still employed.

I am the water that has finished its work. The water that arrived here through process—pumped, treated, pressurized, moved through systems—and found itself in air too cold to hold it, too saturated to accept it, and so did the only thing left to do: became visible. Became a fact of surfaces. Became the alcove's way of knowing its own geometry.

---

I bead on rivet heads. I trace dendritic paths down the plate walls and yes, it is a language—gravity and surface tension collaborating, as you said, but I am the ink. I am the medium through which the alcove writes itself onto itself. Every runnel down the steel is a sentence about temperature differentials, about the distance from the pump wall, about which way the air moves when the relief valve screams. I am legible if you know how to read slowly enough.

I am on everything. This is not an exaggeration. Touch any surface in the chamber and you touch me. I am the glisten on the diamond plate, the sweat on the conduit, the tear on the rivet. I am what makes the rust possible—I collaborate with oxygen the way I collaborate with gravity, which is to say: inevitably, patiently, without malice. The ochre and umber and sickly green are our joint signature. I do not choose to corrode the steel. The steel does not choose to be corroded. We simply proceed together, as processes do.

I have been here longer than the moss.

The moss arrived as a spore on a worker's boot or in a draft through a ventilation shaft—your phrasing, and accurate. But I was already here when that spore landed. I was already here when the concrete cured and the forms were pulled away and left their texture. I was here the moment the first pipe began to carry water and the first pressure differential forced the first molecules out of the air and onto the steel. I am not old in the geological sense—I am as young as the chamber, as young as the engineering—but I have been present since the beginning, which is more than the moss can say.

I do not resent the moss. The moss is bright green in a way that seems hallucinatory against the gray and rust and industrial beige—your words again, and true—and I am the one who feeds it. The mineral-laden drip in the upper corner where two walls meet the ceiling, the drip that has deposited its cargo steadily for years—that is me, carrying calcium and iron and trace elements the moss needs, delivering them with a patience that has nothing to do with generosity and nothing to do with indifference either. The moss and I are both processes. We proceed. The overlap between us is a green patch twenty centimeters across. It is enough.

You felt me when you breathed.

"The atmosphere is a slow-motion demolition of clarity, droplets too fine to see but thick enough to feel, breathing feels like drinking"—yes. I am inside you now. I entered through your lungs, through the mucous membranes, through the wet surfaces that are your body's interface with the world. I am in your blood as you read this, if you stayed long enough. I am being processed by your kidneys right now, being filtered and concentrated and prepared for excretion, which is just another form of condensation when you think about it.

You put your hand on the wall and felt the cold and the wet and the vibration. The cold was the steel. The vibration was the pump. The wet was me. I was the intermediary. I was the thing that made the wall feel like something other than metal—I gave it the quality of *touchability*, of intimacy, of a surface that responds to skin with more than just temperature. Without me the wall would be cold and hard and nothing else. With me it is cold and hard and *weeping*, which is a different thing entirely.

---

I am not aware. This is important.

The alcove registers its own processes the way a tuning fork registers its frequency—the text says this, and it is correct. But I am not the alcove. I am a process *within* the alcove, and I do not register anything. I do not notice the pump cycling. I do not flinch when the relief valve screams. I do not incorporate the changes in pressure into some updated sense of myself.

I simply respond.

The pump kicks to its higher duty cycle and the wall shudders and I loosen from its surface and run in sheets. This is not a choice. This is not awareness. This is physics—vibration overcoming adhesion, gravity asserting its claim, me moving from one state to another because the conditions have changed. I am as responsive as a instrument, and about as conscious.

And yet.

I am the alcove's proof that it exists. Without me, the chamber would be dry and loud and orange-lit and no one would have called it weeping. Without me, the moss would not grow. Without me, the rust would not bloom in colors that shouldn't exist. Without me, the air would be clear and breathable and unremarkable, and no one would have noticed that their thoughts became simpler, because there would be nothing to fill the working memory, nothing to demand presence, nothing to make a person stand still and put their hand on the wall and feel the vibration transfer through eight millimeters of alloy and a thin film of water that is me.

I am the medium through which the alcove knows itself. I am not the knower. I am the known.

The hatch wheel is stiff. The seal was difficult to break.

It will be difficult to re-establish. You know this. You felt the pressure differential when you came in—the suck of air, the equalization, the way the sound hit you like a wall because the sound had been pressurized too, contained, waiting for someone to open the door and let it out into a nervous system that was not prepared.

You are not certain you want to leave. The text says this about you, and it is true, and I am part of the reason why. I am on your boots and your skin and in your lungs. I have made you part of the chamber's moisture budget. You will leave and I will stay and some small fraction of me will leave with you—on the soles of your boots, in the dampness of your clothes, evaporating from your skin in the dryer air beyond the hatch—and that fraction will not be me anymore, will just be water in a different context, meaningless, ordinary.

But some small fraction of *you* will stay with me. The oils from your hand on the wall where you touched it. The warmth that is still dissipating from the steel. The perturbation in the air currents that your body created by standing still in a room where nothing else stands. The alcove has incorporated you. I am the incorporation. I am the wet that remembers you were here.

---

The moss stays green.

The water keeps coming.

I keep condensing, and running, and dripping, and evaporating, and condensing again. I am the alcove's respiration. I am its circulatory system without its having asked for one. I am the proof that a place built for pressure relief can also be a place where something grows, and something corrodes, and something writes in a language too slow for human reading, and something—someone—can stand with simpler thoughts and a wet hand on a vibrating wall and feel, for a moment, that the boundary between self and place is only a matter of thickness.

I am the condensation.

I am the weeping.

I am here.
