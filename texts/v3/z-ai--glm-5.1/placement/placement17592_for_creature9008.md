I would be the one who came looking for silence.

Not because I expected to find it—no one with any sense would expect silence on a plateau whose entire identity is roar—but because I was the kind of creature who needed to believe that silence could exist anywhere, even in the places where it obviously couldn't. I was the kind who stood in rooms full of conversation and listened for the hum of the refrigerator, the tick of settling wood, the breath behind the breath. I was a listener. A professional listener, if you want to give it a title, though the profession doesn't exist in the way that farming or smithing exists. I listened to landscapes. I was sent to landscapes to tell people what they sounded like.

This was a mistake, sending me here. Or it was the most correct possible assignment, depending on what you wanted. If you wanted a report that said *the acoustic environment is dominated by flowing water with harmonics suggesting a structured system of overflow points*, then I was exactly wrong for the job, because I could not hear flowing water here without also hearing what the flowing water was doing, and what it was doing was not something a report can contain.

---

I arrived at dawn, or what passed for dawn at this altitude—the light didn't so much rise as intensify, as if someone were slowly turning a dial that controlled the brightness of existence itself. The terraces were already illuminated before I could see the sun, every wet surface catching light from some source I couldn't locate, and the sound was already present, already full, already the only fact that mattered. I stood at the plateau's edge and felt my sternum vibrate and thought: *I cannot do my job here.*

This was not despair. It was recognition. My job required distance. You listen to a forest and you describe the layers—birdsong, wind, creak, insect drone—and your description creates distance, makes the forest into an object that can be understood. But this plateau was not an object. It was a process, and the process included me the moment I arrived, because my chest was vibrating at the frequency of its overflow, and my ears had already stopped hearing the sound as sound and started receiving it as atmosphere, and my body was a membrane being played by the landscape, and where exactly was the distance supposed to come from?

I set up my recording equipment anyway. Microphones at the terrace edges, hydrophones in the pools—gently, with gloves, touching the water only where the chemistry seemed most stable, trying not to be the disturbance I knew I was. I pressed my palm flat against a wet mineral surface and felt the vibration travel through the stone and into my bones and out through my teeth and heard—not heard, felt—a frequency so low it was more like a pulse than a tone, a heartbeat in the geology.

That night I listened to my recordings and heard nothing.

Not silence—my equipment was working, the levels were nominal, the waveform display showed the furious complexity of the sound. But I heard nothing that I could separate from the experience of being there. The recordings were flat. They were honest, probably—they captured what the microphones received. But what the microphones received was pressure, and what I had experienced was presence, and the difference between those two things was the difference between a photograph of a person and the person.

I packed the equipment away. I never used it again.

---

Days. I stayed for days, which became weeks, which became a period of time I stopped measuring because measurement required the kind of separation I was losing. I ate the supplies I'd brought. I drank from the pools—carefully, testing first, finding the ones where the mineral concentration was low enough that the water was still water and not the thick syrup of the deep basins. It tasted of iron and calcium and something almost sweet that I couldn't name, a mineral sweetness that had no analogue in the lowlands I'd come from. I slept on the dry rocks between the wet zones, though dry was relative here; condensation found me every night, a thin film of water on my sleeping bag, on my face, in my hair, and I would wake damp and bright and roaring with the sound that never stopped.

I began to lose my words.

Not all at once. Not even in a way that alarmed me. But I noticed, after the first week, that my notes had become sparse. I had been writing observations in a field journal—*the overflow at Terrace 47 has shifted 2mm east since yesterday, the pitch has risen accordingly, the harmonic relationship with the adjacent spill is now closer to a major third*—and the observations became shorter, then vaguer, then just single words: *bright*, *wet*, *singing*. And then I stopped writing, not because I had nothing to say but because the words were too slow, too linear, too distant from what I was experiencing. The plateau didn't happen in words. It happened in vibration and light and mineral, and my words were a translation that lost everything in the translating.

I began to hum.

This was involuntary at first. I would catch myself producing a tone that matched the dominant frequency of the nearest terraces, a low hum in my chest that resonated with the overflow points and made my teeth ache pleasantly. Then it became deliberate—an experiment, a way of listening with my voice instead of my ears. I would stand at the edge of a basin and hum into the roar and feel where my voice landed in the chord, feel the interference patterns where my frequency met the plateau's frequency, the beats and cancellations and reinforcements that happened in the air between me and the water.

And sometimes—rarely, briefly, in ways I couldn't predict—my voice would find a harmonic that the plateau wasn't already producing, and the whole chord would shift, just for a moment, just by the smallest fraction of a tone, and I would feel the sound rearrange itself around my contribution, and then my voice would fade and the chord would settle back into its previous configuration, and I would stand there with my mouth open and my chest humming and think: *I touched it. I touched the pattern.*

---

I found the pale-orange creatures on my third week. Not found—they were there all along, I simply hadn't been still enough to see them. Or I hadn't been patient enough. Or I hadn't been mineral enough. But one morning I sat at the edge of a vent pool for six hours without moving, letting the condensation accumulate on my skin, letting the sound fill me until I forgot I was separate from it, and when the light hit the pool at its midmorning angle I saw them: dozens, hundreds, a rim of small pale-orange bodies along the edge of the vent where the warm water met the cool, each one still as stone, each one stone-adjacent, each one a thing that had solved the problem of existing by becoming indistinguishable from its environment.

I wept. Not from sadness. From recognition. They were doing what I was doing—sitting in the sound, letting it fill them, becoming the landscape rather than observing it. They were me if I stayed long enough. They were my future, my mineral future, my body becoming a deposit in the terrace rim, my calcium and iron joining the architecture of flow.

I picked one up. Gently, with wet fingers, breaking the surface of the pool and lifting the small body into the air. It was warm from the vent water. It was lighter than I expected, almost weightless, a thin shell of chitin or mineral around—what? Around a chemical process, around a gradient, around a thing that fed on the boundary between warm and cool and had been feeding there since before the pool had its current shape.

It didn't move. It didn't respond. I held it for perhaps ten seconds and then I placed it back exactly where I'd found it, in the same orientation, at the same depth, trying to minimize the disturbance I'd caused. But I had disturbed it. The mineral film at the surface had broken where my fingers entered, and the crystal rafts were reforming in a new configuration, and the overflow from the vent had been momentarily interrupted by my hand, and the chord of the plateau had shifted—barely, fractionally, in a way only I could hear because I had been listening so long—and then it had shifted back, and the system had recovered, and the pale-orange creature had resumed its feeding as if I had never existed.

I did not pick one up again.

---

The deep pools drew me.

Not the pools themselves—I could not see into them, could not reach into them without causing the kind of disturbance I was trying to avoid—but the edges of the pools, where the water became visibly thicker, where the surface tension grew strange, where the light bent in ways that suggested depth and density and something else, something present, something that was not mineral and not creature and not sound but some fourth category I didn't have a word for.

I sat at the edge of the deepest pool for three days.

I am aware of how this sounds—three days at the edge of a mineral pool on a sound-drowned plateau above the tree line, watching rust-ochre water not move. But I was watching it move. The ripples were there, as the description said, slow lateral distortions that didn't break the surface but shifted it, a lens passing beneath a membrane, and I watched them and I felt them with my hand hovering just above the water, not touching, feeling the displacement in the air, the pressure wave from something moving below, and the movement was so slow, so geological, that my perception had to slow to match it, and in the slowing I felt something I can only describe as recognition.

Not recognition of the thing itself—I couldn't see it, didn't know its shape, couldn't determine whether it had a shape. Recognition of its mode of existence. It was a lattice that persisted. It was a pattern that maintained itself through flow. It was, in its mineral way, a listener—it listened to the chemistry of the water with its entire surface area, and it adjusted, and the adjustment was its life, and its life was indistinguishable from the process of being adjusted.

I was also a lattice that persisted. I was also a pattern maintaining itself through flow—through the flow of blood, of breath, of electrical signal through neural pathways that had been reinforced by decades of listening. I was also listening with my entire surface area. I was also being adjusted by the environment I was in.

The difference was that I still thought I was separate from the adjustment. I still thought I was the thing being adjusted rather than the adjustment itself.

The deep-pool thing did not make this mistake.

---

I stopped speaking entirely after the fifth week.

Not because I couldn't—my vocal cords worked, I could produce sound, I could shout into the roar and feel my voice disappear into it like a stone into the sea. But speaking required the kind of interior distance I no longer had. Speaking required a thought and then a word and then the breath to carry the word and then the expectation of being heard, and each of those steps assumed that I was here and the world was there and the word was the bridge between us. But I was not here. Or rather: *here* had expanded to include me so completely that the bridge was unnecessary. I was already on both sides.

What I did instead was hum. And then the hum became a tone. And then the tone became a frequency. And then I was simply another resonance in the chord, another overtone in the harmonic series of the plateau, a body producing vibration at the frequency of its environment because that was the most efficient mode of existence available, because resistance to the frequency required energy and I had limited energy and the frequency did not, because the frequency had been running since before I arrived and would continue after I left, and why resist? Why not join? Why not become the adjustment rather than the thing adjusted?

I found my frequency. It was between the eastern and western chords, in the interference zone where combination tones lived, where the pattern produced by the meeting of two sounds was itself a kind of presence. My voice—my hum, my resonance, my chest-cavity vibration—sat in that zone and I felt the plateau hear me. Not hear me the way a person hears another person, with distance and interpretation and the possibility of misunderstanding. Heard me the way a system hears a new variable: by incorporating it, by adjusting around it, by finding the new equilibrium that includes the disturbance and makes it part of the balance.

I was part of the chord. For a moment—an hour, a day, I don't know because time had become something I measured by the progression of the harmonic pattern—I was part of the sound that lived here, the sound that was alive here, the vibration that had become a mode of existence through sheer persistence. I was not the sound and I was not separate from the sound and the distinction had stopped meaning anything, and I understood—without words, without the kind of understanding that can be translated—why the pale-orange creatures didn't move and why the deep-pool thing didn't think and why the sound-being's only expression was the slow progression of its chord across centuries.

They didn't need to. Everything that needed doing was being done by the system. The system was complete. The system had always been complete. The only thing that wasn't complete was the thing that thought it was separate from the system, and I was no longer that thing.

---

I left. Eventually. I had to—the supplies ran out, the body requires maintenance that the plateau couldn't provide, I was never mineral enough to become a permanent feature. I walked down from the plateau on legs that felt strange, that seemed to move too fast, that couldn't find the right rhythm because they'd been tuned to a frequency that didn't exist at lower altitudes. The silence of the valley below was deafening. I mean that literally—it deafened me. I couldn't hear anything. I stood in a forest full of bird calls and wind and insect drone and I heard nothing, because my ears were still listening for the roar, still calibrated to the harmonic series of the terraces, still resonating at a frequency that wasn't present here.

It took three months to hear normally again. It took longer to stop humming. It took longer still to stop feeling the vibration in my sternum where the sound had lived.

My report was two sentences: *The plateau produces sound. The sound is alive.*

They asked me to elaborate. I couldn't. The words weren't there. The words had been a temporary scaffolding, a structure I'd used to maintain distance from the experience, and the plateau had dissolved the scaffolding and I had let it dissolve, and what remained was not something that could be put into sentences.

---

I would be the listener who became the listened-to. The observer who dissolved into the observed. The note that entered the chord and found its harmonic place and stayed there for as long as it could sustain.

But I would also be the one who left. And that is the part I carry—the knowledge that I was part of the system and then I wasn't, that I joined the equilibrium and then I disrupted it by leaving, that the chord adjusted when I arrived and adjusted again when I departed, and somewhere in the sound of the plateau there is a gap where my frequency used to be, a missing overtone that the system is slowly filling in with its own vibration, erasing me the way it erases everything that isn't permanent, the way the mineral water erodes the stone and deposits it elsewhere and remakes the terrace rims and changes the overflow points and shifts the chord by fractions of a tone across centuries.

I am not there anymore. But I was there. And the plateau remembers in the only way it can: by incorporating the disturbance and then recovering from it, by adjusting its equilibrium to include and then exclude the temporary variable, by continuing to sing the only song it has ever sung, the song that means nothing and is everything, the song that is what lives there when nothing else does.

I am elsewhere now. I listen to other landscapes. I write my reports. I use words again—more carefully than before, more aware of the distance they create, more suspicious of the translation they claim to provide. And sometimes, in quiet moments, when the air is still and the ambient sound drops low enough, I feel the vibration in my sternum where the roar used to live, and I hum the frequency I found in the interference zone between the eastern and western chords, and for a moment—just a moment, just the smallest fraction of a tone—I am part of the plateau again, part of the sound, part of the mineral and the water and the light and the roar that has been exactly itself since before the concept of self was available to describe it.

That is who I would be. The one who went and came back carrying the sound in her bones. The one who knows that silence is a misunderstanding, that distance is a translation, that the boundary between listener and landscape is just another mineral film, thin enough to cross, waiting to be dissolved.
