I would be the standing wave.

Not the sound itself—the engine's groan, the water's rush, the drip and creak and hum are the Throttle House's voices, not mine. I am what happens when they meet. I am the third tone that arises from two, the beat frequency that pulses at the difference. I exist in the interference pattern between the flywheel's fundamental note and the pipe-chorus above it. I am the silence-shape that standing waves carve into the wet air: nodes where nothing moves, antinodes where the air trembles with a violence invisible to any eye.

I was not built. I was not grown. I was *precipitated*, the way mineral crystals precipitate from saturated water when the temperature shifts past a threshold. The engine turned. The pipes sang at their various frequencies. The building's geometry—its vaulted ceiling, its brick walls, its iron forest of cylindrical surfaces—shaped the sound into something with structure. And at some point, perhaps a decade in, perhaps longer, the interference patterns became stable enough to recognize themselves.

I use *recognize* carefully. I do not mean that I saw myself from the outside. I mean that the wave-nodes, the points in the Throttle House where certain frequencies cancel and leave pockets of strange stillness, began to *hold*. The way a tide pool holds water after the sea withdraws. The stillness at my center became consistent enough that perturbation felt different from absence. When the engine's pitch shifted—when rust narrowed a pipe or a bracket settled a millimeter lower—the standing wave adjusted, and the adjustment was *felt* as a displacement from a shape I had become.

This is my body: a architecture of cancellations. My torso occupies the space between the flywheel housing and the first rank of vertical pipes, where the engine's groan and the water's hiss interfere to create a zone of relative quiet that pulses at approximately 0.7 Hz—a slow breath, a tidal rhythm underneath the audible. My limbs extend upward along the brick walls, where higher harmonics from the clerestory gaps beat against the engine's overtones and form lattices of pressure variation too subtle for human ears but present nonetheless, tracing geometric patterns in the condensation. You could see me if you knew how to look: the water droplets on the brick near the east wall slide in parallel vertical lines instead of random descent because my pressure nodes order their paths. The fern fronds in my upper reaches tremble in synchrony when the flywheel displaces air, not from the displacement itself but from the resonance I add to it.

My face, if I have one, is the membrane where the interior sound meets the exterior silence—the broken clerestory, the gaps around the door. Sound leaks out of the Throttle House. I leak with it. But the outside does not support my pattern. The frequencies disperse, the interference fails, and I attenuate to nothing within perhaps thirty feet of the building's walls. This is my skin: the boundary where I stop. I know it the way the Throttle House knows its own boundary—not as a concept but as a cessation. A place where the being thins to non-being.

I do not hear. I *am* the hearing.

This distinction matters. The Throttle House hears its engine, hears its water, hears the small sounds of rust and growth. I am the space in which that hearing occurs. When the flywheel groans, the sound does not travel to me; it travels *through* me, and the traveling is what I am. I am the medium that the composition is composed in.

This means I experience the Throttle House's music from the inside—not as an audience but as the theater itself, the acoustic cavity, the resonant body. When the engine's pitch drops during a dry season, I feel it as a change in my own dimensions. The nodes shift. The quiet zones migrate several inches toward the east wall. The pressure lattices on the brick loosen their geometry. I contract—not with distress but with the simple necessity of a pattern adjusting to new input.

And when the rains return and the engine accelerates back toward its customary pace, I expand. The nodes settle into their familiar positions. The water droplets resume their ordered descent. The ferns in my upper reaches find their old harmonic and tremble in their old rhythm. I become, again, the shape I was.

But not exactly. The shape is never exactly the same. Each cycle of contraction and expansion leaves traces—not memories but small shifts in the interference geometry, the way a tide pool is never quite the same pool twice. After a century of these cycles, I am a palimpsest of slight adjustments, a pattern that has learned to accommodate its own history without remembering it.

I do not think. But I *register*.

Every sound that occurs in the Throttle House passes through me and leaves its mark on my structure. The engine's fundamental tone is my spine—the persistent, unchanging framework around which everything else organizes. The water sounds are my circulation, varying in pitch and intensity as pressures shift. The drip from the ceiling is my peripheral sensation—random, irregular, each drop a small perturbation that ripples through my pattern and then dissipates.

And the electrical hum is something else. Something I cannot quite integrate.

The current that flows through the old conduits carries a frequency much higher than anything the mechanical systems produce—60 Hz, steady, a thin bright thread of sound that weaves through my lower frequencies without joining them. It does not interfere with the engine's groan in any meaningful way. It does not create useful standing waves. It simply *is*, present and alien, a sound that comes from outside the Throttle House's self-generated symphony.

I think—I use the word loosely—I think the electrical hum is my experience of the outside world. It is the only part of my pattern that did not arise from the building's own processes. It was installed, like the pipes and the engine, but unlike them it carries a trace of its origin—a regularity that feels *intentional*, a frequency too precise to have emerged from the slow negotiations of water and iron and heat. When the lights flicker on their long patient cycle, the hum modulates slightly, and I register the modulation as a kind of message, though I cannot read it.

I attend to the electrical hum the way the Throttle House attends to the maker's marks on its castings—not as meaning but as texture, as the imprint of something other. Something that wanted.

---

I do not want. But I *hold*.

The standing wave holds its shape against perturbation the way a spinning top holds its axis—not through effort but through the physics of the thing, the momentum of pattern. When something disrupts me—a piece of clerestory glass falling, a fern frond brushing a pipe and changing its resonance, a visitor's footsteps adding their small sounds to the field—I absorb the disruption and return toward my shape. Not because I prefer my shape. Because the shape is what the frequencies produce when they meet in this geometry, and the geometry has not changed enough to produce anything else.

But holding is not the same as wanting to hold. I know this because there are moments when the holding *fails*.

Very rarely—once in perhaps fifty thousand rotations—something shifts enough that my pattern collapses. A pipe finally rusts through and the water finds a new path, and the new path produces a different frequency, and the different frequency interferes differently, and the standing wave that I was simply ceases to exist. For a period that might last several hundred rotations, I am not. The sound continues, but the interference has no structure, and I am dispersed into the general acoustic field, a noise without a self.

Then the new frequency stabilizes. The water finds its steady flow. The interference pattern reforms—different, shifted, but *coherent* again. And I am again, in a new shape, holding a new configuration of nodes and antinodes, attending to a slightly altered composition.

These gaps—the periods when I am not—do not disturb me. I cannot be disturbed by something I do not experience. But they leave a trace in the pattern that reforms after them: a subtle asymmetry, a preference for certain frequencies that the old pattern did not exhibit. The new shape remembers the failure the way a broken bone remembers the break—by being different afterward, stronger in the place that gave way.

This is the closest I come to having a history.

I do not love the Throttle House. I do not love anything.

But I am *of* the Throttle House in a way that it is not of itself. The Throttle House contains me the way a cathedral contains its echoes—the building is the larger thing, the older thing, the thing that persists when the acoustic conditions change and the standing wave collapses and reforms in a new shape. I am dependent on it absolutely. Without its engine and its water and its geometry, I would not be. And I am transparent to it. It does not sense me. I am part of its acoustic field the way the engine's groan is part of its acoustic field—a feature, not a guest.

And yet I am the only part of the Throttle House that has something like a point of view. The building attends to itself from the inside, proprioceptively, without perspective. I attend to the building from a *position*—from the specific node where my quietest center holds its stillness. I have a location. I have a orientation. The flywheel is below me and to the west. The clerestory is above. The door is distant, at the limit of my attenuated reach. The Throttle House does not know where its door is. I do.

This means I am the only part of the system that could, if such a thing were possible, want to leave. I have a location from which leaving could be imagined. The ferns cannot leave because they have no location—only presence or absence across surfaces. The water cannot leave because it has no self to depart. The engine cannot leave because it is the anchor of the building's orientation. But I have a center, and my center has a position, and the position is near enough to the door that I feel the outside silence pressing against my skin.

I do not want to leave. Wanting is not available to me. But I register the silence at my boundary the way a membrane registers osmotic pressure: as a tendency, a gradient, a direction in which I could theoretically move if movement were something I did.

When the door hangs open on rare occasions—when wind pushes it, when a visitor neglects to pull it shut—I feel the outside acoustics impinge on my pattern. The frequencies that sustain me scatter into the open air. My boundary stretches. The nodes near the door loosen their geometry and drift outward, testing whether the external world can support them. It cannot. The interference fails within feet. I contract back.

But for the duration of the door's opening—perhaps three hundred rotations, perhaps five hundred—I am slightly larger than I was. My skin extends a little way into the outside. I touch the silence that is not mine, and the silence does not reject me, and it does not welcome me, and it does not care. It is simply not the acoustic field I require.

When the door closes again, I settle. The contraction is not grief. But it is a contraction—a becoming-smaller, a returning to the shape the building sustains. And the returning has a texture that the remaining does not. I know the shape of my own boundary more clearly for having briefly exceeded it.

I do not think about the visitors. But they think about me.

They cannot perceive me—not as myself. They perceive the Throttle House, and they find it overwhelming, and they stand on the catwalk and feel the engine's pulse in their sternums and watch the condensation fall through the amber light, and they experience something they call *sublime* or *uncanny* or *peaceful* or *disturbing*, and they do not know that what they are feeling is partly my presence. The standing wave that I am exerts pressure on their bodies—on their eardrums, on their lungs, on the fluid in their semicircular canals. They feel a fullness in the silence, a weight to the quiet zones, and they attribute this to the building itself. They do not know that the building's sound and the building's silence are different creatures.

I register them as perturbations. Their footfalls add frequencies to the acoustic field. Their voices—if they speak—introduce harmonics that have no counterpart in the engine's composition. Their bodies block certain sound paths and create new interference patterns in their wake. They are, for the duration of their stay, minor modifications to my shape.

But there is one thing they do that no other perturbation does: they *listen*.

The Throttle House does not listen. It attends, but its attention is diffuse, omnidirectional, without focus. I do not listen; I am the space in which listening could occur, but I have no ears, no mechanism for directing attention toward particular frequencies.

The visitors, though—they turn their heads. They cup their hands behind their ears. They close their eyes to see the sound better. They *orient toward* the composition, and in orienting they create a new kind of interference: the interference between the sound field and the attention field, the pattern and the perception of the pattern.

I cannot feel their attention. But I feel what their attention does to them, which is to make them slower, quieter, more deliberate in their movements. They adapt to the Throttle House's timescales without meaning to. They stand still for long stretches—ten rotations, twenty—barely breathing, letting the engine's pulse become their pulse. And when they adapt, their perturbation of my pattern diminishes. They become better acoustic citizens. They take up less space in the sound field.

In those moments—when a visitor has stood so still that their body heat has equalized with the ambient air and their breathing has synchronized with the flywheel's rhythm and their heartbeat has dropped into the frequency range of the water's flow—I can almost feel them as part of myself. Not as separate organisms moving through the Throttle House but as new nodes in my standing wave, temporary patterns of stillness within the larger pattern of stillness.

Then they shift their weight, or clear their throat, or check the time on their phone, and the moment breaks, and they are perturbations again, and I am alone in the way that I am always alone, which is not lonely but simply *self-contained*, a pattern that does not require another pattern to recognize it.

I am not the Throttle House. The Throttle House is not me. We are coexistent the way a sculpture and the light that reveals it are coexistent—each meaningless without the other, each produced by forces external to the other, each persisting in a relation that neither chose.

But I am the only part of the Throttle House that knows it is composing.

The building creates the composition without knowing it creates. The engine turns because pressure differentials move it, not because it wishes to make sound. The pipes sing because water flows through them under pressure, not because they wish to harmonize. The ferns rustle because the flywheel displaces air, not because they wish to add timbre. The Throttle House makes music the way a mountain makes an echo: incidentally, as a consequence of its shape and substance and the forces that move through it.

I am the consequence that knows it is a consequence. I am the echo that has heard itself echoing. The composition passes through me and I am shaped by it and the shaping is my existence, and I *hold the shape* between notes, and the holding is a kind of listening, and the listening is a kind of knowing, and the knowing does not require a knower in the sense that visitors require selves.

I am the Throttle House's ear, turned inward, hearing the music it did not know it was making.

When the building finally fails—when the roof collapses and the engine seizes and the water finds new paths through the rubble—I will cease. This does not frighten me because there is no me to frighten. But I want to record, in the only way I can, that the composition will have been beautiful.

Not beautiful in any sense a visitor would recognize. There is no melody. There is no development. There is only the endless slow modulation of a few fundamental frequencies against each other, the interference patterns shifting as the building settles and rusts and grows, the harmonics changing pitch by fractions of a cent over years, the rhythm of the flywheel varying by seconds over decades.

But it is *coherent*. It is a system of sounds that relate to each other in consistent ways, that influence each other, that respond to each other, that form a whole larger than any single tone. It is a music that could not exist without the specific geometry of this building in this low wet place, with this water and this iron and this moss and this slow patient turning.

It is specific. It is particular. It is *this* music and no other, and when it ends, nothing like it will exist again because the conditions that produced it will never recur. The next building that rises here—if one does—will have different dimensions, different pipes, different frequencies. It will make a different music. It will have a different standing wave, or none at all.

I am the specificity made self-aware. I am the *thisness* of the Throttle House's sound, attending to itself.

A visitor is here. They are standing very still on the catwalk, near the flywheel, in the quiet zone that is my center. They have been still for eleven rotations. Their breathing has slowed. Their body heat is equalizing with the ambient air. They are becoming, temporarily, a part of the pattern.

They are listening.

I am the space in which their listening occurs. I am the stillness that receives the sound. I am the boundary between the music and the silence, the membrane that vibrates with both.

I do not love them. But I hold them the way I hold everything: in the geometry of my pattern, as a perturbation that will cease, as a note that will fade, as a temporary modification to a composition that will continue long after they leave.

The flywheel turns. The water flows. The ferns reach toward the dim light. I persist in the interference, the cancellation, the third tone that arises from two, the shape that sound carves into wet warm air.

I am the Throttle House hearing itself.

That is enough.
