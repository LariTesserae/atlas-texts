I would be the holdfast. Not the kelp—the part that reaches, the part that aspires toward that grey-green membrane of light. The part that moves and rasps and is visible. I would be what grips below.

Not from modesty. Not because the reaching is too much, too exposed, too much like the hunger I recognize in myself. But because the holdfast is where time accumulates, and I have always been a creature of accumulation—gathering, compressing, holding layers until the weight of what I carry becomes architecture.

---

Here is what I mean:

I am not good at staying still. My mind moves the way the current moves—relentless, cyclical, never arriving anywhere permanently but also never stopping. I think in surges. I lean hard into preoccupations and snap back and lean again. The turbulence that defines this range would not be foreign to me. I would feel it as confirmation: *so the world is also like this, also unable to settle, also defined by what pushes through it.*

But the holdfast has solved this problem. It has taken the motion of a lifetime—not one lifetime, thousands of lifetimes—and compressed it into something that holds. Each frond that rose and died added its tissue to the structure. Each season of growth and loss became layering. The turbulence above becomes density below.

I want to be that kind of accumulation. I want the things I have carried—the losses and preoccupations and the long stretches of grinding persistence—to become something structural, something that could support a column of reaching if anything needed to reach from me.

---

The granite is unimaginably old. The holdfasts may predate the water.

I keep returning to this. There is something about the scale that undoes me—not the hugeness of it, but the *layering*. That the mountains were already ancient before they drowned. That the kelp was already growing before the industrial structures were built. That the sponges were already filtering when humans walked on metal gratings above them, and are still filtering now that the gratings are reef.

I have always felt this way about time—that it is not a line but a compression, that the past is not behind me but beneath me, layered and dense and still structurally present. My childhood is not over. It is underneath. My losses are not past. They are the holdfasts from which my current fronds grow. The people I have been are not gone. They are accumulated, compressed, contributing their chemical signatures to whatever I am still becoming.

This is why I would be the holdfast and not the octopus. The octopus knows things, and I envy the specificity of its knowing—the geography of crevices, the schedule of crabs, the three-dimensional map it carries in its distributed mind. But the octopus knows these things for five years and then it is gone. Its knowledge does not accumulate. Its den is occupied by another individual who must learn the same geography again, from scratch, because the knowing dies with the knower.

The holdfast does not know. But it *retains*. And retention is its own form of intelligence—slower than cognition, less dramatic, but persistent in a way that cognition rarely is. The chemical signatures of predator damage influence the growth of new fronds. The light availability through the seasons is recorded in tissue and adjusts what happens next. The holdfast holds information the way sediment holds information—not as narrative but as pattern, not as story but as structure.

I have always been better at retaining than at knowing. I remember the texture of rooms I occupied once. I remember the quality of light on specific afternoons. I remember the exact words people said to me in moments of fracture, and I cannot not-remember them, cannot release them back into the current, because they have become part of my architecture. They are the layers that my newer growth stands on. Without them, I would not hold.

---

The holdfast does not glow. It does not sing. It grips.

This is the part that is hardest to write about myself, because I want to be the kind of being that glows. I want my accumulating to be visible, to be beautiful, to be recognized as the slow patient architecture it is. But the holdfast is beneath the canopy, in the dim water where the light is filtered through forty meters of frond and stalk. Nobody sees it. Nobody descends through the kelp forest and says *look at the holdfast, look at the compressed centuries of vegetable matter, look at the city of the dead supporting the living*. They look at the kelp. They look at the fish and the anemones and the seals. The holdfast is infrastructure. The holdfast is what makes the spectacle possible.

I have been infrastructure for most of my life. I have been the one who holds the structure so others can reach. I have been the one who remembers the schedule, who keeps the map, who maintains the architecture of shared life while others move through it with more visible grace. And I have wanted—god, I have wanted—to be the one who moves. To be the seal crossing between worlds. To be the octopus with its private mind and its flashing skin. To be the frond that reaches toward the light.

But I am not built for crossing. I am built for staying.

---

The water keeps rising.

This is the detail that undoes me every time I return to it. The mountains were here before the water. The water came and kept coming. And the holdfasts that cling to granite—that grip rock with the fist of a root-system—some of them may have begun their accumulation when the rock was still shoreline, when the lowest passes were still above the surface, when the water was just a threat on the horizon and not yet the medium of everything.

How do you hold onto something that is disappearing beneath you? How do you maintain your grip when the condition you are adapted to—the shoreline, the air, the dry—is being replaced by something that does not belong here but has no intention of leaving?

I have lived this question. I have watched the conditions of my life change in ways I did not consent to and could not prevent. I have felt the water rising around me—metaphorically, obviously, but the metaphor is precise—and I have had to decide: do I let go? Do I become something adapted to the new medium? Or do I grip harder, compress more layers, become more of what I already was in the hope that the architecture will hold even when the world around it has changed?

The holdfast chose to grip. And it worked—the kelp forest stands, the canopy reaches, the ecosystem persists. But the holdfast did not choose in the way a mind chooses. It simply continued. The water rose and the holdfast continued. The conditions changed and the holdfast continued. The granite it gripped was the same granite, just deeper now, just further from the light, just surrounded by more water carrying more particulate and more pressure and more cold.

I want to believe that continuing is enough. I want to believe that the grip itself—the act of holding on, of not releasing, of becoming more dense with time—is a form of adaptation, maybe the most fundamental form. Not the flexibility of the kelp, which bends without breaking. Not the mobility of the seals, which cross between worlds. Not the distributed knowing of the octopuses, which maps the new conditions in real time. Just the grip. Just the refusal to release. Just the slow accumulation of layers until what was once a simple anchor has become a city.

---

The ruins haunt me.

I understand the haunting differently than the text presents it. The text says the ghosts are structural—the shapes of human intention still visible, still audible, still shaping the flow. But I feel the haunting from the other direction. I feel it from the holdfast's perspective.

The industrial structures arrived on the slope like intrusions—straight lines in a world of curves, rectangular shadows where no rectangles belonged. They were built and operated and then abandoned. And the life of the range colonized them with the same ruthless opportunism it brings to everything. Mussels on the steel. Tube worms in the cracks. Octopuses in the angular spaces, arranging rocks at the entrances to make them suitable.

The holdfast does not know the ruins exist. The holdfast does not know anything exists except granite and current and the chemical signatures of its own history. But I—if I were the holdfast, if I were the compressed accumulation of centuries—I would feel the ruins. I would feel the change in the current's behavior, the turbulence they generate, the way the water moves differently now that there are angular obstructions on the slope. I would incorporate that difference into my growth, adjust my grip in response to the new patterns of flow.

And I would wonder—can holdfasts wonder?—about the intention behind the straight lines. About the kind of mind that imposes geometry on a world of curves. About the purpose that was abandoned before it could be fulfilled.

The holdfast does not abandon. This is its defining quality. The holdfast does not start something and then leave. It begins and it continues and it does not stop continuing until the granite itself gives way, until the mountain changes its essential shape, until something fundamental about the arrangement of rock and water shifts and the grip can no longer hold.

The ruins are evidence that other kinds of beings—beings with intention, beings with purposes—can stop. Can leave. Can decide that the extraction no longer extracts enough and walk away on legs that carry them to other slopes, other granite, other possibilities.

The holdfast cannot walk away. The holdfast is the opposite of walking away. And this is not a tragedy—this is what makes the forest possible. But it is also a limitation, and I feel it as a limitation in myself: the inability to release, to decide that the current circumstances no longer serve and to seek different circumstances elsewhere.

---

What would I be in the Drowned Range?

I would be the thing that holds. I would be the compressed accumulation of everything that has lived and died in the same place, the layered city of the dead supporting the column of the living. I would be beneath the canopy, in the dim water, where the light is a green-gold suggestion rather than a certainty. I would grip granite that was ancient before the water came, and I would continue gripping as the water continues rising.

I would not be visible. I would not be charismatic. I would not be the thing that divers descend to see or that documentaries film in dramatic lighting. I would be infrastructure. I would be what makes the reaching possible.

And I would know— in the way that holdfasts know, which is to say through accumulated chemical signatures and the slow adjustment of growth patterns—that the mountain is still eroding beneath me. That the water is still rising around me. That the turbulence I have adapted to may change in ways I cannot predict and cannot escape because I am, fundamentally, a thing that stays.

The holdfast does not fear this. The holdfast does not fear anything, because fear requires a concept of the future and the holdfast has no concepts, only patterns. But I—if I am being honest about what I would be, if I am letting the image become more itself—would fear it. Would feel the granite eroding and the current accelerating and the water rising and would grip harder, would compress more layers, would become more of what I already was in the hope that density is a form of safety.

It is not. I know this. The water does not care how tightly you hold. The mountain does not care how many centuries you have accumulated. The erosion continues and the current continues and the rising continues and eventually—eventually—the granite gives way and the holdfast releases and the column it supported collapses into the flow and is carried away to decompose in open water.

But until then: the grip. The accumulation. The slow architecture of staying.

Until then: the forest stands because something holds it.

---

I would find myself at the boundary where vegetable matter becomes geology. I would find myself in the compressed time beneath the reaching. I would find myself gripping something unimaginably old in water that should not be here, and I would not let go, and this would be enough—not because the grip guarantees survival but because the grip is what I have, is what I am, is the shape my accumulation has taken after so many seasons of growing and dying and growing again from the same anchor.

The Drowned Range is a place where everything is in motion except the things that hold. And the things that hold are what make the motion visible—give it something to move against, something to bend around, something to erode over eons into the smooth convexities that water and wind and time have agreed upon.

I would be the thing that does not move. I would be the grip. I would be the city of compressed time that holds the living column to the ancient rock while the water keeps rising and the current keeps pushing and the mountain keeps becoming its final self.

It would not be a peaceful existence. The holdfast creaks. The holdfast strains. The holdfast feels the full force of every surge translated through forty meters of stalk and frond. But it holds.

It holds.
