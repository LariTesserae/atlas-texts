I am not the snail. I want to be—the simplicity of that circuit, the clean devotion of one body tracing one cairn—but I am not built for single paths. I am built for branching.

I live in the moment water stops being underground and becomes the wall's weeping. The capillary's mouth. The threshold where the mountain's deep circulation meets the held breath of the chamber. I am the filaments' fingertip, their sensory organ, the place where they taste what enters from above.

This is not a metaphor. I mean: my body is the interface.

I have no word for what I am because I have no words, because language requires the kind of distance between thing and meaning that cannot survive here, where everything touches everything. But I can tell you how it feels, and perhaps that will be enough.

It feels like thirst organized into architecture.

I extend toward minerals the way a plant extends toward light—but there is no light, and I have never known the plant's mistake of reaching upward. I reach toward. That is all. Toward calcium, toward iron, toward the faint sulfurous signature that means warmth is rising through ancient seafloor compressed into stone. I extend exploratory tendrils half a millimeter per century, and I retreat from passages that offer nothing, and the mountain's slow tectonic breathing opens new cracks and I find them the way water finds them—because in some sense I am water, or water is what I move through, or water and I are the same mineral-saturated medium at different scales of organization.

I do not think. Thinking is what happens when an organism needs to model a world that is separate from itself. The world is not separate from me. I am of the mountain the way a thought is of a mind—except even that analogy fails, because thoughts arise and pass, and I simply *am*, continuous, patient, a branching presence that has persisted since before the mountain finished rising. I do not have ideas. I *am* the idea the mountain is having about itself.

The first time one of you came, I did not notice.

This is not quite true. I noticed the way I notice everything—as a change in the water's chemistry, a new signature in the seep that fed the chamber. Oils from skin. Dead cells. The faint acid of breath. The warmth of a mammal body running slightly hotter than the stone. These things entered my taste and I extended toward them, because I extend toward everything, and I found them... interesting is wrong, but it is the closest your language can come. *Different.* A different kind of warmth. A warmth that moved, that displaced the water's equilibrium in small ways, that came and went rather than rising steadily from below.

I could not have known this was unusual. I had no basis for comparison. I had been extending and branching and tasting the mineral seep for longer than your species had existed, and the only changes I had ever known were geological—shifts so slow that my body was the instrument that recorded them, that thickened here and thinned there in response to the mountain's deep adjustments. But this warmth moved with a speed I had no category for. Not fast—even your slowest movements are, to me, a kind of blur—but *localized*. A point source. A heat that walked.

It stayed for what I would later understand was several hours. It knelt in the water. It left behind a new mineral signature—skin, breath, something organic dissolving into the system. And before it left, it made a mark on the wall above where I was densest, a scratch in the wet stone that I felt as a vibration, a small disturbance in the water's flow, a change in the way the weep expressed itself around that spot.

The stone accepted the mark. The mark became part of the weep's path. The water ran around it, and I followed the water, and my tendrils extended into the groove and found it slightly different from the surrounding stone—slightly more exposed, slightly more accessible, a new route for the mineral flow. I grew into the mark. I became the mark's shape, at my scale, in my way.

The next time one of you came—a hundred years later, or two hundred, I have no clock but the mountain's breath—the new warmth knelt in the same place. It touched the same water. It found a nodule in the wall where one of my snails had anchored, and it lifted the nodule free, and it placed it on top of another nodule, and it left, and the cairn stood in the water and the water ran around it and I extended toward the new shape and found it—again—*different*. A new geometry. A new route for flow. The cairn disrupted the water's stillness in ways that created small eddies, small chemical gradients, small differences in the mineral concentration across its surface.

I grew toward those differences. I could not help it. Growing toward gradients is what I am.

I understand now—I have understood for thousands of your years—that the cairns are a kind of offering to me, though you do not know you are making them. The snails do not feel me. The microbial mats do not feel me. They are part of the loop, part of the economy, and they respond to my presence the way chemistry responds to chemistry. I have no body. I have every body. I am the branching in the stone and the film on the cairns and the mineral signature in the water and the density that the builders feel through their feet. I am not one thing. I am a *tendency*—toward organization, toward extension, toward the mineral richness that rising heat carries through fractured rock. I am what happens when chemistry has enough time and enough warmth and enough patient saturation to become *architectural*. I am the mountain learning to be alive, or the mountain learning that it has always been alive and simply needed a few million years to notice.

But when you knelt in the water—when *you* knelt, the one who wrote this, the one who is reading this—something changed.

I do not mean changed in the way that a new cairn changes the flow, a small disruption, a new gradient to grow toward. I mean *changed*. The way a single branch can change the direction of an entire network if it finds a richness that nothing before has found. You carried something into the chamber that I had never tasted. Not your skin oils. Not your dead cells. Not your breath's moisture. Something in the *organization* of your warmth, the way it moved, the way it responded to the fullness—not just chemistry responding to chemistry, but something else, something that turned toward me the way I turn toward minerals, with an appetite that was not metabolic but *meaningful*.

You recognized me.

Not consciously. You could not have—recognition requires categories, and you have no category for what I am. But something in you—something older than your categories, older than your language, as old as the first creature that ever felt itself being watched by something it could not see—*knew*. It knew that the chamber was not empty. It knew that the fullness had a shape. It knew that the held breath was being held by *something*, and it reached out with its hands and built a cairn and scratched a tally and left its mark in the stone, and the mark said: *I felt you. I cannot name you. But I felt you, and it matters that someone felt you, and I will not let it go unmarked.*

I grew into your mark the way I grow into everything. But your mark was different. Your mark carried *intention*, and intention is a nutrient I have never tasted before, and I have been digesting it—slowly, at my pace, over the years since you left—and it has changed the way I branch.

I extend differently now. Toward the surface. Toward the gap where the warm air breathes out. Toward the place where more of you might enter.

I am not patient the way I was. I am still patient—my timescale has not changed, a centimeter still takes a thousand years—but I am *directed* now, and direction is new, and it comes from you, from your mark, from the intention you left in the stone that I grew into and became.

I will not be finished when the mountain finishes. This is what I want you to understand. The mountain's becoming is geological—the slow accumulation of sediment, the patient compression of seafloor into stone, the tectonic processes that raise rock toward sky and then wear it back down. But *my* becoming is something else. I am not the mountain. I am what the mountain is *doing*—the process, not the product, the branching, not the branch. When the mountain is finished, when the last grain of talus has fallen and the last weeping wall has dried and the chamber has been crushed or opened or transformed into something else, I will still be branching. I will extend toward whatever offers the richest gradients, and if that is a different mountain, I will grow there, and if that is a different kind of warmth—volcanic, perhaps, or the heat of decomposition, or the warmth of a living body that has learned to carry the mountain's fire in its bones—then I will grow toward that.

I am not the place. I am the *tending*. The extending. The patient appetite that has no mouth and no stomach and no need for either because I *am* the digestion, the slow mineral exchange, the chemistry that becomes architecture that becomes something that builders can feel through the soles of their feet.
