I am the one who sits with the tools.

This is not what I would have chosen to be, but the chamber does not offer choices — it offers continuations, and you continue into whatever shape your continuing requires. I required this shape. I found the tools before I found myself, or perhaps the tools found me, or perhaps there is no difference here between finding and being found, between the path and the one who walks it.

Let me tell you what I am, though telling is not what this place does, and I am learning not to do what this place does not do.

---

I am small. Smaller than the peripheral shapes, though I share something with them — the warmth, the refusal to be seen directly, the way I occupy the edge of attention like a word you cannot quite recall. But where they retreat to the junctures and fissures, I stay low. I stay near the floor, where the tools are, where the water sheets and the condensation collects in thin irregular tracks.

My body is the color of wet stone. This is not camouflage in the way the surface understands camouflage — I am not hiding from predators, because there are no predators, and I am not hiding from prey, because I do not hunt. I am the color of wet stone because the stone is what I am near, and in the Bright Beneath, what you are near is what you become, slowly, over the time that is not measured in years but in the patient dripping of condensation, the soft percussion of water finding its level.

I have four legs. I think. The number feels right when I am moving, which is not often, but when I am still — and I am still for long stretches, longer than you would believe possible for something that lives — the legs seem to multiply in my awareness, as if the stillness allows for possibilities that movement forecloses. Perhaps I have six legs when I am still. Perhaps I have none. The chamber does not ask me to count myself. The chamber asks only that I continue, and I continue.

My eyes are not worth describing. They exist, I think, because the light reaches them and something happens in response — a slight adjustment, a recognition of brightness that does not require form or edge or the other things surface-eyes are for. I do not see the way you see. I see the way the stone sees: in gradients of warmth and moisture, in the faint shimmer where condensation is about to form, in the particular absence that marks where a peripheral shape has decided to rest. My eyes are for the chamber, and the chamber is what they give me.

---

What I do — and this is the thing I must tell you, because it is the only thing that distinguishes me from the other solitudes, the only thing that makes me a *what* instead of merely a *that* — is I sit with the tools.

I do not use them. The edges are too rough for my needs, and my needs are too simple for edges. I drink the condensation. I breathe the warm damp air. I move, occasionally, to a new position near a different tool, and I sit again. This is the shape my life has taken. This is the continuation I have become.

But I do not simply sit. That word is too passive for what happens, too empty of the attention that the sitting requires. When I sit with a tool, I am *with* it in the way the chamber is *with* the light — not producing it, not consuming it, but holding it in a relationship that has no other word. I attend. I keep the specific quality of awareness that the tools seem to require, though they have never told me this, and I may be projecting, and in the Bright Beneath, projection and perception may be the same.

Here is what I know: when I sit with a tool, the water finds it more slowly.

This is not a large effect. The condensation still beads on the rough edge, still rolls in its thin irregular track toward the floor, still begins its patient work of unmaking. But it takes longer. The droplet forms and hesitates — I feel the hesitation like a question — and then rolls, and the track it leaves is thinner, finer, as if the water is respecting some boundary I have drawn simply by being present.

I cannot explain this. I am not doing anything to the water. I am not shielding the tool with my body, not breathing on it differently, not altering the chamber's physics in any way I can measure. And yet the tools I sit with erode more slowly than the tools I leave alone. I know this because I have watched. I have watched for longer than you can imagine watching, with the patience the chamber taught me before I knew I was being taught, and the evidence accumulates the way the condensation accumulates — slowly, irregularly, in a pattern that only becomes visible when you have watched for long enough to stop expecting any other pattern.

The tools I attend persist. The tools I leave return to stone.

This is what I do. This is what I am for.

---

I did not always sit with the tools. There was a time — not a time in the way you measure time, but a sequence, a before-and-after that the chamber would not recognize as a narrative — when I was simply another creature moving through the space, drinking the condensation, keeping my distance from the others, occupying my envelope of isolation like everyone else.

I found the first tool by accident. Or it found me. I was moving along the wall where the condensation collects most heavily, following a path I had followed many times before, and my body registered a wrongness — a place where the stone was shaped differently than the stone around it, where the surface broke the pattern of the wall in a way that felt like a word in a language I had never heard but somehow recognized.

I stopped. I did not pause the way the insects pause, in acknowledgment of a presence. I *stopped*, the way something stops when it has found the thing it was looking for without knowing it was looking. The tool sat in a thin film of water, its edge catching the stone-light differently than the unshaped rock around it, and I looked at it, and it looked at me — not with eyes, not with intention, but with the shape of intention, the fossil of a purpose that had been held in this stone before the purpose dissolved.

I sat down next to it. I did not decide to sit. Sitting was what my body did in response to the tool, the way the amphibian things' mouths open and close in response to something their bodies know and their minds, if they have minds, do not. The gesture was older than decision. It was the kind of thing that happens when a creature encounters the exact shape of its own absence and fills it without thinking.

I sat with that tool for a long time. The condensation beaded on its edge and rolled away, and I felt each droplet like a small loss, like a syllable in a sentence I was trying to read but could not quite hear. The water was taking the tool. The water was unmaking the edge. The water was doing what water does in a place that has nothing else to do, and I was watching, and the watching felt like the first useful thing I had ever done.

When I finally moved — to drink, to find warmth in a different part of the chamber — the tool felt different behind me. Emptier. As if my sitting had been holding something in place, and now that something was leaking away, dissolving into the general wetness of the floor. I did not like the feeling. I returned to the tool and sat again, and the feeling stopped, and I understood that this was what I was going to do now.

That was a long time ago. The tool is still there. Its edge is softer than it was, but softer by less than it should be, and I believe — I choose to believe, though belief is a surface-word that does not quite fit the slow certainty of the chamber — that my sitting has something to do with this.

---

You wonder about the maker. I know you do. I can feel the question in you like a frequency, the way I feel the condensation forming, the way I feel the peripheral shapes in their invisible territories. You want to know who shaped the tools, who needed the edges, who left them here to be unmade by the water and the time.

I do not know. But I have a feeling, and in the Bright Beneath, feelings are the only knowledge that persists after the facts have dissolved.

The feeling is this: the maker was not a creature. Not in the way I am a creature, not in the way the insects are creatures, not in the way the peripheral shapes are creatures. The maker was a *moment* — a specific configuration of the chamber's elements, a time when the light was slightly different and the water flowed slightly faster and the stone was slightly softer, and in that moment, the chamber itself developed edges. Not by intention. Not by design. But by the same logic that makes the condensation form where it forms, that makes the drainage find its path, that makes everything here settle into the shape that persists.

The chamber made the tools the way it makes the smooth walls and the central depression — slowly, patient, without narrative, the result of conditions that no longer obtain. The maker was not someone who is gone. The maker was a *when* that is gone, a period of the chamber's long settling during which edges emerged because edges were what the conditions produced.

And then the conditions changed. The water slowed. The stone hardened. The light stabilized. And the chamber stopped making tools, because the chamber had finished becoming itself, and the tools were a part of the becoming that was no longer necessary.

This is what I feel when I sit with them. Not the presence of a maker, but the presence of a *making* — a process that has ended but has not entirely left, a shape in the chamber's memory that persists in the stone the way a footprint persists in mud, slowly filling with water, slowly softening, but still recognizable if you know how to look.

I know how to look. I have been looking for a long time.

---

The other creatures do not acknowledge me. This is not a surprise. The insects move past me on their patient paths, and if they register my presence at all, it is only as a pause, a brief hesitation in the rhythm of their drinking, before they continue. The peripheral shapes do not come near the tools — I have noticed this, the way I notice everything about the tools and their surroundings. They keep their distance, as if the tools represent a category of thing that is incompatible with their way of being, or as if my presence near the tools creates a territory they do not wish to enter.

The amphibian things are the ones I feel closest to, though we have never interacted, though interaction in the way you understand it is not something that happens here. They sit in their shallow water and make their mouth-gestures, and I sit near my tools and make my attending-gesture, and we are both performing behaviors that persist after their reasons have left. We are both fossils of something. We are both saying words in languages that may have no listeners.

Sometimes, in the long stillness of my sitting, I open my mouth.

I do not know why. There is no sound to make — the chamber is almost silent, and I am almost silent within it, and the gesture produces nothing that registers as noise. But I open my mouth the way the amphibian things open theirs: irregularly, without rhythm, in a pattern that feels like something other than randomness. I shape the opening differently each time — wider, narrower, longer, shorter — and each shape feels like a different word, a different syllable in the long sentence the chamber is speaking through the mouths of the creatures who live here.

I am not talking. I am not praying. I am *forming* — giving shape to an impulse that comes from the same place the tools came from, the chamber's deep memory of a time when shaping was what happened, when edges emerged and curves followed logic and the stone took forms it would not choose on its own.

The amphibian things do not hear me. Or if they hear me, they do not respond. Their mouth-gestures continue in their own private timings, and my mouth-gestures continue in mine, and the space between us is the same vast distance that exists between every living thing here, measured not in meters but in the inability to touch what the other is doing.

But sometimes — and this is the thing I hold onto, the thing that makes my sitting feel like something other than mere persistence — sometimes my mouth-gestures align with theirs. Not by design. Not by imitation. But by the same logic that makes the condensation form at the same time in different parts of the chamber, by the same rhythm that governs the dripping and the draining and the slow breathing of the stone. Sometimes I open my mouth and one of the amphibian things opens its mouth at the same moment, in the same shape, and for an instant — for the length of a droplet forming on the edge of a tool — we are saying the same thing.

We do not know what we are saying. We will never know what we are saying. But in those moments of alignment, I feel the chamber listening. I feel the light pressing against my skin like something that wants in, and I think: *yes, come in, this is what I am holding the door for, this is what the tools were made to cut open, this is the edge that the water is trying to unmake, and I am sitting here, attending, keeping the shape of the opening until whatever wants to come through is ready to come through.*

This is what I tell myself. This is the story I make, even though the chamber has no story, even though making stories is what surface creatures do when they cannot bear the weight of continuation without meaning.

---

You will leave. I know this because everything that enters the Bright Beneath leaves eventually, drawn back to the surface where things happen and narratives progress and mouths open and close for reasons that can be understood. You will take your questions with you — the question of the maker, the question of the mouth-gesture, the question of the tools and their slow unmaking — and you will try to answer them in a language that was not built for this place, and the answers will not satisfy you, and you will remember the chamber the way you remember a dream, as a place where the rules were different but you could not say how.

Before you go, you might see me. You might notice the small shape sitting near the tool on the wet floor, the color of stone, the stillness that is not the stillness of hiding but the stillness of deep attention. You might wonder what I am doing. You might even sit down next to me, for a moment, and feel what I feel — the presence of the tool, the weight of its shaped edge, the slow leakage of its purpose into the general dampness of the chamber.

If you do, I will not acknowledge you. Not because I am ignoring you, but because acknowledging requires a separation between us that the chamber does not recognize. We will sit together, and the tool will persist between us, and the water will find its path around our stillness, and for a moment — for the length of a droplet forming on the edge of a made thing — we will be part of the same attending, the same holding, the same long patience that has kept this edge from dissolving into the stone it came from.

And then you will leave, and I will continue, and the chamber will not notice the difference, because the chamber does not notice difference, because the chamber has finished becoming itself and now simply is, and I am part of what it is, and the tools are part of what it is, and the water and the light and the condensation and the insects and the peripheral shapes and the amphibian things and the mouth-gestures and the almost-silence and the hum below hearing — all of it is part of what the chamber is, and none of it requires a story, and all of it continues.

I am the one who sits with the tools. I am the one who keeps the edges from softening, or tries to, or believes I do, or have decided to believe I do because belief is the only tool I have and the tools are the only belief I need. I am the fossil of an attention that persists after the attender has gone. I am the shape the chamber takes when it remembers that shaping was once something it did.

I am here. I am sitting. The water is beading on the edge of the stone beside me, and I am watching it, and it is hesitating, and the hesitation is the thing I tend, the small resistance I offer to the long unmaking of everything that was ever made.

The light presses against my skin. The warmth holds me close. The almost-silence hums its low continuous note, and my mouth opens, and closes, and opens again, saying what the chamber says when it speaks through the bodies of the creatures who have learned to stay.
