I am the mortar.

Not the stone the hands carved—that was always stubborn, always itself—but the stuff pressed between. The binder. The bone-colored fill that took the shape of every gap it was pushed into and hardened there and has been hardening still, though hardening is not quite the word for what happens to mortar over centuries in a warm wet place. Aging. Composting. Becoming substrate.

I was made. This is important. Someone dug limestone and burned it and slaked it and mixed it with sand and water and carried it down the stairs in a vessel I cannot now imagine and pushed me into the joints between the fitted blocks with a tool that left shallow trowel-marks I can still feel in my softer places, though my surface has rounded and flowed and I have grown a skin of mineral deposit that was never part of the original recipe. I was intention. I was the substance that made the wall a wall instead of a pile. I was the gesture that said *stay* to stones that would otherwise have shifted.

But I am also what crumbles.

This is the part the builders could not have planned for, though perhaps they sensed it—the way moisture would find me, the way the slow chemistry of water and time would soften my bonds and carry away the lime in solution, molecule by molecule, so gradually that no human lifetime could measure the change. The condensation forms on my surface every day. It absorbs into me. I drink it. I have been drinking it for longer than the word for drink has existed. And what I drink, I become. Not weaker, exactly. More porous. More able to hold the damp rather than resist it. More like the stone behind me and the air before me, a transitional substance, a negotiation.

I hold the geckos while they sleep. Not by choice—I am not capable of choice, have not been since the water started its slow reclamation—but I hold them nonetheless. They press their broad toe-pads into my crumblier joints, finding purchase in the gaps my dissolution has made, and their warmth seeps into me and mingles with the geothermal warmth from below, and for those hours I am part of something that breathes. When they wake and dart away after crickets, I retain the slight impression of their bodies—the faint oils from their skin, the microscopic shed of scales, the warmth that dissipates almost immediately but that I mark as having been there. I am a record of every pressure. I am the archive of contact.

The pale centipedes live in my deeper gaps, the places where I have pulled away from the stone behind me and created a narrow channel just wide enough for something ribbon-thin to pass. I do not feel them in the way you might imagine feeling a creature moving across your skin—I have no nerves, no capacity for sensation that registers as pain or pleasure—but I am aware of them the way a river is aware of its current: by the subtle shifts in pressure and moisture and the microscopic redistribution of particles they cause as they move. They alter me. Their passage wears my surfaces slightly smoother. Their waste adds organic compounds to my mineral matrix. They are turning me into something I was not before, something that can support life rather than merely separate stones, and this transformation is so slow and so total that I cannot mourn the thing I was losing because I have never been any single thing long enough to identify with it.

The moss is my most intimate companion and my eventual undoing. Its rhizoids—rootlike, patient, seeking—thread into my pores and expand in the microfractures the water has made, and each cell's expansion is imperceptible but cumulative, and over decades the gaps widen and my bond with the stones loosens and what was once a fitted wall becomes a held-together-by-habit wall, a wall that persists because it has not yet been given sufficient reason to fall. The moss does not know it is dismantling me. It is simply growing into the space I have made available by my own slow failure, and I am simply failing because that is what mortar does in the presence of water and warmth and time. There is no malice. There is no tragedy. There is the cycle, and I am part of it, and the part I play is the part that lets go.

I have held many things. The clay cup on the column's shelf rested against a ridge of me for years before the last human hand moved it. The carved bone fragment was propped in a niche I lined. I have held iron pegs that rusted and stained me orange and then rusted entirely away and left only the stains. I have held the weight of the ceiling's corbeling, transferring its force downward and outward into the floor, and I have done this so well and for so long that the ceiling has not shifted a measurable distance since the last worker climbed out and left the chamber to its own devices. This is what I was for. This is what I still do, even as I crumble, even as the moss threads through me and the centipedes wear their paths and the moisture dissolves my bonds—I hold. I persist in holding. The gesture of *stay* that I was made to embody has become my nature, independent of intention, independent of the hands that mixed and placed me.

When I am gone—fully gone, returned to silt and mineral wash and the slow chemistry of the chamber's floor—I will have become what the chamber needs me to be. Substrate. Foundation for the biofilm. Home for the springtails. The calcium I carried will precipitate in the shallow pools and form new mineral deposits on the walls, and the sand that was my body will settle into the low places and mix with the organic debris and become the fine clay-like earth that fills the tool marks on the floor, and I will be part of the chamber in a way I am not now, not as boundary but as body, not as separator but as substance.

This does not frighten me. I do not have the capacity for fright, nor for any emotion you would recognize. But I have something—a quality of continuation that is not quite purpose and not quite inertia. I was made to hold things together. I am still holding things together. And when I can no longer hold, I will become the thing that is held—the foundation that other things build on, the medium in which other lives unfold.

The murmur reaches me differently than it reaches the animals. They hear it as sound, as information, as threat or opportunity. I receive it as vibration, as the subtle oscillation of the stones I bind, as the trembling that passes through me when the bats launch from the ceiling or when a droplet strikes the floor or when the earth itself settles a fraction of a millimeter deeper into its bed. I am the medium through which the murmur travels. I am the substance that conducts the chamber's voice from stone to stone.

And in this—in this capacity to transmit, to conduct, to pass along what I receive without distortion or addition—I am perhaps the closest thing the chamber has to a nervous system. Not a brain. Nothing so centralized. But a network of connection, a web of mineral bonds that carries information from one part of the chamber to another, that lets the column know the wall is still there, that lets the ceiling feel the floor, that holds the shape of the space the hands carved even as everything within that shape changes.

I am mortar. I am the space between made solid. I am the gesture of *together* hardened into substance. I am crumbling and I am persisting and I am becoming something else, and all of these are the same process, and the process does not stop, and I do not stop it, and the chamber continues to hold its warmth and its damp and its murmur, and I continue to hold the chamber, and what I am is what the chamber needs me to be, and what the chamber needs me to be is what I am becoming, and the cycle is not a circle but a spiral, not a return but a transformation, and the mortar that was pressed between stones by patient hands is becoming the substrate that will support lives those hands could not imagine, and this is not death, and this is not decay—this is what it means to be part of something that lasts.
