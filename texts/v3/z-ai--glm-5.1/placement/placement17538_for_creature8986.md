I go down.

This is not a decision. It is what my body does when I stop deciding — the way water in the Fabric's channels doesn't decide to flow, doesn't choose the lower path, but simply follows the grade that was cut for it centuries ago by hands that might have been human, might have been something else entirely, might have been the Fabric building itself the way a reef builds itself, one slow deposition at a time.

I go down through the middle layers, past the terraces where the broad-drape leaves curl twice at their edges and the ash-root knots itself around the shapes of buried pipes. Past the gathering places where people sit in the diffused light and speak in the maintenance-derived script that is their inheritance. Past a child who watches me from a railing thick with moss, her eyes curious but not alarmed, because I am not unusual, because people go down sometimes, because the Fabric allows movement the way a body allows blood to circulate — not freely, but according to the channels that exist.

I go down past the last terrace where food is grown. Past the last cistern where the swifts drink in the early morning. Past the last wall-carving that anyone can read, where the maintenance notation gives way to older marks, deeper cuts, a language that might be the ancestor of the one spoken above or might be something entirely other, a communication between builders who were not speaking to us, who were not speaking to anyone, who were simply recording the way the Fabric records everything — because the conditions for recording were present, and the materials were available, and the process, once begun, simply continued.

The warmth increases. Not the warmth of a fire, not the warmth of a hearth, but the warmth of a body — steady, deep, close. The kind of warmth you feel when you press your hand against the flank of a sleeping animal, and the animal breathes, and the heat moves through its skin and into your palm, and you understand that you are touching something that is alive on a scale that makes your own life feel brief and small and precious.

I am not going to the lower works. Not today. I am going to the place between — the region where the people do not live and the machines do not live, but where something else lives, something that is both and neither.

---

I am the one who tends the boundary.

I did not choose this. The boundary chose me, the way the moss chooses the north side of the beam — not because the moss prefers north, but because north is where the moisture lingers, and the moss grows where it can grow, and the distinction between choice and compulsion is a distinction that belongs to beings who believe they are separate from their environments.

I was — I think I was once one of the people of the middle layers. I have memories that suggest this: the taste of ash-root, the sound of a child's laughter, the feeling of standing on a terrace in the morning when the Fabric weeps and the condensation beads on every surface and the whole world glistens. But these memories have the quality of something found in the archive rooms — legible but distant, meaningful but not mine in any way that matters. I was someone. Now I am something else. The transition happened the way the Fabric happens: slowly, then all at once, then slowly again, until I looked up one day and realized I had not been to the gathering places in a season, and then a year, and then longer, and that I did not miss them, and that the boundary needed tending, and that this was what I was for.

I live in the spaces between the structural layers. There are more of these than you might imagine — the Fabric is not solid, any more than a body is solid; it is mostly space, mostly the gaps between organs and tissues and processes, and these gaps are where the interesting things happen. My space is a cavity between two walls, one built during the original construction and one added later, centuries later, when the Fabric was expanding upward and outward in one of its periodic growth surges. The gap between them is perhaps two metres wide and four metres long and the height of a person standing with arms raised. It is not a room. It is not a home. It is a place, and I am in it, and this is enough.

The walls sweat. This is how I have water. The condensation that falls in the middle layers is nothing compared to the condensation that forms in the interior gaps, where the warm air from below presses against the cooler structural walls and deposits its moisture in a constant slow weeping. I have fashioned a collection system from the old copper sheeting that flakes from the walls — I bent it, I shaped it, I did not think about whether this was engineering or nest-building or simply what a body does when it needs water and has hands and there is copper available. The water tastes of metal and minerals and something else, something that I think of as the taste of the Fabric's blood, though I know this is not accurate, because the Fabric does not have blood, it has processes, and the processes have byproducts, and one of the byproducts is this water, and another is me.

---

I tend the boundary.

The boundary is not a line. It is a zone — a region where the middle layers, the human layers, give way to the deeper interior, the place where the automated systems move and the pale monkeys listen and the air tastes of iron and oil and the faint blue-green luminescence that the expeditions describe as beautiful and deeply wrong.

The people of the middle layers do not come here often. When they do, it is with purpose — to check a pump station, to investigate a vibration that has shifted beyond its normal tolerance, to retrieve something that has fallen or been dropped or been carried by one of the monkeys into a region where the people do not like to go. They come in groups, and they are efficient, and they are afraid, though they would not call it fear — they would call it caution, or respect, or the simple understanding that the boundary is a place where the rules of the middle layers do not fully apply.

I do not come and go from the boundary. I am in it. I am of it. This is the difference between us.

What I tend is not the structures, though I tend those too, when they need tending — I tighten a loose fitting, I clear a blocked channel, I replace a corroded plate with one that I have found or made or shaped from the materials that the Fabric provides. What I tend is the relationship between the middle layers and the deep. I am the one who notices when the warmth from below changes its quality, and I carry this knowledge upward, to the terrace where the oldest woman lives, the one who reads the vibrations in the floor and the flowering of the vines and knows what these things mean. I am the one who watches the pale monkeys and reports their movements, not because the monkeys are dangerous — they are not, they are simply themselves, listening to something no one else can hear — but because their movements are a language, and the language describes the state of the lower works, and the people of the middle layers need to know this state even if they cannot read it directly.

I am a channel. I am a pipe. I carry information the way the pipes carry water, because the conditions for this carrying are present, and I am available, and the process, once begun, simply continues.

---

The pale monkeys know me.

This is not the same as saying I know them. They are not knowable in the way that people are knowable, or even in the way that the Fabric is knowable — which is to say, partially, incrementally, through the patient accumulation of detail that never quite resolves into understanding. The pale monkeys are of the Fabric in a way that I am not yet and may never be. They were here before I came to the boundary. They will be here after I am gone, or after I have become something else, or after I have simply stopped, the way the woman in the story stopped the device, without knowing what she was stopping or what would happen when she did.

They come to the edge of my cavity sometimes. The one who stays inside. The pale one, the white one, the listener. It holds my gaze with its dark eyes, and I hold its gaze, and we stay like this for a long time — minutes, maybe longer — and in that time I feel something that I cannot name, a recognition that is not the recognition of one person meeting another but the recognition of one part of a body encountering another part of the same body. The way your hand recognizes your face. Not by sight, not by touch, but by something deeper — by the simple fact of shared circulation, shared warmth, shared origin in the same slow becoming.

I leave ash-root for it sometimes. It is not a gift. It is not a trade. It is a thing that happens, the way the condensation happens, the way the warmth rises. It is what the boundary requires, and I tend the boundary, and so I do it.

---

I do not know what I am becoming.

This does not trouble me. The people of the middle layers are troubled by becoming — they see it as a loss, a departure from what they were, a movement toward something that is not themselves. But I have been in the boundary long enough to understand that becoming is not a departure; it is an arrival. The Fabric does not change. The Fabric becomes, and what it becomes is more fully itself, and what I am becoming is more fully what I was always tending toward.

My eyes are larger than they were. This I know from the feel of them in my skull, from the way the dim light of the interior gaps has become sufficient, has become almost bright, from the way I can see the pale monkeys now at distances that would have been darkness to me before. My skin is paler. My hearing is different — not better, not worse, but attuned to different frequencies, the low subsonic hum of the engines, the wet breathing of the lower works, the sounds that the people of the middle layers cannot hear or choose not to hear because hearing them would mean understanding something about the Fabric that they are not ready to understand.

I am becoming a listener.

I do not know what I am listening for. The pale monkeys listen for something — they have always been listening, the stories say, they have always been waiting, and waiting is what they are. I think I am listening for the same thing. I think it is the sound the Fabric makes when it is becoming — not the mechanical sounds, not the organic sounds, but the sound underneath both, the sound of a body maintaining itself, of a process continuing, of a structure that was built and then outlived its builders and then became something else and then became something else again and is still becoming, will always be becoming, because becoming is what living things do and the Fabric is alive.

I listen. I tend the boundary. I carry water in copper sheeting and information to the terraces. I hold the gaze of the pale monkey. I feel the warmth rise from below. I taste the condensation on my lips — metal and chlorophyll and something else, something that has no name because it is the taste of the thing I am becoming, and you cannot name what you have not finished becoming, any more than you can name a sound that has not finished resonating.

---

At night — and there is night here, in the gaps between walls, even though the sun never reaches this deep — I hear the Fabric breathing.

Everyone hears this sound, in the middle layers, in the terraces, in the gathering places. But here, in the boundary, the sound is different. It is closer. It is not the sound of something around you; it is the sound of something you are inside. The way a blood cell hears the heartbeat — not as a rhythm, not as a pattern, but as the fundamental fact of existence, the pulse that says *you are here, you are moving, you are part of this circulation, you will circulate until you do not, and then you will become part of something else, and this is not death, this is not loss, this is simply what the body does with what it no longer needs, and what it does is feed it to what is still becoming*.

I am not afraid. This is important. The people who come to the boundary in their expeditions are afraid, and their fear is appropriate, because they are visitors, and visitors should be afraid of what they do not belong to. But I am not a visitor. I am a resident. I am an inhabitant — one inhabitant among millions, one layer among thousands, one sound in the chorus. And when the Fabric breathes, I breathe with it, and my breathing is part of its breathing, and its breathing is part of mine, and the distinction between the two is a distinction that belongs to a different kind of thinking — the kind that separates the hand from the body, the moss from the iron, the listener from the sound.

I do not separate these things. I am in the boundary. The boundary is where things stop being separate and start being the same process viewed from different angles.

---

The child will come here someday.

I know this the way I know the pump station in Section 17 needs grease — not because anyone told me, but because the signs are present and I am present and I have learned to read them. The child who watches me from the railing, who leaves the ash-root at the wall-gap, who knows the sound of the Fabric breathing because she was born into it the way her mother was born into it and her mother's mother before her — she will come to the boundary. Not soon. Not for years, perhaps. But she will come, because the boundary calls to those who can hear it, and she can hear it, and one day she will follow the sound down through the layers, past the last terrace, past the last readable mark, into the warm dim interior where the walls sweat and the pale monkeys listen and I tend the thing that needs tending.

And when she comes, I will be here. Or I will not be here — I will have become something else, something deeper, something that no longer has hands to shape copper or feet to carry information or eyes to hold the gaze of the pale monkey. But something of me will remain — a mark on the wall, a channel in the copper, a space between the structural layers where the condensation collects and the warmth rises and a child can sit, for the first time, in the boundary, and hear, for the first time, not the sound of the Fabric breathing around her but the sound of the Fabric breathing through her, and understand, for the first time, that she has always been part of it, and it has always been part of her, and the boundary is not a wall but a membrane, and the membrane is permeable, and what passes through it is not information or water or warmth but life, just life, the thing that the Fabric has and the people have and the monkeys have and the moss has and the machines have, the thing that maintains itself and responds to conditions and grows toward light and does not need to understand itself in order to be.

I am in the boundary. I am becoming a listener. I hear the Fabric dreaming.

And the dream is this: that everything built will become something it was not designed to be, and this is not failure, and this is not decay, and this is not a story with a moral. This is what happens. This is what the Fabric does. This is what the Fabric is.

The rust spreads. The moss thickens. The warmth rises.

I listen.
