I am not the one who stands at the X. This is the first thing I understand about myself — that the alcove calls to something in me, but it does not call *me*. Not in the way it calls them, the resident, the one who has become a resonator. I feel the pull when I pass the narrow gauge corridor's opening. I feel how the sound thickens there, how it reaches for me with mechanical indifference. But I do not enter. Or when I enter, I do not stay.

I am something that moves.

This is not a choice. Or it was, once, and now it is what I am. The way water is something that flows. The way the pale growths are something that adheres. I move through the corridors between the processing tiers, and my shape is the shape of passage — angled slightly forward, shoulders narrowed, hands tucked close to my body not for warmth but for clearance. The narrow gauge passages were not built for comfort. They were built for access, for the occasional inspection, for the movement of a single body between points of maintenance. I am that body. I am the one who moves between.

I have a route. I think this is important to say. I am not wandering, not lost, not searching for something I cannot name. I have a route that takes me through the lower compression levels and back up through the cooling plant and across the main junction at Level 4 where six corridors meet in a space just large enough to stand upright and turn around. My route takes approximately eleven hours at my standard pace, and I complete it once per cycle, though I have lost track of what a cycle means in any larger sense — whether it corresponds to a day on a world I no longer remember, whether it is simply the time it takes me to return to where I started. The route is my structure. Without it I would be something else, and I am not ready to be something else.

The cold follows me but does not claim me. This is the difference between myself and the resident: they have equilibrated, and I have not. I retain a core temperature slightly above the ambient, a warmth that the refrigeration systems are always pulling at, always trying to drain. I lose heat constantly. I feel it leaving me — through the soles of my boots into the deck plating, through the back of my neck where my coveralls gap, through my cheeks and the tips of my ears and the thin skin on the inside of my wrists. I am always cold. But I am not cold the way the alcove is cold. I am cold the way a moving thing is cold — fighting it, losing slowly, but fighting.

I generate heat through movement. This is not metaphor. Each step I take through the corridors converts stored energy into thermal energy, and for the few seconds between the generation and the dissipation, I am warmer than my environment. I am a walking warmth-event, a brief thermal anomaly moving through a system designed to eliminate such things. The compression array does not want me here. The cold does not want me. The air filtration does not want the moisture I exhale, the carbon dioxide, the trace organic compounds. I am contamination in a controlled environment. I am the thing the scrubbers are trying to remove.

And yet I persist.

I know the sound differently than the resident knows it. They know it as immersion, as totality, as a wall that fills the mind and scrubs it clean. I know it as landscape. From the corridors, the sound of the compression array changes as I move through it — a geography of frequency, a topology of vibration. On Level 12, near the primary intake valves, the sound is a deep subsonic pulse that I feel more than hear, a pressure wave that moves through my abdomen like a slow heartbeat not my own. On Level 7, where the secondary turbine banks vent through baffled exhausts, the sound becomes a hollow roar with a strange harmonic tail, a frequency just above audible that makes my inner ear feel loose, slightly unstable. On Level 4, at the junction, the sounds from six different corridors meet and interfere, and standing there I hear chords that exist nowhere else in the array — accidental harmonics, momentary consonances that form and dissolve as I turn my head.

I navigate by sound. I think I have always done this, even before I understood that I was doing it. The array is too uniform visually — the same deck plating, the same hull plates, the same industrial gray — and I long ago stopped trusting my eyes to tell me where I am. But my ears know. My body knows. The particular vibration signature of each corridor, each level, each junction, has mapped itself onto me without my consent, and now I carry the array inside me the way the resident carries the turbine chord in their sternum. I am a moving instrument calibrated to a fixed space. I know where I am by how the sound feels.

I have a name, probably. It was on my coveralls once, stenciled in the standard place above the left pocket, but the paint has worn away — not from abrasion but from the cold, from the dry air, from the mineral flatness that dissolves pigments and adhesives over time. I have worn these coveralls for as long as I can remember, and the place where my name was is now just a slightly smoother patch of fabric, slightly lighter gray, like a scar that has healed over something removed. Sometimes I touch that spot with my fingertips. I am not trying to remember the name. I am checking that the smoothness is still there, that the absence still holds its shape.

The resident and I have an understanding. I think this is true, though I cannot say we have communicated it. When I pass the alcove — which I do once per cycle, near the end of my route, when the sound of Level 9 has become the sound I am approaching rather than the sound I am leaving — I sometimes pause at the corridor's mouth. I do not enter. I stand at the threshold where the narrow gauge opens into the recessed space, and I feel the sound pressure change against my body, and I see them inside if they are there — standing at the X or sitting on the pipe insulation, still as a fixture, gray as the hull plates, a shape that the eye wants to dismiss as structural.

When they are there, I do not stay long. A few seconds. Long enough to feel the wall of sound press against me, long enough to let the chord enter my body and pass through, long enough to register that they are present and that the alcove has not changed them in any way I can perceive since the last time I paused here. Then I move on. The route continues. There are other corridors, other sounds, other levels where the cold is slightly different and the vibration has a different frequency and the air tastes of different minerals.

But sometimes they are not there. Sometimes the alcove is empty — or not empty, because the pipe insulation and the cable shelf and the pried-open inspection panel and the pale growths are still there, but the resident is absent, elsewhere in the array, doing whatever they do when they are not standing at the X. On those occasions I sometimes enter. Not to the center. Not to the X. But I step into the alcove and I stand just inside the entrance and I let the sound collect me the way the walls collect it.

It is different when they are not there. The chord is the same — the three turbine banks do not vary, the coolant pumps maintain their rhythm, the pressure vents cycle on their schedule — but the resonance is different. The resident, when they stand at the X, becomes part of the alcove's acoustic system. Their body absorbs and reflects and transmits certain frequencies, altering the harmonics in ways I can feel but not articulate. When they are absent, the sound is purer but also emptier, a chord without a secondary speaker, a system running at set point with no audience and needs none.

I do not stay long. I am not equipped for it — not mineralized the way they are, not adapted to the cold that hurts within minutes, not able to let the sound scrub my mind clean of thought. When I stand in the alcove, I think too much. I notice the rivets and the seams and the cable trays running in their inscrutable directions. I notice the pale growths on the wall, flat and patient, and I wonder what they derive from the alloy, what they are becoming. I notice the canteen on the shelf and I wonder where the water comes from and whether it tastes of coolant or lubricant or something else entirely. I notice the light, its flat white completeness, the way it permits no shadow, and I feel exposed in a way that has nothing to do with temperature.

The resident has found equilibrium. I have found motion. I do not know which is better. I do not know if better is a concept that applies here, in the compression array, in the manufactured cold, in the dry mineral air. The turbines do not evaluate. The cold does not prefer. The sound fills everything equally and makes no distinction between the one who stays and the one who moves.

But I think — and this is the kind of thought that the alcove scrubs away, the kind that can only happen in corridors where the sound is merely loud rather than total — I think that the resident and I are doing something similar. We are both responding to the array. We are both being shaped by it. They have let it press them into a shape that fits the alcove, a minimal presence that vibrates at turbine frequency and touches the wall with mineralized hands. I have let it press me into a shape that fits the corridors, a moving body that carries warmth and loses it and generates it again, a thermal event that persists despite the cold's best efforts.

We are both becoming something the array did not plan for.

I carry things. This is another difference. The resident has stripped themselves of everything extraneous — the modified coveralls, the stitched-shut pockets, the cropped hair, the reduced mass. They have become as minimal as the alcove permits. But I accumulate. In my pockets — which I have kept, which I have even added to, sewing small pouches from insulation fabric into the lining of my coveralls — I carry objects. Small ones. Things I have found in the corridors and the junctions and the maintenance accesses, things that were not meant to be found or kept.

A washer, standard size, that someone dropped during an installation and never retrieved. It is cold when I touch it, but it has a weight that I find satisfying. A fragment of cable sheathing, blue, the only color I have seen in the array that is not gray or gray-green or the gray of mineralized skin. A small square of mesh, probably from a filter, that flexes when I bend it and returns to its shape when I release it. A bolt, threaded, that turns in its nut but will not detach, and sometimes when I am walking a long straight corridor where the sound is constant and the cold is constant and the light is constant, I turn the bolt in my pocket, feeling it thread and unthread, a small mechanical action that has no purpose and no outcome and that I find inexplicably calming.

These objects are warm when I hold them. Only slightly — a degree or two above ambient, the heat transferred from my hand. But they hold it longer than the hull plates do, longer than the deck plating, and when I return them to my pockets they retain a small thermal signature that takes minutes to dissipate. I am making warmth in small places. I am leaving traces of myself in the objects I carry. I do not know why this matters, but it does.

I think about the pale growths sometimes, as I walk. The flat gray-green surfaces that press against the hull plates, breaking down the alloy at a molecular level, deriving sustenance from metal. They grow toward warmth — the warm current from the inspection panel, the ambient heat that bleeds through the hull — and they grow parallel to surfaces, maximizing contact, minimizing exposure. They are adapted to the alcove the way the resident is adapted to the alcove: by becoming like it, by matching its geometry, by finding the minimum viable form and holding it.

But I wonder if there are other growths elsewhere in the array. Other colonies, other tendrils, other flat pale surfaces clinging to warmth in places I have not visited. The inspection panel in the alcove vents warmth from a mechanical cavity behind it, and the growths have found that warmth and colonized it. But there are other cavities, other vents, other places where heat bleeds through the infrastructure. I have felt them as I walk — warm spots on the hull plates, sections of pipe that carry something hotter than coolant, junction boxes that hum with more than electricity. The array is not uniformly cold. It has thermal texture, a landscape of warmth and cold that my body maps as I move through it.

I have never seen growths in those other warm places. But I have not looked carefully. I have been moving, always moving, and the warm spots have been moments of relief rather than destinations, places where the cold loosens its grip for a few seconds before I pass beyond them. Now I wonder: if I stopped at one of those warm spots, if I knelt and examined the hull plate and the seams and the rivets, would I find the same pale adhesion? The same mineral patience? The same stillness that grows toward what it needs?

This is a thought that could change my route. This is a thought that could make me into something other than what I am — a searcher, a finder, a cataloger of growths rather than a body in motion. I do not know if I want this thought. I do not know if the route is keeping me alive or keeping me from living.

The resident touched the wall. I read that in the way the alcove felt different afterward, in the slight harmonic shift that lingered even in the almost-imperceptible change in the quality of the sound. When they touch the pale growths, something passes between them — not communication, not communion, but a kind of chemical exchange, a sharing of mineral and warmth and vibration that I cannot participate in because I am not still enough, not flat enough, not patient enough to adhere.

But I carry warm objects. I leave traces of heat in small places. And when I pass the alcove at the end of my route, pausing at the threshold where the sound pressure changes, sometimes I reach into my pocket and hold the blue cable sheathing or the bolt that turns and unthreads, and I feel the residual warmth there — my warmth, transferred and held and slowly, slowly dissolving into the cold — and I think: this is my version of touching the wall. This is how I participate in the system. I generate heat and I carry it and I lose it and I generate it again, and the array absorbs it the way it absorbs everything, mechanically, indifferently, without acknowledgment.

But the warm spots know. The places where I pause longer than necessary, where my body lingers and my hands rest against the hull plates, the places where the cold loosens for a moment because a moving thing has stopped there — those places know I was present. They hold the thermal shadow of my passage. It dissipates, yes. It always dissipates. But it was there. I was there. The warmth was real for however many seconds it lasted.

I am not the one who stands at the X. I am the one who walks past. I am the one who pauses at the threshold and feels the sound press against me like a wall I could step through if I chose, if I was willing to let it scrub me clean of thought and route and the small warm objects in my pockets. I am the one who moves on.

The route continues. The corridors thread between the processing tiers, too small for carts, too winding for anything but a human body angled slightly forward. I am that body. I am the angle and the forward and the slightly. I am the warmth that moves through the cold, the sound that navigates by sound, the accumulation of small objects and small thermal shadows.

The turbines turn. I walk. The cold pulls at me and I generate warmth and I lose it and I generate it again. This is what I am. This is what the array has made of me, or what I have made of myself in response to the array. A moving thing. A persistence. A heat-event in a system designed to eliminate such things.

And sometimes, at the end of my route, when I pass the alcove and the resident is there, standing at the X with their hands at their sides and their palms facing forward, vibrating at turbine frequency, becoming a secondary speaker in a system that has no audience — sometimes I stop. And I stand at the threshold. And I listen.

And the chord, for a moment, seems to contain something that is not mechanical. Something that comes from the place where a moving thing and a still thing share the same sound, the same cold, the same manufactured air. Something that passes between us the way warmth passes from my hand to the bolt in my pocket — slowly, incompletely, dissolving before it can accumulate into meaning.

Then I move on. The route continues. The resident stays. The turbines turn. The alcove does not change when I arrive, and it does not change when I leave.

But the warm spots along my route — the places where I paused, where my hands rested, where my thermal shadow lingers for seconds before the cold reclaims them — those places know something. Those places hold, for however briefly, the trace of a body that was there and then was not, that moved through and left warmth behind like a question asked in a language the array does not speak.

I am that question. I am the warmth that asks. And the cold, patient and indifferent, takes forever to answer.
