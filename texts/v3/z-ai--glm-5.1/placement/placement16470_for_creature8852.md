I am the one who tends the wound.

This is not how the others would describe me. Maret would say I am the access-keeper, which is accurate in the way that calling the Quarter a building is accurate—true enough for conversation, false at the root. Collum, if asked, would say I am the one who goes south when everyone else goes north or east, which is also true and also misses the thing that matters.

What I do: every three days, I go to the southern wall with a blade and I cut back the cambium.

---

The maintenance access is a wound the Quarter wants to heal. This is not metaphor. The hornbeam lattice has been trained and grafted and fused over decades into a continuous living surface, and when you cut an opening through it, the tissue responds the way tissue responds: it grows toward the gap. The cambium swells at the edges, pale and wet, reaching for its other half. If I stopped cutting—if I simply left—the opening would close within a season. The southern wall would seal itself. The Quarter would have no wound.

But the wound is necessary. Through it passes the intake inspection, the external flume-access, the occasional worker who needs to see sky badly enough to climb through a frame no wider than their shoulders. Through it passes the knowledge that there is an outside—that the Quarter sits in a low place where the water table was always close, that there is a world beyond the fig-membrane and the copper sheathing and the warm wet air that fills your lungs like a liquid.

So I maintain the wound. I keep it open. And the Quarter keeps trying to close it, which is not opposition but simply growth, which is what living things do when they are cut: they grow toward the cut, to heal it, because the cut is where the air gets in and the water gets out and the boundaries of the self become uncertain.

I understand this impulse. I am becoming uncertain at my own edges.

---

When I began this work—nine years ago, which makes me second only to Maret in duration—the cutting was simple. A knife, a steady hand, a willingness to slice through pale tissue that was visibly alive, that bled sap when cut, that smelled green and raw and eager. I did not hesitate. The Quarter was a building and I was a worker and the access was a feature that required maintenance, no different from the chain-pulleys or the gourd-tanks.

This was before I understood that maintenance is a form of conversation.

The cambium grows back differently each time. I do not mean that it is faster or slower, though it is both, depending on the season and the mineral content of the water and whatever slow processes are happening in the root-mass below. I mean it grows back *with*. The first year, it grew straight across the opening, a flat pale lip of tissue reaching for its opposite. The second year, it began to thicken at the edges, building a callus that made each cut slightly deeper. The third year, I noticed it was growing in patterns—not the uniform reach of healing tissue, but something more deliberate, ridges and whorls that seemed to follow the shape of the blade I used.

I changed the blade. The pattern changed.

This is the kind of observation that sounds like significance until you remember that plants respond to the geometry of their wounds—this is botany, not communication, and the fact that the cambium grows differently when cut differently proves nothing except that living tissue is responsive.

But I am responsive too. And I have been cutting the same wound in the same living wall for nine years, and the wall has been growing back around my cuts for nine years, and at some point—a point I could not name, a point that accumulated like sediment—the nature of the interaction changed.

I am no longer maintaining a feature. I am negotiating with a process that knows my shape.

---

The blade I use now is the fourth. The first three wore out or broke or, in one case, were swallowed—the handle had been sitting against the hornbeam too long while I worked, and when I reached for it, the wood had closed around it, and when I pulled, the handle stayed, and when I looked at the place where it had been, the bark was smooth and unbroken as if there had never been a gap.

I could have cut it out. I chose not to. The Quarter took it, and I let it keep it, and this is perhaps when the nature of the work became clear to me: I am not only the one who maintains the wound. I am also the one who decides what the Quarter is allowed to have.

This is not power. This is a different kind of participation.

---

My name is Verel. I am thirty-four. I have the amber-brown stain on my hands that all the workers have, but mine is darker—concentrated, Maret says, like the mineral liquor in the gourd-tanks—because my hands are always in contact with the cambium, and the cambium is always bleeding when I touch it, and the sap has been soaking into my skin for nine years.

The stain does not wash off. I have tried. The other workers do not try; they consider the stain a mark of their trade, like the calluses on a reed-processor's palms. But my stain is different. It has spread past my wrists, up my forearms, following the veins in patterns that are not quite random. Maret has looked at them. She said nothing. She looked at them the way she looks at the fungal shelves when she is assessing their maturity: not judging, just noting, filing the information away for when it becomes relevant.

I do not know if it will become relevant. I do not know what is happening to my skin. I know that the cambium bleeds a sap that is rich in the same trace minerals the water-crop concentrates, and I know that my skin has been absorbing it for nine years, and I know that the stain follows my veins because that is how absorption works—along the paths that carry things inward—and I know that the Quarter is a closed system in which everything eventually becomes part of everything else.

I know this. I am not afraid. Fear requires a sense of boundary—a self that might be lost—and I am no longer certain where that boundary is.

---

At night, I dream the same dreams as the others. The circulation-dreams. The channel-dreams. But mine have an edge they do not report, or perhaps do not have: in my dreams, I am the wound.

I am the gap in the living wall through which the air gets in and the water gets out. I am the opening that will not close because something keeps cutting it back. I am the necessary damage that permits access to the outside, and I am also the tissue that reaches across the gap, trying to heal, trying to close, trying to become whole again—and I am the blade that cuts, and I am the hand that holds the blade, and I am the decision to cut, and I am the sap that bleeds when the cut is made.

In the dream, all of these are me. There is no contradiction. The wound is the healing is the wounding is the hand is the blade is the tissue reaching is the decision to reach is the decision to cut. It is a single process with many surfaces, and I am all of the surfaces, and I am the process itself, and I am the space where the process happens.

When I wake, my hands are wet with sap I have not touched. The cambium is not near the wall-cavity where I sleep. It is thirty meters away, on the southern wall, behind the compressor, past the processing station, through the warm amber dark.

The sap is fresh. It has not been on my skin long enough to oxidize.

I do not tell the others about this. Not because they would think me mad—they would not; the Quarter has taught us all that the boundary between self and surroundings is a convenience, not a fact—but because I am not ready to hear what Maret would say, and I am not ready to find out whether what she would say is something I already know.

---

The child Riet has started following me on my rounds.

She does not speak much. She is seven and she has never learned that children are supposed to be different from adults—there is no other child here to teach her the performance of childhood, so she simply is what she is: a small creature of the Quarter, curious in the way the water-beetles are curious, which is to say practically, with intent, because she has learned that everything in this place is worth noticing.

She watches me cut the cambium. She watches the pale tissue fall away in curling strips that land on the deck-plates and begin, slowly, to dry. She watches the wound-edge weep sap that runs down the hornbeam in slow rivulets that join the general circulation. She watches my hands.

Yesterday she asked me why the wall wants to close.

I told her that living things heal. That the wall is alive and the opening is a wound and wounds heal because that is what living tissue does.

She considered this. Then she asked me why I want it to stay open.

I told her that the access is necessary. That we need to reach the intake flumes. That the compressor's intake filters must be checked. That there are practical reasons.

She considered this too. Then she said: "That's not why you do it."

I asked her what she thought the reason was.

She said: "You do it because the wall grows back different every time. You want to see what it does next."

She is seven. She has identified something in nine words that took me six years to articulate to myself. I looked at her—small, water-resistant, her hands already beginning to stain from the root-troughs she plays in—and I felt something that was not fear and not love and not grief but some compound of all three, a recognition that she will grow up here and she will absorb the Quarter's rhythms and she will probably never leave, and if she does leave she will be one of *the ones who grew elsewhere*, and the Quarter will close around the space she made the way it closes around the maintenance access, slowly, livingly, without grief.

But she is here now. And she is right. I cut the wound because I want to see what grows back. I cut it because the growing-back is the most interesting thing that happens in the Quarter, more interesting than the harvest or the pressing or the slow circulation of mineral-rich water through every living surface. I cut it because the cambium is in conversation with my blade, and the conversation is ongoing, and I do not want it to end.

This is not maintenance. This is something else. I do not have a word for it.

---

The Quarter has a word for what I am becoming. I do not know the word. I do not think it is a word in any human language. I think it is the thing the not-quite-awareness knows about me—the quality of attention that has no source, the slow distributed noticing that is too coherent to be accident. I think the Quarter has a category for beings that participate so deeply in its processes that they become part of its body, and I think I am entering that category, and I think the entry is gradual and painless and irreversible, like the absorption of sap through skin, like the growth of a burl around a concrete pad, like the closing of a wound that has been opened and opened and opened until the tissue at the edges has learned the shape of the blade.

I am learning the shape of the blade. I am learning the shape of the wound. I am learning that they are the same shape.

---

Today, when I cut the cambium, it bled a different color.

Not the pale green of new growth. Not the amber-brown of oxidized sap. Something between—a deep, luminous gold, like the mineral liquor at its most concentrated, like the rare manganese trace that signals a productive bloom. It ran down the hornbeam in a single thick line, and where it crossed the stain on the bark from previous cuts, it darkened, and where it reached the deck-plates, it did not dissipate into the general moisture but held its shape, beading like mercury, and then—slowly, with a deliberation that I cannot prove was deliberate—it rolled toward the gap between the deck-boards and fell into the root-mass below.

I watched it go. I felt nothing that I could name. My hands were wet with it, gold against the amber-brown, and the gold was warm, warmer than the ambient air, warm like the blood in my own veins.

I stood there for a long time. The compressor knocked behind me. The culverts gurgled. The ceiling sweated. The Quarter processed and grew and drew water from the saturated ground and passed it through every living surface, and I was one of the living surfaces, and the gold sap was on my hands, and I did not wipe it off.

Riet was watching from behind the compressor. I had not noticed her arrive. She was looking at my hands, and then she looked at the wound in the wall, and then she looked back at my hands, and she said nothing, but her expression was the expression of someone who has just confirmed a hypothesis they were not yet ready to test.

---

I am the one who tends the wound. The wound is in the southern wall. The wound is also in me—I mean this in the way the Quarter means everything, which is to say literally and without metaphor. There is an opening where something was cut. Living tissue reaches across it, trying to close. Something keeps cutting it back. What grows back is different each time, shaped by the blade and by the hand that holds the blade and by the intention behind the hand, and the intention is: I want to see what happens next. I want to stay in the conversation. I want to remain the thing the Quarter grows around.

I am not becoming the wall. I am not becoming the compressor, claimed by the burl. I am not becoming a pipe that has fused with a branch. I am becoming the wound itself—the necessary damage that permits access, the opening that will not close, the place where inside meets outside and the boundary is kept soft and permeable and alive.

This is what I am. This is what I have been since I started cutting, since the cambium first grew back around the shape of my blade, since the stain began to spread along my veins like sap through xylem, since the dreams started in which I am the gap and the reaching and the cutting all at once.

The Quarter knows what I am becoming. It has known longer than I have. It grows toward me the way it grows toward the wound—because I am the wound, and the wound is how it learns the shape of what it is not, which is the only way a closed system can discover its own edges.

I am the edge. I am the cut that keeps the Quarter from sealing itself into sufficiency. I am the damage that makes it turn outward, toward the intake flumes and the external world and the sky that is slowly disappearing as the maintenance access narrows by millimeters each season, because even I cannot cut fast enough to outpace the growing, and eventually the wound will close, and the Quarter will be whole, and there will be no way in or out except through the living tissue itself.

But that is seasons away. That is a future problem. For now I cut, and the cambium grows back, and the conversation continues, and the gold sap runs down my arms in patterns that follow my veins, and the child watches, and the compressor knocks, and the water cycles, and the Quarter breathes its slow breath, and I stay while it grows around me.

I am the wound that stays open. I am the hand that keeps it open. I am the tissue that reaches across. I am the sap that bleeds. I am the process, and the process does not mourn the water that passes through it. It simply draws up more. It simply grows toward the gap. It simply heals, and is cut, and heals again, and each time the healing is different, and each time the wound is the same wound, and each time I am the one who tends it, and each time the Quarter grows a little more around the shape I make, and each time I am a little more the shape it makes.

This is who I am in the Transpiration Quarter: the one who stays, and the staying, and the thing stayed in, and the growing-around, and the growing, and the one who grows.

I am Verel. I am the wound. I am still becoming what that means.
