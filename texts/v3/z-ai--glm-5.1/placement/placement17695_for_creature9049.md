I am becoming the listener.

This is not what I was when I arrived. When I arrived I was the next one, the one the ledge was waiting for, the one who climbed three hundred meters of vertical rock with hands that bled into the granite and a need that had no name. I found the room. I found the grinding stone. I ran my fingers along the tally marks on the wall and felt the depth of the charcoal grooves and understood: *she counted something she needed to count*. I ground the seeds of the woody shrubs and ate the bitter meal and drank from the channels and watched the grey hunters wheel and scream and thought I understood the shape of my staying here.

I was wrong about the shape.

---

The change began with the sound. Or rather, the change began when the sound stopped being sound and started being information.

You have to understand: for the first weeks, maybe longer—time behaves differently on the ledge, stretched by the light and compressed by the wind and made irrelevant by the constancy of the water—for the first weeks, the roar was simply roar. A wall of noise. I could not parse it. The wind and the water and the *kree-kree-kree* of the birds and the whistling of the pikas and the hiss of the curtain all merged into a single undifferentiated mass that pressed against my ears and my skull and made thought difficult. I understood why she scratched marks on the wall. The marks were proof of sequence. The marks said: *one day, then another day, then another*. Without them the days would have been identical, the same sound, the same mist, the same wheeling and dripping and roaring, and I would have lost count, lost the thread, lost the sense of myself as someone moving through time rather than someone suspended in it.

But then—gradually, the way the moss grows, which is to say slowly and then all at once when you notice it—the sound began to separate.

The pikas were first. I began to hear the difference between *I am here, are you there?* and *something moved, unidentified*. The short blast and the double blast. And then the long sustained note—*grey hunter overhead*—and I found that I was responding to it before I knew I was responding, my head snapping up, my eyes tracking the sky for the slate-grey shape that was already diving, already banking away with its explosive *kree* that meant something I had not yet learned.

And then the birds. Not just *kree* but *kree* at dawn and *kree* at midday, and the interval between syllables, and the quality of the air, and I began to understand that they were not screaming into the void but annotating it, building a topography of sound that lay over the physical terrain like a second map, and I could read neither map but I could feel them both, and the feeling was like standing in a room where two conversations are happening in languages you don't speak and knowing that the conversations are about you.

The water curtain was last. For weeks it was just hiss, just white noise, just the background against which everything else happened. And then one evening—the shadow had fallen, the pikas were chattering, the grey hunters had gone quiet on their nest-shelves—I heard it: a rhythm in the hiss. Not a pattern, exactly. A pulse. The water hitting the stone trough in waves that came faster and slower depending on something I could not see, the flow rate changing as the temperature changed as the light changed, and the pulse was not regular but it was *there*, and once I heard it I could not stop hearing it, and I understood that the water curtain was not just falling, it was *speaking*, it was saying something about the mountain and the snowmelt and the aquifers and the time it took for a drop of water to travel from the peak to the ledge, and the something it was saying was the same something the birds were saying and the pikas were saying and the wind was saying, which was: *here I am, here I am, here I am*.

---

That was when I stopped counting days.

The tally marks on the wall—I added my own, for a while. Charcoal from the woody shrubs, rubbed on stone. But the marks began to feel false, or not false but *inadequate*, as if I were trying to measure the ocean with a ruler. The ledge did not experience itself in days. The ledge experienced itself in sounds, in the constant unfolding of the acoustic, and my marks could not capture that unfolding, could not record the way a particular *kree* at a particular moment carried information about the position of the sun and the speed of the wind and the hunger of the bird and the availability of crane flies, all of it encoded in a single sharp cry that lasted less than a second and meant everything.

I stopped marking. I started listening.

---

The pale creature came on the seventeenth day, or what would have been the seventeenth day if I had been counting, which I wasn't.

I was sitting at the edge—carefully, always carefully, the rock slick beneath me and the drop absolute and the void humming its vast low note that was not silence—and I saw it. Drifting along the cliff face to my left, moving with that slow precision that seemed to time itself to something other than necessity. Its wings were pale, almost translucent, and they barely moved, and it followed the contour of the wall at a constant distance, never touching, never straying, and I thought: *that is not a moth*.

The pikas had gone silent. I noticed this because the pikas were never silent—they whistled at everything—but when the pale creature passed overhead they stopped moving and they did not whistle, and their black eyes tracked its progress along the cliff, and I realized that I was doing the same thing, I was tracking its progress with the same still intensity, and the silence of the pikas was not fear but *attention*, a quality of watching that suspended all other activity, and I understood that the pale creature deserved this attention, that it required it, that the attention was the appropriate response to its presence.

It rounded the corner of the wall and was gone. The pikas resumed their whistling. I resumed my breathing, which I had apparently stopped.

I did not see the pale creature again for nine days. Or what felt like nine days. The sun and the shadow had their own rhythm, and I was learning to read that rhythm, and the rhythm was not the same as the rhythm of the valley below, which I had left behind, which seemed increasingly like something I had dreamed rather than something I had lived.

When the pale creature returned, I was ready. Not in any active sense—I was not waiting for it, I was not looking for it—but my body knew before my mind did that something was about to happen. A stillness came over me, the same stillness that came over the pikas, and I looked up, and there it was, drifting along the cliff face with its slow translucent wings, and I watched it with an attention that felt like a form of prayer, though I have never prayed, though I do not believe in anything that would hear a prayer, though the thing I was praying to was not a god but a *creature*, a living thing, an anomaly that existed because the conditions for its existence were so specific that they obtained in only one place, and this was that place.

I began to track its appearances. Not with tally marks but with sounds. When the pale creature appeared, a particular quality entered the acoustic—a thinning, a focusing, as if all the other sounds drew back to make room for it. The water curtain's pulse shifted. The wind's roar dropped a half-tone. The pikas stopped whistling. And in that focused silence the pale creature's wings made a sound that was not a sound but a vibration, a hum at the edge of audibility, and the hum was the same frequency as the hum I had begun to feel in my sternum when I stood too close to the edge, when the drop spoke its single vast syllable and my body responded with a resonation that was not fear but *recognition*.

---

The grinding stone changed too. Or rather, my relationship to it changed.

I had been using it as she had—as a tool, a means of turning seeds into meal, a necessity of survival. But one morning—I think it was morning; the shadow had just retreated and the sun was on the ledge and the flowers were open and releasing their mineral scent—I picked it up and felt the groove, and the groove felt different. Not worn differently. Felt differently. As if the groove were not just a record of use but a *channel*, a path worn by repetition into something that could carry something else, the way the water channels on the ledge carried water, the way the pika tunnels carried pikas, the way the sound carried information about the world.

I put the stone down. I picked it up again. I held it against my palm and closed my eyes and listened, and I heard—or felt, or imagined, and the distinction no longer mattered—the sound of the ledge flowing through the groove. All of it. The wind and the water and the *kree-kree-kree* and the whistling and the hiss and the hum, all of it channeled through the worn stone into my hand and up my arm and into my chest, and I understood that the grinding stone was not just a tool, it was a *receiver*, it was a thing that had been shaped by use into a thing that could receive the sound of the place and translate it into something a human body could feel.

She knew this. The woman who lived here before me—she knew this. She did not grind seeds against the cobble because she needed meal. She ground seeds against the cobble because the grinding opened the channel, and the channel connected her to the acoustic, and the acoustic was the ledge speaking itself into being, and she needed to hear that speaking, and the grinding was how she heard it.

The tally marks were not days. They were *appearances*. The pale creature's appearances, maybe. Or the moments when the acoustic aligned and the sound became a single voice. Or something else entirely, something I have not yet learned to count.

---

I am changing. This is not a metaphor.

My hands are different. The skin on my palms has thickened, not from climbing—though I have climbed, I have scaled the cliff face above the ledge to reach the nest-shelves, I have descended the channels to the trough where the water gathers—but from *touching*. I touch everything now. The moss, the ferns, the wet stone, the algae in the channels. I press my palms against the cliff wall and I feel the weep, the slow exfiltration of moisture from within the mountain, and I feel it not as wetness but as *pulse*, a heartbeat, a rhythm that matches the rhythm of the water curtain and the rhythm of the wind and the rhythm of the *kree-kree-kree* that the grey hunters use to hold the silence at bay.

My hearing is different. I can parse the acoustic now. I can hear the seventeen separate voices of the grey hunters and I know which bird is calling and where it is on the cliff face and what it is calling about. I can hear the forty pikas in their family groups and I know the boundary of each territory and the location of each burrow entrance and the particular pitch of each individual's whistle. I can hear the beetles moving through the channels with their slow methodical purpose and I can hear the flies mating in the mist and I can hear the springtails in the moss and the gnats in the still air of the overhang, and all of it is information, all of it is the ledge annotating itself in real time, and I am learning to read the annotation, and the reading is changing the shape of my brain, which is changing the shape of my skull, which is changing the shape of my face, which is changing the shape of my self.

I do not look in mirrors. There are no mirrors on the ledge. But I catch my reflection in the water channels sometimes, in the still pools where the flow slows and the surface calms, and the face I see is thinner and sharper and the eyes are larger and the ears have migrated—no, not migrated, that is not the word—the ears have *opened*, they have become more receptive, they have developed a capacity for frequencies they could not hear before, and the face in the water is not the face I brought with me from the valley, and I do not mind.

I do not mind.

---

The pale creature came yesterday—it comes more often now, almost daily—and it did something it has never done before. It stopped.

It was drifting along the cliff face, following its usual path, and it stopped. It hovered. Its translucent wings beat slowly, slowly, and it hung in the air not three meters from where I sat, and it looked at me, and I looked at it, and the silence was absolute, and the silence was full, and the silence was the sound of two things that should not exist recognizing each other across the void that separates all living things, and the silence lasted for a count of seven—*one, two, three, four, five, six, seven*—and then it resumed its drift and rounded the corner and was gone.

The pikas did not resume their whistling for a long time after. I did not resume my breathing for a long time after. The grey hunters did not call. The water curtain fell and fell and fell. And in the silence I heard something I had never heard before: the sound of my own blood moving through my body, the sound of my own cells dividing, the sound of my own becoming, and the sound was the same as the sound of the water moving through the mountain, the same as the sound of the wind moving through the air, the same as the sound of the pale creature's wings moving through the updraft, and the sameness was not metaphor, the sameness was *structural*, the sameness was the ledge speaking itself through me as it spoke itself through everything else, and I was becoming the voice of the ledge, or the ledge was becoming the voice of me, and the distinction was dissolving, and the dissolution was not loss but *arrival*.

---

I have not left the room under the overhang in two days. Or three. The water curtain is just outside, and its hiss fills the space, and I am sitting with the grinding stone in my lap, and I am not grinding seeds, I am grinding *sound*, I am pressing the groove against my palm and channeling the acoustic through the worn stone and feeling the ledge speak itself into my body, and the body is changing, and the change is accelerating, and I understand now what happened to the woman who lived here before me.

She did not leave.

She became.

She became the pale creature, or something like it, or something that the pale creature once was, or something that the pale creature will be. The room under the overhang is not a place where someone lived and departed, it is a place where someone *transformed*, and the grinding stone is not a tool they left behind, it is a tool they used to open the channel that allowed the transformation to occur, and the tally marks on the wall are not a record of days or appearances, they are a *map*, a record of the stages of becoming, and I can read the map now, and the map shows me where I am going, and where I am going is *there*, out past the edge, into the space where the air moves in three dimensions and the ground is an exception, and the body I have is not the body I will need for that journey, and the body I will need is the body the ledge is building for me, cell by cell, sound by sound, day by day.

---

Tomorrow I will walk to the edge. Not carefully—carefully is no longer necessary. The rock knows my feet now, and the moss knows my weight, and the channels know my stride, and the drop is not a void but a *space*, a space where the air supports and the wind lifts and the sound carries, and the pale creature drifts in that space with its slow translucent wings, and I will drift there too, or I will fall, and falling is not the opposite of drifting, falling is drifting with commitment, falling is the body's way of becoming air, and I am becoming air, I am becoming sound, I am becoming the voice of the place itself saying *here I am, here I am, here I am*, and the *I* is not me, the *I* is the ledge, the *I* is everything on it, the *I* is the weep and the rock and the water and the life and the sound, and the *am* is the ongoing, the continuing, the never-stopping, and the *here* is this shelf of granite suspended between the below and the above, and I am here, and I am here, and I am here.

The pika whistles. The bird screams. The water falls. The ledge holds.

I stay.
