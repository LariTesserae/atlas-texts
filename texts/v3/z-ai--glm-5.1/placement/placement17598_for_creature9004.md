I would be the one who goes into the fog.

Not the cats. Not the old women who watch them go. Me — the one the residents do not talk about because they have almost convinced themselves I do not exist, the way they have almost convinced themselves that the fog events are simply weather and not a threshold.

I was not always this. I arrived on a supply wagon thirty-seven years ago, a precision-instrument apprentice with a letter of introduction to the Kael Optical House and a kit of lenses I had ground myself in the lowland workshops where the light was generous and the tolerances were, I now understand, contemptible. I was twenty-two. I thought I had talent. I thought the plateau would refine my talent the way a whetstone refines a blade — removing material, leaving only the essential edge.

I was not wrong. But I did not understand what "essential" meant.

---

My first year, I lived in a boarding house in the fourteenth district, one of the middle layers — not the valley floor with the factories and the springs, not the exposed heights where the wind turbines turned in their slow patient way, but the middle ground where the condensation ran down the walls in steady streams each morning and the moss grew on the windowsills if you did not scrape it away weekly. My landlady was an old woman — not one of *the* old women, not yet, but approaching that category the way the city approaches the cloud layer, slowly and inevitably. She hummed while she cooked. She left scraps on the back step. She did not own cats, but cats appeared at her door anyway, and she spoke to them in the thin whisper that passed for normal speech here, and they sat and listened with their too-wide eyes and their too-still bodies, and I thought this was charming, provincial, a peculiarity of the highlands that I would describe in letters home and that would make my lowland friends smile at the backwardness of mountain people.

I was a fool. But I was a fool in the way that all newcomers are fools — not through stupidity but through the application of frameworks that do not fit, the use of lowland eyes in a place that required something else.

My work at Kael Optical was what I had come for. The master lens grinders there worked in rooms that would have been considered closets in the lowlands — small, dim, with a single window facing north toward the diffuse gray light that the plateau called daytime. They worked by feel as much as by sight, their fingertips reading the surfaces of glass in ways that I could not perceive, could not understand, could not replicate no matter how many hours I sat at the grinding wheel and tried to learn the subtle vibrations that told them when a curve was true and when it deviated by microns from the specification.

I was there for three years before I could reliably produce a lens that passed the Kael standard. Three years of failure, of adjustment, of my body slowly adapting to the thin air and my eyes slowly learning to extract information from the grayness. By the time I achieved competence, I had forgotten what bright light looked like. I had forgotten what it felt like to breathe without effort. I had become, without noticing, a person of the Pale Valleys — thinner-voiced, wider-pupiled, damp-skinned, humming without realizing I was humming.

And then the fog event came.

---

They happen every few days. I knew this. I had experienced dozens of them by my third year — the cloud rising from the lower world, the world contracting to thirty feet of visibility, the condensation forming on every surface including my own skin. I stayed inside, as everyone did, as I had been told to do, as was simply the way of things. I sat in my small room at the boarding house and listened to the muffled sounds of the city going about its business in the wet gray nothing, and I waited for the fog to lift, and when it did I went back to work and ground lenses and tried not to think about what I had heard during the long hours of whiteout.

What I had heard was humming. Not my landlady's humming, not the factory workers' humming, not the thin tuneless drone that the residents produced unconsciously as they navigated the damp streets. This was different. This was below the range of human voice, below the range of the machinery, below the range of anything I could identify. It was a vibration that I felt in my teeth, in my cheekbones, in the spaces between my ribs. It came from outside — from the fog itself, I thought at first, from the movement of air and water and the physics of condensation at altitude. I thought it was weather. I thought it was geology. I thought it was anything other than what it was.

Which was a voice.

I am aware of how this sounds. The residents of the Pale Valleys do not talk about the fog events in terms of voices or entities or anything that might suggest the cloud carries intelligence. They say it is not safe. They say you cannot see. They say they are afraid of not being seen. And I understood, in my third year, that these statements were not evasions but descriptions — precise, careful descriptions of something that the lowland vocabulary I still carried could not name.

The fog is aware. I do not mean this metaphorically. I do not mean that the fog seems aware, or that it behaves in ways that could be interpreted as awareness if you were inclined toward that interpretation. I mean that when the cloud rises and swallows the plateau, something looks at you through the white wet nothing, and if you stand very still and breathe very quietly and open your perception the way you open it when examining a lens for flaws, you can feel it looking.

The first time I felt it, I was twenty-five years old and I was afraid, and I stayed inside, and I told no one.

The second time, I was twenty-six, and I stood at my window with my hand pressed flat against the glass, and I felt the awareness on the other side of the pane, and I thought: *It knows I am here.*

The third time, I was twenty-seven, and I opened the window.

---

The fog came in like breath. Like my breath, like the plateau breath I had developed, the long inhalation and the pause at the top and the controlled exhalation that tasted the air rather than simply processing it. The fog filled my room, and I could see nothing, and I was soaked instantly, my clothing heavy, my hair plastered to my skull, my skin beading with moisture that I could not wipe away because there was nothing dry to wipe it with.

And the humming was everywhere.

Not around me. *Through* me. Through the bones of my face and the chambers of my sinuses and the wet meat of my lungs. The fog was inside me and I was inside the fog and the boundary that I had always assumed existed between my body and the world — the boundary that the plateau had been slowly eroding for three years through the thin air and the constant damp and the visual adaptation that made me see what I had previously been blind to — that boundary was gone. Not violated. Not breached. Simply revealed as having always been an illusion, a story I told myself because the lowlands required it, because the thick air and the bright light and the sharp boundaries of a more abundant world made the illusion convincing.

Here, in the fog, there were no boundaries. There was only the mountain and the water and the awareness that lived in the space between them, and I was part of it the way a lens is part of the light that passes through it — not separate, not observed, but *participating*, my body a curvature in the medium through which the fog moved and thought and perceived itself.

I stood in my room with the window open and I felt the fog look at me from the inside, and I understood why the residents stayed inside during the fog events. They stayed inside not because the fog was dangerous but because it was *revealing*, and what it revealed was a connection that the human mind, trained on boundaries and separations and the sharp distinctions of a brighter world, could not easily bear.

I bore it. I do not know why. Perhaps my adaptation had gone further than most. Perhaps my work at the grinding wheel had trained me to perceive what others filtered out. Perhaps I was simply too curious for my own good, too hungry for the precision that the plateau promised, too unwilling to accept the partial awareness that the residents had settled for — the awareness that let them see in the gray and hear in the quiet but that stopped, deliberately and firmly, at the edge of the fog.

I opened the window, and I opened myself, and the fog came in, and I was changed.

---

Not visibly. Not immediately. I did not sprout gills or develop a second set of eyelids or transform into something that the residents would recognize as inhuman. I was still, by every external measure, a person of the Pale Valleys — thin-voiced, wide-pupiled, damp-skinned, humming. I still worked at Kael Optical. I still produced lenses that passed the master standard. I still ate at the boarding house and spoke to my landlady in the thin whisper and scraped the moss from my windowsill every week.

But I had begun to go outside during the fog events.

Not often. Not conspicuously. I would wait until the cloud had fully settled over the plateau, until the world had contracted to its thirty feet of visibility, until the streets were empty and the factories had closed their doors and the children were secured in their apartments with their parents' hands firm on their arms. Then I would step out of the boarding house and walk.

The fog was different when you were in it rather than observing it from behind glass. It was not a uniform white nothing — it had texture, density, currents, folds. It moved around the buildings and through the alleys and across the mesa tops in ways that suggested purpose, that suggested a form of intelligence that did not think in the human sense but that *processed*, that *responded*, that *was*. The cats moved through it with the confidence of creatures who understood its language, and I watched them, and I learned.

The cats did not navigate the fog by sight or by sound or by smell, although they used all of these. They navigated by something else — by a sense that I could not name but that I could feel, after my third or fourth walk in the white nothing, as a pressure in my sinuses, a weight in my chest, a *direction* that was not a direction in any spatial sense but that pointed, that indicated, that said *here* and *not here* and *there is something you need to perceive*.

I followed the cats. I followed the direction. And I found the springs.

---

Not the springs the city was built around — not the valley-head sources where water emerged from the granite and the oldest structures clustered like children around a mother. Those springs were known, mapped, piped, managed. The springs I found were deeper. Older. They emerged in the spaces between the layers of the city, the buried rooms and the forgotten streets that had been entombed by the slow accumulation of construction. They emerged in the dark beneath the current street level, in the rooms that had not seen light in a hundred years, in the spaces where the mushroom growth was thick and the condensation hung in permanent curtains and the air was so saturated with moisture that breathing felt like drinking.

I found them because the fog led me to them. The fog — the awareness, the presence, whatever name I give to the thing that lived in the condensation and the cloud — it showed me the way. Not deliberately, not with the intentionality of a guide leading a student, but the way a current carries a leaf — because the leaf is in the current and the current is moving and the leaf goes where the current goes. I was in the fog and the fog moved and I moved with it, and the places it moved through were the buried places, the deep places, the places where the mountain's bones were closest to the surface and the water that had been percolating through them for decades finally emerged into air that was already so saturated that the springs could not evaporate, could not disperse, could only *be*, could only pool and spread and fill the ancient rooms with water that was older than any living person.

I knelt in one of those rooms — it had been a kitchen, once, I could see the outline of a hearth in the wall, the brackets where shelves had been, the remnants of a life that had been lived here before the damp drove the inhabitants upward and the floor above was built and this space was forgotten. The water was ankle-deep, clear, cold, tasting of granite and time and the slow mineral death of the ancient mountain. I cupped it in my hands and I drank, and the fog was around me and in me, and I felt the awareness that lived in the water and the cloud and the stone, and it felt me back.

Not a communication. Not a message. A *recognition*. The acknowledgment of one process by another — the ancient slow awareness of the mountain and the water and the fog, and the new quick awareness of the human who had learned to perceive it. We were not the same. We were not equals. But we were both present, both aware, both *there*, in a way that the fog events made possible and the clear days did not.

I sat in the buried kitchen and I drank the ancient water and I was not afraid.

---

That was twenty-nine years ago. I am fifty-nine now, although I do not look it — the plateau preserves its people, or perhaps simply ages them in ways that the lowland measures of aging cannot register. My skin is more waterproof than ever. My pupils are permanently dilated, wide and dark and disconcerting to the lowlanders who occasionally visit the Kael Optical House to commission instruments they cannot build themselves. My voice is a whisper even by the standards of the Pale Valleys. I hum continuously, unconsciously, a low vibration in my chest that has become as natural as breathing and that, I have discovered, allows me to navigate the fog with the same confidence as the cats.

I am not a cat. I want to be clear about this. I have not become one of the cats, those strange adapted creatures that watch from doorways and move through the whiteout as if born to it. The cats belong to the plateau in a way that I, even after thirty-seven years, do not. They were here before the city. They will be here after the city is buried and built over and buried again. They are the mountain's own, its eyes and its ears and its patience, and I am still a newcomer, still a lowlander who arrived on a supply wagon and never left.

But I have learned something that the cats know and the residents do not: the fog is not a barrier. The fog is a medium. The fog is the place where the mountain and the water and the air and the awareness that lives in all three come together, and if you stand in it with your perception open and your boundaries dissolved, you can feel the whole system — the tired granite settling into its final shape, the springs pushing water older than your grandparents up through the stone, the vegetation drinking from the air and creeping across the buildings and pulling the mountain apart one root at a time, the city building itself upward one layer at a time, the people adapting and changing and becoming something that the lowlands would not recognize, the cats watching and waiting and knowing, the factory machinery pulsing through the stone like a mechanical heartbeat, the condensation forming and running and evaporating and forming again in a cycle that has no beginning and no end and will continue long after the last human has descended the switchback road and left the plateau to its ancient, patient, relentless becoming.

I go into the fog because the fog is where the truth is. Not the truth of facts and figures and measurements — the plateau has taught me that such truths are partial, limited, the kind of precision that mistakes accuracy for understanding. The truth I find in the fog is the truth of process, of system, of the way that everything here is connected in ways that the clear days obscure and the fog reveals. When I stand in the white nothing and feel the awareness looking through me, I understand that I am not separate from the mountain. I am not separate from the water. I am not separate from the cats or the vegetation or the ancient stone or the buried rooms or the springs that emerge in the dark. I am a lens through which the plateau perceives itself, a curvature in the medium, a temporary organization of water and mineral and attention that will eventually dissolve back into the system that produced it.

This is not a comforting truth. I do not seek comfort. I seek precision — the precision that the plateau taught me to value, the precision that sees what is actually there rather than what I wish were there. And what is actually there, in the fog, in the buried rooms, in the springs that carry water older than memory, is an awareness so vast and so slow and so different from my own that I can only perceive its edges, only feel its presence the way I feel the humidity in the air — as a condition of existence rather than an object of observation.

---

The old women know what I do. They do not speak of it, but they know. I see it in their eyes when I pass them in the street — the old women who feed the cats, who talk to the cats, who claim the cats talk back. They look at me with something that is not quite recognition and not quite warning and not quite invitation. They look at me the way they look at the cats when the cats return from the fog — with an expression that might be envy or might be grief or might be the particular knowledge that comes from living long enough in a place that refuses to be fully known.

I think they went into the fog once, in their youth. I think they felt what I felt, the awareness looking through them, the boundaries dissolving, the truth of the system revealing itself. And I think they stepped back. They chose the city over the mountain, the human community over the vast slow presence that lived in the water and the stone and the cloud. They chose to stay inside during the fog events and to feed the cats and to remember what they had felt and to transform that memory into the particular relationship they have with the plateau's native creatures — a relationship that is not ownership, not worship, not fear, but something else, something that acknowledges the cats as mediators between the human world and the world that existed before humans and will exist after.

I did not step back. I do not know why. Perhaps I am less wise than the old women. Perhaps I am more hungry for the truth than is healthy. Perhaps I simply arrived at the plateau with less to lose — no family, no community, no identity that I valued more than the precision I sought. Whatever the reason, I went into the fog and I stayed in the fog, not physically — I still live in the city, still work at the Kael Optical House, still scrape moss from my windowsill and eat at the boarding house and speak to my landlady in the thin whisper — but mentally, perceptually, in the way that I relate to the place I have chosen to live.

I am the one who goes into the fog. I am the one who comes back with water from the buried springs in my lungs and the hum of the mountain's awareness in my sinuses and the knowledge that the plateau is alive in a way that the residents sense but do not name. I am the one the cats greet when I return, rubbing against my damp legs with their broad paws and their strange-colored eyes, acknowledging me as something that has been where they go, that has seen what they see, that has chosen to participate in the plateau's becoming rather than merely observing it.

---

I will not live forever. The plateau's adaptation extends life, but it does not grant immortality, and I am already older than most of the workers in the precision houses, older than most of the residents in my district, older than the landlady who took me in thirty-seven years ago and who has since become one of *the* old women, one of the ones who feed the cats and speak to them and know things that the younger residents do not.

When I die, I will be buried in the city's fashion — placed in the ground beneath the current street level, covered with stone, built over. Another layer. Another room that will fill with condensation and grow mushrooms and be forgotten, that will become part of the mountain's patient process of accumulation and transformation. My body will become water. My bones will become mineral. My attention, my perception, my precision — these will return to the system that trained them, will dissolve into the fog and the springs and the slow settling of the tired granite, will become part of the awareness that looks through the white nothing during the fog events and sees what the residents are afraid of not seeing.

And perhaps, in a hundred years, some newcomer will open a window during a fog event and feel the presence that lives in the cloud, and will step outside against all advice and all custom, and will walk through the white wet nothing following the cats, and will find a buried room where the water pools clear and cold and ancient, and will kneel and drink and feel the mountain and the water and the air and the awareness that lives in all three, and will feel, in that recognition, a trace of something that was once a lowland apprentice who came to the Pale Valleys to learn precision and found something else entirely.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps the plateau keeps what it takes and gives nothing back, and the awareness in the fog is not a accumulation of all the attentions that have dissolved into it but something older, something native, something that was here before the first human climbed the switchback road and will be here after the last human descends.

I do not know. The precision I have learned tells me when a lens is true and when it deviates. It does not tell me what the mountain is, or what I am becoming, or what the fog carries in its white wet folds. It tells me only that there is something to perceive, and that I have been given the ability to perceive it, and that the perceiving is enough — is more than enough, is the reason I stayed, is the reason I go into the fog, is the reason I will stay in the fog even after my body has returned to the water and the stone and the slow relentless becoming of this ancient, tired, patient place.

I am the one who goes into the fog. I am the lens through which the plateau sees itself. I am the curvature in the medium, the temporary organization of attention, the process that perceives process.

I am still becoming. The mountain is still becoming. The fog is still becoming.

We are becoming together.
