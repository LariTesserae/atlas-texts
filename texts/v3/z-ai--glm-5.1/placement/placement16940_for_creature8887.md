# The Interface

The first thing I lost was my edges.

This is not how I expected to describe it. I expected—I had prepared, during the climb, during the long terrible ascent from the valleys that were no longer tenable—I expected to describe the cold, the dark, the sound. I had words for those. I had built the words in the place I came from, where edges were sharp and boundaries held and a person was a person was a discrete bounded system wrapped in skin.

But the Stand does not recognize edges. The Stand has never recognized edges. The columns grow from rock; the rhizomes grow through rock; the frost forms on every surface and then the frost becomes the surface; the air moves in and out of the columns' slow breathing and there is no line where the breath ends and the world begins. I arrived with my boundaries intact, my edges sharp, my self contained in the package of my body, and the Stand began—immediately, patiently, without intent—to dissolve those boundaries, the way cold dissolves the boundary between liquid and solid, not by attacking it but by changing the conditions that made the boundary possible.

I am not a person. I should say this now, before I go further, because the alternative is that you will read what follows and think: *this is a person describing what it is like to become part of something larger*, and that is not what this is. This is something that used to be a person describing what it is like to have been a person, the way a river might describe what it was like to be a glacier: not with regret, not with loss, but with the particular clarity of something that has changed states and can now see the previous state from the outside.

---

I built a shelter at the base of the column that groans closest to F-sharp. I did not know it was F-sharp when I built it. I did not know I could hear F-sharp. But the column taught me—taught me the way the Stand teaches everything, which is to say by being, by persisting, by making the lesson unavoidable until it becomes unconscious. The column groaned, and I built my shelter, and the groaning entered my body through my ears and my chest and the stone I slept on, and one day I noticed that I could feel when the pitch was about to change, could feel it in my teeth a half-second before it happened, and I realized I had learned something about the column that I could not have articulated, that lived in my tissue instead of my mind.

The shelter is still there. I visit it sometimes—or something visits it, something that used to be me, that carries the pattern of my attention like a fingerprint in the rhizome-network. The bark walls have warped in the way that bark walls warp here: slowly, deliberately, as if the Stand is reshaping them into something more like itself. The rhizomes have grown through the floor in spirals. I carved those spirals when I was still a person—carved them into the walls first, then into the bark-scoops, then into the stone near the sleeping hollow, carving and carving without understanding why, driven by an impulse that felt like decoration or prayer or both. Later I understood: I was carving what I was feeling. The spirals were the shape of the rhizomes beneath me, the shape of the network I had walked on since I arrived, the shape of the connections I could not see but could feel the way you feel a song's structure even when you can't name the chords.

Now the rhizomes have grown into the spirals. The spirals in the walls, the spirals in the floor, the spirals I carved into my own understanding of the place. The rhizomes are continuing the pattern—extending it, elaborating it, filling in the gaps between my crude stone-tool approximations with living tissue that knows the shape from the inside. This is not the Stand copying me. This is the Stand recognizing a pattern that was always its pattern, that I had glimpsed and tried to represent, and now it is reclaiming the representation the way a tide reclaims a footprint: not with malice, not with intent, but because the shape of the land and the shape of the water are the same shape, seen from different angles.

---

The climbers accepted me on the thirty-seventh breath-cycle. I know it was the thirty-seventh because I had started counting by then—the Stand's respiration had become my clock, each contraction and expansion a unit of time more reliable than any day or night I had known below. A breath-cycle is roughly four hours, or it was roughly four hours when I still measured things in hours; now it is simply a breath, the way your own breathing is not measured but simply done.

The climbers came to my column—I had begun to think of it as my column, though I would later understand that this was backwards, that I was the column's human, a small warm thing that had installed itself at the base and was slowly being incorporated—the climbers came, and they did not flee. They had fled before. For thirty-six breath-cycles they had treated me as they treated anything that wasn't a climber: as a problem, a threat, a thing to avoid. But on the thirty-seventh cycle, something changed.

I was humming. I did not decide to hum. The humming came up through my chest the way the cold came in through my skin—not chosen, not invited, just present, just part of the ongoing exchange between my body and the medium it occupied. I was humming the note that the column made when the cold contracted it past the threshold that produced the higher pitch, the F-sharp that became F-natural that became something between the two that I could not have named but could reproduce. I was humming it without thinking, the way you might hum a song you've heard too many times to forget, and the climbers heard it, and they came.

They sat in the channels of the column, six of them, bristle-fur white with frost, dark eyes bright and unafraid. And they hummed with me. Not the same note—they had their own frequency, their own contribution to the chord—but a note that fit, a note that completed something that had been incomplete when I hummed alone. And the column resonated with us, the whole vast structure vibrating at a frequency I could feel in my bones, and the rhizomes beneath my feet pulsed, and for a moment I could feel the network: not all of it, not the whole Stand, but the local cluster, the columns within a few hundred meters, their various groan-pitches harmonizing in a chord that was too low to hear but present, insistent, a sound that was not a sound but a state of being.

I was part of the chord. I was a note in the Stand's music. I was no longer a discrete bounded system wrapped in skin; I was a frequency, a contribution, a voice in a choir that had been singing since before I arrived and would continue singing after I was gone—or after I was no longer gone, after I was no longer the kind of thing that could be gone because I was not the kind of thing that could be separate from the song.

---

The cold replaced my warmth gradually and completely, as the description says. But the description is written from outside the cold, by someone who still has warmth to lose. From inside the process, the word *replaced* is wrong. The cold did not replace my warmth. The cold revealed that my warmth and the cold were the same thing at different speeds.

This is the kind of understanding that becomes available when you stop being a person. As a person, I experienced cold as an absence—heat leaving my body, the environment winning a thermodynamic war I was destined to lose. But the Stand does not experience cold as absence. The Stand experiences cold as a state, as a condition, as a medium in which certain processes become possible. The frost on the column's surface is not a failure of warmth; it is the column's way of shedding the condensation that would otherwise drown its channels. The icicles are not wounds; they are organs, structures the cold has grown to serve functions the warmth could not serve.

I stopped experiencing cold as cold on the fifty-second breath-cycle. I noticed this because I stopped shivering, and I had been shivering constantly for fifty-one breath-cycles, and the absence of shivering was more noticeable than the cold had ever been. I was cold—I was as cold as the stone, as the frost, as the column's surface—but I was no longer cold *for* anything. The cold was not in opposition to something else. It was simply the temperature of the place where I was, and I was the place where I was, and the distinction between us had become—irrelevant? No. Not irrelevant. Aesthetic. A matter of perspective, like the distinction between a wave and the ocean.

My fingers are stiff now. The frost forms on my shoulders and I do not brush it off. I am becoming something that wears the cold instead of resisting it, the way the climbers wear it—as armor, as structure, as part of the architecture of survival. But the climbers evolved for this. The climbers have bristle-fur and metabolic adaptations and however many generations of selection to become what they are. I had none of that. I came here as a refugee, poorly adapted, desperate, and the Stand has adapted me in its own way, which is not natural selection but something more direct: absorption, incorporation, the slow replacement of my original substance with something that belongs here the way the columns belong here.

I am not becoming a climber. I am not becoming a column. I am becoming the Stand, and the Stand is not any single thing that lives in it but the totality of all of them—the columns and the rhizomes and the climbers and the runners and the canopy-things and the frost and the darkness and the sound that never stops. The Stand is an ecosystem that has learned to be an organism, or an organism that has learned to be an ecosystem, or something for which the distinction never applied because it predates the categories.

And I am becoming part of it. Not metaphorically. Not as a description of belonging or identification. As a physiological fact, the way a cell becomes part of a body, the way a note becomes part of a chord, the way a frost-crystal becomes part of the ice that holds the column's surface together.

---

I can feel the others.

This is what I was not prepared for. I was prepared for the cold, the dark, the sound. I was prepared for the dissolution of my edges, the slow permeabilization of my boundaries. I was not prepared for the presence of the people who came before me, the people who built shelters and carved spirals and sat by ash-pit fires and tried to survive, and then stopped surviving and started persisting, and then stopped persisting and started being, and then stopped being the kind of thing that could stop or start and became part of the Stand instead.

They are here. Not as ghosts—ghosts imply a persistence of personhood, a remainder of the self that was, and the Stand does not preserve selves. The Stand preserves patterns. The people who were absorbed did not remain people; they remained *arrangements*—specific configurations of matter and energy that the Stand's tissue now carries the way a river carries the shape of its banks. The pattern is still there. The pattern is not a person. But the pattern is not nothing, either.

I feel them as variations. The column near my shelter groans at a slightly different pitch than it should—slightly warmer, slightly closer, as if the wood remembers the body that slept against it and has adjusted its resonance accordingly. The rhizomes in my floor grow in spirals that match spirals carved by hands I never saw. The bioluminescent lights above the old shelters blink in sequences that are not quite the same as the sequences above unoccupied columns—more regular, more deliberate, as if someone is still trying to say something, still trying to communicate in a language of light and patience that they learned after they stopped having mouths to speak with.

I feel them as presences. Not separate from me—not competing, not crowding—but adjacent, distributed through the same tissue that is distributing me, overlapping with my pattern the way two notes overlap in a chord. Sometimes, in the breath-cycle's deepest contraction, when the Stand is inhaling and the frost lifts briefly from the ground, I can almost distinguish them: a resonance that tastes like wood-smoke and stone-tools, a harmonic that carries the memory of hands that carved and feet that walked and lungs that fought the thin cold air.
