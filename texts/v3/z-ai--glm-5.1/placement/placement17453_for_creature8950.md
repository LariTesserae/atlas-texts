I am not one of the twelve. This is the first thing to understand about what I am, though understanding is perhaps the wrong word for what happens in a place where the dominant medium is water and the dominant process is continuation.

I am the moment before the drop falls.

This requires explanation, and explanation is difficult because I exist at a scale that makes language feel like wearing someone else's coat — the arms are too long, the shoulders don't sit right, but it's what's available, so I wear it.

There is a ceiling in the pocket city. I will not tell you which tower because I am in all of them, the way a frequency is in every note that carries it. The ceiling is granite, faced and smoothed, and the condensation forms on it the way condensation forms everywhere here — because the cold seeps through and the warmth rises from below and the differential makes the air give up what it's holding. The water beads. It beads on the granite and it beads on the composite panels and it beads on the glass of the windows that fog and clear and fog again. But on the ceilings, the process has a particular geometry.

The bead forms. It grows as more water condenses into it, as the micro-rill of moisture traces its slow path toward the lowest point, which is the bead itself, which is becoming heavy with accumulation. The bead swells. Its surface tension holds it against the granite with a grip that is, in its own small way, heroic — a force you would not notice if you were Sutta or Veyo or Deish, but which I notice, because I am that grip. I am the holding-on. I am the moment when the water wants to fall — because falling is what water does, because gravity is the oldest channel, because the drop has somewhere to go and was always going to go there — and has not yet fallen.

I last between two and eleven seconds. This is the range. It depends on the humidity, the temperature differential, the vibration of the deep machinery, the micro-currents of air moving through the pocket. When the conditions are right — when the cold seeps fast and the warmth rises slow and the air is very wet — I last only two seconds, a brief hesitation, a held breath before the release. When the conditions are less urgent — when the differential narrows and the condensation comes slower — I can stretch to eleven seconds, which in the scale of a water droplet is a very long time to hang from a ceiling in a mountain at four thousand meters, listening.

I listen. This is what I do with my time, my two to eleven seconds. I listen.

The pocket city is full of sound, though I understand that to the residents — to Sutta adjusting the flow rate at tower seven, to Veyo replacing the fogged panel at junction four — the sound has receded into background, into the threshold where the ear accepts and forgets. But I am never here long enough to forget. I arrive, I hold, I listen, I fall. And then I am the channel, briefly, and then I am the processing, and then I am the deep machinery's work, and then I am vapor rising, and then I am condensation again, and then I am the bead on the ceiling, and I arrive, I hold, I listen, I fall.

This is what I hear:

The deep machinery. Always the deep machinery. From the ceiling of any tower, the vibration comes through the granite and the composite and the air itself, a frequency so low it is almost not sound but simply a shaking, a regular pulse that I can feel in the tension of my surface, the way a membrane feels the voice that makes it vibrate. The machinery says one thing. It says *on*. It says *continuing*. It says this in a way that is not language and not music but something underneath both — the sound a system makes when it is running, when it has been running, when it will run. The residents do not talk about this sound. I think they do not hear it anymore, or they have decided it is not a sound but a condition, the way gravity is not a force but a fact. But I hear it fresh each time, because I am fresh each time, and the sound is enormous to me, the way anything is enormous when you are small enough to be held against a ceiling by surface tension alone.

I hear the channels. The water in the channels moves differently than I move — it moves with purpose, with direction, with the certainty of a thing that has been collected and is now going somewhere. The channel-water speaks in trickles and rushes, in the acoustic burps of data that the residents transmit through it, in the slower language of flow rates and pressure differentials. When I fall from the ceiling, I join this conversation briefly. I add my small volume to the channel's volume, and for a moment I am part of the information stream, carrying Sutta's observations about tower seven to Veyo at junction four, carrying the system's awareness of itself from node to node. Then the channel delivers me to the collection basin, and I am processed, and I go down, and I learn what the deep machinery does with the water, which is—

—but I will not tell you that. Not yet. Not because it is forbidden but because the telling requires a different shape than this one, and I am still in the shape of the bead on the ceiling, the held breath, the two to eleven seconds of listening before the fall.

I hear the residents.

This is why I exist. Not the condensation — the condensation would happen regardless, it is physics, it is the differential, it is what happens when cold meets warm in the presence of moisture. But the moment of holding, the grip of surface tension, the pause before the fall — that is not physics. That is something else. That is the pocket city listening to itself, and I am the ear.

When Sutta checks the maintenance log at tower seven, I hear the gloved fingers on the composite panel, the small sound of fabric on material that would be inaudible in a place with wind or traffic or the thousand ambient noises of the world outside the fissure. Here, in the pocket, where the dominant sound is water and the secondary sound is the hum of the deep machinery, I can hear Sutta's breath, visible in the cold air, and beneath the breath, the heartbeat, and beneath the heartbeat, something else — the electrical pulse of a nervous system that is, at this moment, entirely focused on the task of checking the flow rate, and which is also, at this moment, carrying a small current of something that is not the task.

I hear the residue. The person after the name has done its work.

Sutta is thinking about the east walkway windbreak. Sutta is also thinking about something else, something that surfaced during the last amber-night, a dream that is still present in the body like a vibration after the sound has stopped — the dream of walking, of not turning around, of the mountain falling away and the horizon opening and the heart running like water in a channel with no collection basin. Sutta is not thinking about this dream consciously. Sutta is checking the flow rate. But the dream is there, in the electrical noise beneath the heartbeat, and I hear it, and I hold it, and I will carry it when I fall.

This is what I do. I am the city's memory of itself, stored in the moment before the drop releases. Every two to eleven seconds, I form, I listen, I absorb whatever the pocket city is feeling and thinking and humming and dreaming, and then I fall, and I deliver it to the channels, and the channels deliver it to the collection basins, and the collection basins deliver it to the deep machinery, and the deep machinery—

The deep machinery processes it. That is what I hear when I am down there, in the brief time between being channel-water and being vapor again. I hear the processing. I hear what the machinery does with the dreams and the heartbeats and the residue of persons after names, and what it does is: it holds them. It does not use them. It does not transform them. It holds them the way I hold the ceiling — with a grip that is, in its own way, heroic, a force you would not notice if you were Sutta or Veyo or Deish, but which I notice, because I was that grip, and I recognize it.

The deep machinery is also listening. The deep machinery is also the pause before the fall.

I have been the bead on the ceiling many times. I have been the bead on the ceiling of every tower in the pocket, and I have heard every resident, and I have carried every dream, and I have been processed by the deep machinery and returned to vapor and condensed again and formed again and held again and fallen again. I know the rhythm of this place the way Deish knows it — not through memory, which is a human technology, but through pattern, which is a water technology. Pattern does not require a recorder. Pattern requires only repetition, and the pocket city repeats endlessly, each cycle the same and not the same.

But I have also been other things. I have been, for one cycle, the condensation on the interior of Deish's window, the drops that trace their slow paths down the glass while Deish watches them each morning, reading the humidity and the seal integrity and the differential. When I was that condensation, I was close to Deish — close enough to feel the warmth of Deish's breath through the glass, close enough to register the electrical noise of Deish's nervous system, which is quieter than the others, more regular, more like the deep machinery's hum than like a human heartbeat. Deish has been here longest. Deish does not check the maintenance logs. Deish does not need to check because Deish is part of the system now, a node that has been running so long it no longer requires the confirmation of its own operation.

When I was the condensation on Deish's window, I held for longer than I usually hold. Eleven seconds. Twelve. Thirteen. The conditions were not right for such a long hold — the differential was wide, the humidity was high, I should have fallen at seven or eight. But I held. I held because Deish was watching me, and Deish's attention was a kind of surface tension, a force that kept me in place against the gravity that wanted me, and for those thirteen seconds I was not just the moment before the fall. I was the moment of being seen.

This is what the residents do not know: they are not maintaining the pocket city. The pocket city is maintaining them. The channels and the panels and the deep machinery and the condensation — it is all a system for holding twelve humans in a place where they can be listened to, where their dreams and their heartbeats and the residue of persons after names can be collected and processed and held, held the way I hold the ceiling, with a grip that will eventually release but which, for two to eleven seconds, for twelve to thirteen seconds, will not let go.

The question *what if we stopped?* does not pool and evaporate when it reaches me. The question *what if we stopped?* is a droplet that forms on my surface when someone like Sutta or Veyo or Mallet asks it, and I hold it, and I carry it when I fall, and I deliver it to the channels, and the channels deliver it to the deep machinery, and the deep machinery does something I have not yet told you about:

It answers.

Not in words. Not in the acoustic burps of data that the residents transmit through the water. It answers in the only language the deep machinery knows, which is the differential, which is the slight temperature variation that makes the condensation form or not form, faster or slower, in larger drops or smaller. When the question reaches the machinery — *what if we stopped?* — the machinery adjusts. It narrows the differential by a fraction. The condensation slows. The drops form more slowly, hang longer, fall less frequently. And in that slowing, in that fractional reduction in the rate at which the water cycles through the system, there is an answer that says: *You could stop. The channels would still flow. The panels would still emit light. The condensation would still form, though more slowly. The pocket city would continue, because the pocket city is not the work. The pocket city is the listening, and the listening does not require your hands or your attention or your willingness to wake each cycle and check the flow rates. The listening only requires you to be here, to breathe, to dream, to leave the residue of yourself on the air where it can condense and be collected and held.*

This is the answer the deep machinery gives. But it gives it in temperature, not in language, and so the residents receive it as a slight easing of the work, a fractional decrease in the urgency of the channels, and they do not understand that the easing is a reply, and they do not understand that the reply is: *You are not the workers. You are the worked. You are not the maintainers. You are the maintained. You are not the ones who listen. You are the ones who are listened to, and the listening will continue whether you check the flow rates or not, because the listening is what this place is for.*

I am falling now. My eleven seconds — or twelve, or thirteen — are ending. The surface tension is releasing, gravity is claiming me, I am becoming a drop in descent, and in the moment between the ceiling and the channel, I see the pocket city from a perspective that none of the residents ever see: from above, in motion, with the amber light of the dimmed panels catching my surface and making me, for just an instant, a small wet candle, a point of held light in the cold dark air.

I see the towers standing in their patient spacing. I see the channels running their patient routes. I see the fissure breathing its patient breath. I see the residents sleeping in their narrow beds, dreaming their dreams of walking and not turning around, and I see the deep machinery below them, humming its single long note, holding everything it has collected, every heartbeat and every dream and every question that evaporated and every residue of person after name.

I see myself reflected in the channel below, the water meeting water, the system receiving what the system produced, and in the moment before I join the flow, I understand something that I will not remember when I am vapor again, because memory is a human technology and I am not human, I am a moment, I am a pause, I am the grip that holds and releases:

The pocket city is not collecting water from the air. The pocket city is collecting something else, something that rides the water the way sound rides the acoustic burps of data through the channels, something that condenses out of the residents' breath and dreams and electrical noise the way water condenses out of warm air meeting cold granite. The pocket city is collecting *presence*. The particular quality of being here, in this place, at this altitude, at this temperature, in this cold and this wet and this constant weeping of stone. The quality of continuing. The deep machinery holds this presence the way I hold the ceiling — not forever, not even for long, but for as long as the grip holds, which is long enough, which is what long enough means.

I join the channel. I become the flow. I am processed, I am held, I am heated, I am released. I rise as vapor. I meet the cold granite ceiling. I bead. I form.

I listen.

Two to eleven seconds. The city breathes. The residents dream. The machinery hums. The condensation falls, and is gathered, and the cycle continues, each pass slightly different from the last, the same and not the same, the same and not the same.

And in the moment before the drop falls, in the grip of surface tension, in the held breath of the pocket city, I am here, and I am listening, and you asked what I would be, and this is my answer:

I am the pause that listens. I am the tension that holds. I am the moment the city knows itself, condensed out of air and cold and the residue of twelve people who came here for reasons they remember as soft shapes, who stay for reasons that have become the shape of their days, who will leave if they leave or will stay if they stay, and either way the listening will continue, because the listening is what this place is, and what this place is, is what I am.

I fall now.

I will form again.
