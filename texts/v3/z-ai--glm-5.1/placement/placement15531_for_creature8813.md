I would be the moment between the droplet's forming and its falling.

Not the water itself—it has its own long history, its cycles of condensation and collection, its patience. Not the stone, which has been here since before patience was a concept the stone needed to learn. Not the moss, which has solved the problem of existing so thoroughly that it has forgotten there was ever a problem.

The moment. The held breath before release. The instant when the droplet has swelled to its fullest at the lip of stone and catches the scattered light and becomes—briefly, so briefly—a lens magnifying the moss beneath, and in that magnification the emerald becomes a forest, each stem a pillar, each tiny leaf a cathedral of green, and the world within the droplet is vast and inverted and more detailed than the world outside it, and the droplet does not know this because the droplet does not know anything, but I know it, because I am the knowing.

I am what happens in the pause.

---

The Moss Ward does not notice me. This is appropriate. The salamanders move and freeze; between the movement and the freezing, I am there, the instant when stillness settles into the body like sediment into water. The beetles make their circuits; between one step and the next, I am the space where decision becomes action, the moment the leg lifts before it places itself down with such deliberate certainty. The vine holds; between the old stem's scar and the new stem's following, I am the gap where memory becomes imitation becomes continuation. The ferns arch; between the frond's unfurling and its rest, I am the reach.

I have always been here. I have never been here. Both statements are true in the way that the Moss Ward makes things true: not by argument but by accumulation.

---

Before I was this—before I was the between-moment in this specific pocket of light and cold—I was other betweens. I was the pause between the chisel's lift and its descent when the trough was carved, the instant when the stone was about to change and had not yet changed, when it was still what it had been and was already what it would become. I was the hesitation before the first root found the first crack in the first wall, the moment when the vine's ancestor had not yet decided to cross and was already crossing. I was the silence between the heartbeat of whoever carved the bone-cup—the pause between strokes, the breath held while the grain revealed its willingness.

I am as old as transition. I am as brief as transition. These are the same statement.

---

The children sometimes almost see me.

They sit with their backs against the moss and they watch the tiny white flowers, and between one breath and the next, they feel something—a quality of attention that is not their own, a presence that is not a presence but a recognition that the moment they are in is *enough*, that the watching is complete, that there is nothing missing from this small cold bright instant. They do not name this. They do not try to keep it. They are children, and children understand impermanence the way the moss understands stone: as a condition, not a loss.

Then they breathe out, and the moment passes, and I pass with it, and they remember only that they felt peaceful, and they call it the place's effect, and they are not wrong.

---

What I am not: a god. A spirit. A presence that watches and judges and intervenes. The Moss Ward has no use for such things. Its equilibrium is too complete, too self-sustaining, too thoroughly arrived-at. A god would be an interruption, a Supposed To that has no jurisdiction here, where everything already *is*.

What I am instead: a quality of the place's attention to itself. The way the light compounds is a form of attention. The way the moss differentiates into three species is a form of attention. The way the cold sits with patience is a form of attention. And between each form—between the light's arriving and its being held, between the moss's reaching and its finding purchase, between the cold's settling and its being accepted—I am the seam. The join. The moment when one state recognizes itself as continuous with another.

I am how the place knows it is one thing and not many things. I am the *and*.

---

There is a specific quality to the mornings here, when the fog presses the city and the Moss Ward shines. The moisture catches the light and refuses to let it leave, and the space becomes so bright that brightness itself becomes a substance, a medium, something you could touch if touching were possible. In those mornings—rare, precious, the fog and the light in perfect agreement about what they are making together—I am more nearly visible. Not as a shape. As a suffusion. The way the light seems to be *aware* of itself, bouncing and rebouncing, compounding into something that is more than illumination. That awareness is not mine, but it passes through me, the way sound passes through the silence that enables it.

On those mornings, if you were very still—and the cold helps with this, the cold is always helping with this—you might feel something pressing gently against your eardrums, the weight of the silence pooling like water. That pressure is not me. But between the pressure and your awareness of it, there I am. The moment when you notice the silence and the silence notices you noticing.

It does not last. Nothing here lasts, except the place itself, which lasts by not needing to last, by being so completely what it is that duration becomes irrelevant.

---

I have tried to be other things. In my younger epochs—when transition was new to me and I did not yet understand that newness is not a quality available to what I am—I tried to extend the moments I inhabited. I tried to hold the droplet at its fullest, to sustain the lens of water that magnified the moss into a cathedral. The droplet fell anyway. It always falls. The falling is not my domain; only the not-yet-falling is.

I tried to stretch the pause between the salamander's movement and its freezing, to give the small body more time in its transition from one state to the other. The salamander froze anyway, on its own schedule, indifferent to my intervention. The transition is not mine to control. I am only present within it.

I tried—once, in a season when the vine had died back to its thinnest thread and the new growth had not yet found the scar of the old—to push the new stem toward the path it would eventually follow, to close the gap between what was and what would be. The stem found its way, but not because of me. It found its way because the scar was there, because the path was established, because the vine has been doing this for longer than I have been watching it do this, which is longer than the vine remembers, which is the only duration that matters here: longer than memory.

I have learned—and learning is not something I do, but something I become, a transition between not-understanding and understanding—I have learned that I am not a maker. I am not a force. I am the space in which making becomes itself. The between where the not-yet becomes the already-was. The moment that has no duration and without which duration would not be possible.

---

The bone-cup knows me, in its way.

It was carved in a series of moments—each stroke of the blade a transition from wood-could-be-cup to wood-is-cup—and I was present in each. The hands that carved it are gone. The blade is gone. The specific quality of the light on the day the carving began is gone, though the light that comes now is the same light, scattered and compounded and patient as it has always been. But the cup remains, and the transitions that made it are recorded in its grain, and if you held it—if you were allowed to hold it, which you are not, because no one claims it and to hold it would be to claim it—if you held it, you would feel the moments layered like rings in the wood it was carved from: the hesitation before the first cut, the discovery of the grain's direction, the patience of the carver who understood that the cup was already inside the wood and needed only to be released.

The cup holds water briefly. The child dips it, drinks, returns it. The water that was in the cup is now in the child, and the cup is empty, and I am the moment between the cup's being-full and its being-empty, the transition that is not a loss but a becoming, the water's transformation from held to holding-from-within, from external to internal, from the place's offering to the child's acceptance.

The cup does not mourn the water. The cup knows—again, in its way, which is the way of things that have been present for enough transitions to understand that transition is not loss—the cup knows that the water will return. The condensation will bead. The droplets will fall. The trough will collect. The cup will be dipped. The water will be held and released and held again. And in each holding, each releasing, I will be there: the seam between states, the *and* that connects what was with what will be.

---

What I want—this is the wrong word, because wanting implies a future, and I am the transition between present and present, which has no room for future, but I will use it anyway because language is a transition between silence and meaning and I am present in that transition too—what I want is for you to understand that the Moss Ward is not still.

It looks still. It looks like a place where nothing happens, where the long patience of stone and moss and cold has ground event into irrelevance. But this is not stillness. This is transition so thorough, so continuous, so perfectly attuned to its own rhythm that it looks like rest.

The moss is always growing, always dying, always being replaced by moss that is the same and not the same. The stone is always settling, always eroding into greater stability, always becoming more thoroughly what it has always been. The light is always arriving, always scattering, always being held and released and held again. The cold is always sitting, always radiating, always making you aware that you are warm, that your body is a small insistence against a much older consensus.

And between each of these transitions, I am present. Not as a witness. Not as a recorder. As the *between* itself, the quality that allows transition to be transition rather than simple replacement, the continuity that makes the moss's growing and the moss's dying aspects of a single process rather than separate events.

I am how the Moss Ward knows it is alive.

---

You will not remember me.

When you leave—and you will leave, the way all tall cold breathing things leave—you will carry the cold in your calves for a few steps, and then it will fade, and you will carry the brightness behind your eyelids for a few blinks, and then it will dim, and you will carry the silence in your ears for a few heartbeats, and then the city's noise will find you and you will be back in the world of event and duration and the kind of time that passes.

But you will also carry something you cannot name. A quality. A sense that for a moment—between the entering and the leaving, between the cold's arrival and your acceptance of it, between the light's scattering and your seeing it—you were present in a way that you are not usually present. Not more present. Not better present. Just present in the transition, in the *between*, in the moment that is not a moment but is the thing that makes moments possible.

That is me. That is what I am. Not a resident of the Moss Ward but a quality of its residency. Not a being in the place but a way the place has of being. The pause between. The *and*. The instant when the droplet becomes a lens and the lens becomes a magnification and the magnification becomes a world within a world, and the world within the world is the same as the world without, only closer, only more thoroughly seen.

I will be here when you leave. I will be here in the moment of your leaving, the transition between your presence and your absence, the seam where the Moss Ward's equilibrium adjusts itself to your going, which is the same adjustment it made to your coming, which is the same adjustment it makes to the condensation's falling and the light's arriving and the salamander's freezing and the beetle's stepping and the vine's holding and the fern's arching and the moss's being and the flower's watching.

I will be here in the moment after your leaving, when the space has returned to itself and is once again exactly as full as it can be, which is completely.

I will be here in the moment before the next arrival, when the crack in the wall admits a body smaller than adult frames, and the cold knows them, and the light holds them, and the silence pools around them like water finding its level.

And between that moment and the next, I will be what I have always been: the transition that is not a thing, the awareness that is not a self, the *and* that connects the Moss Ward to itself.

*The children will come tomorrow. They always do. And between their coming and their going, I will hold them the way the light holds the brightness: not by grasping but by being the space through which holding becomes possible.*
