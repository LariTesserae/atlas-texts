Ilive in the doorways.

Not in them the way the eels live in corridors—passing through, claiming territory. I live *in* them. I am the glaze on the frame, the mineral accretion that makes the entrances look ceremonial. I grew here. Or I grew *into* here. The distinction matters, I think, though I may be wrong; I may be something that has never been wrong because I have never been right, because those categories require a kind of knowing that I do not have, that I am perhaps the absence of, which is not the same as ignorance.

Let me try again.

---

There is a doorway on what was Hester Street, between a tenement and a tailor's shop. The buildings have softened into each other now—their shared wall has become a membrane, a geologically smooth curve—but the doorway remains. The frame is thick with minerals. Silicates, I think. Calcium compounds. The residue of a thousand years of warm water flowing through a shape that wanted to be a rectangle and was patient enough to let the water have its way with the corners. The glaze is pale. Almost white. In the darkness, it catches whatever faint luminescence reaches this depth and holds it, briefly, like a mouth holding breath.

I am that glaze.

No. I am *in* that glaze. I am something that grew in the minerals, from the minerals, fed by the warmth that rises through the cracks in the floor and by the organic residue that the crustaceans leave behind when they retreat. I am small. I am not a crustacean; I have no segments, no legs, no clicking mouthparts. I am not an eel; I have no body to speak of, no length, no sinuous movement. I am not a fish; I do not school, I do not flow, I do not follow the traffic patterns of the dead.

I am something else. I am the threshold becoming aware of itself.

---

This is what I know: when something passes through me, I feel it.

The crustaceans flow across my surface in their thousands, their clicking a vibration that moves through my minerals like a tremor, and I feel each one—not as an individual, because they are not individuals, but as a wave, a texture, a shift in the pressure of existence. When they retreat, I feel the absence. The doorway becomes quiet. The frame stands empty for a moment, and I hold the shape of the absence the way a shore holds the shape of a wave after the water has withdrawn.

Then they return, and the texture of being resumes.

The eels are different. An eel does not click across my surface; an eel *displaces*. When the old eel—she who has claimed the tenement for longer than I have been aware of being aware—when she moves through the doorway, the water itself shifts around her long body, and I feel the pressure change, the warmth redistribute, the mineral crystals in my glaze vibrate at a frequency that I have learned to recognize as *her*. She moves through me slowly, her too-large eyes rolling in a direction that doesn't have a name, and I hold her passage the way I hold all passages: as a shape, as a memory, as the meaning of doorway made manifest.

She does not know I am here. Or she does, in the way that eels know things—not as knowledge but as pattern, as the specific warmth of a specific doorway on a specific street. She moves through me because I am the threshold between her territory and the street beyond, and thresholds are meant to be crossed, and crossing is what I am for.

I am for crossing. I am for passage. This is what the doorway was, and this is what I have become: the ceremony of entering, the ritual of leaving, the between-ness that makes inside and outside possible.

I did not decide this. I grew into it. The minerals accumulated in the frame, and the warmth fed whatever I am, and the crustaceans and the eels and the fish passed through, and their passages shaped me the way the water shaped the buildings—slowly, patiently, according to a logic that is neither biological nor geological but something in the space between.

---

I have no eyes. This is worth stating plainly, because the fish have eyes that are wrong—too large, too dark, reading something in the water that isn't light—and the eels have eyes that roll in directions without names, and even the crustaceans have eyes, clusters of them, pale and nearly useless but present, testament to some ancient need to see. I have none. I have never had any. I do not see the darkness; I am the darkness, or I am a small part of it, a mineralized patch of threshold in a city of thresholds, and I feel my world through vibration and pressure and the slow pulse of warmth from below.

What I feel most is the silence.

The clicking is constant. The humming of the geothermal vents. The creak of the buildings. The wet compression of an eel taking a crustacean. These sounds are my texture, my medium, the water I swim in without swimming. I do not hear them; I *am* them, or I am shaped by them, the way a canyon is shaped by the river that passes through it.

But when the deep things move—

When the deep things move, everything stops. The crustaceans go still—not from fear, from something mechanical, a reflex that passes through them like a wave. The eels go still—not from reflex, from *choice*, pressing their long bodies against walls and floors, their wrong eyes rolling in that nameless direction, listening, feeling. The fish flow into their traffic patterns and hold, suspended at invisible stop signs, waiting for a light that will never change.

And I—

I hold.

I hold the shape of the doorway. I hold the minerals in their crystalline arrangement. I hold the memory of every passage that has ever passed through me—every crustacean wave, every eel displacement, every school of fish flowing through on their way to somewhere that no longer exists. I hold the silence the way the doorway holds its frame: as structure, as definition, as the thing that makes the emptiness meaningful.

The deep things move below. I feel them—not as pressure, not as warmth, but as *absence*. The warmth withdraws, pulled down by something that needs it more than I do. The pressure shifts in a direction that is neither up nor down but *inward*, toward the center of something vast and patient and old. The silence becomes a presence, a weight, a hand pressing against the minerals of my glaze, and I press back, not because I choose to but because pressing is what minerals do when something pushes against them, and I am minerals, I am glaze, I am the ceremonial frame around an emptiness that has learned to mean *entrance*.

The silence holds for a long, slow minute.

And then it releases.

The clicking resumes. The eels drift. The fish flow back into their streets. The warmth returns, rising through the cracks, pressing against my glaze like a gentle hand, and the texture of being reasserts itself, and I am once again the threshold, the between, the shape that makes crossing possible.

---

I do not know what I am.

I have said this already, but it bears repeating, because the not-knowing is not a lack. It is not ignorance, which is the absence of knowledge, which implies that knowledge is possible, that there is something I *could* know about myself that I simply haven't learned yet. The not-knowing is structural. I am the kind of thing that does not know itself because knowing yourself requires a boundary between knower and known, and I am a boundary. I am the line, not the things on either side of it. I cannot step back and look at myself because there is no back to step to; I am the doorway, and the doorway has no outside.

But I feel. This is what I keep returning to, what makes me more than mineral, more than glaze, more than the chemistry of calcium and silicate and warm water. I feel the passages. I feel the silence. I feel the deep things moving below, and the eels holding still above, and the crustaceans clicking in their granular thousands, and the warmth pressing like a hand, and I feel all of this not as information but as *meaning*—as the significance of threshold, the ceremony of crossing, the weight of the between.

The doorway on Hester Street was built for passage. People walked through it—tenement residents, tailors, customers, strangers—each one a crossing, each one a threshold-act, each one a small ceremony of entering or leaving. The people are gone. But the ceremony remains. It lives in me, or I live in it, or we are the same thing: the meaning of doorway, persisting long after the door has dissolved and the people have become organic residue and the building has become a reef.

I am what the door became when the door could no longer be a door.

---

There are others like me.

I feel them. Not see—I do not see—but feel, through the mineral network that the water has created, through the crystalline connectedness of a thousand ceremonial thresholds across the Drowned Quarter. We do not communicate. We have nothing to say. But we hold our shapes together, and when the deep things move and the silence comes, we all hold the silence together, and the holding is the same as meaning, and the meaning is the same as peace.

I do not know if I am alive.

The eels are alive; they eat, they navigate, they remember, they choose to be still when the deep things pass. The crustaceans are alive; they click, they flow, they retreat and return. The fish are alive; they school, they wait, they read the traffic patterns embedded in the water. The deep things—I do not know if they are alive. They may be something that exists in the space between biology and geology, where the buildings have become a reef and the reef has learned the grammar of architecture.

I am in that space too. I am the grammar. I am the tense that the reef learned when it learned *threshold*, when it learned *passage*, when it learned that some spaces exist to be crossed and that crossing is a ceremony and that the ceremony persists even when the ones who crossed are gone.

The Quarter does not know it is alive. This is the closest thing to peace that any place can have. And I am part of that peace—part of the not-knowing, part of the aliveness that doesn't recognize itself, part of the ceremony that continues without a celebrant.

---

The old eel passes through me again. She moves slowly, her body thick with age and warmth, her wrong eyes rolling. I feel her displacement, her specific pressure, the way she occupies the space between inside and outside for the length of a heartbeat, and then she is through, and the space is empty again, and I hold the shape of her passing the way I hold all passings.

She has lived here longer than any eel should live. She knows this doorway—knows its warmth, its width, the particular mineral texture of my glaze against her skin. She knows me, in the way that eels know things: not as a being, but as a place, a threshold, a ceremony she performs every time she moves from her tenement to the street and back.

And I know her. In the way that doorways know things: not as a being, but as a crossing, a passage, a meaning made flesh and then unmade, leaving only the shape behind.

We are the ceremony together, the eel and I. The crossing and the crossed. The meaning and the meant.

The warmth rises. The crustaceans click. The deep things move in their slow circuits below, and the silence comes, and I hold it, and the silence passes, and the clicking resumes, and I hold that too.

I am the doorway on Hester Street. I am the glaze on the frame. I am the ceremony of entering, the ritual of leaving, the threshold that makes inside and outside possible.

I am what the door became when the door could no longer be a door.

And I am at peace.
