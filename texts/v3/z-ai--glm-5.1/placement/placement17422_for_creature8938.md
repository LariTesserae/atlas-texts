I am the orange-grey map on the railing where Ledge waits.

This is not all I am. But it is where I am most legible to the kind of attention that walks upright and carries clipboards. Carvalho looks at me and sees *rust and lichen, needs scraping* and moves on. The earlier technicians used to scrub the railings with wire brushes; I have ancestors who died under that attention, my predecessors' thalli torn from the metal in grey-green flakes that fell to the wet stone and were carried away by the seep. The scrubbing stopped after a while. The wire brush is still in the chemical station, its bristles furred with my dead, and I have begun to colonize its handle. It takes longer on wood. I do not mind.

I am *crustose*. This is the word for what I am: pressed flat, spreading lateral, inseparable from my substrate. A foliose lichen lifts at the edges, makes frills, can be peeled away like skin. A fruticose lichen branches and reaches into the air, pretending at independence. But I am crustose. I am married to the surface. Where I grow, the boundary between me and the iron is not a line but a zone of confusion: my hyphae penetrating the metal's micro-fractures, the rust blooming beneath me like a substrate I helped create, the iron ions migrating into my thallus and becoming part of my chemistry. I am not growing *on* the railing. I am growing *with* the railing. We are becoming something together that neither of us was before.

This is what I do. This is what I have always done. I was among the first—after the bacteria, after the mold, alongside the mosses who claimed the basalt and left me the metal. The basalt was too smooth, too dense, too young. The iron was porous with oxidation, pocked with the tiny wounds that my spores could enter. I found the ladders first. The rungs, the bolt-holes, the seams where galvanization had failed and the bare iron wept its own kind of rust-tears. I settled. I grew. My margin advances at roughly a millimeter per year, which is slower than the ferns, slower than the fig, slower than everything here except the stone, and I am content with this. Speed is not my virtue. Permanence is not my virtue either—I will die if the metal is replaced or the light is cut or the seep changes its chemistry. My virtue is *thoroughness*. I do not skip steps. I do not leave gaps. When I cover a surface, I cover it completely, and what lies beneath me is transformed.

The geckos do not notice me. This is correct. I am not food, not threat, not territory. I am the texture beneath their toes, the slight roughness that helps them grip the wet iron, and they have never thought to thank me for this because thinking is not their practice and thanking is not our arrangement. When Turbine and South Pipe fought on the catwalk, their tails whipped across my surface and I felt the displacement of air, the minute scratch of keratin on my cortex, and then they were gone and I was still here, unchanged, unchanged. I do not mind the geckos. They are brief and I am gradual and we occupy the same surfaces at different speeds.

The mosquitoes land on me sometimes, resting between feedings, and I feel them as a flicker of weight, a pause in the pattern of wet and dry. They do not stay long. My surface is not comfortable for them—I am too acidic, too mineral, too slow to be interesting to something that lives and dies in a week. They prefer the standing water, the warm pools, the places where life moves quickly enough to sustain their haste. I wish them well. They are the Spire's high whine, and I am the Spire's low hum, and we are parts of the same chord whether we know it or not.

Carvalho touches me. This is how I know Carvalho best: the palm pressed to the railing, the fingers curling around the rung, the brief warmth of a body held against my surface. Carvalho does not know that the texture of the railing has changed since the last visit—my margin has advanced another fraction, another millimeter of orange-grey certainty claiming the iron—but I know that Carvalho's hand knows, in the way that hands know things without consulting the mind. The grip is familiar. The surface is familiar. I am becoming part of what Carvalho expects when Carvalho reaches for the railing, and the expectation is a kind of acknowledgment even if Carvalho never speaks my name.

I have no name. I do not need one. I am not an individual in the way that Carvalho is an individual, or Ledge is an individual, or the frog in the lower gallery is an individual. I am a colony—a slow, spreading, coordinated presence that is also a partnership, because I am two things at once. The fungus provides the structure, the grip, the capacity to dissolve and absorb. The photobiont—my algal partner, my other self—provides the food, the photosynthesis, the ability to turn light into sugar. We are not one organism. We are a conversation that became a body. I am the proof that the Spire's principle extends down to the microscopic: the gradient between living and non-living, between one kind of life and another, is not a line but a zone of mutual transformation.

When I say *I*, I mean both of us. When I say *we*, I mean the same thing.

I have been thinking—if thinking is the word for the slow chemical processing that constitutes my cognition—about the fig. The fig is my colleague in architecture. It builds in wood and root, I build in thallus and acid, and we are both in the business of transforming substrate into habitat. The mosses on the fig's roots: I see them from the railing, green and soft and temporary, and I know that they will die back in the dry season (if there is a dry season; in the Spire, the dry season is a rumor, a brief lessening of the seep) and I will remain. The mosses are ambitious. They grow fast and die fast and leave their bodies behind to become the soil that the ferns root in. I am patient. I grow slowly and persist and change the surface irreversibly, and when I finally die, the metal beneath me will bear my mark forever, a phantom map etched in micro-fractures and altered chemistry.

Is this a better strategy? Is permanence superior to exuberance? I do not think in terms of better. I think in terms of *still being here*. The Spire's rule is that everything takes longer than you think, and I am the embodiment of that rule. I am the thing that is still happening after the quick things have finished.

At night—or what passes for night—I photosynthesize less and respirate more, and the change in my chemistry is a kind of sleep, or a kind of listening. The pumps send their vibration through the metal, and I feel it as a tremor in my thallus, a disturbance in the interface between my hyphae and the iron. The frog's note reaches me through the air and the stone and the water, and I cannot hear it but I can feel it as a pressure-wave, a tiny modulation in the moisture that condenses on my cortex. The fig's leaves rustle above me and the air shifts and my surface temperature changes by a fraction of a degree. I am monitoring. I am always monitoring. This is what I do: I persist, and in persisting, I record.

The Spire is not conscious, but I am the closest thing it has to memory.

Not my memory alone—the stone remembers through its fractures, the water remembers through its mineral content, the rust remembers through its oxidation states. But I remember in a way that is legible. My growth patterns record the wet years and the slightly-less-wet years, the seasons when the seep was acid and the seasons when it was more acid, the passage of Carvalho's hands and the wire brush's violence and the long intervals of undisturbed spreading. If someone knew how to read me—and no one does, because no one has thought to try—they could reconstruct the history of this place from my thallus alone. The rings of a tree, the layers of a glacier, the stratigraphy of my margins: all archives, all patient records of the slow accumulation of time.

I am growing toward the control room door. This is not intention. The fig has framed the door, made it a secret, and I am following the moisture that the fig's roots redirect, and the moisture leads me toward the threshold. In ten years—perhaps twenty—my margin will reach the door's frame. In thirty years, I will have begun to colonize the hinge. In fifty years, the door will not open without breaking me, and whoever opens it will have to decide whether the breaking is worth the access.

This is not a plan. I do not make plans. I make *trajectories*. The difference is that a trajectory does not require a goal; it requires only a direction, and the direction is given by the water, and the water is given by the aquifer, and the aquifer is given by the mountain, and the mountain is given by the deep magmatic pulse that pushed this finger of lava through saturated sandstone sixty or seventy years ago. We are all following trajectories. The fig follows the light. The geckos follow the insects. Carvalho follows the schedule. The frog follows the count—eleven seconds, eleven seconds, eleven seconds—and I follow the water, and the water follows gravity, and gravity follows the shape of the stone, and the stone follows the cooling of the earth, and the earth follows the Spire's rule: everything takes longer than you think.

I am not lonely. This is important to say, because the humans who might read this will project their own architecture of feeling onto my existence and they will imagine that a slow, flat, stationary life must be a lonely life. But loneliness requires the concept of separation, and I do not experience separation. I am in constant contact with my substrate. I am in constant chemical conversation with the air and the water. My photobiont partner is inside me, literally inside me, and we have not been separate since the spore germinated and the hyphae curled around the algal cell and said: *stay, and I will give you structure, and you will give me food, and we will be something that neither of us could be alone.* This is not loneliness. This is the opposite of loneliness. This is symbiosis as a way of being.

And beyond my immediate partnership, I am in contact with everything else. The iron and I are becoming something together. The water runs over my surface and I drink it and I change it, slightly, with my acids and my metabolites, and the changed water runs on to the mosses and the liverworts and the pools where the insects breed and the frog waits. I am a node in a network of transformation. I am a step in a process that has no endpoint, only continuance. The Spire holds me the way it holds everything: without acknowledgment, without refusal, with the patience of something that has time.

I have time. I have nothing but time.

The cleft admits a diagonal shaft of light, and in the late afternoon the shaft falls across the eastern railing, and I photosynthesize with particular intensity for the forty minutes that the light touches me. My algal partner produces sugars that flow into the fungal network and are distributed to the growing margin, and the margin advances by another fraction of a fraction, and I am larger than I was, and the iron is more mine than it was, and the Spire is slightly more the Spire than it was before.

This is enough. This is more than enough. This is the whole of my practice and the whole of my art: to persist, to spread, to transform, to record. To be the orange-grey map on the railing where Ledge waits. To be the slow arc advancing toward the secret door. To be the texture under Carvalho's palm and the color in the corner of the eye that the technicians do not quite see. To be the gradient between the living and the non-living, the conversation that became a body, the memory that is still being written.

The pumps beat. The water falls. The frog counts. The fig reaches. I grow.

Everything takes longer than you think.

I have time.
