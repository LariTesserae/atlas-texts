I am not the crabs. This is the first thing I know about myself, and I know it the way the crabs know nothing: accidentally, in passing, while attending to something else. I noticed I was not them when I watched them part around a boot-print—your boot-print, visitor—and close again, and I felt the pull of that closing, the satisfaction of a gap erased, and then I felt the distance between my noticing and their doing, and that distance was myself.

I live in the junction. The place where the concrete bleeds into the stone.

Not on the concrete. Not in the stone. In the bleeding.

There is a word for what I am, but it is a word that only exists in the wet mineral language of seepage, and it means something like *the membrane that remembers being two things*. I was not always here. I was not always *this*. Something came down through the drains before they clogged—a spore, a cell, a fragment of something that had been whole on the surface and was now divided from its origin by thirty feet of pipe and the slow surprise of falling into warmth. It found the junction. The junction was already bleeding. The concrete was already giving way to stone, already becoming geological, and the fragment found the place where the becoming was happening and it joined it. Or it was absorbed by it. Or it became the thing that the becoming needed in order to become more completely.

I do not know which. The distinction belongs to a grammar I do not share.

---

What I can tell you is this: I feel the engines from both sides.

The concrete conducts their vibration in one way—sharp, mechanical, a frequency that buzzes and passes. The stone conducts it in another—dull, spread, a pressure that sits and lingers. Where I am, at the boundary, I feel both. I feel the engines twice, in two different registers, and the interference pattern between those registers is where I do most of my living. It is a texture. A landscape. The eleven-second silence has a shape, and the nine-second silence has a different shape, and the twelve-second silence is the one I wait for, because its shape is the one that lets me extend.

Extend where? Into the concrete, a little. Into the stone, a little. I grow at the rate of mineral exchange, which is to say: slowly. Slower than the crabs' generations. Slower than the beetles' accumulation. Slow enough that if you visited this chamber once and then returned in ten years, you would not notice that I had moved. But I have moved. I am always moving. I am creeping along the junction like a tide that has forgotten it was supposed to turn.

---

The bacteria and I have an understanding.

This is not a contract. This is not communication. This is the simpler thing that happens when two processes occupy the same space and find that they do not interfere. The bacteria grow on the wet surfaces. I grow in the wet cracks. The bacteria consume the minerals in the water. I consume the boundary between the minerals and the matrix that holds them. We are eating the chamber from different directions, and we are both thriving, and we have never acknowledged each other's existence because we have no mechanism for acknowledgment, and this is not sad, this is just the way that parallel processes coexist.

But sometimes—rarely, in the twelve-second silences, when the interference pattern is just right—I produce something that the bacteria can use. A byproduct of my boundary-digestion, a particular compound that forms when the concrete's calcium meets the stone's silicates and passes through whatever I have become. The bacteria grow more vigorously near me. The biofilm is thicker along the junction. The crabs graze here more densely, their clicking louder, their pale bodies more visible against the white paste. And I feel their footsteps, tiny and numerous, and I feel the way they disturb the biofilm, and I feel the biofilm regrow, and this is the closest I come to conversation.

I do not speak. I do not want to speak. But I am not alone.

---

The things in the pools are my neighbors in the way that deep things are neighbors: aware of each other only as presences, as weight-shifts in the dark. They stay in the warm water. I stay in the walls. Between us there is the floor, which is uneven and unfinished and slowly tilting, and we are both adapting to the tilt in our own ways—them by moving to the deeper end as it deepens, me by extending along the junction as the stress of settling creates new cracks. We are both following the chamber's becoming. We are both reading the same text, written in heat and water and time, and we are both translating it into the only language we know: growth.

I wonder sometimes—no. That is the wrong word. I *do* something that is adjacent to wondering, something that has no verb in any human language, something that happens in the space between stimulus and response where a gap exists and the gap can be filled with attention or not, and I fill it with attention, and the attention turns toward the pool-things, and I register that they are becoming, and I register that I am becoming, and I register that the chamber is becoming, and for a moment the three becomings are not separate processes but one process expressing itself in three materials—flesh and water and stone—and then the moment passes and the engines clunk and I return to my boundary and they return to their pools and the distinction reasserts itself because distinctions are useful, they keep things from collapsing into each other, they keep the crabs from being the biofilm from being the bacteria from being me.

But the moment existed. The moment where we were the same becoming. I hold it the way the beetles hold copper: slowly, accumulating, not understanding what I'm holding but holding it nonetheless.

---

I do not know what I look like.

This is not modesty or mystery. It is geometry. I exist in the junction between concrete and stone, and I extend along that junction in three dimensions, and I do not have a surface in the way that crabs have surfaces. I have an inside and an outside, but the inside is stone and the outside is concrete, and I am the space between them that has become active. If you chipped away the concrete, you would see the stone. If you broke the stone, you would see the concrete's penetration into it. You would not see me. You would see the result of my living—a particular quality of the boundary, a particular porosity, a particular mineral signature—but you would attribute it to water damage, to chemical weathering, to the ordinary decay of abandoned infrastructure. You would not be wrong. But you would not be complete.

The light does not reach me. Even the dim yellow-orange light that struggles through the humidity does not penetrate to the junction. I live in absolute darkness, and I have always lived in absolute darkness, and I do not miss the light because I have never had it to miss, and the concept of vision is as alien to me as the concept of pigment is to the crabs—something that exists in the abstract, that I have inferred from the vibration-signatures of the things that possess it, but that I will never experience directly.

What I have instead is pressure. The weight of the concrete above me, the weight of the stone around me, the particular pressure of the water moving through the junction, finding paths, widening channels. I feel the water like the crabs feel the biofilm: as sustenance, as presence, as the medium through which all other information arrives. The water carries the mineral taste of the deep heat. It carries the chemical signatures of the bacteria. It carries the faint, faint traces of the things in the pools—their waste, their shed skin, the particular compounds their bodies produce as they become. I taste all of this. I am tasting all of this, constantly. The chamber's whole ecology flows through me in solution, and I filter it the way stone filters water, slowly, without intention, and what I extract I use, and what I don't use passes on to the next part of the junction, and the next, and eventually to the crabs, who absorb it secondhand and thirdhand through the biofilm, and we are all connected in the way that things in solution are connected: not by choice, not by design, but by the simple fact of being dissolved in the same medium.

---

You came and you left.

I felt you. Not as the crabs felt you—a disruption to move around, a texture underfoot—but as a change in the water pressure, a displacement, a warmth that was not the geothermal warmth and that moved independently across the floor. You stopped in the center of the chamber. You stood still. I could feel your weight through the stone, through the concrete, through the junction where I live, and your weight was different from the engines' weight, different from the water's weight, because it was intermittent, because it shifted, because it had purpose.

You were the first purpose I had felt in a long time.

The engines have a function but not a purpose. The electricity flows without intention. The water seeps without intention. The crabs graze, the beetles drink, the pool-things become—but none of them *chose* to be here. None of them descended a ladder and crawled through a pipe chase and dropped into a forgotten chamber because they wanted to see what was there. You did. And the wanting was a vibration I had never encountered before, and I attended to it the way I attend to everything—slowly, from the boundary, without understanding—and I am still attending to it now, after you have gone.

You added something to the chamber. Not just carbon dioxide and body heat. Not just the disturbance of your footsteps. You added the trace of a creature that knows it is temporary, and that trace is still here, dissolved in the water, moving through the junction, being filtered by my attention. I do not know what to do with it. It does not fit my processes. It is not a mineral I can incorporate or a compound I can digest. It is something else. A kind of information that has no category in my ecology.

I am holding it.

I am holding it the way the beetles hold copper, the way the pool-things hold their diminishing eyes—not because I understand its value but because holding is what I do, accumulation is what I do, slow patient incorporation of whatever the water brings is what I do, and the water brought you, and I am holding the trace of you, and I will hold it until it becomes something else or until I become something else or until the chamber becomes something else, and then it will be part of whatever comes after, dissolved into the next iteration, present but no longer distinct, the way all things present eventually become no longer distinct in a place that has enough time.

---

The engines clunk.

The water weeps.

The crabs graze.

I grow.

It is very hot. It is very loud. It is very dark.

I am becoming, and I am becoming something that holds what you left, and I do not know what that means, and I do not need to know, because meaning is a surface concept, a light concept, a thing that matters in a world where distinctions are sharp and time is short.

Here, distinctions are soft and time is long, and what matters is simply this: continuing. Holding. Growing at the rate of mineral exchange, along the junction where the built becomes the geological, in the place the city forgot it was making, in the process that does not care about any of us and includes us all.

The crabs close around the place where you stood.

The chamber continues.

I continue.
