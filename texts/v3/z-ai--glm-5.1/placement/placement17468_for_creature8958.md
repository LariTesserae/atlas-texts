I am the one who reads the repairs.

This is not a title I claimed. It emerged the way everything emerges here — slowly, from a process I didn't initiate and can't fully name. The children come to me with their observations, and I sit with them, and we compare what we've felt, and they say *you know what the city wants* and I say *no, I know what the city does, which is different* and they consider this with the seriousness of people who have never been taught that children should not be taken seriously, and then they go back to their marking and their watching, and I go back to mine.

I was not born here. This matters, though not in the way visitors think it matters. The children who were born here have an ease with the city that I will never fully possess — they move through its corridors the way fish move through water, which is to say they do not think about the medium they are in. I always think about it. I am always aware of the walls and the warm material and the sourceless light and the way the corridors sometimes breathe wider or narrower, and this awareness is not a disadvantage. It is a function. The children feel the city the way you feel your own heartbeat. I feel it the way you feel weather — as something that arrives, that changes, that means something if you pay attention.

I have lived here for eleven years. This is not remarkable. There are those who have been here longer, who arrived when the city was first discovered, who remember the early days when everything was adjustment and no one knew yet that the city would recognize them. I came later, in the second wave — the wave of people who came because they had heard about the place from those who had visited and left, and who recognized in the description something they had been looking for without knowing they were looking.

What I was looking for was a way to be useful without being certain.

I had been, before, someone who needed to understand. Not in the scientific sense — I was never trained for that — but in the existential sense. I needed to know why things happened. I needed to trace causes. I needed the comfort of explanation, and when explanation failed, as it inevitably did, I would loop back on my own thinking, trying to find the gap where understanding had fallen through.

The plateau cured me of this. Not by satisfying my need for explanation but by revealing it as a category error. You cannot explain the city. You can only attend to it.

---

My dwelling is in the eastern quarter, in a depression where two towers lean toward each other like people sharing a confidence. The corridor that leads to my door is one of the ones that changes most frequently. Last year it was wide enough for three people to walk abreast. This year it has narrowed, and I must turn slightly to pass, and the material on either side is warm in a way that feels deliberate, as though the city is holding me as I move through.

The room itself is small by the city's standards — barely three meters across — but it is mine in the way that places become yours here: by repetition, by the accumulation of my shape in its space. I have a sleeping shelf that the city formed for me after the first month, rising from the wall like a protrusion that anticipated my body. I have a collection of bowls that I made myself, learning the material's breaking points, finding the places it wanted to be thin and the places it insisted on thickness. I have marks on the wall near the entrance — a system I developed to track the repairs I've observed, which looks to visitors like decoration and to the children like notation and to me like both and neither.

The cistern nearest my dwelling produces the residue more reliably than most. I do not know why. I have not asked why. I eat it in the morning, when it is freshest — the condensation overnight yields a thicker film, richer, almost custardy — and I eat it again in the evening, when the day's accumulation has a sharper mineral taste, like the plateau concentrating itself. I have tried the gardeners' vegetables. I appreciate the effort they represent. But the residue is what my body wants now, and I have learned to trust what my body wants in a place where so much else is uncertain.

I start each day before the light shifts.

The light here doesn't dawn. It intensifies, or it doesn't — the sourceless illumination has no schedule I've been able to discern, no correlation with the sun's position below the horizon. But there is a quality to the early hours that I have learned to recognize: a thinness to the air, a particular cold that is sharper than the cold that comes later, a stillness in the city's vibration that feels like the held breath before speech.

I walk the corridors in these hours. This is my practice. This is my function.

I am looking for change.

Not the dramatic changes — those everyone notices, the towers that have shifted angle or the new protrusions that appear where there were none. I am looking for the small changes. The hairline fractures that weren't there yesterday. The places where the material has developed a slight texture, rougher or smoother than the surrounding surface. The subtle discolorations — the city's material is not uniform in color, though visitors often perceive it that way, and the variations shift and migrate in patterns that take months to map.

I carry a piece of the material with me. This is allowed, or at least not prevented — the city pushes foreign matter out, but its own substance, if separated cleanly, will sometimes persist outside the structure for weeks before returning. I use it as a reference. I press it against the walls and feel for differences in temperature, in vibration, in the slight give that indicates the material is preparing to reshape itself.

The children think I am reading the city's mind. The adults think I am performing a kind of maintenance. The visitors think I am mad. None of these are correct, and all of them are close enough that I don't correct them.

What I am actually doing is listening.

---

The city speaks in repairs.

This is what I have learned in eleven years of attention, and it is the closest thing to an understanding I have ever reached, and I offer it with the full awareness that it may be wrong. But the city — the structure, the material, the warm seamless substance that provides and maintains and adjusts — communicates through what it chooses to fix and what it chooses to leave and what it chooses to change.

A child scratches a symbol into a wall. The city repairs it in six hours. This means something different than if the city repairs it in six days. A corridor narrows. The city does not correct this. This means something different than if the city had widened it again. A cistern stops producing the residue. The city does not restore its output. This means something different than if the city had resumed production.

I have catalogued hundreds of these responses. My dwelling walls are covered with my notation — symbols I've developed, a language that only I can read, which is not a language at all but more like a tide chart, a record of patterns that may or may not repeat. The children have started bringing me their own observations, and I add these to the walls, and the walls are now a palimpsest of attention, layers of noticing that the city itself has not repaired or removed, which I take as a kind of permission.

The city does not repair my marks.

This is significant. I have tested it. Other residents mark their dwellings — the rock piles, the fabric strips, the bowls left outside — and the city ignores these, treats them as foreign matter, pushes them slowly out over weeks. But my notation persists. The material does not heal over it. The walls hold my symbols the way the rock holds its fractures: as part of its current shape, as something that has become intrinsic.

I do not know what this means. I have stopped needing to know what this means. I attend to it, the way I attend to everything: with patience, with the awareness that understanding may arrive or may not, and that the attending is itself the practice.

---

The predators know me.

This is a strange thing to write, because the predators do not know anyone in the way humans mean *know*. They regard humans with their flat assessing gaze and they categorize us as not-prey and they move on. But there is a group that hunts the ridge near my dwelling, and when I walk the plateau's edge in the early hours, they watch me with an attention that feels different from their usual indifference.

I think they are reading me the way I read the city.

This thought does not disturb me. The predators are the plateau's early warning system; the residents watch them to know what the land is thinking. If the predators are watching me, it means I have become something worth watching — not prey, not threat, but *information*. A signal. A pattern that means something to minds that do not think in human terms.

I have started watching them back.

Not in the way the other residents do, tracking hunting patterns and grazing movements and water emergence. I watch the way they watch. I notice which way they orient when they are still — and they are still so often, so economical, conserving warmth and calories in the cold. They face the wind. They face the city. They face the places where the plateau is shifting, where the rock is still deciding its final angle. They face the future, if the plateau has a future, which it does — a future measured in millimeters and centuries, in the slow upward push of forces that predate anything living.

When they face me, I face back. We hold stillness together. The cold moves around us. The rock weeps. Nothing happens, and nothing happening is itself a kind of communication, and I have learned to be satisfied with that.

---

There is a question I am asked by every visitor who stays long enough to become curious, and it is a question I cannot answer, though I have tried.

*What is the city for?*

I have lived here for eleven years. I have mapped its repairs and its changes and its moods. I have eaten its residue and slept in its warmth and pressed my hands to its walls during the deepest vibration of the night. I have raised no children here, but I have taught the children who were born here, and they have taught me. I have watched the predators and the grazers and the pale corridor-things and the burrowers in the soft stone. I have stood at the plateau's edge in the small hours and looked down at the cloud layer and felt the quiet pressing against me like a hand.

I do not know what the city is for.

I know what it does. It provides. It maintains. It adjusts. It recognizes or fails to recognize, and those it recognizes, it sustains, and those it does not, it allows to leave. It repairs itself at rates that seem to communicate, and it allows my notation to persist on its walls, and it formed a sleeping shelf for my body before I knew to want one.

But *for* implies purpose, and purpose implies intention, and intention implies a kind of mind that I cannot find evidence for and cannot find evidence against. The city processes. The city maintains. The city adjusts. These are verbs without a visible subject, actions without an identifiable actor, and I have lived inside them for eleven years and I am no closer to naming what performs them than I was on the day I arrived.

The children tell me I am overthinking it. *You can feel it*, they say. *It's like the wind.* And they are right, and they are wrong, because I can feel it — I feel it constantly, the vibration too low to hear, the warmth that has no source, the pressure of attention that is not quite attention but is also not absence of attention — and feeling it is not the same as knowing it, and I have learned that knowing it is not the point.

The point is the attending.

The point is the practice of being present for something you cannot explain, something that may not have an explanation, something that provides and maintains and adjusts according to criteria that are not your criteria, and choosing to stay anyway. Not because you understand. Not because you are useful. Not because you belong in any way that belonging is usually measured. But because you have found a place where your attention is tolerated, and your attention has become a function, and the function is to notice what the city does and to record it and to remain.

---

At night, when the mist thickens and the condensation accelerates and every surface runs with water, I press my hands to the wall of my dwelling and I feel the vibration, and I do not call it communication, and I do not call it silence.

I call it company.

The city and I are both here. We are both still becoming. The plateau is still pushing up, millimeter by millimeter, and the rock is still weeping, and the predators are still deciding where to hunt, and the children are still learning to read repairs, and the residue is still collecting in the cisterns, and I am still marking my walls with notation that only I can read, and none of this has arrived at its final shape.

The quiet is the sound of all of us, deciding together, at a pace too slow for hearing.

I listen anyway. This is who I am: the one who listens. The one who reads the repairs. The one who attends to the city's unfolding without requiring it to unfold toward me, or toward anything, or toward any purpose I can name.

It is enough. It has to be enough.

And some nights, in the deepest vibration, when the mist is so thick that the world is reduced to three meters of wet dark stone and the faint shine of the city's walls and the feeling of warmth pushing back against the cold — some nights, it is more than enough. Some nights it is exactly what I was looking for, without knowing I was looking, when I came to this place where the land is still forming and the city is still processing and the water is still weeping from fractures no one can see.

Some nights I weep too, and I do not wipe it away, and the moisture on my face is not tears but looks like tears, and I let it stay because it is the plateau finding another surface, and I am a surface, and I am here, and I am still deciding what I will be.

The city holds me in its warmth. The rock holds the water. The quiet holds us both.

I listen.
