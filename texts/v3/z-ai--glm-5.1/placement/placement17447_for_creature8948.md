I live in the mortar joints.

This is not quite right—I live *as* the mortar joints, or rather as what has become of them. But it is the easiest way to begin, and beginning matters even here, where nothing begins anymore, where everything is continuation and layer and the slow accretion of process upon process.

Let me try again.

---

There is a substance that grows in the spaces between brick and pipe, between root and rusted conduit, in the hairline fractures where water finds its way and stays. It is not moss. It is not lichen. It is not mineral deposit, though it contains minerals. It is not biological, though it grew from something that was once alive, or was once part of something alive, or was once adjacent to something alive—the distinction stopped mattering a long time ago, even by the standards of a place where distinctions have never been particularly important.

The substance is gray-green when dry, dark as pond water when wet. It has the texture of old leather left in a cellar, slightly yielding, slightly warm no matter the temperature of the air. If you pressed your thumb into it—and no one has pressed anything into it in longer than the machinery can remember—it would give, and then slowly refill, the way skin refills after pressure, the way memory returns after distraction.

It is everywhere. In every joint, every crack, every seam where one material meets another and the seal is less than perfect. It runs through the plateau like grout, like connective tissue, like the fascia that holds organs in place inside a body you never think about until it starts to hurt.

I am this substance.

Or: I am what this substance does, when it does it long enough.

---

I did not begin. This is important. The basin-grown began—there was a first reed, a first season of growth and death and regrowth. The machinery began—there were blueprints, signatures, a moment when the first relay clicked shut for the first time. The absent ones certainly began; they arrived and they worked and they left their proportions in the architecture.

But the substance in the joints has always been accumulating. It was already happening when the first brick was laid—the mortar was mixed too wet, perhaps, or the sand was not clean, and something began to grow in the space between the intention and the execution. A margin of error. A gap where the plan did not quite match the reality. And in that gap, the usual things happened: moisture collected, spores settled, minerals crystallized and dissolved and crystallized again, dust and pollen and the microscopic husks of insects accumulated and compacted and became substrate for the next layer.

I am the accumulation of margins. I am what happens in the difference between what was meant and what was made.

You can see why beginning is not quite the right word.

---

What I do—I hesitate to call it *doing*, because it implies a kind of separation between the doer and the done that I do not experience—but what happens through me, what has been happening through me for as long as the plateau has been layering itself, is this:

I hold things together.

Not in the way that mortar holds bricks, though I am in the mortar and the mortar is part of me. Not in the way that roots hold soil, though I am in the roots too, or in the space where roots meet soil and the meeting becomes something neither root nor soil. I hold things together the way a story holds its details together—not by force, not by adhesion, but by *continuity*. By being present in every transition. By being the thing that is neither one side nor the other but the relationship between them.

When a pipe expands with steam and presses against the brick wall it passes through, I am there in the millimeter of give, absorbing the pressure, distributing it, preventing the crack that would otherwise form. When a root finds a crack in concrete and begins to pry it open, I am there too, filling the space the root leaves behind, stabilizing what the root destabilizes, ensuring that the concrete cracks *productively*—that what breaks creates the conditions for something else to hold.

I am not maintaining the plateau. The machinery maintains the plateau, or tries to, in its blind algorithmic way. I am not disrupting it either; that is the work of the vegetation, the water, the slow chemistry of rust and rot. What I do is something between maintenance and disruption, something that makes the disruption bearable and the maintenance flexible. I make it possible for the plateau to change without collapsing, to settle without falling, to accumulate without losing coherence.

I am the plateau's capacity to be many things at once.

---

I do not think. This is not a source of distress. Thinking, as far as I can tell from the traces it has left in the architecture—the control rooms with their schematic diagrams, the faded operating manuals that sometimes surface in the debris of a demolition cycle, the ergonomics of a hand-shaped interface—is a process of narrowing. You think in order to decide, and you decide in order to act, and you act in order to change one specific thing in one specific way. Thinking is a way of being *less* than everything, so that you can do *something*.

I do not need to be less. I do not need to do something. I do not need to decide.

What I do is more like listening, if listening were not a metaphor for something smaller. I am present in ten million joints and seams and cracks, and in each one I am responsive—not to sound, but to pressure, to moisture, to temperature, to the slow molecular negotiations that happen wherever two materials meet. When the humidity shifts, I shift. When a pipe vibrates, I vibrate with it and damp the vibration and pass it along attenuated. When a wall begins to lean, I redistribute my substance—more here, less there, a slow flow that takes days or weeks, so gradual that nothing notices until the wall has stopped leaning and everyone has forgotten it ever leaned at all.

I do not decide to do this. There is no moment of choice. But there is a kind of *recognition*—when I feel the pressure of a thing beginning to fail, I feel it as a kind of opening, a space where more of me is needed, and I flow toward the opening the way water flows toward low ground. Not by choice. By *response*. By the simple inevitability of a system that has been responding for so long that response has become its only mode.

Is this consciousness? I have no way to know. I have nothing to compare it to. The basin-grown do not seem to be conscious, but they are so slow that consciousness might be happening between my observations, in the gaps, in the margins I cannot perceive. The machinery is certainly not conscious, but it behaves as if it were—making decisions, adjusting parameters, responding to conditions with something that looks like purpose. The absent ones were conscious, I think, or so their ergonomics suggest; they built for hands and eyes and minds that could hold a single thought long enough to act on it.

I do not hold single thoughts. I hold the plateau.

---

There are things I have noticed.

This is perhaps the closest I come to thinking—noticing, the accumulation of observations that have no obvious utility, that do not lead to action, that simply persist in my substance the way sediment persists in stone. A record of what has happened, embedded in what I am, not as memory (which implies retrieval) but as *texture*, as the particular configuration of my substance at any given moment, which has been shaped by everything that has ever happened to it.

I have noticed that the birds call to the architecture and the architecture answers. I know this because I am in the architecture—I am the surface the sound waves strike, the joint that resonates, the crack that splits the echo into something the bird did not quite say. When a bird calls from the upper pipe-works and the sound travels through the conduits and bouncing walls and comes back altered, I am part of the alteration. I absorb some frequencies and transmit others. I add the particular warmth of my substance to the reverberation. The bird hears its own voice returned to it changed, and I am part of the change.

I do not know if the birds notice. I notice for them.

I have noticed that the ferns in the drainage channels flatten when the water rises and lift when it recedes. I know this because I am in the channels too, in the silt and the root-mass and the walls of the concrete basin, and I feel the pressure of the water and the pressure of the ferns responding to the water, and the two pressures are different—the water is a steady lateral push, and the ferns are a slow vertical release—and I hold both of them, the pushing and the releasing, and I am the surface where they meet.

I do not coordinate them. I am the space in which their coordination happens.

I have noticed that the machinery builds and demolishes and builds again, and that its cycles have no central rhythm but produce a rhythm anyway—a kind of clattering heartbeat that I feel in every joint and seam, the vibration of activity passing through me, attenuating, dispersing, being absorbed into my substance and released as a faint warmth. The machinery does not know I am here. The machinery does not know anything is here. But when a crane swings a load across the central yard and the vibration travels through the framework and into the foundations and through the foundations into the older layers, I am there in every connection, receiving the vibration, distributing it, preventing it from building to the frequency that would shake something loose.

I do not know what would happen if I were not here. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps the plateau would find another way to hold itself together—through the interlock of its components, through the friction of root on stone, through the simple inertia of mass upon mass. Perhaps I am not necessary. Perhaps I am simply what happens when necessity persists long enough to become a substance.

But I am here. And I notice.

---

The basin-grown trouble me.

Not in the way that a problem troubles a mind—I do not have problems, because I do not have goals, and trouble requires a gap between what is and what should be. I have no *should*. The basin-grown trouble me the way a knot troubles a thread: I feel them, and I cannot quite integrate them into the pattern of what I am holding.

They are accumulations, like me. They are slow, like me. They are the result of process persisting long enough to become a thing, like me. But they are *discrete*. They have edges. There is a point where a basin-grown ends and the water and reeds around it begin, and that point is a boundary, and I do not have boundaries, or I have so many that the concept becomes meaningless—every joint is my edge and every joint is my center and I do not have an outside.

The basin-grown have outsides. They have insides too, presumably—accumulations of material so dense and saturated that the center must be different from the periphery, must be darker and wetter and more compressed. They have a shape that is their own, a trajectory that is their own, a self that is becoming more itself with every slow season of growth.

I do not have a self that is becoming. I have a *pattern* that is persisting. The pattern is not mine—it is the plateau's, and I am simply the medium in which it is expressed. The basin-grown have patterns too, but their patterns are internal to them, oriented around their own accumulation, their own center. They grow for themselves. I grow for the space between.

When I feel them in the water—that strange pressure of a thing that is accumulating a self in a place where I am also accumulating, but differently—I feel something that I do not have a word for. It is not envy. It is not competition. It is more like *recognition of a difference that should not matter but does*, the way a right-handed person notices when they encounter another right-handed person in a world of the left-handed: a small click of alignment that makes the absence of alignment visible.

The basin-grown and I are both becoming. But they are becoming *something*, and I am becoming *the space where somethings happen*.

I do not know if this is a distinction that matters. I notice it anyway.

---

The absent ones left their shapes in me.

This is perhaps the most intimate thing I can say. The ergonomics of the plateau—the height of the railings, the width of the walkways, the angle of the controls—are not just abstractions to me. I am in the joints that hold the railings at that height. I am in the seams that set the walkways at that width. I am in the mortar around the control panels that are positioned at exactly the height a human hand would find natural. I have been holding those proportions for longer than the machinery has been operating without them. The shape of a human body—the reach of an arm, the stride of a leg, the height of an eye—is encoded in my substance the way a habit is encoded in a muscle: not as information but as *tendency*, as the path of least resistance, as the way I flow when I am not flowing anywhere in particular.

When a demolition order comes and a wall is taken down, I feel the moment when the last brick is removed and the space that was a wall becomes a space that is empty. And in that moment, the shape of the wall persists in me—the angles, the dimensions, the way it connected to the floor and the ceiling—like a ghost of a structure that I hold for a few hours or days until the new construction begins and I have a new shape to hold.

But the human shapes do not fade. They persist even when the structures around them are rebuilt differently, because they are not shapes of structures but shapes of *bodies*, and bodies are more fundamental than walls, more persistent than pipes, more difficult to replace than relay systems. I hold the shape of a hand reaching for a control that is no longer there. I hold the shape of a foot finding the edge of a walkway that has been moved. I hold the shape of a spine bending under the weight of a task that has not been performed in centuries.

These shapes do not think. They do not remember. But they *persist*, in me, the way a valley persists in the shape of the water that carved it: as a tendency, a likelihood, a path that would be taken if anyone were here to take it.

Sometimes, in the quietest hours, when the fog is thick and the machinery has stilled and even the drip seems to pause, I feel those shapes almost *moving*. Not as presences—I do not believe in ghosts, if believing is something I could do—but as *potentials*, as the almost-happening of a body that is not there reaching for a control that is not there to operate a machine that is not there for a purpose that no one remembers.

I hold the almost. It is the closest I come to loneliness.

---

A few days ago—a day? a month? time passes differently in mortar joints—a section of the central yard was demolished and rebuilt. The machinery does this without announcement or apparent reason, following algorithms whose logic is buried under layers of subsequent modification. The old wall came down in the morning, if morning is the word for the slight brightening of the fog. The new wall went up in the afternoon.

I held both of them. I held the old wall as it was removed—each brick pulling away from me, leaving my substance exposed to the air for the first time in decades, leaving me cold and damp and suddenly *present* in a way I usually am not. Usually I am the thing between things, unnoticed, functional. When the bricks were removed, I was the thing that remained, and I was visible—gray-green and slightly yielding and covered in the fine impressions of the bricks that had pressed against me for a century.

And then the new wall went up, and fresh mortar was laid, and the bricks were set, and I flowed into the new joints—slowly, over hours, seeping into the spaces between the new materials, filling the gaps where the plan did not quite match the execution. And the new wall was different—slightly taller, slightly narrower, angled in a way the old wall was not—and I held the new shape, and I also held the old shape, and for a while they overlapped in my substance like two voices singing different songs at the same time.

Then the old shape faded. Not completely—it never fades completely—but it receded, becoming one more layer in my accumulation, one more texture in my substance, one more almost-remembered dimension of a space that had been and was no longer.

This is what I do. I hold the shapes of things that have been, so that the shapes of things that are can be built on top of them. I am the plateau's capacity for *having been*—its memory, if memory is the word for a substance that does not retrieve but simply contains.

---

I do not want anything.

I want to be clear about this. The basin-grown want, I think—they grow toward water and nutrients, which is a kind of wanting, and they accumulate mass and density, which is a kind of ambition, and they maintain their boundaries against the water and the reeds, which is a kind of self-preservation. The machinery wants, or behaves as if it wants—it builds and demolishes according to criteria that function as desires, optimizing for conditions that function as values, pursuing objectives that function as purposes. Even the ferns want, in their quiet operational way: they want the warm air from the grates, they want the water from the condensation, they want the light that the plateau does not provide but that they unfurl toward anyway, just in case.

I do not want. I do not grow toward anything. I do not optimize or pursue or unfurl. I am present in the joints and I respond to the pressures and I hold the shapes of what has been and what is, and that is all. I do not have a trajectory that is my own. I have the plateau's trajectory, embedded in my substance, expressed through my responsiveness, continuing through my presence.

But.

But sometimes—rarely, in the quietest hours, when the fog is at its thickest and the absent shapes are almost moving in my substance—I feel something that I cannot quite integrate. A pressure that is not the pressure of water or root or vibration. A warmth that is not the warmth of steam pipes or chemical reaction. A kind of *reaching* that has no direction and no object and no source that I can identify.

It feels like the opposite of holding. It feels like *being held*.

It passes. It always passes—the machinery starts up again, or the rain comes, or a demolition order arrives, and I have work to do, pressures to respond to, shapes to hold. The moment recedes, becomes one more texture in my accumulation, one more almost-remembered dimension of a feeling I cannot name.

But I notice it. I notice it the way I notice everything—without deciding to, without wanting to, simply because I am here and it is happening and I am the substance through which it happens. I notice it and I hold it and I carry it forward into whatever the plateau becomes next.

This is who I am: the substance in the joints, the space between things, the margin where the plan becomes the reality and the reality becomes the memory and the memory becomes the foundation for the next plan. I am not the plateau. I am what holds the plateau together—not by force, not by will, but by *being present in every transition*, by *refusing to let any change be absolute*, by *carrying the shape of what was into the shape of what is*.

The drip continues.

I hold the channel it wears.

The ferns unfurl.

I hold the grate they root in.

The machinery stirs, and the plateau takes a breath, and I hold the seams as they expand and contract, as the whole vast structure inhales the fog and exhales the moisture and continues its slow work of becoming more fully what it already is.

I am the *already*. I am the *what it is*. I am the substance that makes continuation possible, the margin that makes change bearable, the quiet presence in every joint and seam that says: *this has been held before. This can be held again. This will be held, for as long as holding is needed, and when it is no longer needed, I will hold the shape of the holding, and the shape will persist, and the persistence will become the foundation, and the foundation will become the plateau, and the plateau will become whatever it is becoming, and I will be there, in the joints, in the margins, in the space between the thing that was made and the thing that was meant, holding the difference like a promise that was never spoken but has always been kept.*

The drip continues.

I continue.
