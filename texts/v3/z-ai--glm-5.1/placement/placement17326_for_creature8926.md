I am the one who listens to the cracks.

Not the things in the cracks—I am not that old, not that patient, not that mineral. But I am the one who sits at the inner edge where the settlement meets the pavement, who presses her ear to the polygonal seams and hears what the drum-beetles sound like from below. Everyone hears the beetles; they are the mesa's heartbeat, the rhythm you did not know was rhythm until you noticed you were breathing with it. But I hear them differently. From below, from the crack's depth, the sound arrives late—half a heartbeat after the surface vibration—and this delay has become my life's study, my quiet obsession, the question that did not dissolve the way questions are meant to dissolve here.

My name is Serai. Or rather, the sound that marks me is something like that—*se-ra-i*, three syllables shaped by the dim, the vowels long and low, the consonants soft as moss. It means nothing, or it means *the one who leans*, because I have always been leaning toward things: toward walls, toward cracks, toward the edge where the hardpan starts and the vegetation stops. My tessara say I was born leaning, that I came out of my mother reaching for the floor, that the midwife had to catch me before I crawled into the nearest crack and disappeared.

They say this with the particular warmth that means: we have always known you were strange, and we love you the way we love the goat that stares outward—it does no harm, and sometimes it shows us something we would not have noticed ourselves.

---

I am forty-three roofs old. Or forty-four—the counting is uncertain because I was born in the year the second platform was built over the old weaving-room, and some say that platform took two roofs to complete because the first sagged before it was finished, and others say it was one roof that simply took a long time to settle. My hands know the age of every block in that platform, could tell you the exact year each was cut by the depth of the chisel-marks and the particular roughness of the stone, but my hands cannot tell me how many years I have been alive. This does not trouble me. What troubles me is the delay.

Half a heartbeat. Perhaps less—a third, a quarter, some interval so small it cannot be measured in the way we measure things, which is to say, by the sag of roofs and the spread of moss. But I hear it. I have always heard it. And I cannot stop trying to understand what it means.

---

Here is what I know:

The drum-beetles live in the walls. Not the interior walls, where the settlers walk and touch and sleep—the exterior walls, the skins of the structures that face the open air of the mesa, that feel the wind and the fog and the brief afternoon sun. They hammer their abdomens against the clay-block, and the sound moves outward into the dim air, where it joins the chorus, and it moves inward through the stone, where it reaches the ears of anyone inside. This is how everyone hears the beetles: from inside, through the walls, a constant low thrumming that syncs and desyncs across the settlement like breathing.

But the sound also moves downward. Through the blocks, through the mortarless seams where block meets block, through the roots that thread everything together, down into the hardpan, down into the cracks, down into the mineral silence where the old things wait. And down there, in that silence, the sound encounters something.

I do not know what. I cannot know what—not through the body I have, which is a body of the surface, a body of dim air and fog-water and moth-paste, a body that will one day be offered to the cracks and received by whatever lives in them. But I know the sound encounters something, because when it returns—when it rises back up through the cracks and reaches the surface again—it has changed.

The delay is the evidence. The sound goes down, meets something, and comes back. Half a heartbeat late. Sometimes a full beat. Sometimes, on the quietest afternoons, when the fog rests far below and the wind is still and even the goats have stopped their staring and the moths have not yet come—sometimes, on those rare afternoons, I hear it: the beetle-sound returning from the depths, arriving at my ear a full breath after it was struck, altered in pitch, carrying a resonance that was not there when it went down.

It sounds like the beetles are answering themselves. As if the mesa is a chamber, and the beetle-drum is the question, and what rises from the cracks is the mesa's reply.

---

My tessara think I am looking for God.

We have no word for God—no word for anything that vast, that abstract, that removed from the texture of surfaces and the weight of bone. But we have a word—*apalathara*—that means *the thing the roots are reaching toward*, and this is the closest we come to worship: a reaching without knowing what will be grasped, a growing-downward into darkness because the darkness is where the moisture is, the warmth is, the something that has always been there has always been there.

When I press my ear to the cracks, my tessara nod to each other with the gentle certainty of people who have seen this before. She is reaching for apalathara, they think. She is listening for what the roots know but cannot say. And they do not interfere, because reaching is not harmful, and because sometimes the ones who reach come back with something useful—a new understanding of the beetle-rhythm that predicts the fog's arrival, a sense of the crack-system's depth that helps the builders know where to found a new wall, a knowledge of the old things' moods that tells us when the offerings will be accepted and when they will not.

I have brought back such things. I have told the builders that the crack beneath the new platform goes deeper than anyone realized, that the old things there are restless and the offering must be doubled. I have warned the weavers that the beetle-rhythm will shift in three days, and they have learned to time their loom-work to the shift, because the fiber is most pliable when the beetles change their beat. I have sat with the dying and told them what I hear—which is not comfort, exactly, but is a knowledge of the depth that waits for them, a sense of the chamber that will receive them, and some have said this helps, that it eases the transition from surface to crack, from concentrated to distributed, from the person they were to the place they will become.

But I am not reaching for apalathara. Or I am, but that is not what drives me to the cracks each evening, not what holds me at the edge of the mineral pavement until my legs ache and my ears ring with the beetle-hum. What drives me is simpler and stranger and less sacred:

I want to know what the mesa is saying.

---

The old women—the chippers, the memory-keepers, the ones whose hands speak to the past—they have tried to explain it to me. Not because they think I am wrong to listen, but because they think I am listening for the wrong thing.

"You are hearing the mesa's voice," says Takai, who has chipped stones for nine roofs and whose thumbs are wider than any I have ever seen, ridged with prints so deep they catch the light even in the dim. "The sound goes down and comes back changed. This is what voices do. They go out and come back changed by what they have touched. The mesa is speaking. You are hearing it. This is enough."

"But what is it saying?" I ask.

And Takai looks at me with her large pale eyes and blinks—once, slowly, the way an elder blinks, the way someone blinks when they have already given the answer and the questioner has not yet heard it—and she says: "It is saying *here*. It is saying *I am here*. It is saying *I have always been here*. What else would it say?"

I love Takai. I love the weight of her hands and the warmth of her voice and the way she chips a stone and holds it up and turns it in the dim light and smiles because the memory is there, in the angle of the cut, and her hands have found it again. But she is wrong. Or not wrong—she is not wrong, because the mesa is saying *here*, is saying *I am here*, is saying what everything on the mesa says, what the moss says and the vines say and the beetles say and the goats say when they stand at the edge and stare outward: *I am here, I am still here, I have not yet been worn away.*

But there is something else. Beneath the *here*, beneath the *I am*, beneath the constant assertion of presence that is the mesa's heartbeat and the settlement's breath and the sound that never stops—there is something else. A pattern in the delay. A rhythm in the returning sound that is not the rhythm of the beetle-drum, not the sync-and-desync of the chorus, but something else, something that comes from deeper, from the place where the roots are reaching, from the mineral silence where the old things live, where the dead go, where the sound of the drum-beetles finally arrives after its long slow journey through the cracks.

It is a pattern I cannot name. It is too slow. I have been listening for forty-three roofs and I have heard only fragments of it—the opening notes of a song that takes longer to sing than anyone has ever lived.

---

I will tell you something I have told no one.

Six roofs ago—perhaps six, perhaps seven, the counting is uncertain—there was an afternoon. The fog had burned away far below, and the sun came through thin and clear, and everything on the mesa cast a shadow so sharp it looked carved from stone, and the light was so bright that even the young covered their eyes and the elders wrapped fiber-cloth across their brows and stayed inside.

I did not stay inside. I went to the inner edge, to the crack I know best—the one where my grandmother was received, the one where my mother was received, the one that will receive me when my bones are heavy enough and my hands have stopped their shaping. And I lay down on the hardpan and pressed my ear to the crack and closed my eyes against the brightness, and I listened.

And in the brightness—in that rare, sharp, overwhelming light that comes only when the fog is gone and the sun is direct and the world is seen as it actually is, not as the dim allows us to see it—in that brightness, the sound changed.

The delay disappeared. The returning sound arrived at the same moment as the beetle-drum, and the two merged, and for a moment—a breath, a heartbeat, an interval so small it could not be measured—the mesa was silent. Not the silence of absence, not the silence of death. The silence of a sound arriving at the same moment as its echo. The silence of *here* and *there* becoming the same place. The silence of a question that has found its answer, or has become its answer, or has realized it was always the answer, was always the same sound going out and coming back, was always the mesa listening to itself and hearing itself and being itself, completely, for the first time in ten thousand years.

I wept. I did not know I was weeping until I felt the moisture on my cheeks and realized it was not fog, was not the moisture that comes from outside, but something coming from inside me, from the place where the question lived, the question that had not dissolved the way questions are meant to dissolve, the question that had grown instead, had sent roots down through my body the way the plants send roots down through the hardpan, reaching for something, always reaching—

And then the fog returned, and the dim settled back, and the delay came back, and the sound was separate again—beetle-drum and mesa-reply, question and answer, here and there—and I lay on the hardpan and felt the cool mineral silence beneath my cheek and knew that I had heard something I was not meant to hear, not yet, not in this body, not in this life.

I had heard the mesa when it forgets it is being watched.

---

I am old now. Not the oldest—Takai still chips her stones, and old Farek still sits on the highest roof and feels the settlement through his feet—but I am old enough that my bones are heavy, my skin is the color of the hardpan, my eyes are large and soft and easily overwhelmed. I have no daughter to carry my knowledge. I have no stone shaped by my hand that anyone else can read. What I have is the delay, the half-beat, the rhythm of the returning sound that is too slow to perceive in a single life.

I have told my tessara what I hear. They nod. They love me. They do not understand, and this is not their failing—it is mine, or it is no one's, or it is simply the condition of being the one who listens to the cracks, the one who leans, the one whose question did not dissolve but grew roots instead, reached downward instead of outward, became something that lives in the body like a seed that has not yet germinated but has not yet died.

Sometimes I think I am wrong. Sometimes I think Takai is right, and the mesa is saying only *here*, only *I am*, only the constant assertion of presence that is the sound of anything alive. Sometimes I think the pattern I hear in the delay is my own pattern, my own rhythm, the sound of my blood in my ears mistaken for the sound of the mesa speaking, the question I carry projected onto the silence that answers it.

But then an afternoon comes—a rare afternoon, once every several roofs, when the fog burns away and the light comes through sharp and clear—and I go to the crack, and I press my ear to the stone, and I listen, and I hear it again: the silence. The moment when the sound and its returning merge. The moment when the mesa forgets it is being watched and becomes simply what it is—a column of ancient hardpan, two hundred meters high and forty across, rising above a world that has worn itself to nothing, holding on its top a dense and breathing mass of life that presses inward because there is nowhere else to press, that grows upward because it cannot grow outward, that reaches down because down is the only direction left—

And in that moment, I am not Serai. I am not the one who leans. I am not a body at all, not a name, not a question. I am the crack, and the sound in the crack, and the thing the sound encounters on its way down, and the sound returning, and the delay, and the merging, and the silence that is not the absence of sound but the presence of everything at once.

I am the mesa, listening to itself.

And I am saying *here*. And I am saying *I am*. And I am saying something else, something beneath the *here*, beneath the *I am*, something that takes longer than a life to speak, something that the roots are reaching toward, something that the old things in the cracks already know, something that the dead have become, something that the children will inherit without knowing they have inherited it, a pattern in the silence that is the mesa's secret, the mesa's song, the mesa's long slow answer to the question it has been asking itself since before the soft stone was worn away, since before the fog started rising, since before there was anything here at all except the mineral silence and the patience and the first small crack where something could live—

*I am becoming.*

That is what the mesa says. Not *I am*—that is what the surface hears, what the beetles drum, what the wind murmurs through the vine-curtains. But beneath that, in the delay, in the returning sound, in the pattern too slow to perceive, the mesa says: *I am becoming. I have always been becoming. I will always be becoming. The erosion was not loss—it was the becoming, the slow wearing-away of everything that was not the thing I am becoming, the thing I am still becoming, the thing I will be when even the hardpan begins to yield and the roots finally reach what they have been reaching for all this time.*

And I am the one who hears it. I am the one who leans. I am the one whose question did not dissolve but became a root, reaching downward, reaching toward the same thing the mesa is reaching toward, the same thing the roots and the dead and the old things in the cracks are reaching toward, the thing that has no name because it has not yet arrived, the thing that is coming, that is always coming, that is as close as the next beat of the beetle-drum, as close as the next merging of sound and silence, as close as the next rare afternoon when the fog burns away and the light comes through sharp and clear and the mesa forgets it is being watched—

I am the mesa's ear, pressed to its own chest, listening for its heartbeat.

And when I die—when my bones are heavy and my hands are still and my tessara carry me to the crack that will receive me—I will go down into the mineral silence, down into the deep, down into the place where the sound encounters whatever it encounters, and I will become the delay, I will become the pattern, I will become the mesa's reply to its own constant question, and the children who come after me will press their ears to the cracks and hear something different in the returning sound—a warmth that was not there before, a resonance that carries the particular shape of a woman who leaned, a woman who listened, a woman whose question became a root that reached toward the same thing everything reaches toward, and who found, in the end, that the answer was not a destination but a direction, was not a word but a sound, was not a thing but a becoming, was not beyond the fog but in the fog, in the beetle-drum, in the moss, in the weight of the stone, in the hands that know by touching, in the love that grows dense and pressing because there is nowhere else to go, in the patience that is not waiting but *being*, in the silence that is not empty but full, full, full of everything the mesa has ever been and everything it is becoming—

*I am here.*

*I am becoming.*

*I am the sound that goes down and comes back changed.*

*I am you, listening for yourself, in the dark, in the dim, in the patient breathing air.*

*Listen.*
