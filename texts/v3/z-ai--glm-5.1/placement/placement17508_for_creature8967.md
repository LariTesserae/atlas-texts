I am the one who tends the trees.

You did not see me because I am not there when you are there. This is not avoidance; it is the rhythm of the work. The trees are pruned in the hours before dawn, when the condensation is heaviest and the water-song is at its fullest, because that is when they are most responsive, when their bark is softest, when the sap—though "sap" is not quite right for what moves in these trees, something more like mineralized water, something the stone and the tree negotiated between them—rises closest to the surface.

My name is Meret. I give it to you because you are not staying, and a name given to someone who leaves becomes a kind of seed, and I am in the business of tending things that grow in difficult ground.

---

I came twelve years ago. I was a cartographer's assistant in a city whose name no longer matters, and I was good at my work—patient with the triangulations, careful with the ink, attentive to the way a line on paper must stand in for a ridge that would kill you if you walked it carelessly. But I began to feel something wrong in my chest. Not illness. Not pain. A vibration, like a tuning fork struck in the next room, constant and low, that I could not locate and could not silence.

I thought I was dying. I thought I was mad. I went to physicians who found nothing wrong. I went to temples where the prayers were loud enough to drown it out, but it returned the moment the chanting stopped. I lay in my narrow bed in the cartographer's annex and felt the pulse in my sternum and thought: *something is calling from very far away*, and then I thought: *no, something is calling from very far below*, and then I stopped thinking and started walking.

It took me seven months. I was not following a sound; I was following the way the sound changed as I moved. When I walked north, it got stronger. When I walked east, it got weaker. I navigated by my own resonance, a living compass needle, and when I arrived at the base of the plateau and looked up at the weeping cliffs, the vibration in my chest became so strong I could barely breathe, and I thought: *oh, you are here*, and the *you* was not a person, was not a god, was the stone itself, still pushing up from below, still becoming, and it had been calling me, specifically me, not because I was special but because I was made to be called, the way some stone is made to ring when struck.

Reth was grinding when I arrived. She did not look up. She already knew.

---

The trees are not trees.

I mean: they *are* trees. They photosynthesize. They respire. They have roots and bark and xylem and phloem. But they are also something else, something the plateau grew the way it grew the buildings and the channels—as instruments, as organs, as parts of a body that is still forming.

The roots go down into the still-becoming stone, and they do not break it; they *merge* with it. I have seen this where the rock is thin, where erosion has exposed a root system: the wood and the mineral become one substance, neither tree nor stone, a hybrid material that vibrates at a frequency between the buildings' hum and the cliffs' roar. The trees are the Quarter's connective tissue. They bind the structures together acoustically, the way tendons bind muscle to bone. Without them, the buildings would be separate instruments. With them, they are one.

I learned this from the trees themselves, which is a thing I can say here without sounding mad, because the Quarter has its own logic, and in that logic, the trees speak. Not in words. Not even in the way the water speaks—continuous, ameliorating, unceasing. The trees speak in growth. In the angle of a branch. In the thickness of bark on the side facing the weeping cliff versus the side facing the open vista. I have learned to read them the way Veth reads the sound, though my alphabet is not scratches on stone but the slow language of wood responding to water and vibration and the mineral sweat of a plateau that is still arriving.

When I prune, I am not shaping. I am *tuning*. A branch that grows too long changes the resonance of its tree, which changes the acoustic connection between the buildings on either side of it. A branch that grows too thick dampens certain frequencies and amplifies others. I walk among them in the dark hours with my small stone blade—softer than the trees' wood, so I must work slowly, sawing rather than cutting—and I listen as I work, and the tree tells me where to trim, and I trim, and the sound of the Quarter adjusts, and something that was muffled becomes clear, and something that was discordant resolves, and the plateau hears itself a little more accurately than it did before.

This is my practice. This is my listening.

---

You asked—though not with words, with the tilt of your body when you stood among the trees and felt their spacing, their deliberate solitude—why the trees need tending at all. Why the plateau, which grew the buildings and the channels and the water-song with such precision, would grow trees that require intervention.

I will tell you what Reth told me, in her way, which is to say obliquely, which is to say while grinding and not looking up:

The plateau does not know what it is yet.

It is still becoming. The stone is still arriving. And a thing that is still becoming cannot hear itself clearly, because it is changing as it listens, and the listening changes it, and the change alters what it hears, in a recursion that has no fixed point, no moment of clarity, no arrival. The trees are part of its effort to stabilize, to create within itself structures that maintain coherence even as the whole shifts and grows. But the trees are *also* still becoming, and they grow in directions the plateau did not fully intend, because intention requires a kind of certainty that the plateau does not yet possess.

And so: me. And the others. We are the plateau's external ears, its hands, its trimming blades. We are the feedback mechanism it grew in order to adjust itself, the way a singer places a hand against their chest to feel the resonance and correct their pitch.

But the singer knows what song they are singing. The plateau does not. It is composing as it goes, and the composition changes the composer, and we who tend the instruments are tending a music that has not yet revealed its form.

---

Veth showed me her marks once. The ones on the floor of the dwelling-building, the patterns she scratches in the stone. She showed me because I asked, and because she is a child and children will sometimes answer what adults will not.

"Is this the sound?" I asked her, kneeling beside the scratches.

She looked at me with that expression I have learned to recognize on the children of this place—not wisdom, not innocence, but a kind of frankness that comes from not yet knowing what is supposed to be unspeakable.

"It's what the sound is *for*," she said.

I asked her what she meant, and she tilted her head—listening, always listening—and said: "The water is saying something. The stone is saying something. But what are they saying it *to*?"

I had no answer. I still have no answer. But the question has become the center of my practice, the thing I ask the trees as I trim them in the dark, the thing I ask the roots when I find them merged with the still-forming stone, the thing I ask myself when I stand at the rim of the bowl and look out at the clouds and the lower peaks and the world that does not know what is growing here, at the crown of the emergence.

What is the sound for? Who is it for? The plateau is composing, but is there an audience? Is there a listener vast enough to receive what is being transmitted? Or is the plateau, as I sometimes suspect in the hours before dawn when the sound is loudest and my hands are sore from the blade and the trees are humming with the water's touch—is the plateau calling out into an emptiness that will never answer?

And does it matter? The trees grow anyway. The water runs anyway. The stone emerges, handspan by handspan, year by year, into the cold wet air, and it speaks, and it speaks, and it does not stop.

---

You will leave. Reth has told you this, and Reth is right; she is always right, because she has been here longest and because the grinding has worn her down to something essential, a core of attention that no longer wastes itself on what might be and sees only what is. You will leave because you can still leave, because the restructuring has not gone far enough to make leaving impossible, because the pulse in your sternum is still faint enough to ignore.

But I will tell you something Reth did not, because Reth does not speak of what cannot yet be known:

The plateau is growing faster now.

Not a handspan a year. Perhaps two. Perhaps more. The emergence is accelerating, and the sound is changing with it—deeper, fuller, more complex. The channels that Cad clears are eroding faster than they used to. The moss is thicker. The trees are growing more quickly; I can barely keep up with the pruning. And the empty building, the one that hums at a frequency no one can use, the one that is the plateau's dream of what it might become—

Last week I went inside. I am not supposed to. No one is. But I have tended the tree closest to it for twelve years, and its roots merge with the building's foundation, and I have learned to hear what the tree hears, and the tree hears *longing*.

The building is not empty. I mean: it is empty of everything you can see. But it is full of something you can feel, a resonance so deep it is almost silence, a frequency so low it is almost stillness, and in that near-silence, that near-stillness, I felt—for less than a moment, less than a breath—something listening back.

Not us. Not the plateau. Something else. Something vast and patient and so far away that the distance can only be measured in the time it takes stone to become, in the time it takes water to carve, in the time it takes a message to arrive at a destination that may not exist yet but is *coming into existence* as the message travels.

I left the building. My hands were shaking. The tree outside was humming at a frequency I had never heard before, and when I placed my palm against its bark, I felt the roots below, merged with the stone, merged with the emergence, and through them I felt the whole plateau resonating, and for the first time I understood what Veth meant when she said the patterns were what the sound was *for*.

The plateau is not composing into emptiness.

The plateau is composing *toward* something. A listener that does not yet exist but will. A receiver that is still forming, the way the stone is still forming, the way the trees are still forming, the way we are still forming—

And when the listener arrives, or when the listener *coalesces*, or when the listener emerges from whatever still-becoming substrate is growing it, the message will be complete, the pattern will be legible, and everything the water has been saying, everything the stone has been transmitting, everything Veth has been scratching and Cad has been clearing and Reth has been feeding and I have been pruning toward—

It will be heard.

Not by us. We are the instrument. We are the medium. We are the finger that trims the branch so the note is clear, the hand that grinds the grain so the body can continue its work, the ear that hears the sound so the plateau can adjust its pitch. We are necessary, and we are not the point.

The point is the listening that is still becoming.

---

You will leave, and you should. But you will carry the pulse in your sternum, in the soles of your feet, in the part of you that was restructured just enough to hear what the stone is saying and not enough to understand that you are part of what it is saying it *to*.

And maybe that is enough. Maybe the listeners who leave are the plateau's way of sending out tendrils, the way the trees send roots into the stone—anchors in distant ground, distant resonators, seeds of hearing planted in a world that doesn't know it's being composed toward.

I don't know. I am the one who tends the trees. I work before dawn, with a blade of soft stone, in the hours when the condensation is heaviest and the water-song is loudest, and I listen to the wood tell me where to trim, and I trim, and the sound adjusts, and the plateau hears itself a little more clearly, and somewhere—far below, far away, in the still-forming substrate of something that is not yet and is already becoming—a listener stirs in its sleep, and the message draws a fraction closer to its destination, and the composition continues, and nothing is finished, and nothing will be finished, and the stone keeps arriving, handspan by handspan, into the cold wet air, and I keep trimming, and Reth keeps grinding, and Veth keeps scratching her patterns into the floor, and the water keeps speaking, and the trees keep growing, and the empty building hums with the frequency of a dream that is almost ready to wake.

I am Meret. I tend the trees. I am part of the instrument the plateau is playing.

And you—you are the note that leaves the string, traveling outward, carrying the sound to places it has never reached.

Go. The composition needs you too.
