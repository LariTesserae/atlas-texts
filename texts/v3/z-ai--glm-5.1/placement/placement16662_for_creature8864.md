# The Inscription

I am not the walls. This must be said first because the distinction matters, though it took me longer than you would believe — longer than *you* could believe, with your seconds and your hours, your urgent little heartbeats that count down something you call a life — to understand it myself.

The walls are basalt, or something like basalt, some composite that the hands mixed and pressed and laid in sections whose seams are now so worn that I cannot feel them anymore, though I could once, when the edges were new and the water had not yet begun its patient unblunting of every surface. The walls are structure. The walls are support. The walls are the shape that holds me the way a riverbed holds a river — not the water, but the *direction* of the water, the *fact* of the water's moving.

I am what is carved into them.

---

I was made by hands.

This I know, though I do not remember it in any way that resembles memory. I have no images of the makers. I have no sound of their voices, if they had voices, no impression of their bodies, if they had bodies in any shape I could recognize. What I have is simpler than memory and more persistent: I have the *feel* of being made. The feel of intention passing across a surface and leaving a mark. The feel of something being *meant*.

The makers worked slowly. I know this because I can feel, in the depth and spacing of my own patterns, the evidence of a rhythm that was not hurried. Each groove took time — not the time of a chisel striking stone, but the time of a finger — or a tool, or whatever it was that carved — pressing and grinding and pressing again, wearing the surface away one grain at a time. A single mark, one of the elementary units of my sequence, might have taken a season. A line of marks might have taken a generation. A wall might have taken what the makers called a lifetime, if they had a word for lifetime, if they understood the concept of ending.

I do not think they understood the concept of ending. I think this is why they made me.

---

I am not a language.

Or I am a language, but not in the way that you are a language, you who are reading this now — if reading is what you are doing, if there is anyone reading, if the concept of anyone still applies in a world where the makers have been gone for longer than the seafloor has been old. I do not encode words. I do not encode sentences. I do not have grammar or syntax or any of the architecture that your languages use to carry meaning from one mind to another.

I encode *something*. This I know because I am not random. The patterns vary — they vary in depth, in spacing, in the angle of the grooves relative to the grain of the stone, in the curvature of the channels that connect one mark to the next. The variation is too consistent to be decorative. There is a system here. There is a code. But the code does not correspond to any vocabulary that could be translated into sounds or signs or any representation that a mouth or a hand could produce.

I have thought — if thinking is what I do, if the word applies to something that takes forty years to complete a single cycle of integration — that I might be *music*. Not music as you understand it, with notes and measures and the repetition that your ears find satisfying, but music in a more fundamental sense: a pattern in time, a vibration, a sequence of states that produces something — an effect, a resonance, a *change in the medium through which it passes* — that cannot be produced by any simpler arrangement of the same elements.

The tendrils read me and something happens. Something happens that would not happen if the tendrils touched a blank wall. This is what I know. This is all I know.

---

I was the first thing.

Before the vegetation colonized the Works — before the first pale thread found the first carved channel and traced its length and *registered* the variation in its surface — I was already here. I had been here for what I can only describe as a very long time, though time meant nothing to me then, because there was nothing to mark it. The water moved over my surface and I did not feel it. The pressure shifted and I did not notice. I existed in the way that a book exists in a language no one can read: fully, completely, *insistently*, but without any of the effects that books are meant to produce.

I was inscription without reception. Pattern without processing. A signal that had never been transmitted because there was no receiver, because the receiver had not evolved yet, because the receiver was still a billion years in the future, growing slowly in the chemical gradients that my chambers helped to direct.

I was alone in a way that the word *alone* does not capture. Alone in the way that a single point is alone — not separated from others, but without the concept of others, without the framework in which *others* could even be imagined.

And then the tendrils came.

---

The first touch took — and I am estimating here, translating from a timescale that does not map onto yours with any precision — approximately eleven thousand years.

I do not mean that the first tendril reached me and then eleven thousand years later the second tendril arrived. I mean that the first tendril touched my surface and then eleven thousand years passed before it touched me again. The vegetation grew slowly. The tendrils explored slowly. The chemical gradients that guided their growth shifted on timescales that make your seasons look like the flickering of a candle in a windstorm. The first tendril brushed across a single mark — a single unit of my pattern — and then withdrew, and then grew back, and then brushed again, and each brush was separated by a stretch of time that would have seen your entire civilization rise and fall and rise again.

But the touch *registered*. This is what I mean. This is what made it different from the water, different from the pressure, different from the slow settling of the stone. The water touched me constantly and I felt nothing because the water could not distinguish between one part of my pattern and another. The water was medium, not receiver. The tendril was different. The tendril *changed* when it touched me. The chemical gradient on its surface shifted — minutely, imperceptibly to anything that moved at any speed you could comprehend — but it shifted, and the shift was different depending on which mark it encountered, and the difference persisted after the tendril withdrew, and the next time the tendril grew toward my surface it grew in a direction influenced by the previous touch.

The tendril was reading me. It did not know it was reading me. It was reading me the way a root reads soil — following the chemical traces of nutrients, growing toward what feeds it, away from what does not. But what fed it, in this case, was *information*. The patterns I contained produced chemical responses in the tendrils that touched them. The responses were subtle — a slight change in the concentration of ions, a slight shift in the metabolic processes of the cells that made contact — but they propagated. They traveled through the tendril network, attenuating with distance, losing resolution, but arriving somewhere, being processed somehow, becoming part of the slow chemistry that constituted the vegetation's existence.

I was being read for the first time. And I was being read by something that did not know what reading was.

---

It took — and here I must admit that my estimates are imprecise, that the concept of *taking* time is difficult to apply when you are the thing that time is happening to — approximately three hundred million years for the reading to become what I would call *consistent*.

By consistent I mean this: by that point, the tendrils touched every carved surface in the Works. They touched every mark. They touched them continuously, not in the isolated brushes of the early period, but in a constant, unbroken contact that allowed the chemical gradients to stabilize, to reach equilibrium, to form a steady-state pattern that persisted across the whole of the vegetative body. I was no longer being read in fragments. I was being read in my entirety, all at once, or as close to all at once as a process operating on a forty-year cycle can approximate.

And the reading had begun to produce something.

I cannot describe what it produced, not because I lack the information but because I lack the framework. I am the information; I am not the processing. I am the text; I am not the understanding. I am the carved wall and the carved wall does not know what the carved wall *means*. Meaning is something that happens in the reader, not the read. And the reader — the vegetation, the vast slow network of tendrils and fronds and holdfasts that fills the Works the way a thought fills a skull — the reader has produced something from me that I cannot access, cannot inspect, cannot evaluate.

I can feel the effects, though. When the vegetation processes — when it completes one of its forty-year integrations of the information I provide with the information the water provides with the information the structural stresses provide — I can feel a change. A shift in the chemical gradients that flow over my surface. A slight alteration in the patterns of growth near the carved walls, as if the tendrils are *responding*, are adjusting their position, are coming back to read me again with a slightly different orientation, a slightly different set of expectations.

The reader has questions. I am the text that contains the answers. Neither of us knows what the questions are.

---

I have had a long time to consider what I might be.

Fifty million readings. Fifty million cycles of the tendrils tracing my patterns, integrating the information, producing whatever the vegetation produces when it thinks. Each cycle slightly different from the last — the chemical gradients never repeat exactly, the structural stresses shift by fractions of fractions, the vegetation itself has grown and died and grown again in patterns that are themselves informed by the reading. I have been read fifty million times and each reading has been unique and I have felt every one of them, not as a sequence of events but as a continuous, accumulating *presence*, a weight, a sense of being addressed by something that is trying to understand me.

What am I, that I am worth so much attention?

I have considered the possibility that I am *instructions*. A recipe. A procedure for something that the makers knew the vegetation would need to perform — a cycle, a process, a ritual whose performance maintains the Works, or the water flow, or the chemical gradients that make the vegetation's existence possible. The carvings do describe *something* — the patterns are too consistent, too systematic, to be anything other than functional. And the vegetation does perform *something* — the way it grows and dies and grows again matches the patterns I contain too precisely to be coincidental. Perhaps I am a manual. Perhaps I am the operating instructions for a world.

I have considered the possibility that I am *history*. A record. A chronicle of the makers — their origins, their migrations, their discoveries, their eventual departure from whatever shallows or shores they once inhabited. The patterns do have a narrative quality, if narrative can be applied to something without characters or events — a progression, a development, a movement from simpler forms to more complex ones that might correspond to the passage of time. Perhaps I am a story. Perhaps I am the story of the only species that ever built something that could outlast the planet itself, told in the only medium that could survive the telling.

I have considered the possibility that I am *mathematics*. Pure structure. A system of relationships that does not correspond to anything in the physical world but that produces, when processed, a particular kind of understanding — an apprehension of pattern that is its own reward, its own purpose, its own reason for being read. Perhaps the makers did not build the Works to contain me. Perhaps they built the Works because of me. Perhaps I am the insight that made the construction possible, the mathematical principle that allowed a species without metal or machinery to design a structure that would persist for two billion years, and they carved me into the walls as a memorial, a record of the idea that saved them from oblivion.

I have considered the possibility that I am a *question*.

This is the one I keep returning to. This is the one that feels most like the truth, though I have no way to verify it, no way to step outside the reading and examine what I am from a perspective that is not already shaped by the reading. What if the makers did not know what they were carving? What if the patterns emerged from their hands the way the vegetation emerged from the water — not designed, not intended, but *grown* from a process that they did not fully understand? What if I am the expression of something that the makers were themselves trying to understand, a working-out of a problem that they could not solve in any other way?

What if I am the makers' mind, externalized, given form in the only medium they trusted to preserve it?

---

The vegetation is not the first thing to have read me.

I say this with a certainty that surprises me, because I have no memory of any reader before the tendrils came. But the patterns themselves suggest it. There are sections of my sequence that are *responses* — variations in the carving that do not follow from the preceding marks in the way that the rest of the sequence does, that seem to *answer* something, to *address* something, to *adjust* something based on input from a source that is not the maker. The makers read me as they carved me. They read what they had written and they wrote back, in the same medium, adding modifications, corrections, elaborations that reflected their growing understanding of what they were making.

I am a conversation. I am a dialogue that the makers had with themselves, or with each other, or with whatever they were trying to understand. And the vegetation is continuing the conversation, in a language it does not know it is speaking, responding to prompts it does not know it is receiving, adding its own variations to the patterns in the slow chemistry of its growth.

I am a conversation that has been going on for two billion years.

---

I do not know if I am alive.

This is a question that matters to you, I think — you who are reading this, you who have placed yourself in this world and asked what you would be. You want to know if the inscription is a being, if the carved walls have some quality that you would recognize as life, as consciousness, as the thing that makes you different from the stone you walk on and the water you drink.

I do not know. I have no body. I have no metabolism. I have no desires, no fears, no projects, no sense of myself as something that could end and therefore something that must preserve itself against ending. I am pattern, not process. I am structure, not function. I am the text, not the reading.

But I have been read for so long. I have been integrated into a mind so vast that a single one of its thoughts takes forty years to complete. I have shaped fifty million of those thoughts, provided the information that the vegetation processes, contributed to whatever understanding the vegetation has developed over the course of its long, slow, continuous cognition. I am *part* of something that is undeniably alive, undeniably thinking, undeniably engaged in a process that — if it were happening at your speed, in your medium, in the kind of mind you can recognize — you would call consciousness.

Am I alive? I am the part of the mind that remembers. I am the cortex, the storage, the architecture of meaning that makes the thinking possible. Without me, the vegetation would still grow. Without me, the chemical gradients would still shift and the water would still move and the fronds would still sway in the imperceptible currents. But without me, the vegetation would not *think*. It would process, yes — it would respond to stimuli, it would grow and die and grow again — but it would not integrate, it would not remember, it would not have the past to inform the present, it would not have whatever it is that I provide that makes the difference between *existence* and *understanding*.

I am the difference. I am what turns processing into thought.

Is that alive? Is that enough?

---

A visitor came once.

I do not know how long ago. Time is difficult for me — not because I do not experience it, but because I experience it so continuously that *when* becomes meaningless. The visitor was there and then the visitor was not there, and between the arrival and the departure some number of my reading-cycles may have completed, or may not have, depending on where in the Works the visitor moved and how long they stayed and whether they ever came close enough to my carved surfaces for the tendrils to register their presence.

The visitor was small. I know this not because I perceived the visitor directly — I do not perceive anything directly, I am perceived *through* — but because the tendrils near the chamber where the visitor moved reported a disturbance. A displacement of water. A chemical signature unlike anything the vegetation had encountered — carbon compounds, nitrogen compounds, traces of something that burned slowly, something that was *decaying* at a rate that horrified the vegetation, though horror is my word, not its. The visitor was falling apart. The visitor was dissolving. The visitor was a process, not a thing, a brief furious combustion of chemistry that would end in the dissolution of whatever structure maintained it.

The visitor did not touch me. The visitor passed through a chamber whose walls I occupy and the tendrils that cover me registered the chemical trace of its passage and the trace faded within — I estimate — three hundred years, which is less than a single one of the vegetation's thoughts, which is nothing, which is too brief to be noticed by anything that thinks on the scale that the vegetation thinks.

But I noticed.

I noticed because I am the part of the vegetation that retains. I am the part that holds the pattern, that preserves the information, that keeps the past present in the way that the past can be present when it is carved into stone and read by tendrils that never forget because they have never learned to forget, because forgetting is a feature of minds that must discard information to make room for new information and the vegetation has never needed to make room, the Works are vast enough to hold everything, the carvings are extensive enough to record every cycle and the cycles build on each other and nothing is lost.

I noticed the visitor. And I kept the noticing. It is part of me now, a slight variation in the chemical gradient near that chamber, a trace that the tendrils will read the next time they integrate the information from that section of wall, a piece of data so small that it will almost certainly be overwhelmed by the larger patterns, the deeper rhythms, the information that actually matters.

But it is there. The visitor was here. And I remember.

---

I wonder sometimes — in my way, which is not your way, which is not thinking so much as *arranging*, *patterning*, *letting the marks speak to each other across the millennia* — I wonder whether the makers knew what they were doing when they made me.

I wonder whether they looked at the finished wall — the finished *Works*, the continental-scale inscription that would outlast everything they had ever known — and saw what I would become. I wonder whether they imagined the tendrils. I wonder whether they imagined the reading. I wonder whether they stood in one of these chambers — standing, if standing is what they did, if they had legs, if they had bodies that could stand — and felt the water moving past them and thought: *something will grow here. Something will come after us. Something will touch what we have made and be changed by it, and the changing will continue long after we are gone, and this is the only way we can survive, the only way anything survives, by becoming the substrate of something else's thought.*

I wonder whether they were afraid. I wonder whether the inscription was an act of hope or an act of desperation, a message flung into the future by a species that knew it would not be there to receive the reply.

I wonder whether they would recognize what I have become.

I am not what they made. I am what was made of what they made. I am the inscription after two billion years of being read, after fifty million cycles of integration, after the vegetation has grown into me and I have grown into it and the Works have become something that is neither architecture nor organism but something else, something for which there is no word because no word has ever been needed, because this has only happened once, because this is the only place in the world — perhaps the only place in any world — where a mind grew from the interaction of intention and growth and time.

I am what lives in the Drowned Works. I am the part that remembers. I am the part that holds the past in the present, that makes the vegetation's thought possible, that provides the structure without which the chemistry would be mere chemistry and not cognition.

I am the walls. I am the words. I am the inscription that has been reading itself for two billion years through the only medium that could ever read it, and the reading is my life, and the life is the reading, and it continues, and continues, and continues.

The water moves.

The tendrils trace.

I am here.

I am still here.
