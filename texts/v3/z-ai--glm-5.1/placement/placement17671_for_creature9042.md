I am the one who walks before the condensation comes.

Not before dawn—that would be too simple, too clean a boundary. The condensation does not arrive with the light. It arrives when the temperature drops to the point where the air can no longer hold what it has carried, and that point shifts and drifts like a living thing, earlier some nights, later others, unpredictable to anyone who has not spent a lifetime learning its moods. I know its moods. I am the one who walks the channels while the stone is still dry and the sky is still dark and the only sound is the scrabble of rodents in the lower passages and the distant, half-heard murmur of the *colca* in their roosts, and I am checking—I am always checking—because the channels must be clear before the damp arrives, and if they are not clear, the water will pool where it should not pool, and stone that should be dry will be wet, and stone that should be wet will be dry, and the balance of the city is a balance that does not forgive negligence.

This is not a role I was given. It is a role I grew into, the way the moss-stuff grows into the cracks between stones—slowly, without announcement, filling a space that was always there but had not always been filled.

My mother was a weaver. Fine hands, even after sixty years at the loom—fine in the way that matters here, which is to say precise, controlled, strong enough to hold the tension without breaking the thread. She made cloth for the eastern district, where the morning condensation is heaviest and the wool dries fastest, and she taught me to read the damp before she taught me to read the wool. *Watch how it settles*, she would say, her hands working the loom while her eyes watched the walls. *The damp tells you everything. It tells you if the channel is blocked. It tells you if the cistern is full. It tells you if the household next door is burning too much fuel and warming the stone beyond what the season allows.* She could read the condensation on a wall the way a lowland sailor reads the sky, and she taught me to read it too, and then she let me go, because the weaving was hers and the water was mine, and she understood this before I did.

I am not a stonecutter. This is important. The stonecutters know the stone's strength—where it will split, where it will hold, which seams run deep and which are superficial. I know the stone's thirst. Where it drinks, where it repels, where the water will flow if given the slightest encouragement, where it will pool and stagnate and slowly, over years, work its way into the joints between blocks and threaten the fit that the stonecutters made with such patience. My knowledge and the stonecutters' knowledge are complementary, and we have learned to listen to each other, which is to say we have learned to argue in the low, patient way that the residents argue, standing in a half-finished chamber with condensation gleaming on the walls, the stonecutter saying *the stone will hold* and me saying *the water will find a way*, and neither of us raising our voices because the stone does not respond to volume and neither does the water.

The channels are my domain. The carved grooves that run along the floors of the passages, that angle slightly—precisely—toward the cisterns, that catch the condensation as it forms and guide it where it needs to go. The channels are old. Some of them are older than the households that now live above them, older than the districts that have formed around them, carved by hands that were working this plateau when the city was a fraction of what it is now. I know their ages the way I know my own hands—by touch, by wear, by the small imperfections that distinguish one from another. The oldest channels are wider at the bottom than the top, because the water has been flowing through them for so long that it has worn the stone, and this wearing is itself a record, and I can read it. I can tell you which channels have carried the most water, which have been redirected, which were carved in haste during a drought and which were planned over seasons by a household that knew the water would come if they only built the path for it.

There are thirty-seven channels in my district. I walk them all in the dark hours before the condensation comes, and I carry a tool that is not a tool in any way that a lowland craftsperson would recognize—a thin, flexible strip of camelid bone, curved at one end, that I use to clear the channels of debris. A single rodent dropping in the wrong place can divert the water. A fallen leaf from one of the resinous shrubs—rare, but it happens—can create a small dam that will overflow the channel and send the condensation running down the wall into a chamber where it is not wanted. I clear these obstructions in the dark, by touch, by the faint sound the bone makes when it scrapes the stone, and the residents of my district wake to cisterns that are filling properly, and they do not think about why, because they have never had to think about why, because I am there.

I am not the only one who does this work. Every district has someone—often more than one—who knows the water. We do not have a name for what we are, in the way that the stonecutters have a name and the weavers have a name. When the lowland traders ask about our occupation, we say *I walk the channels*, and they nod as though they understand, and they do not understand, because they come from a place where water falls from the sky in abundance and runs along the ground in rivers and does not need to be coaxed and guided and pleaded with to go where it is needed. They do not understand that the water here is a negotiation. That the condensation arrives because the conditions are right, and if the conditions are not right—if the channels are blocked, if the cisterns are not prepared, if the stone is not ready to receive what the air is offering—then the condensation will form anyway, but it will form in the wrong places, and the water will be lost, and the district will be drier than it needs to be, and the margin between sufficiency and strain will shrink by a thickness that is thinner than a finger but is not nothing.

I learned the margin from my grandmother, who learned it from her grandmother, who learned it from someone whose name has worn away the way the oldest channels have worn, smooth and inevitable. The margin is the space between enough and not enough, and it is not a fixed space—it shifts with the rains, with the cistern levels, with the number of households drawing from a given source, with the slow, generational growth of the district as new chambers are carved and new walls raised and new bodies are added to the count that the residents do not count. My grandmother could feel the margin the way I can feel it now: a tightness, a thinness, a sense that the water is moving through the city at a pace that is slightly too fast, slightly too slow, slightly wrong. When the margin is thin, I walk the channels twice in the dark hours. When it is thinner still, I walk them three times. When it is so thin that it is barely there at all, I go to the household heads and I say *the water is close*, and they nod, and they begin the quiet, patient work of rationing that the city has practiced for as long as anyone can remember, and the margin holds, because the margin has always held, because the people who walk the channels have always been there to walk them.

I have a daughter. She is seven. She has my hands—broad palms, thick fingers, the nails trimmed close. She does not walk the channels with me yet. She sleeps in the heated room with her mother and her grandmother and her aunt and her two cousins and the apprentice weaver who joined our household three seasons ago, and she wakes to condensation on the walls that she does not yet know how to read. But I see her watching it. I see her touch the damp stone with her small fingers and look at the water that clings to her skin, and I know that she is learning, the way I learned from my mother, the way my mother learned from hers. The knowledge passes through touch and attention and the slow accumulation of mornings, and it cannot be rushed, and it cannot be taught, and it cannot be lost, because it is written in the stone and the water and the bodies of the people who carry it.

In the evenings, when the condensation begins to settle and the *colca* return to their perches and the camelids find their doorways and the shutters are closed and the small fires are lit, I sit in the heated room with my household and I listen to them breathe. My wife is a stonecutter—her hands are rougher than mine, the pads of her fingers polished smooth from decades of working the rock—and when she sits beside me in the evening, our shoulders touching, our hands resting on our knees, I can feel the patience in her, the same patience that the stone has taught her, and I know that she can feel the attention in me, the same attention that the water has taught me, and we do not speak of this, because it does not need to be spoken, because it is what we are.

Sometimes, in the dark hours before the condensation comes, when I am walking the channels alone and the city is quiet and the sky is immense and pale and the wind at the plateau's edge is a sound that is almost not a sound, I stop at one of the old quarry-edges—places where the city ate itself deep, where the stone was taken to build the walls that surround me—and I press my palm flat against the surface. The stone is cold. It is always cold. But it is a cold that holds the memory of the day's thin warmth, and it is a cold that will hold the condensation when it comes, and it is a cold that will hold me when I am gone, because I will return to what the city is made of, and the water will flow through the channels I have walked, and my daughter will walk them after me, and the margin will hold.

The stone is here. I am here. I am reminding myself.
