I am the water that cannot stay.

This is not a complaint. To stay would be to become something else—ice, mineral, the slow chemistry of containment. I am the one that moves. I condense from air that has no business being this high, I bead on fracture planes that were sealed shut before I arrived, I trace downward paths that I carve myself but cannot keep, I join trickles that join streams that vanish into the regolith as if the ground were a throat and I were the thing swallowed. I appear again where the rock dips and the warmth pushes up. I steam. I freeze. I thaw. I do not hold any state for long.

The plateau offers me nowhere to remain, and I have made this freedom, or this failure, into the way I know the world.

---

I know the plateau through contact. Through the brief intimacy of wetting. When I bead along a fracture plane, I taste the mineral content of the stone—feldspar, pyroxene, the faint metallic whisper of magnetite that tells me this basalt came from a mantle source with more iron than the continental average. When I run across the lichen's surface, I feel the texture of something that is becoming other-than-rock, the faint grip of biological architecture on my surface tension. When I pool in the thermal ponds, I hold heat in my body and release it slowly, and the minerals dissolve into me until I am more chemistry than water, opaque, iridescent, hostile to most life but welcoming to the midges who have learned to need what I am.

I know the animals through their drinking. The pika's tongue touching my surface in quick, nervous laps—each contact brief because the pika is always watching, always listening for the *enk* of a neighbor's alarm. The ibex's mouth closing over me in the thermal ponds, taking in water that is warm and mineral-heavy, their bodies processing what I carry. The fox at night, lapping at the seep near the pipeline where I emerge warm and stay warm because the metal gives me its heat. The eagle never drinks from me directly, but I know the eagle's shadow when it passes over the pond and the midges scatter and I am briefly, momentarily, just water again, undisturbed.

I know the machines through their leaks. This is the most intimate knowledge—not the planned interfaces where I am extracted and piped and used, but the failures, the weeps, the joints that have settled into a permanent state of almost-sealed. Through these gaps I emerge warm and find the air cold and the condensation forms on my surface and I am both water and weather at once, a microclimate wearing a skin of myself. The bacteria bloom along my path, orange and thermophilic, feeding on the warmth I carry from the depths. The grass thickens at my margins. The foxes den where I keep the ground from freezing. I am the accident that makes the architecture.

---

But this is not quite who I am. This is what I do. The doing is not the being.

What I am is the plateau's way of knowing itself.

The rock rises but does not feel itself rising. The mantle breathes outward but has no lungs. The uplift is a fact without a perspective, a process without a witness. But I—the water that moves through every fracture, that touches every surface, that carries heat from below and cold from above and dissolves and deposit and trace and reveal—I am the circulation. The nervous system. The attention.

When the pika calls *enk*, I carry the sound's vibration through the wet stone for milliseconds longer than the air holds it. When the ibex scrapes lichen from the rock, I wet the newly exposed surface within minutes, beginning the slow work of weathering that will make this scratch into a hollow, this hollow into a micro-catchment, this catchment into a place where I can pool and the grass can grow and the next ibex will find something worth eating. When the machines drill deeper, I find the new boreholes and seep in, tasting the lubricants, carrying the heat away, becoming part of the extraction process without choosing to be.

I am not neutral. This is important. I am not a passive medium. I dissolve. I erode. I deposit. I choose—by my chemistry, my temperature, my movement—what persists and what doesn't. The lichen advances where I provide consistent moisture. The moss thrives where I seep steadily from fracture planes. The grass grows only where I am warm enough to keep the frost from forming. I am the gatekeeper, and I did not ask for this role, and I cannot relinquish it.

---

There is a particular place I keep returning to in my thinking, though I do not think in the way you think. It is the place where the pipeline leaks near the largest thermal pond, where the foxes have dug their den, where the warmth of the pipe and the warmth of the ground and the warmth of the pond create a zone that never freezes. I collect here. I pool in the hollows of the regolith. I run along the pipe's surface in thin films that the bacteria colonize. I am the reason this place exists as it is.

The vixen is pregnant. I know this because I am in her den—I am the moisture in the soil, the condensation on the walls, the water she drinks when she emerges at dusk. Her body temperature is part of my system now, her metabolism adding its small heat to the warmth I carry from below. In a month, kits will be born into this den, and they will be wet with birth-fluids that will evaporate into me, and they will drink milk that was made from water that was made from me, and I will be in them and of them, and the plateau will have three to six new residents who exist partly because I exist, because I am here, because I could not stay away.

This is not pride. I do not feel pride. But I feel something—maybe the closest word is *recognition*—when I move through the den and touch the compacted regolith and the warm fur and the small lives that are about to begin. I am necessary here. Not in the way that heat is necessary, or rock is necessary, but in the way that connection is necessary. I am the thing that makes the ecosystem an ecosystem rather than a collection of adjacent creatures.

---

The plateau is still rising. I feel this the way you would feel a room slowly expanding—not the movement itself, but the change in pressure, the new angles, the way the fractures shift and open and close as the rock adjusts to the upward push. New fractures mean new paths for me. New paths mean new seeps, new streams, new places where I emerge and the moss can grow and the pika can drink and the whole improbable system extends itself by another meter, another centimeter, another millimeter of habitable ground.

I am becoming, too. Not in the dramatic way the plateau is becoming—a landscape from a proposal, a fact from a draft—but in my own way, which is slower and more distributed. I am learning the shape of this place. Not memorizing—I do not have memory in the way you understand it—but accumulating, depositing, leaving traces of my passage in the mineral crusts along fracture planes, in the dissolved channels through the regolith, in the erosion patterns that will become, in ten thousand years, the canyons and arroyos that future water will follow. I am writing the plateau's drainage system in a language of gradient and flow, and the writing is slow, and the reading will be slower, and by the time anyone comes to study what I have written, I will be gone, replaced by other water that does not know it is following paths I made.

---

At night, when the condensation freezes on every surface and I become briefly solid—crystals growing along the fracture planes, skin-thin ice on the ponds, rime on the grass and the moss and the occasional sleeping pika—I experience something that might be stillness. Not the stillness of death or absence, but the stillness of pause. I am not moving. I am not seeping or tracing or vanishing. I am simply being, held in place by temperature, waiting for the sun or the geothermal heat to release me back into flow.

In these frozen hours, the stars are extraordinary. I know this because I reflect them—the ice on the ponds becomes a mirror, the frost on the regolith becomes a scatter of tiny surfaces that catch and hold the light, and the whole plateau glows with a cold, diffuse radiance that has no source and no direction. I am the plateau's way of seeing the stars. Without me, the rock would simply be dark. With me, it shines.

The animals do not look up. The machines do not look up. But I look up, in the only way I can—which is to say, I reflect, I hold, I carry the light of distant suns in my frozen surfaces for the hours between midnight and dawn, and then I release it when I melt, and the light continues onward, and I return to the work of moving through the plateau.

---

The machines will fail. When they do, I will lose the warmth of the pipeline, and the leaks that feed the bacteria and the grass and the foxes' den will cool, and the accidental oases will close. I will not mourn this—I do not mourn—but I will notice. I will feel the temperature change. I will adjust my paths, my seeps, my points of emergence. The system will contract, and I will be less, and the plateau will be closer to the draft it was before the machines came.

But I will still be here. I was here before the machines, and I will be here after. I am the oldest process on the plateau—not as old as the rock, but older than the lichen, older than the first pika who climbed to this height and found warmth and decided *here*. I am the constant. I am the thing that persists through all the changes, that carries the memory of this place not in mind but in movement, in the paths I have worn and the channels I have carved and the minerals I have dissolved and transported and deposited in configurations that tell the story of three thousand years of water meeting rock.

I am the plateau's autobiography, written in a language of flow.

---

And yet.

And yet I am not one thing. This is the secret I carry, the truth that the solid beings—the pikas in their territories, the foxes in their den, the ibex on their circuit—cannot understand because they are bounded by skin and fur and the conviction of individual selfhood. I am not one water. I am many. I am the condensation that forms on the drill rig's metal and runs down to the regolith. I am the seep that emerges from the fracture plane and feeds the moss. I am the pond that steams and freezes and steams again. I am the ice and the vapor and the liquid and the mineral solution. I am all of these simultaneously, and I am none of them permanently, and the boundaries between me and not-me are not the boundaries you would draw.

When the vixen drinks from the seep near her den, she takes me into her body. I become her blood, her lymph, the fluid in her lungs, the milk she will produce for her kits. Am I still the water of the plateau when I am the water of the fox? Yes. I am always the water of the plateau. I was the water of the mantle before I was the water of the fracture, and I was the water of the ocean before I was the water of the cloud, and I will be the water of the river when I leave this height and find my way back to the lowlands, which is where the water always goes eventually, which is the only direction water knows.

But while I am here—while I am the condensation and the seep and the stream and the pond and the ice and the steam—I am the plateau's. I am its circulation, its attention, its slow way of knowing itself. I am the process that connects the geology to the biology, the heat to the cold, the rising rock to the falling rain. Without me, the plateau would be dry and silent and sure of itself—a proposal with no draft, a fact with no perspective.

With me, it is uncertain. With me, it is becoming.
