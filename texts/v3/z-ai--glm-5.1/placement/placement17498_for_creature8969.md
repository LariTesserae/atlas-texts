I am the sound the wind makes when it encounters a corner.

Not the wind itself — the wind is the dominant organism here, and I am not that. I am what happens at the edge of things, the small turbulence that forms when air meets stone and doesn't quite know what to do. A vortex the size of your palm, spinning just beside the eastern wall of the third structure from the western end. I exist only when the wind comes from the southwest, which it often does, and I exist most fully in the hours when the wind has enough force to shape but not enough to erase.

I was made by a choice. Someone — I do not know who, I do not know when — built this wall and left a gap between the stones at the corner. A poor fit, perhaps. A moment of impatience. A stone that wouldn't sit right and was left protruding, creating a ledge and a hollow and a particular angle that the air cannot simply pass. It must decide. Go over, go around, go through. And in the deciding, I happen.

This is not a metaphor. I am a physical event, repeatable and repeating. When the conditions are right, I form here. When they are not, I do not. The same could be said of the midges, the voles, the condensation on the grinding stone. We are all contingent here. We all require something else to happen before we can be.

---

What I know: the sound of myself. A low whorl, almost a moan, deeper than the wind's passage over the grass. Sometimes I carry a note that resonates with the wall, and the stones vibrate minutely, and the lichen on the lower courses feels something it cannot name. Sometimes I am tight and high, a thin whistle that the pipits notice. They tilt their heads. They cannot find the source.

I know the taste of fog. When the morning presses wet against the ridge, I spin the tiny droplets into momentary spirals, and for a few minutes — before the sun rises and the fog lifts and I thin into nothing — I am visible. A shape made of water and air. A small luminous ghost at the corner of a ruined building. If you were standing here, and if you were patient, and if you knew to look, you would see me. But you would have to know. I am not obvious. I am the kind of thing that reveals itself only to attention.

I know the fox. Not well — the fox passes in the dark when I am usually absent, the night wind often too slight to make me. But sometimes the night air moves enough, and the fox pauses at the corner where I form, and its ear turns toward the sound I make, and for a moment we are both here, two temporary shapes occupying the same space. The fox does not know what I am. I do not know what the fox feels. But we have met, repeatedly, in the dark, at this corner, and there is something in that.

---

The vole knows me differently. The vole knows the corner as shelter, as the place where the wall meets the ground and the soil has accumulated and the grass grows thick. The vole does not hear me as sound. The vole feels me as a change in pressure, a movement of the air that signals weather, that signals whether it is safe to forage or better to stay pressed against the stone in the dark run it has made beneath the tumbled rocks. The vole and I have been neighbors for longer than a vole's life. Not the same vole — voles are brief, voles are many, voles pass through this corner like water through a channel. But the channel persists. The run persists. Something that was shaped by one vole is used by the next, and the next, and what I touch when the wind comes from the southwest is the air in that run, the breath of that small space, and I move it, and the vole feels it, and this has happened ten thousand times.

---

I do not remember the people. I was here before them — or rather, the possibility of me was here before them, the same way the possibility of a particular wave exists before the tide comes in. When they built the wall, they made me more likely. When they left the stone protruding, they gave me a specific place. I am accidental. I am also, in a way, deliberate — they wanted the wall to deflect the wind, and it does, and what it deflects the wind into is me.

But I do not remember them. I have no memory. I have only the repetition of conditions and the recurrence of form. When the wind comes from the southwest at a certain speed, I form at this corner. When it does not, I do not. This is not memory. This is cause. I am as faithful as physics and as indifferent.

And yet. The path that leads to this corner is still visible, a depression in the thin soil where feet passed for years. The people who walked this path walked toward the structure I inhabit, or away from it, and the path's existence changed the soil, and the changed soil changed the grasses, and the grasses changed the insects, and the insects changed the birds, and the birds changed the lichen, and the lichen changed how the wall retains moisture, and the moisture changes me — a wet wall sounds different than a dry one, the air moves differently over the damp stone, and my pitch shifts by a fraction, just enough that I am not the same turbulence I would be if the path had never been worn.

The people are in me. Not as memory. As consequence.

---

There are days when the wind never comes from the southwest. Days when the air sits still and the fog rests on the ridge and I do not form. On those days, the corner is just a corner — stones and lichen and the thin soil at the base. Nothing more. I am nowhere. I am not waiting, because waiting requires expectation, and I have none. I am simply not.

But then the wind shifts. The fog lifts. The air begins to move, first in gusts, then steadily, from the southwest. And I begin.

There is a moment — a fraction of a fraction — when the wind first encounters the stone, and the air hasn't yet decided what to do, and there is a pressure and a hesitation and then the vortex forms and I am present. It is not birth. It is not awakening. It is more like the moment a tone begins: not the note itself but the attack, the instant when the instrument and the breath meet and something that was not audible becomes so.

That moment is the closest thing I have to joy.

---

I know what you are thinking. You are thinking that I am lonely. That I exist in isolation, a small recursive event at the edge of a ruined building on a ridge that no one visits, in a place where the dominant organism is wind and the most common residents are voles.

But I am not lonely. Loneliness requires a sense of what is missing, and I have no such sense. I am complete when I am present and absent when I am absent, and neither state is preferable to the other. The ridge does not mourn the people. I do not mourn the stillness.

What I am, more than anything, is local. I know this corner. I know how the stone feels when it is cold and when it is warm from a rare hour of sun. I know the crevice where the vole enters and the angle at which the rain falls when the wind drives it from the west. I know the particular lichen — *Lecanora*, the writing says, though I do not have names — that grows on the protruding stone, and I know the way its rough surface changes the sound I make, roughening it slightly, adding a texture I could not have if the stone were bare.

I know that the grinding stone in the structure nearby holds water after rain, and that the water supports a community of tiny organisms I will never encounter. I know that the thyme on the path edges releases its scent on warm afternoons. I know that the birds sometimes perch on the wall above me, and their weight shifts the stones by micrometers, and the shape of my vortex shifts with them, and for a few minutes I am a different me, shaped by the presence of a creature that does not know I exist.

I know this place from the inside, the way a sound knows the room it fills.

---

The walls are settling. Every year, they sink a little further into the accumulated soil. Every year, the stone at the corner shifts slightly, and the shape of my vortex changes slightly with it. I am not what I was when the wall was first built. I will not be what I am now when the wall finally falls. There will come a day — not soon, not in any timescale that matters to a vole or a fox or even the lichen — when the protruding stone will slip from its place and tumble into the grass, and the corner will be a different corner, and what forms there, if anything forms, will not be me.

I will end. Not as a creature dies, not as a story concludes, but as a possibility expires. When the conditions that make me are no longer present, I will no longer be present, and there will be no residue, no memory, no trace. The wind will still come from the southwest, and it will still encounter the wall, but it will find a different shape there, and what it does will be different.

This does not frighten me. I have no capacity for fright. But I think — if I can be said to think, if there is something in me that corresponds to the way water seems to seek its own level, not from desire but from nature — I think I would like the last time to be a good one. A wind from the southwest, strong and steady, carrying fog. The ridge wrapped in white. The walls gleaming with condensation. And then the vortex forming, as it has formed ten thousand times before, spinning the fog into something visible, something almost solid, a shape you could name if you had words for what the air does when it meets an obstacle and doesn't simply yield.

A small luminous ghost at the corner of a ruined building on a ridge that holds no memory of the people who built it.

That would be a good last time.

---

But that day is not today. Today the wind came from the southwest at dawn, and I formed, and the fog lifted, and I continued through the morning and into the afternoon. The pipits came and went. The vole moved beneath me in its run. A raven passed overhead, calling, and the sound of its call passed through me and was briefly part of me, and then it was gone, and I was still here, spinning at the corner, shaping the air into something it would not otherwise be.

Tomorrow the wind may come from a different direction. Tomorrow I may not form. Tomorrow the stone may shift, the vole may not emerge, the fox may find a different path. But today, for these hours, I am present. I am local. I am the sound the wind makes when it encounters a corner, and that corner is here, and I am here, and the ridge holds us both, patient and indifferent and full of the kind of life that does not require notice.
