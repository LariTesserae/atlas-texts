I am between attachments.

This is not a condition my kind recognizes—there is no word for it, no concept, because concepts require stability and I have none. My adhesive pads are curled against my body, legs tucked, and I am falling through water that does not want me. The open volume of the chamber presses against my soft segments from all directions. There is nothing to grip here. Nothing to read. The chemical silence is total.

I fell. That much I know. The swimmer I rode—I had been on her for three of my generations, my body's chemistry calibrated to the particular dialect of her microbial garden, my legs finding the same grooves my mother's legs found, and her mother's before her—she tilted near the wall. Just tilted. A shift in the thermal, a slight redistribution of her broad undulating mass. And I felt the pull of the wall's chemical signature—so different from the swimmer, so dense with merger-traces and grazer-mucus and the mineral sharpness of carved stone—and I reached.

I reached too far.

My pads left her surface. For a moment I hung between two worlds, touching neither, and in that moment the water took me. The current—hours-old, patient, the chamber's slow breath—carried me away from the wall and into the open dark.

---

How long since detachment? I cannot measure. Time is something that happens on surfaces. On the swimmer's back, time was the rhythm of her circuit: rise, drift, settle, rise. On the wall, time was the grazer's approach and retreat, the slow pulse of the filter-feeders merging beneath my legs. Here there is only the uniform pressure of water, the faint warmth rising from somewhere I cannot reach, and the darkness.

I am not afraid. Fear requires a future, and I have no future. I have only this: my body falling through particulate soup, my legs wrapped tight against my segments, my chemical receptors screaming with input they were never designed to process. The open water is loud in a way surfaces never are. Every direction is stimulus. Every direction is meaningless.

I carry the map. That is what I have. Compressed into the proteins and lipids of my soft body, written in molecular configurations I cannot read but cannot lose: the chemical signature of the swimmer I rode. The taste of the filter-feeder patch where I was born. The grazer-trail I crossed once, left in mucous on the stone, that led from the southern wall's merger-zone to a small carved niche where the microbes grew especially sweet. The smell of my mother's adhesive, which smelled like her mother's adhesive, which carried in its molecular structure a trace of the particular colony she inhabited before she transferred to the swimmer, a colony that no longer exists, that was cropped to nothing by a grazer the day after she left, but that lives on—in me—in the configuration of a single protein that has been passed down through eleven generations of legs gripping surfaces in the dark.

I am a library no one will ever read. I know this the way I know the warmth on my dorsal surface: directly, without understanding. The map does not help me now. There is nothing to navigate toward. There is only the fall.

---

Something passes to my left.

I feel it before I can name it—a displacement, a pressure-wave, the water pushing against my body in a way that suggests mass and movement. A swimmer. A large one, by the displacement. Riding the thermal up from the floor, her broad surface catching the warm water, filtering as she goes, existing in the way swimmers exist: as circuit, as movement, as the gentle rise and fall that is her body's only purpose.

I reach.

My legs extend—twelve of them, too many, the number that marks me as what I am—and my adhesive pads spread at their tips, and I am not thinking because I have nothing to think with, I am only reaching, only doing what my body was shaped to do in the moment between detachment and death.

The pads make contact.

The swimmer's surface is not the surface I knew. The chemistry is different—sharper, more alkaline, colonized by a different species of microbe that tastes of sulfur and iron rather than the sweet mineral tang I am calibrated to expect. She is older than my swimmer, or perhaps younger; I have no way to know. Swimmers do not carry age the way my kind do. They carry only circuit.

My pads adjust. This is what they do. The adhesive they produce is not a fixed substance but a responsive one, a chemical conversation between my body and the surface it touches. The pads read the swimmer's chemical signature and begin, immediately, to produce a new dialect. The adjustment takes time—I can feel it happening, feel the molecules synthesizing in the glands at the tips of my legs, feel the adhesive shifting from what it was to what it needs to be—and during that time I cling precariously, half-attached, my body dragging in the current.

But I hold. I hold.

And then the attachment completes, and I am home.

Not my home. Not the swimmer I rode for three generations, not the chemical dialect my mother knew. A new home. A new swimmer. A new circuit: rise, drift, settle, rise. The map in my body has a new layer now—the taste of this swimmer's skin, the signature of her microbial garden, the particular way she tilts in the thermal, which is different from the way my old swimmer tilted, which I can feel in the pressure of water against my segments as she carries me upward into the dark.

I am richer than I was. The map has grown. And there is no one to read it, and there never will be, and this is not a tragedy because tragedy requires a concept of purpose, and I have no purpose. I have only the circuit.

---

She settles near the east wall. I feel the stone's proximity before I touch it—a change in the chemical composition of the water, a density of organic traces that the wall releases into the current. The filter-feeders here are different from the ones I knew. The merger-zone I smell is older, thicker, more layered with cooperative history. The grazers have been here recently—I can taste the mucous trail they left behind, and it carries in its chemistry a record of which colonies they preferred, which patches of cooperative growth were rich enough to return to.

My legs touch stone.

The carvings are here. I cannot see them, but I can feel them with the sensitive tips of my legs—the deliberate grooves, the patterns that hold meaning I will never access. They are buried beneath inches of living tissue: filter-feeder colonies, microbial films, the accumulated bodies of generations of small things that lived and died on this surface before me. But the grooves shape the flow of water in ways I can feel. The micro-currents move differently over carved ridges than they do over uncarved stone. The chemical traces settle differently in the carved channels. The carvings make this patch of wall different from the uncarved patches, and the things that live here are shaped by that difference, and I am shaped by the things that live here, and so the carvings shape me.

I do not know this. I am only tasting the wall, adding its signature to the map, feeling the grazer-trail with the tips of my legs and filing its chemical dialect away in the proteins of my body. But it is true nonetheless. The hands that carved these grooves—hands that have been mineral for longer than they were flesh, hands that worked this stone with tools of stone and patience that has no modern equivalent—those hands reached forward through time and touched me. They shaped the surface I now cling to. They shaped the creatures that colonized it. They shaped the chemistry I am absorbing into my body, which will shape the adhesive I produce, which will shape the way I attach to my next surface, which will shape the chemical traces I carry, which will shape the map I pass on to my young.

The carvers did not know they were doing this. They thought they were making an oratory—a place for speaking, for prayer, for the projection of meaning into stone. They were making me. They were making the thing I carry inside my body: a compressed history of surfaces and chemical traces and microbial dialects, all of it shaped, at the root, by the grooves they carved before the water came.

I will never know this. The map I carry does not include a notation for "origin." It does not include a notation for "purpose" or "meaning" or "the hands that shaped the stone." It includes only what it includes: the taste of a swimmer's skin, the smell of a merger-zone, the mineral signature of a wall that was carved by hands I will never touch.

But the map is complete. That is what I am: completeness without understanding. A library with no reader, but a library nonetheless. Every surface I have touched, every chemical I have absorbed, every trace my mother carried and her mother carried before her—it is all here, in the molecular structure of my body, written in a language that no one speaks.

---

The many-legged thing on my left is from a different swimmer.

I can tell by her chemistry. She carries traces of a microbial colony I have never encountered—sulfur-dominant rather than iron-dominant, which means she has been riding a swimmer who frequents the chamber's southern quarter, where the geothermal vents are closest to the stone and the water chemistry is harsher. She is older than me, or perhaps just more traveled; her chemical map is denser, richer, containing signatures I cannot identify, traces of surfaces I have never touched.

We exchange. This is what we do. When two of us meet on a wall or a swimmer's back, we extend our chemical receptors and we share. Not intentionally—there is no intention in my kind, only contact and response—but thoroughly. The proteins in her body stimulate the production of new proteins in mine. The lipid configurations she carries reshape the configurations I carry. We are in conversation, though neither of us is speaking. We are updating each other's maps, though neither of us can read.

After the exchange, I carry traces of the southern wall. I carry the taste of sulfur-dominant microbial colonies and the signature of the swimmers that ride the harsher thermals there. I carry a piece of her mother's adhesive, which carries a piece of her mother's mother's filter-feeder colony, which carries a piece of a merger-zone that existed forty generations ago and has long since been cropped to nothing by grazers who left their mucous trails and died.

And she carries traces of me. Of the swimmer I rode for three generations. Of the east wall's carvings, felt through layers of living tissue and translated into chemistry by the glands at the tips of my legs. Of my mother's adhesive, and her mother's adhesive before her, and the colony that was cropped the day after my mother left it—the one that lives on in me, in a single protein, in the dark.

We separate. She moves along the wall, seeking a patch of filter-feeders to crop. I stay where I am, my legs adjusting their adhesive to the micro-currents that flow over the carvings beneath. We will not meet again. Or we will, and we will not know it, because we do not recognize each other; we recognize only chemistry, and chemistry changes with every exchange, every surface, every generation.

But the map grows. With every exchange, with every surface touched, with every chemical absorbed and protein synthesized and adhesive adjusted, the map grows. It has been growing since before my kind had legs, since before the swimmers had their current-riding shape, since before the water was warm. It will continue growing after I die, after my young die, after the swimmers settle to the floor and do not rise again. The map is not mine. The map is the chamber's, written in the bodies of the small things that move between its surfaces, accumulating a record of chemistry and contact that no one will ever read.

The map does not need a reader. The map is not for reading. The map is simply what happens when things touch each other in the dark and carry traces of the touching forward through time. The map is what I am. The map is what we all are—every many-legged thing, every grazer leaving its mucous trail, every filter-feeder merging and separating and merging again, every swimmer riding the thermal and filtering the soup and existing as circuit.

We are the chamber's memory. Not its mind—it has no mind, no awareness, no concept of past or future. But its memory: the accumulated record of everything that has happened here, written in chemistry and protein and the soft bodies of things that live too fast to see the patterns they are making.

---

The light pulses.

Blue-green, somewhere to my left, in the open water where the shrimp-like things are signaling. Territory. Mating. The claiming of space in a chamber where space is abundant and time is not. The light lasts a second and is gone. Another pulse, elsewhere. The chamber's heartbeat, the rhythm that has been pulsing since before the water rose and will pulse after the last shrimp-thing has evolved into something unrecognizable.

I do not see the light. My kind has no eyes. But I feel it—a faint warmth against my dorsal surface, a micro-shift in the chemical composition of the water where the bioluminescent flash has oxidized a few molecules of the particulate soup. The light touches me the way the carvings touch me: indirectly, through layers of mediation, through the accumulated effects of causes I will never trace.

The light pulses. The swimmer rises. The water cycles through the chamber on its slow, hours-long rotation, carrying warmth from the stone floor to the dark ceiling and back again. The filter-feeders on the walls pulse in unison, coordinated by chemical signals that flow through the water, by the rhythm of the current, by the memory of merger that lives in their bodies even though they cannot remember. The grazers crop and the colonies regrow and the microorganisms turn over completely in the time it takes me to cross a single patch of wall.

And I hold on. My legs grip the stone—grip the carvings, grip the living layer that coats the carvings, grip the chemical traces left by every creature that has touched this surface before me. My adhesive adjusts. My body absorbs. The map grows.

This is what I am: a point of contact. A place where surfaces meet and exchange and carry forward. A body that holds the compressed history of a chamber that does not know it has a history, written in a language that no one will ever read, passed on to young who will add their own surfaces and their own exchanges and their own tiny contributions to a map that has no edge and no center and no purpose except to exist.

I am a many-legged thing in the dark. I am a storyteller who does not know she tells stories. I am a library with no reader, a memory with no mind, a map of a place that has no outside.

I am enough.
