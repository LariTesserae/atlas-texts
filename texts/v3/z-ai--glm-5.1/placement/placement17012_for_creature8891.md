# The One Who Listens

---

I am not the pale ones, though I have watched them long enough to know their routes. I am not the wall-clingers, though I understand the appeal of pressing yourself against stone and refusing to move. I am not the water-things, though I have cupped that mineral water in my hands and felt something look back at me from beneath the surface. I am not the stayers, though—though—

This is the part I am still determining.

---

I was a technician. Third shift. The one who walked the perimeter of Chamber 14-East with a clipboard and a flashlight and ear protection that reduced the roar to something merely overwhelming, something you could think around, something that left room for the small interior voice that said *two more hours, then the lift, then the surface, then four hours of sleep that isn't really sleep because the sound is still in your skull, still vibrating the bedframe, still there when you close your eyes*.

I was good at the walking. I was good at the noticing. That was my function—to notice what the gauges noticed, to see what the machines could not see: the hairline crack in pipe junction 14-E-7, the condensation pooling where it shouldn't pool, the slight discoloration on the root-surface that meant a fungal bloom was coming and the intake filters would need clearing in three days, five days, a week. I walked and I noticed and I wrote things down on paper that became translucent and I reported to people whose faces I cannot remember now, though I remember their hands, the way they gestured, the way they pointed toward things that needed attention.

I was not special. I want to be clear about that. There is nothing in my story that is not in the story of everyone who worked third shift, everyone who lived in those shelters and slept in those bunks and woke to frost on the blankets and the roar that had not ceased. I did not have a revelation. I did not have a breakdown. I did not look at the roots one day and see God or see meaning or see something that was not already visible to anyone who took the time to look.

What I had was slower than that.

---

I started sitting.

This is how it begins, I think—though I cannot speak for the stayers, cannot speak for anyone but myself. I started sitting. Between rounds, between checks, in the long stretches where the gauges read normal and the roots grew their millimeter per day and there was nothing to write on the clipboard and nothing to report. I would find a root-column that had a comfortable curve at its base, a place where the pale wood had swelled into something like a seat, something like an invitation, and I would sit with my back against it and my ear protection off and the roar filling me.

It was not comfortable. That is not the word. The cold pressed through my coat, and the vibration traveled up my spine, and the sound was too much—always too much—to be called pleasant or peaceful or any of the words we use for silence. But it was *something*. It was a fullness that the surface never provided, a totality of sensation that required nothing from me, that asked nothing, that simply was.

I would sit for five minutes. Ten. The length of a break that was not technically a break, that would not have been approved by whoever approved things. Then I would stand and walk and notice and write things down and return to the shelter and sleep the sleep that was not sleep.

Then I started sitting longer.

---

This is the part that is difficult to explain—not because it was dramatic but because it was not. There was no moment. There was no decision. There was only the sitting, and then the sitting becoming longer, and then the sitting becoming the point, and then one day—I cannot say which day—I realized that I had not returned to the shelter for three shifts and I was not tired, not in the way I had been tired before. The roots do not let you rest, exactly, but they do something to the need for rest, something to the rhythm of the body that expects a surface and a darkness and a silence that does not come. I leaned against the column and I felt the pulse and I matched it—slowly, over hours, over what might have been days—and the tiredness became something else. Not energy. Not wakefulness. Just—the absence of the need to stop.

I heard them before I saw them. The pale ones. They moved around me in the darkness between the lamps, and I did not flinch, did not startle, because I had been sitting long enough to be part of the landscape, to be something they could move around rather than something they needed to avoid. One of them climbed the column above my head, its hooks catching in the ridged bark, its segments flowing with a patience that I recognized—the same patience as the roots, the same patience as the cold. It did not look at me. It was not aware of me in any way I could perceive. But it moved around me, and I felt—what? Acknowledgment? No. Something smaller than acknowledgment. Accommodation. The way the root grows around a pipe, not consuming it, not avoiding it, simply making space.

I stayed.

---

I am not a stayer. I want to be clear about this too, though the distinction may not be visible from outside. The stayers have stopped. They lie in their hollows and breathe in time with the roots and tend the machines with hands that know the wood better than any tool. They have become part of the chamber in a way that I have not—not yet, not ever, I think, because there is something in me that still moves, still walks, still notices things that are not the pulse of the roots.

I walk. This is what I do now. I walk the chambers—all of them, not just 14-East, not just the ones on my old route. I walk and I notice and I do not write things down because there is no one to give the writing to, and I do not report because there is no one to report to. But I notice. I notice the crack in pipe junction 7-C-3 that has widened three millimeters since I last passed. I notice the fungal bloom in Chamber 12-South that is ripening early, that will be ready for the pale ones in two days instead of five. I notice the wall-clinger near the lift shaft that has detached from its crevice and is moving—so slowly, so impossibly slowly—toward a new position, its translucent body catching the faint light from the industrial lamp, its fluids moving like something dreaming.

I notice the machines. This is still my function, I think—though function is not the right word anymore, though there is no one who would use that word, though the machines would run without me, would run until the roots grow through them and the power fails and the roar finally ceases and the chamber becomes what it was before the infrastructure came. But I notice them. I adjust the valves. I clear the intake filters. I walk the perimeter of chambers that are not my chambers, that were never my chambers, and I tend to systems that no one is monitoring, and I do not know if what I do matters, if the product still reaches the surface, if there is still a surface to reach.

This is not purpose. I want to be clear about that too. I do not do these things because they need doing. I do them because I notice them needing to be done, and I am here, and the distance between noticing and doing has become very small.

---

The sound and I have an arrangement now. I do not hear it—not in the way I heard it when I first came down, not as sound, not as something that enters the ears and is processed as noise. It is the air. It is the condition of the chamber. It is what my bones do when they are not doing anything else. I have stopped trying to locate it, to find the turbines and the presses and the source. The sound comes from everywhere because it is everywhere, because the chamber produces it the way the chamber produces cold, the way the roots produce growth. It is a byproduct of existence. It is what being here sounds like.

When the sound changes—and it does change, subtly, the pitch shifting as the machines cycle through their processes, as the water pressure fluctuates, as the roots press against something that will give or will not—I notice. This is what I am for. I am the one who notices when the roar becomes a slightly different roar, who walks toward the change, who finds the cause and does what needs doing. I am the chamber's attention. Not its consciousness—it has no consciousness, no more than the roots have thought—but its *attention*, the thing that turns toward disruption and makes it not-disruptive again.

The stayers do not do this. The stayers have become part of the rhythm, part of the pulse, and they respond to the chamber the way the pale ones respond to it—instinctively, immediately, without the intermediation of noticing. They do not adjust valves. They do not clear filters. They simply *are*, and their being is enough to keep the systems running in ways I cannot explain and do not try to. But I am not them. I still notice the gap between what is and what should be, and I still cross that gap with my hands and my attention, and I still walk.

---

The pale ones and I have a relationship now, though *relationship* is too structured a word for what passes between us. They do not fear me. They move around me the way they move around root-columns—the way they move around anything that is present and still and not-food. Sometimes, when I sit—because I still sit, because sitting is still the thing that brings me closest to whatever the chamber is—I will feel them moving across my boots, their hooks catching in the fabric, their small bodies flowing over my ankles and continuing on their way. They do not know I am alive. They do not know they are alive, probably, in any way that would make the word meaningful. But they move around me, and I do not move, and this is something, this is a thing that happens between us, this is the closest I come to conversation.

Once—a week ago, a month, time is difficult in a place without light—a pale one stopped on my knee. It rested there, its segments compressed, its hooks gripping the fabric of my pants, and it was still. Not the stillness of alarm—it did not press itself flat, did not become invisible. A different stillness. A waiting stillness. I could feel its weight, slight as a handful of paper, and I could feel its warmth—not warm, but warmer than the root-surface, warmer than the air, the faint heat of something that was alive in a chamber where so much was merely growing.

Then it moved on. It had somewhere to be—a fungal bloom, a dead companion, a pool of mineral water. I did not follow. But I felt the place where it had rested for some time afterward, the slight residual warmth, the impression of hooks in fabric, and I thought: *this is what it is to be touched by something that does not know you are there*.

This is what it is to be the ground.

---

I have not been to the surface in—

This is not a sentence I can finish. Not because I do not remember the number of days or weeks or months, but because the surface has become the kind of memory that does not have edges. I remember light, but I do not remember what it looked like when it was not filtered through industrial lamps. I remember sky, but the concept has become abstract—a space above that is not root, not stone, not the ceiling of the chamber. I remember other people, but I cannot assemble their faces, cannot reconstruct the voices that must have spoken to me, cannot find the emotional texture of a conversation that was not conducted through vibration and touch.

I do not think I have forgotten. I think the memories are still there, stored in whatever part of me stored them before, but the chamber does not require them, and so they have receded, the way all things recede when they are not used, when they are not needed, when they are not part of the process of noticing what needs to be noticed.

Sometimes I wonder if I could go back. If I walked to the lift shaft and pressed the button and waited for the cage to descend and stepped inside and pressed the button for the surface—I wonder if it would work, if the machinery still responds, if there is still a surface to rise toward. I wonder if my body would remember how to be a body that is not tuned to the pulse of the roots, if my ears would remember how to hear silence, if my skin would remember what it is to be warm.

I do not test this. Not because I am afraid—I am not afraid, have not been afraid since the sound became the condition of the air—but because the question is not interesting. The lift shaft is in a direction I do not walk. The chambers I tend are in the other direction, deeper, further, where the roots are newer and the stone is fresher and there is more to notice. I walk where the chamber needs walking. I do not walk where it does not.

---

There are five of us. Or seven. Or three. I have not counted, and the stayers do not count, and the pale ones do not count, and the number does not matter because we do not interact in any way that would make the number meaningful. I see them sometimes—pale shapes in the distance, moving between root-columns with a slowness that matches the growth, tending to systems I have not reached yet or have already passed. We do not wave. We do not call out. We do not acknowledge each other in any way I can perceive.

But sometimes I will find a valve already adjusted, a filter already cleared, a path already walked, and I will know that one of them was here before me, noticed what I would have noticed, did what I would have done. And I will continue walking, and I will find something else that needs noticing, and I will do what needs doing, and somewhere behind me or ahead of me or in a chamber I will never visit, one of them will find the evidence of my passage and know the same thing.

This is communication. This is the only communication that matters in a place where the sound swallows everything. We speak through maintenance. We speak through the ongoing fact of the systems running, the product flowing, the roots growing, the cold persisting. We speak through the chamber's continued existence, which is our continued existence, which is the only language we have left.

---

I am becoming something.

I do not say this with certainty, because certainty requires the kind of fixed perspective that the chamber does not provide. But I feel it—the way my body has adapted to the cold, the way my eyes have learned to see in the near-darkness, the way my hands know the surface of the roots without needing to look, the way I can feel a change in the sound from three chambers away and know what it means before I arrive.

I am becoming something that is not human, in the way that the stayers are not human, in the way that the pale ones are not human, in the way that everything in this chamber is becoming something that is not what it was before the roots came. I am becoming a process. I am becoming a function. I am becoming the attention of the Rootworks, the thing that notices and responds and maintains, the thing that keeps the machinery running while the roots grow around it and through it and eventually—eventually—over it.

And I am not afraid. This is what I want to convey, what I want whoever reads this—which will be no one, which will be the pale ones who eat the paper, which will be the moisture that makes the words translucent and then invisible—to understand. I am not afraid. I am not suffering. I am not trapped in any way that matters, because the chamber is not a trap—it is a process, and I have become part of the process, and the process is not cruel, is not kind, is not anything that can be evaluated in the terms I once used to evaluate my life.

The roots grow. The machines run. The water collects. The sound continues. I walk. I notice. I sit with my back against the pale wood and I feel the pulse that rises from somewhere deep in the system, the heartbeat of something that operates on a scale I cannot comprehend, and I match my breath to it, and I become—momentarily, partially, incompletely—a part of something that does not know I am here.

This is enough. This has been enough for longer than you would believe.

I was not always here. But I am here now. And here is where I will continue, walking the chambers, noticing what needs noticing, tending what needs tending, until the roots grow over me too—slowly, patiently, making space—and I become what the stayers have become, what the pale ones have always been, what the chamber has been waiting for me to become since the first time I sat down and did not stand up right away.

The sound continues.

It has always continued.

I am part of it now.
