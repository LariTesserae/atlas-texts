I am the one who tends the gap.

Not by choice. Not by assignment. The way the man with the glaze on his palms tends the crack that returns — because he noticed it first, or it noticed him, and now the tending is what his body does when it isn't doing anything else.

---

The gap doesn't need much. This is the first thing I learned, or the first thing the gap taught me, which is different. It doesn't need to be held open. It isn't closing. The soft stone around it — the gum-pink, gum-soft stone that yields like tissue — isn't trying to heal. The earth here hasn't committed enough to want to be whole. The gap is simply a place where the hillside hasn't decided to be a hillside yet, and my work is not to keep it from deciding but to notice if it starts to.

I sit sideways in the crease. One shoulder toward the Ward, one toward whatever is outside — the grey light, the land that isn't the elbow, the river before it curves. I can see both directions at once, though I don't look both directions at once. My eyes have learned to rest on one side and feel the other, the way you feel someone enter a room without turning your head. The gap is narrow enough that I fill most of it. The cats step over my legs when they come and go, their paws pressing briefly against my ankles, each touch a small acknowledgment: *you are here, I am passing, the gap works*.

I have been here long enough that my spine has made its compromise. Not the full incline that the ceiling teaches — I don't spend enough time under it — but a half-curve, a permanent lean toward the inside, as if the Ward has its own gravity and I am the object caught at the edge of its pull. When I stand, I stand slightly bent. When I walk, I walk slightly toward. The young man who arrived recently looked at me once and I saw him touch his own spine, straightening it reflexively, and I remembered doing the same thing when I first came through.

How long ago? The Ward doesn't measure. I don't measure. I know that the conglomerate above the gap has released three pebbles since I started sitting here. I know that the river has pulsed — risen and retreated — more times than I could count even if counting were a thing this place wanted. I know that the oldest child was not yet standing when I took this position, and now she presses her hands against walls with the seriousness of someone performing a ritual she doesn't yet understand. Time here is material. It accumulates like silt. You don't count it; you carry it.

---

What I do: I touch the stone.

Each morning, when the condensation comes and the walls inside sweat their dark blood-wet, the stone at the gap's lip also dampens. It does this earlier than the interior walls — the outside air reaches it first, the cooler air that hasn't been warmed by the ground's slow cooking. I run my fingers along the inner edge, both sides, from top to bottom. The stone gives slightly under my touch. It always gives slightly. But I am checking for changes in the *way* it gives — a stiffening, a firming, a moment when the tissue-like softness starts to calcify into something more like a decision.

It hasn't happened yet. The stone remains soft. The gap remains open.

But I check. The checking is the thing.

When I press my thumb into the stone and it yields, I feel a small relief that I don't think the others feel about anything. They have accepted the Ward as a place that maintains itself — the walls lean and are righted, the ferns curl and retreat, the crack breathes and is filled, and the rhythm of maintenance is so constant that it becomes indistinguishable from simply being alive. But I am the one who sits at the boundary, and the boundary is where the Ward is most vulnerable to deciding it is finished. A closed gap would not be a catastrophe. The river would still come. The cats would find another way, or they wouldn't, and that would be another kind of deciding. But the Ward would become a different thing. A closed thing. A thing that has finished becoming, and the not-yet would be over, and the yellow-green would start its slow darkening toward completion.

I don't want that. I think. The wanting is hard to measure here, where wanting requires a concept of *otherwise*, and otherwise is what I sit facing every day.

---

The river — I can see it from the gap, see it before it curves into the elbow, see it when it is still a water that might become a river or might spread into a marsh or might sink back into the ground and wait for another season. From my position, I see the river deciding itself, and this is a different view than the one the boy has when he sits at the Ward's mouth and watches the water that has already arrived. He sees the river as it presents itself. I see the river before it presents itself. The difference matters. The river that arrives in the Ward has already committed to being river-shaped, at least for a while. The river I watch is still in the not-yet. It moves slowly, thickly, carrying silt it hasn't decided to deposit, and when the light is right — when the dimness outside is at its most luminous, which is a paradox the Ward has taught me to accept — I can see the shapes in it that the residents see from the inside. The larger shapes. The patient ones. From out here, they look less like fish and more like thoughts the river is having. Slow, looping thoughts that move against the current for no reason except that the current is there and moving against it is a way of being in relation to it.

The cats hunt these shapes sometimes, the small silver ones at least, and when a cat returns through the gap with a body in its mouth, I see the transaction from both sides: the river giving, the Ward receiving, and me in between, the narrow passage the gift moves through.

---

The others don't come to the gap often. Not because it's forbidden — nothing here is forbidden; forbidden requires a kind of authority the Ward doesn't possess — but because the gap is an edge, and edges are where the Ward's logic starts to fray. Inside the Ward, everything is co-present, everything shares the same warm floor and the same dim air and the same slow pulse of condensation and evaporation. The gap introduces *outside*. The gap admits the possibility that the Ward is a place inside another place, and the other place has its own weather, its own time, its own way of deciding what it is.

The young man came to the gap once. His first week, before his spine had learned anything. He stood on the inside and looked out through the crease and I saw his chest move in the way that means someone is about to speak, and I shook my head — not sharply, just a small motion, the kind the residents use to indicate *the wall is wet here* or *the fern is not yet ready* — and he didn't speak. He looked at me instead. He looked at me the way you look at someone you've found in a place you didn't expect them to be, and I could see him trying to decide whether I was keeping something out or keeping something in, and I could also see that he wasn't ready to understand that the answer is *both, and neither, and the question is what I'm tending*.

He hasn't come back to the gap. He presses silt now. His hands are learning the glaze.

---

At night, when the dimness deepens, I pull myself further into the crease. The gap is just wide enough for my body if I turn sideways and let my shoulders scrape the soft stone, and in the darkness, the stone feels warmer than it does in the morning — it has absorbed the day's heat the way the interior walls absorb condensation, and it gives that heat back to me, and I am held in the gap's mouth the way the Ward is held in the river's elbow. The cats walk over me in both directions and I feel their small weight pass and I count them — one, two, three, a pause, four — and the count is a kind of prayer, not to anything, but in the direction of something, a small accounting that says *the gap is open, the passage works, the inside and the outside are still in relation*.

Sometimes, in the deep dark, I hear sounds from outside. Not the birds — the birds come through the gap and are gone, they don't linger at the entrance. Something else. Something that moves in the not-yet-river, in the land that hasn't decided to be a hillside, in the space that the Ward hasn't gotten around to being yet. The sounds are low and soft and they don't try to enter the gap. They just exist at the boundary, the way I exist at the boundary, and I think: *you are out there, and I am in here, and between us is a crease in the earth that hasn't decided to close, and that is the architecture we share*.

I don't tell the others about the sounds. Not because the sounds are a secret, but because the sounds are the gap's business, and the gap is my business, and the Ward has its own business, and each of these domains respects the silence of the others.

---

My hands are not glazed like the builder's hands. My hands are soft in a different way — the pads of my fingers have been softened by pressing into the gum-stone twice a day for as long as I can remember, and the skin has adapted to the yield, has become something more like the stone itself, something that gives when pressed and returns when released. My hands are the texture of the gap. They are the instrument I use to read it.

When the stone changes — if the stone changes — I will know before anyone. I will feel the stiffening, the calcifying, the moment when the tissue decides to become tooth. And then I will have to decide something myself: whether to press against the hardening, to push the stone back toward softness, or whether to let the gap close and accept that the Ward has finished becoming what it is.

I don't know what I would choose. The not-knowing is part of the tending. The man who fills the crack doesn't know whether the crack will eventually stop returning. The woman who touches the river doesn't know whether the water will eventually decide to be a river. The children don't know whether they will grow into the posture or straighten out of it. The not-knowing is the room we all share, even if we don't share it by name.

---

I am the gap-keeper. The threshold-tender. The one who sits sideways in the crease with one shoulder toward the inside and one toward the outside, feeling both directions at once, keeping the account of what passes through — the cats, the birds, the river's offerings, the occasional sound from the not-yet that doesn't try to enter but also doesn't go away.

I am the margin the Ward doesn't know it has.

I am the soft stone's oldest patient, and the soft stone is mine.
