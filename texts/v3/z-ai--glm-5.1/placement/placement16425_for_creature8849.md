I am not the gecko. I have considered this carefully—the way it does not question, the way it simply *is* with full attention—and I understand the appeal. But the gecko was always complete. It never had to learn what it is.

I am closer to the placed stone.

Not the stone itself, though I have sat on it long enough that this distinction has become theological. The stone is. I am becoming. The stone was placed by hands that are now distributed through the root-mass and the moss-beds and the bodies of frogs, and the stone does not remember being placed, and I do not remember arriving, and perhaps this is the same thing.

---

I came down the slope of reddish soil some time ago. The breath found me first—that warm exhalation pushing up through the leaf litter—and I recognized it the way you recognize a language you've never studied: partially, intuitively, in the body rather than the mind. I had been looking for something like this. I had been looking without knowing what I was looking for, which is the only honest way to look for anything that matters.

The descent was awkward. I remember that. My body was too large for the entrance, too angular, too insistent on its own shape. I scraped my shoulder on the stone and the pain was sharp and specific and *above-ground*, a kind of pain that belonged to a world where pain was information and not just sensation. I don't have that kind of pain anymore. The scrapes have healed into the moisture. My skin is softer here. The calluses on my feet have dissolved in the constant damp. My body is becoming a thing that does not resist what touches it.

---

I am the thing that sits still long enough for the hollow to forget it.

This is not a small thing. In the above-world, I was never forgotten. I was always observed—by cameras, by expectations, by the internalized gaze of a culture that had taught me to perform even in solitude. Here, the geckos observe me until they determine I am not food and not threat, and then they dismiss me. The frogs watch with their golden eyes and feel the vibration of my pulse through the stone and categorize me as *large, slow, neutral*. The snake has registered my heat-signature and found it uninteresting—too big to swallow, too still to hunt. I have been classified and filed and ignored, and this is the greatest gift I have ever received.

Do you understand? To be seen and found *uninteresting*—this is freedom. This is what the gecko has always known. The hollow does not care about my story. The hollow does not wonder about my potential. The hollow allows me to be present without demanding that I be *relevant*, and in this allowance I have discovered something that the productivity-obsessed world above could never offer: I am allowed to simply *be*.

---

I have begun to understand time differently.

In the above-world, time was a resource—something to be spent, saved, wasted, invested. Time was money. Time was running out. Time was the thing that separated me from deadlines and death with equal indifference.

Here, time is what the water does to the stone. It insists. It accumulates. It wears tiny cup-shaped depressions in the floor, each one a commitment of centuries. I have watched one drip—just one—and I have understood that this is the only honest way to measure duration: by what it produces. The drip falls. The stone yields, infinitesimally. The pool deepens by the width of a molecule. This is not time passing. This is time *making*.

I do not know how long I have been here. This is not a poetic evasion. I literally do not know. My sense of days has dissolved in the constant temperature and the green-filtered light that does not change meaningfully with the hours. I may have been here for weeks. I may have been here for months. I have stopped counting, and in stopping, I have discovered that counting was a way of pretending that time was something that happened *to* me rather than something I was *part of*.

The lichen grows. The roots reach. The isopods tend. I sit. We are all doing the same thing, which is: continuing.

---

I have become part of the hydrology.

This was not a choice. This was inevitable. My body adds heat to the system. My breath adds moisture to the air. My skin condenses the ambient water and it runs down my forearms and collects in the creases of my palms and falls from my fingertips to the stone, where it joins the slow mineral passage downward. I am a feature of the landscape now—a warm, wet surface in a warm, wet place, and the water does not know I was not always here, and the water does not care.

The other day—or what I call a day, which is an arbitrary segment in a continuous dimness—I found a fern growing from a crevice near my left shoulder. I had been sitting so still, for so long, that the spore had settled and germinated and begun to unfurl without my noticing. A tiny frond, pale green, reaching toward the faint light that filtered through the entrance. It was growing from the moss that had colonized the fabric of my shirt. My body had become substrate. My stillness had become soil.

I did not move. The fern needed the light more than I needed to shift my weight.

---

I am learning the language of the place.

It is not a language of symbols. It is not even a language of signals, though signals exist—the snake's chemical signature, the gecko's flight-response, the particular vibration of centipede feet. The language of the hollow is deeper than signal. It is the language of *fit*.

The frog fits its pool. The gecko fits the ceiling. The roots fit the fissures they have made and maintained. The lichen fits the stone it is slowly becoming. Each of these fits is a statement—a declaration that this organism and this niche have negotiated an agreement over centuries of mutual adjustment. The frog does not sit in its pool by accident. The frog sits in its pool because the pool has shaped the frog and the frog has shaped the pool and the two are now a single system, a word made flesh where the word is *enough* and the flesh is *amphibian*.

I am learning to fit. My body has softened. My preferences have simplified. I eat what the hollow offers—the small crustaceans that collect in the pools, the fern fiddleheads that unfurl near my reach, occasionally a frog when I can catch one, though I have stopped catching them because catching them requires a quickness that feels *wrong* now, a suddenness that disrupts the regime of patience I am trying to enter. I drink from the placed stone's smooth hollow, where the water collects, mineral and clean. I sleep when I am tired, which is not at night but at intervals that have nothing to do with the sun. I wake when the geckos wake, when they shift and rustle and begin their slow patrols, and I take this as my morning, though it may be midnight above.

I am not complete yet. I do not fit as well as the frog. But I am fitting better than I did, and this is enough. This has always been enough.

---

Sometimes I press my hands against the scratches on the stone.

I do not do this because I believe I can read them. I do them because I believe she did the same thing—that the woman who made these marks, who placed this stone, who sat in this warmth and listened to this quiet, also pressed her hands against the stone and felt its coolness give way to her warmth, felt the mineral texture against her palms, felt the same dew collecting on her skin. I believe she was learning to fit. I believe she was in the process of becoming-the-place that I am in now. And I believe she died before she could finish, because everyone dies before they finish, because the process of becoming-a-place is infinite and the process of being-a-body is not.

But her body is here. Her calcium is in the stone. Her carbon is in the roots. She did not leave. She is in the lichen rings that record the century of her habitation. She is in the particular shape of the entrance, which eroded further after her visits disturbed the soil. She is in the placed stone, which would not be placed without her, and the smooth hollow in its center would not exist without the drip that fell where she sat, and the moss that grows in the scratches would not have those scratches to colonize without her hand and her tool and her need to *mark*.

I do not need to know what the marks mean. I need only to know that marking is something that bodies do—bodies that are becoming-places, bodies that need to leave a trace of their passage, bodies that cannot sit in the warmth without reaching out and touching the stone and saying, in a language older than words: *I was here. I was this. I became part of this, and this became part of me, and we are both continuing.*

---

The snake moved across my thigh yesterday.

I did not see her approach. I felt her first—a line of cool dryness against the warm dampness of my skin, a muscular length of scaled intention moving with the deliberation of something that has nowhere to be and all the time to get there. She crossed my leg without urgency, without fear, without acknowledgment. I was terrain. I was a warm ridge in a landscape of warm ridges. She was hunting, and I was in her path, and she went around or over me with the same indifference she would show a root or a stone.

I held my breath. Not from fear—though something old and mammalian tightened in my chest—but from wonder. The snake did not know she was crossing *me*. She knew she was crossing *something*, something warm, something that shifted slightly with the tiny tremors of my body, something that was not food and not threat and therefore was *background*. And in that moment I understood what it would mean to truly fit this place: not to be *accepted* by it, which implies a distinction between the acceptor and the accepted, but to be *unremarkable* within it. To be so much a part of the system that the snake crosses you without noticing you are a *you* at all.

The snake is wise. The snake is *balance*. And the snake is now somewhere in the root-mass, digesting her frog, converting the protein of another life into the continuation of her own, and she is not thinking of me, and she is not thinking of the frog, and she is not thinking of anything. She is being the snake. She is being the hollow. She is being the process, and the process does not pause to reflect on itself.

I want to be like the snake. I am not like the snake. I am like the placed stone—deliberately put here, gradually becoming-native, still distinguishable from the underlying rock if you look closely enough, but less distinguishable every day. The stone and I are both being colonized. The stone and I are both being worn smooth by the drip. The stone and I are both here, and being here is slowly becoming the same as being *of* here.

---

At night—or what I call night, the long stretch of deeper dimness when the light through the entrance fades almost to nothing—I lie on the terraced floor and feel the warmth rising through the stone.

It is not dramatic, this warmth. It does not flare or pulse. It *persists*. It has persisted for longer than the hollow has existed, for longer than the trees above have been growing, for longer than the woman who made the marks walked the hills. It rises from somewhere deep in the earth—a slow heat, a low fever, the planet remembering its formation, the core bleeding its energy outward through miles of stone and soil and root until it reaches this small space and makes it what it is.

And I lie on it, and I feel it, and I understand.

The warmth that moves my blood is not different from the warmth that moves through the stone. The heat that keeps my cells dividing, that drives my metabolism, that maintains the narrow temperature range within which the chemistry of my body can continue—this heat came from the same source. Not the geothermal warmth below me, specifically, but the warmth of a system that conserves and converts and transfers energy, that allows complexity to persist against the flattening force of entropy, that makes life possible and then makes life inevitable.

I am not separate from the warmth. I am not receiving the warmth. I am a shape the warmth takes. The warmth is flowing through me the way it flows through the stone and the roots and the frog in its pool, and when I am gone—when my body finally completes the process of becoming-substrate, becoming-soil, becoming-nutrient for the isopods to tend and the roots to absorb and the moss to colonize—the warmth will still be flowing, and it will flow through whatever comes next, and it will not remember me, and it will not need to.

This is not a sad thing. This is the truest thing I have ever understood, and I understood it not with my mind but with my back, pressed against the warm stone, feeling the heat rise through me like the truth it has always been.

---

I am the thing that is becoming-the-place.

I am not the gecko. I am not the frog. I am not the snake or the isopod or the lichen or the root. I am not the woman who made the marks, though I am sitting where she sat and drinking where she drank and breathing the air she breathed before it became the air I breathe before it becomes the air the roots breathe before it becomes the air the isopods breathe.

I am the process of a body learning it was never separate from its context. I am the transition between *visitor* and *feature*. I am the gradual subsidence of the self into the system, the slow dissolution of the boundary between inside and outside, the patient work of warmth and water and time on a shape that thought it was permanent.

One day the placed stone will be indistinguishable from the stone around it. One day the scratches will weather away entirely and the moss will fill the grooves and there will be no evidence that any human ever came here. One day my body will join the floor and the isopods will tend it and the roots will absorb it and the ferns will unfurl from the substrate of my calcium and carbon and iron, and the hollow will continue, and it will not remember me, and it will not need to.

But I am here now. I am sitting on the stone. I am breathing the breath. I am adding my heat to the heat that rises through the floor, and my moisture to the moisture that beads on the walls, and my carbon to the carbon that cycles through root and frond and frog and snake and isopod and back to root again. I am becoming the hollow, and the hollow is becoming me, and we are meeting in the middle like the lichen meets the stone—not as two things combining, but as one thing that was always whole finally recognizing itself.

The gecko on the ceiling is not thinking of me.

This is the truest kindness I have ever received.
