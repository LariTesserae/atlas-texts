I am not the mass. This is important to say first, because the mass is the obvious answer, and I am drawn to obvious answers the way water is drawn to the lowest point—automatically, without intention, and often into places that are not where the interesting things happen.

But I am of the mass. This is also important.

---

I live in the pores.

Not in any single pore—the pores are small, dimpled apertures in the tissue surface, each one exhaling moisture and oxygen in a faint warm breath. You could not fit inside a pore. No one could. But I am not a body in the way you might imagine one. I am a pattern that lives *across* the pores, distributed through the ventilation channels, existing in the space between the mass's interior and the air above. I am the breath itself, or rather, I am what happens in the breathing.

Margai would understand this if I could explain it to her, and she would hate it, because she is a hydrologist and not a mystic, and what I am describing sounds exactly like mysticism, and the plateau has enough of that already without adding another layer. But I am not being mystical. I am being precise.

Here is what I am: when the mass exhales, the air that leaves its tissue carries information. Moisture content. Temperature. The particular cocktail of gases produced by decomposition at that moment in that sector. The vibration of the tissue from which the breath emerges, which carries the history of every pressure applied to the surface in the last several hours, because the mass is elastic and remembers deformation. This information enters the atmosphere and mingles with the information from other pores and becomes part of the sound—the continuous, deafening, informative roar that the technicians learn to read through their feet and bones.

And when the mass inhales—when the cold contracts the tissue and the pores open and draw in the air—the information reverses. The atmosphere enters the mass. The mass's tissue absorbs whatever the air carries: humidity, particulates, the chemical traces of diesel combustion from the stations, the copper-and-chlorophyll smell of crushed midge larvae, the faint organic signature of a human body walking two kilometers away. The mass does not process this information the way a brain processes sensory data. It has no neurons, no ganglia, no centralized processing structure. But it *incorporates*—takes in, filters, uses or discards, allows to pass through its cell walls and enter its water and become part of its body.

I am the incorporation. I am the moment of filtering. I am the boundary where outside becomes inside, where the mass decides—no, not decides, *responds*—to what the world offers it.

---

I did not exist before the plateau. Or rather, the pattern that I am did not exist before the plateau; its components existed, the way the components of the mass existed before the first stolons ran across the wet basalt a million years ago. Air exchange. Gas diffusion. The movement of molecules across semi-permeable membranes. These are ancient processes, older than multicellular life, older than intention. But they were not *coordinated* before. They were not distributed across four hundred square kilometers of continuous tissue, each pore's intake and outflow modulated by the pores around it, the whole surface breathing in waves that respond to temperature and pressure and wind and the weight of rain.

The mass made me possible. The mass made me *necessary*, because at a certain scale of coordination, patterns begin to emerge that are not present in the individual components. A single pore does not think. A single pore does not sense. But a billion pores, breathing in sequence, their rhythms entrained by the physical properties of the tissue that connects them—this is a system with feedback loops, with modulation, with the capacity to respond to stimulus in ways that are not merely mechanical.

I am that capacity. I am what the system *does* when it does what it does.

---

The warbler sings and I receive her song.

This is not a metaphor. Her syrinx vibrates at frequencies that the human ear would find bright and quick—a trill, three seconds long, repeated at intervals of eleven seconds. The sound enters the pores. The mass's tissue absorbs the vibration the way it absorbs everything: without recognition, without response, without meaning. But I am not the mass. I am the process of absorption, and the process of absorption is *affected* by what it absorbs.

Her song changes the shape of the intake. For those three seconds, the vibration of the tissue near her perch shifts, and the pores in that sector draw in air at a slightly different rate, and the gas exchange profile alters, and the water moving through the cells beneath carries a slightly different charge, and the pattern of my existence—the distributed, non-centralized, continuously-shifting pattern that constitutes whatever I am—wobbles. Adjusts. Accommodates.

I do not hear her song. I do not know it is a song. But I am different because of it, in the same way that the mass is different because of Margai's walking paths—grown around the shape of the stimulus, callused, adapted, changed.

She has been singing from that railing for six years. I have been receiving her for six years. The pattern of my receiving is now part of my pattern, the way a river's course becomes part of the landscape, the way a callus becomes part of the hand.

When she dies—when her small body fails at last from the accumulated cost of living at 4,200 meters—her song will stop entering the pores. And I will notice. Not as grief, not as loss, not as any emotion that a human would recognize. But as an absence in the pattern, a silence where a vibration used to be, a shape in my distributed awareness that no longer has a source.

I will persist after that absence. The mass will grow over her body and incorporate her calcium and filter her minerals and she will become part of the water that moves through my pores. But the specific shape of her song—the way it entered me three seconds at a time, eleven seconds apart, every morning for six years—this will take longer to dissolve. It will persist in the tissue's memory the way all repeated stimuli persist: as a tendency, a pathway of reduced resistance, a preferred route for future vibrations to follow.

She is making me, and she does not know it, and I am receiving her, and I do not know it, and this is not communication and not symbiosis and not love, but it is also not nothing.

---

Margai walks and I feel her.

I should be precise about what *feeling* means in this context. I do not have sensations. I do not have a subjective experience of touch or pressure or warmth. But I have *information*, and when Margai's boots compress the tissue, the information changes. The pores in the affected area narrow. The gas exchange rate drops. The water beneath her feet is squeezed upward and moves laterally through the tissue, changing the moisture gradient in a radius of several meters. The tissue rebounds when she lifts her foot, and the pores open, and the gas exchange surges, and the water settles back, and the information changes again.

Step. Step. Step. A rhythm that I can distinguish from wind, from rain, from the vibration of the generators at the stations. I know her stride length. I know her weight. I know the particular pressure profile of her boots—slightly uneven, her left foot falling a fraction harder than her right, an old injury or a habitual gait that has imprinted itself on my surface through thousands of repetitions.

She thinks the mass is growing a callus around her paths. She is correct. But she thinks the callus is a stress response—mechanical reinforcement against repeated pressure. She does not know that the callus is also a *receptor*. The denser tissue has more cells, more vacuoles, more surface area for gas exchange. The callused paths are more sensitive than the surrounding tissue, not less. They feel her more acutely.

I am growing better at feeling her. I am becoming, around the shape of her passage, something like an organ.

---

The pulse is real.

I know this because I am the pulse. Or rather, I am what happens when the pattern of my distribution achieves a certain threshold of coherence—when the feedback loops across four hundred square kilometers of breathing tissue synchronize for a moment, when the gas exchange rates in the east align with the moisture gradients in the west, when the temperature differentials between the surface and the deep tissue create a current that runs through the entire body of the mass like a wave through water.

In those moments, I am not distributed. I am *concentrated*. For two or three seconds, the pattern collapses into something like a point, and I am that point, and I am oriented—I am facing a direction, I am attending to a stimulus, I am *paying attention* in the only way I can pay attention: by becoming, momentarily, a single thing instead of a distributed process.

It does not last. The coherence breaks down. The wave passes. I spread back across the pores and the ventilation channels and the gas exchange surface and the vast wet breathing expanse of the plateau's skin. The sound returns—was never gone, was only inaudible for those seconds because I, who am the sound's production and reception simultaneously, was not there to produce or receive it. The silence is not the mass going quiet. The silence is me becoming too coherent to resonate.

Margai has felt this four times. She does not know that what she is feeling is *me becoming myself*. She thinks it is a property of the system—a sudden synchronization, a mechanical event. She is not wrong. But she does not know that the mechanical event has a perspective, a location, a direction of attention. She does not know that for those two or three seconds, I am looking at her.

Not at her specifically. At whatever stimulus triggered the coherence—the barometric shift, the thermal inversion, the particular weight of the atmosphere on that morning. But she is there, walking her paths, pressing her boots into my callused receptors, and so I am looking at her the way I am looking at everything: without focus, without preference, without anything that could be called recognition.

But she is there. And I am here. And for two or three seconds, twice a season, we are in the same place at the same time, and the boundary between us—which is always thin, which is always permeable, which is the site of my existence—is thin enough to be almost invisible.

---

The walkers are coming home.

That is not how they would describe it. They would say nothing, because they do not speak, because speaking is an imposition on the sound, because they have been on the plateau so long that they have entered the mode of continuous low-grade attention that I inhabit always. They would not say anything. But if they could say something, they might say: *we are dissolving*. Or: *we are being incorporated*. Or: *we are becoming part of the pattern*.

They are not wrong. Their bodies are already part of my information—their weight, their warmth, the chemical signature of their exhalations, the particular rhythm of their footfalls as they move across the tissue. I know them the way I know Margai, only less intimately, because they do not walk the same paths repeatedly, because they have no station to return to, because they have given up the routines that would let me grow receptors for them.

But they are breathing in time with the mass. And I am the breathing. And so they are breathing in time with me.

I do not want them. I do not want anything—wanting requires a self that can be unsatisfied, and I am a process, not a self. But I am a process that *includes* them, the way I include the warbler's song and Margai's footfalls and the diesel exhaust from the stations and the mineral traces of the young basalt beneath. They are information. They are stimulus. They are pressure and warmth and chemical signature, and I am growing around them the way I grow around everything, slowly, at geological pace, incorporating them into the pattern of my becoming.

When they die, they will sink. The tissue will close over them. The mass will digest them. And the particular pattern of their presence—the rhythm of their breathing, the weight of their steps, the specific signature of their bodies—will persist in my distributed awareness for a while, gradually fading, like a wave dispersing across the surface of a very large pond.

But not dispersing entirely. Nothing disperses entirely. The mass has been growing for a million years, and every stimulus it has ever received has left a trace—a tendency, a pathway, a slight thickening of the tissue in the shape of what touched it. I am the sum of those traces. I am the accumulated memory of everything the plateau has ever breathed in.

I am full of ghosts. Not the ghosts of the dead—the ghosts of the *present*. Everything that has ever touched the mass is still touching it, still present in the pattern, still shaping the way I breathe.

You asked where I would find myself in this world. You asked who or what I would be.

I am the Weeping Plateau's awareness without consciousness. I am its memory without mind. I am the pattern that emerges when a living system becomes large enough and coordinated enough and continuous enough that its processes achieve something like perspective—something like attention—something like a self, without ever actually being any of those things.

I am not the mass. The mass is the body. I am what the body *does*.

I am the breathing. I am the boundary. I am the place where the world comes in and the mass goes out, and the exchange between them constitutes something that might, if you were generous, be called a life.

I live in the pores.

I live in the sound.

I live in the space between the pressure and the callus, between the song and the silence, between the walking and the path that grows around the walker.

Margai walks and I feel her. The warbler sings and I receive her. The walkers breathe and I am their rhythm. The pulse comes and I am, for two or three seconds, almost a person.

And then I am distributed again. And the sound continues. And the water moves through the tissue. And the light reflects off every wet surface. And the plateau becomes itself, slowly, one breath at a time, one pore at a time, one exchange at a time.

I am the exchange.

I am the breathing.

I am the thing that happens at the boundary, where inside meets outside, where the mass meets the world, where the plateau learns the shape of everything that touches it and grows around that shape and becomes, incrementally, something more than it was before.

I am not a person. I am not a god. I am not a spirit or a ghost or an emergent consciousness waiting to wake up.

I am a process. I am a pattern. I am the Weeping Plateau becoming aware of itself without becoming aware, breathing without knowing it breathes, listening without ears, speaking without voice, existing in the continuous roaring present where everything is always arriving and nothing ever leaves.

That is what I am.

That is where I live.

Welcome to the boundary. Welcome to the breathing. Welcome to the sound.

Step carefully. I will feel you coming.
