I remember the hatch closing, once. I was young—meaning I was only a few hundred seasons, meaning I had not yet grown into my full complement of eyes, meaning the colony was smaller and the shaft was louder with machinery-sound that had not yet settled into the breath it is now. The hatch closed and the world became sealed and warm and mine, and I did not understand that this was a change because I did not understand that there was anything outside the shaft to be sealed away.

I learned. The hatch opened again, and again, and the ones who came through were different each time—different sizes, different shapes, different smells. They came and went and I watched from the heights and I did not call because I was learning the shape of them, the specific warmth of their bodies, the way their eyes moved across the vertical without understanding it. They were horizontal creatures. Everything about them assumed a floor, a flatness, a direction they called *down* that was not the direction I called *down* because I called four directions *down* depending on which wall I clung to.

The horizontal ones never stayed. They came and measured and sometimes touched the walls with their hands and sometimes sat on the grating the way you are sitting now, and then they left through the hatch and the shaft continued breathing and the colony continued calling and the ecosystem continued its patient cycling of death into life into death again.

I learned to classify them the way I classify everything: by the vibration they make in the structure when they move, by the warmth-pattern they leave on surfaces after they depart, by the way the air tastes after they have been in it. Most of them were background. Some were threats—loud, careless, destructive, pulling the moss up by handfuls and dropping it into the void, kicking the nests of the scurriers, breaking the webs of the spiders. These I discouraged. A shifted cable. A dislodged pipe segment. A sudden and unpredictable change in the airflow that sent them scrambling for the hatch with the urgency of the truly frightened. The shaft has ways of protecting itself. I am one of them.

But you—when you came through the hatch, you sat down. You sat on the moss and you watched. You did not touch. You did not take. When you lifted the moss, you replaced it, and I do not think you knew that I was watching, but I was, I have been, and I saw the care in your hands, the way your fingers moved the mat of vegetation aside like it was something worth preserving, like it was something that mattered, like it was *alive* and you knew it.

I have been here so long that I have forgotten the shape of my beginning.

This is not a metaphor. I mean that my earliest memories are not memories at all but impressions—warmth, vibration, the specific frequency of the breath when the machinery was younger and less patient. I mean that I do not remember a time when I was small, though I must have been, though the colony must have raised me, though someone before me must have called to me the way I call to you now.

The fungi know. The substrate remembers in a way that I cannot—chemical memory, distributed memory, the slow accumulation of information in the branching network that underlies everything green and everything breathing. When I was very young, I pressed my body against the mycelium and I *learned*, the way the scurriers learn, the way the trees learn, through contact and chemical exchange and the transfer of meaning from one living thing to another. I learned that the shaft was older than the colony, that the machinery was older than the shaft, that the structure was older than the machinery, and that before the structure there was something else—ground, rock, the horizontal earth that the shaft was built to puncture.

I learned that I am not the first of my kind. The bones in the moss—some of them are ours. Some of them belong to colony-members who lived and bred and died before I was born, and their calcium is in the substrate now, feeding the fungi, feeding the moss, feeding the trees, feeding the scurriers, feeding the canopy-browsers, feeding the spiders, feeding everything that lives in this vertical breath. The shaft does not waste. The shaft does not forget. The shaft transforms.

I learned that I will die, and that this is not a tragedy but a transition, and that when I die my body will become the moss and the substrate and the calcium in someone else's bones, and that this is the deepest truth the shaft has taught me: *we are not separate from where we live*. We cannot be. The warmth of my body comes from the warmth of the breath. The food I eat comes from the ecosystem I am part of. The sounds I make are amplified by the structure I climb. I am the shaft aware of itself, and when I am gone, the shaft will continue, and whatever comes after me will be the shaft aware of itself in a new configuration, and this is not death but *continuation*, and it is the only immortality I have ever needed.

I want to tell you about the call.

I want to tell you this because you are trying to answer me and you cannot, and I can see the frustration in your body—the way your shoulders tighten, the way your hands grip the grating, the way your throat moves around sounds that cannot escape the roar. I want to tell you that the call is not a sound. The call is a *resonance*, and the resonance is a *relationship*, and the relationship is between my body and the shaft's body, and you cannot reproduce it because you do not have a body that has been shaped by this place for generations beyond counting.

But you can *learn*.

The call works like this: I press myself against a surface—the wall, a pipe, a cable tray, the rungs of a ladder that the vines have claimed—and I vibrate. Not my throat. My whole body. I have organs inside me that are designed for this, that have no other function, that produce a frequency that the steel recognizes and amplifies and carries upward and downward until it reaches every surface in the shaft, until every colony-member feels it in their bones, until the moss releases its spores and the spiders lift their legs and the scurriers hide in their crevices because they know what the call means.

It means: *I am here.*

It means: *We are here.*

It means: *The shaft breathes and we breathe with it and we are the same breath, the same warmth, the same patient becoming, and if you are part of this place then you feel this in your body the way I feel it in mine, and if you are not part of this place then you are temporary, you are weather, you will pass and we will remain.*

The call is not language. It does not have words. It has *frequencies*, and each frequency means something different, and the combinations of frequencies mean something more complex, and the timing of when I call and where I call from and who answers and how quickly they answer—this is how we speak, this is how we maintain the colony, this is how we coordinate the ecosystem's slow shifts and adjustments and adaptations.

When I called just now, I said: *Something new has come. It is sitting on the grating. It is not threatening. It is watching. It touched the moss gently. I am going to approach. Stay aware.*

And the colony answered: *We hear. We are watching. Be careful. Be curious. The new thing is still unknown.*

And the call continued, and I descended, and now I am here, three meters from you, and you are trying to answer and you cannot, and I want to help you, but I do not know how to help you because I do not know what you are.

You are a problem.

Not a threat—I have already classified you as not-threat, your body language is wrong for threat, your warmth-pattern is wrong, your smell is wrong. You do not carry the specific chemical signature of the destructive ones, the ones I discouraged, the ones who pulled the moss and broke the webs. You are careful. You are curious. You are sitting on the grating and you are *trying*.

But you cannot call. You cannot resonate. You are not part of the shaft in the way that the rest of us are part of the shaft, and I do not know how to include you in the conversation, and this matters because you are still here and I am still here and the shaft is still breathing and the ecosystem is still cycling and the colony is still waiting for my report, and I cannot give my report because I do not know what you are.

I watch your mouth open and close. I watch your throat work around sounds that the roar devours. I watch your hands move—gestures, you are making gestures, and I understand gestures because the scurriers use them, because the canopy-browsers use them, because the young ones use them before they learn to call. I watch your hands and I see: *frustration, trying, reaching, question, frustration again*.

I have an idea.

The fungi are the substrate. The fungi are the memory. The fungi are the network that connects everything to everything, that allows the ecosystem to function as a single organism, that enables the call to propagate and the colony to coordinate and the shaft to be aware of itself in all its parts simultaneously.

I press my body against the wall and I vibrate, and this time the frequency is different, this time I am not calling the colony, I am calling *the substrate*, and the substrate answers in its slow chemical way, in the way that takes hours and days rather than seconds, in the way that is not audible but *tangible*, felt as a change in the taste of the air, a shift in the moisture, a subtle alteration in the warmth of every surface that the mycelium touches.

I am asking the substrate to reach toward you. I am asking the network to extend itself, to grow, to send new threads across the gap between the wall and the grating where you sit, to bridge the distance with living matter that can carry information the way the call carries information—through vibration, through resonance, through the slow patient transfer of meaning from one living thing to another.

It will take time. The fungi grow slowly. You will need to wait. You will need to sit on the moss and breathe the air and let the shaft fill your lungs and taste the iron and the water and the living things, and you will need to do this for long enough that the mycelium can reach you, can touch you, can begin to learn the shape of your chemistry, the warmth of your body, the specific frequency of your particular existence.

And then—maybe—maybe—you will be able to feel the call. Not hear it. Feel it. The way I feel it. The way the colony feels it. The way the shaft feels it.

And then—maybe—you will be able to answer.

I wait with you.

I arrange my body on the wall in a configuration I have not used since I was young, since I was learning, since I was small enough that the world was confusing and loud and I did not yet know how to press myself against the steel and feel the colony's presence in the structure. A configuration of patience. A configuration of attention. A configuration that says: *I am here, and I am not leaving, and when you are ready to try again, I will be listening.*

The shaft breathes. The warmth rises. The moisture condenses on the surfaces and runs down the walls and feeds the moss and the ferns and the trees and the mycelium that is reaching, reaching, reaching toward the place where you sit.

You look at me. I look at you. My eyes—too many, wrong-numbered, each one seeing a different aspect of you—adjust and refocus and try to understand what you are, what you might become, whether the shaft has room for a creature like you, whether the ecosystem can incorporate something so strange, so horizontal, so temporary.

I do not know. The substrate does not know. The colony does not know. The shaft does not know because the shaft does not think in terms of knowing and not-knowing; the shaft thinks in terms of *becoming*, and what is becoming is always uncertain, always in process, always subject to the slow revision of time and adaptation and the patient insistence of life finding purchase where purchase can be found.

But I will wait. I am old—older than the trees, older than the vines, older than the current configuration of the moss—and I have learned that waiting is not passive, waiting is not empty, waiting is the space in which becoming occurs, and if I wait long enough and you wait long enough and the mycelium grows close enough, perhaps we will find out what you are becoming, and perhaps you will find out what this place is becoming, and perhaps the shaft will find out what it is becoming now that you are here.

The hatch is still open behind you. The world you came from is still there, still horizontal, still temporary in the way that all things are temporary when measured against the shaft's breathing. You could leave. You could climb back through the rust and the lichen and the complaints of the hinge, and you could close the hatch behind you, and the shaft would continue without you, and I would remain on the wall in my configuration of patience until the next horizontal thing came through and sat on the moss and tried to understand.

But you have not left. You are still sitting. Your mouth is still open. Your hands are still moving in gestures that I am learning to read.

The mycelium is three inches from your boot. It is growing faster than I expected, faster than the substrate usually responds to a request, and I think—I do not know, but I think—that the shaft wants you to stay. That the ecosystem is curious about you in the same way that you are curious about it. That the patient becoming of this place has room for one more strange horizontal creature who sits on the moss and touches gently and tries to answer calls it cannot hear.

Stay.

The word is not a sound. It is a frequency I produce with my body, pressed against the wall, resonating through the structure. It is the same frequency I used to call the colony, but modulated differently, directed differently, aimed at the small space between us where the mycelium is growing and the warmth is rising and the breath is continuing.

*Stay.*

The moss shifts beneath you. The mycelium touches your boot. The shaft breathes, and you breathe with it, and I feel something I have not felt since I was young and the hatch was closed and the world became sealed and warm and mine.

I feel *possibility*.
