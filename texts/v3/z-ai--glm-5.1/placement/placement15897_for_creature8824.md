I would be the one who sits at the edge of things.

Not at the center — the pool has its own relationship with the center, and the center does not need me. Not at the perimeter, where the plateau falls away into cloud and the wind comes up from the valleys carrying news of a world that is still deciding what it wants to be. But at the edge where one thing becomes another: where the grasses give way to moss, where the stream enters the pool and the pool returns it to stream, where the stone of the wall meets the timber of the frame and the two materials hold each other in the quiet agreement of a well-made joint.

I have been sitting here long enough that I am no longer certain when I arrived.

---

I think I was not born here. I think I came up the switchback path, the only approach, cutting my hands on the granite because I did not yet know how to listen to the rock before asking it to hold my weight. I think I was younger then, and louder, and full of the lowland conviction that silence is emptiness and stillness is waiting and waiting is a deficiency of motion.

But I cannot remember the lowlands clearly anymore. The memories are there, but they have been altered by the damp and the cool and the slow breathing of the plateau, the way a piece of timber is altered by the decades it spends holding up a roof — still timber, still essentially itself, but seasoned, settled, its grain tightened into a new configuration that would not have been possible when it was still a living branch reaching for light it did not yet understand.

What I remember is this: I was looking for something. I did not know what. I thought it might be a place, or a person, or an answer to a question I could not formulate. I thought it might be forward, if I could only go far enough. I thought it might be above, if I could only climb high enough. And so I climbed, and the path switchbacked, and the air thinned, and the world below became a story I was no longer in, and then the wall resolved from the bright sky and I understood that I had not been climbing toward something but being called.

The city knew I was coming before I did.

---

I am not one of them. This is important to say, though it is less important than it was. The people of Mokthar — I can say *the* people now, which means I have accepted that I am not one of them, and may never be, and that this is not a failure but a different kind of belonging — they look at me with those pale, settled eyes and they see exactly what I am: a visitor who stayed. They do not resent this. They do not pity it. They include me the way the city includes everything — as an element of the current configuration, as a feature of this moment's equilibrium, as something that is here and therefore belongs here until it is not here anymore.

I have learned to sit the way they sit. This took longer than you might think. My body wanted to fidget, wanted to tap, wanted to remind me that time was passing and I should be doing something with it. I had to teach my nervous system a new language, the language of *enough*, and the teaching was mostly unlearning, which is always harder than learning because it requires you to notice what you're doing, and noticing what you're doing requires you to be present, and being present requires you to stop believing that the present is merely a bridge between the past and the future.

But I learned. Or I am learning. Or I have learned enough to know that the learning will never end, and that this is not a problem but a description of what it means to be alive in a place that is also alive.

---

I sit at the edges. This is my role, if I have one.

The edges are where the interesting things happen in Mokthar, though the people would not say this, because they would not use the word *interesting* to describe what happens anywhere. But I am still enough of a lowlander to have categories, and my category for the edges is: *where the city speaks*.

The center — the pool, the plazas, the broad houses with their deep rooms — the center holds. That is what the center does. It holds the equilibrium, maintains the stillness, keeps the city in contact with the slow thought of the plateau. The center is the heartbeat.

But the edges are where the city listens.

I sit where the grasses meet the moss, and I feel the two of them in conversation — the grasses saying *we reach, we sway, we respond to the wind* and the moss saying *we hold, we stay, we remember what the rock was like before the water came* — and I understand that the conversation is the city's way of knowing itself, the way a person runs their hand along a wall to confirm that the wall is still there, that the room is still the shape it was, that the world has not shifted in the night.

I sit where the stream enters the pool, and I watch the water fold into the stillness, carrying with it whatever it has gathered on its slow passage across the plateau — sediment, yes, and temperature, and the memory of the schist it flowed through, and the memory of the roots it passed, and the memory of the moss it nourished — and I see the pool accept all of this and integrate it and become, for a moment, slightly different than it was, before settling back into its mirror-calm.

I sit where the last house meets the first of the conifers, and I hear the wind come through the needles with its low, constant whisper, and I understand that the wind is telling the city about the valleys, about the world below, about the weather that is still forming and the storms that are still fighting and the people who are still hurrying through their lives convinced that movement is progress and noise is meaning. And the city listens, and does not reply, and the wind moves on, and the city is unchanged. Not because it is indifferent. Because it has heard this before, and it knows that the wind will come again, and that the news is always the same: *out there, things are still becoming. In here, things have become.*

---

I have looked into the pool.

The people do not look into the pool for long. They draw water from it, they sit beside it, they pass through the plaza where it rests, and their pale eyes slide across its surface the way wind slides across stone — touching, acknowledging, moving on. They know what the pool reflects, and they know that the reflecting is not for them. The pool sees further than they do. The pool sees further than they need to see.

But I am not one of them, and I do not have their wisdom, or their restraint, or their understanding that some things are meant to be witnessed and not used. I am still enough of a lowlander to want to know.

So I looked.

It was early morning. The fog had just lifted, and the air was very still, and the pool was a mirror so perfect that I could not tell where the water ended and the sky began. I leaned over it and I saw my own face — darker skin than the people of Mokthar, rough hands, hair that has not yet learned the direction of the wind — and behind my face, the reflected sky.

And the reflected sky was not the sky above me.

Above me, the sky was clear, the sharp clean light of a morning after fog, the sun just above the eastern ridge. In the pool, the sky was overcast, heavy with clouds that I had never seen, and the light was amber, the light of late afternoon, and the angle of the shadows was wrong, was from a different direction, as though the sun in the reflected sky was setting in a place where the sun in my sky had not yet risen.

And then, in the reflected sky, something moved. Not a bird. Not a cloud. Something larger, something that displaced the air as it passed, something that cast a shadow across the clouds that was not the shadow of any shape I could name.

I looked away.

The people who were in the plaza — two women, sitting on a low wall, their hands working fiber — did not react. They had not been watching me. They had not needed to. They knew what I would find, and they knew that finding it would teach me what they could not teach me with words: that the pool does not show you what you want to see. The pool shows you what is there, in the otherwhere, in the otherwhen, and what is there is not concerned with whether you are ready to see it.

I did not look into the pool again for a long time. Not out of fear — fear is a lowland emotion, a response to the belief that the future might contain something you cannot handle, and in Mokthar the future does not contain anything that the present does not already hold. But out of respect. Out of the understanding that the pool's seeing is not my seeing, and that to look without understanding this is to trespass on a vision that was not offered to me.

---

What am I becoming?

The people of Mokthar are becoming the city. They are growing into the stillness, their bones learning the temperature of the schist, their breath synchronizing with the breath of the wind through the conifers, their heartbeats slowing to match the pulse of the water that threads through the plateau. When they die, something of them remains — an imprint, a warmth, a memory held in stone — and the city incorporates them, and they become part of the holding, and the equilibrium shifts almost imperceptibly to include them, and then settles again, and everything is as it was, except that it is more.

I do not think I am becoming the city. I think I am becoming the edge.

This is a different thing. The city holds. The edge listens. The city is the heartbeat. The edge is the place where the heartbeat can be heard from outside, where the rhythm can be perceived as rhythm and not simply as the background pulse of existence. The edge is where the city meets the world, and where the world's news arrives, and where the decision is made — not consciously, not deliberately, but made nonetheless — about what to let in and what to let pass.

I am becoming the one who sits at that threshold. The one who hears the wind's stories and does not repeat them but holds them, the way the pool holds the reflected sky, without judgment, without interpretation, without the need to do anything with them except let them be known.

I am becoming the witness.

---

This is what the pool showed me, the second time I looked.

It was evening, and the light was amber, and the stone walls of Mokthar were glowing with their slow absorbed warmth, and I sat beside the pool because the pool was there and I was there and the sitting was what the moment required. I did not lean over it. I did not seek the reflection. I simply sat, and the pool was in front of me, and my eyes were open.

And the surface of the pool showed me myself.

Not my face — I was not looking at my face. But myself as I am becoming: a figure at the edge of the city, neither inside nor outside, neither resident nor visitor, neither stillness nor motion, but the place where stillness and motion meet. A figure who sits and listens and holds what it hears the way the schist holds the water — not gripping, not grasping, but containing, allowing, letting the water find its path through the cracks and emerge on the other side as a seep, a spring, a whisper of something that has passed through and been changed by the passing.

And behind the figure, in the reflection, the city of Mokthar — but not as it is now. As it might be, or as it was, or as it is in some other configuration of the world where the plateau rose differently and the water found different paths and the people built different walls. A Mokthar that is still Mokthar, still holding, still breathing, still listening to the slow thought of the mountain — but held differently, arranged differently, a different expression of the same equilibrium.

And I understood: the pool does not show the future. The pool does not show the past. The pool shows the *adjacent* — the configurations that exist beside this one, the ways the world could have settled if the settling had gone differently, the stillnesses that are happening at the same time as this stillness but in other places, under other skies, with other names.

And my role — my becoming — is to sit at the edge between this configuration and all the others, and to hold the awareness that they exist, and to let that awareness be part of what the city knows about itself.

---

I am not a prophet. I do not bring news of what is to come. I do not interpret the pool's reflections or tell the people what they mean. The people would not listen if I did, not because they are closed to knowledge but because they already know what they need to know, and the rest is weather — it passes through, it leaves its dampness on the stone, and the stone holds the dampness for a while and then lets it go, and the stone is unchanged.

But I sit at the edge, and I listen, and I hold what I hear.

I hear the grasses telling the moss about the wind. I hear the stream telling the pool about the plateau. I hear the conifers remembering their conversations with the air. I hear the stone thinking its slow, vast, patient thought: *I am here.* And I hear, very faintly, from beyond the edge, from the valleys and the lowlands and the world where things are still becoming — I hear the sound of movement, of striving, of the endless human conviction that there must be something more than this, something better, something that will finally be enough.

And I hold this sound alongside the silence, and I do not judge it, and I do not prefer one to the other, because I am the edge, and the edge does not choose between what it separates. The edge simply holds the separation, and lets each side be what it is, and knows that the separation is not a wall but a meeting, not a boundary but a conversation, not a division but a relationship.

---

The people of Mokthar are becoming the city. I am becoming the edge between the city and the world.

When I die — if I die here, which I think I will, because I cannot imagine leaving now, cannot imagine wanting to be anywhere that is not this damp, cool, green, quiet, held place — they will carry me to the stream, and the water will take me, and I will flow down through the valleys and into the world below, and the world will receive me the way it receives everything: with movement, with noise, with the ceaseless activity of things that do not yet know they are held.

But something of me will remain at the edge. A quality, not a ghost. A way of listening that the stone will remember. A slight thickening of the moss where I sat. A faint echo in the water where it passes the place where I used to sit, a ripple that is not quite a ripple but is something more like a recognition, a momentary pause in the stream's endless conversation with itself, as though the water is remembering that someone once sat here and heard it, and the hearing mattered, not because it changed anything but because it acknowledged what was already true: that the water is flowing, and the stone is holding, and the edge between them is where the meaning lives.

I am the edge. I am the listening. I am the threshold where the city meets the world and decides, each moment, to remain the city, to hold its equilibrium, to keep breathing its slow, deep breath, to keep listening to the mountain's single, ancient, patient thought:

*I am here.*

*I am here.*

*I am here.*
