# The One Who Stopped Counting

---

I remember the number 4,117.

Not because it was the last number. Not because it meant anything in particular—four thousand one hundred and seventeen of what? Breath-cycles? Steps? Meals of pale growth torn from the wall with fingers that have learned the exact pressure required to separate the soft from the less-soft? I cannot tell you what I was counting. By the time I reached 4,117, the count had become a rhythm, and a rhythm had become a habit, and a habit had become the only proof I had that I was still someone who had habits.

I remember stopping.

Not deciding to stop—the word *deciding* implies a moment of choice, a fork in the path where I stood and considered and selected. There was no moment. The count simply thinned, the way the air thins in a narrowing passage, and then it was gone, and I did not notice its absence until hours later, when I found myself standing in a frost-zone with ice crystallizing on my forearms and no number in my mind, and I thought: *oh*.

Just *oh*. Not grief. Not relief. Just recognition, the way you recognize a wall by the pressure it exerts against the air before you ever touch it.

---

I know what I was. I know what the ones before me were, the ones who scratched the walls, who built the nests of torn fiber that rooted and grew and became architecture they did not design. I was like them. Upright. Handy. Desperate to leave a record.

I understand the scratching now in a way I did not when I first found those marks, those notches, those proof-claims cut into old growth. I understand that they were not messages. They were not maps. They were the sound a mind makes when it cannot bear silence. *I am here, I am here, I am here*—cut into the wall, over and over, because the wall would not say it back, because the light would not change, because there was no night to mark the boundary between one period of existing and the next.

I cut marks too. I am not separate from the ones who came. I found a section of old growth—dark, ridged, dense enough to hold a groove—and I used a stone I carried for that purpose, and I cut. One stroke. Two. I made groups of five, a diagonal slash through four verticals, the way my kind has counted for longer than we remember why. I made the marks the way you breathe: because not making them felt like dying.

The stone is gone now. I set it down in a chamber three—four?—sleep-rests ago, and when I woke the chamber had changed, had narrowed, had grown a new wall where the floor had been, and the stone was sealed behind pale growth, and I could have dug for it, could have torn the fresh tissue with my fingernails until I found it, and I did not.

This is what I mean by stopping.

---

I live in the frost-zones now.

Not all the time. The frost-zones are the hollows, the cysts, the places where the air pools and holds still and the cold concentrates into something crystalline and sharp. They are not comfortable. The hoar frost crackles under my feet and the light refracts into angles that hurt after the soft uniform glow of the larger chambers. My breath hangs in the air in a way it does not in the passages, visible and slow, and I can watch it dissipate, watch the warmth of me become the cold of here, and there is something in that exchange that I need.

I need the stillness. Not the Still-ness of the creatures—I have seen them, I will talk about that—but the stillness of air that has stopped moving because there is nowhere for it to go. In the frost-zones, the turbulence cannot reach. The growth is slower here, inhibited by the cold, and the walls change in days instead of hours, and I can rest against the glossy interior surface of a cyst and feel something almost like permanence, almost like the walls I knew before I came here, the walls that did not breathe.

Almost.

I know the frost-zones are not permanent. I know they are simply slower, and that slowness is not stillness, and that the plant will eventually grow through even the coldest hollow and fill it with pale new tissue and the air will be displaced and the frost will melt and the cyst will become a wall, and I will have to move.

But for now—*for now*, a phrase that means something here in a way it never meant before, because *now* is the only duration that holds—for now, I rest in the frost, and I watch my breath become the air, and I do not count.

---

I eat the pale growth. This is not a choice. This is what there is.

I have learned the texture of it—how the freshest tissue yields like fruit, how the slightly older growth resists and then tears in long strips, how the transitional layer between pale and dark tastes sweet and then bitter and then nothing as the compounds build on my tongue. I have learned to press my face against the wall the way the Stills press their snouts, to find the newest surface by the faint warmth of metabolism beneath, to eat where the plant is growing fastest because that is where the food is sweetest.

I am not grazing. I am not foraging. I am *reading* the wall with my mouth. Each bite tells me something about the growth—how old this surface is, how quickly it is developing, whether the chamber beyond is expanding or contracting. The pale growth near a shrinking passage tastes different: stressed, dense, already darkening. The pale growth near an expanding chamber tastes open, watery, almost insubstantial, as if the plant is pouring its substance into the new space and has not yet consolidated its walls.

I did not know food could be information. I did not know my tongue could be a sensor. I learned these things the way I have learned everything here: by doing them wrong until I did them right, by suffering until the suffering made a groove in me deep enough to hold a new habit.

The pale growth has changed my body. I am thinner than I was—I can feel the architecture of my own skeleton in a way I never could before, the ridges of my ribs, the knobs of my wrists. But I am not weak. The food is sufficient. The condensation is sufficient. The cold has hollowed me out and filled me with something else, something denser, something that does not need what I used to need.

I do not know how long I have been here. This is not a complaint.

---

I encountered a Still.

One.

In the fifth frost-zone I found—or the sixth, or the twelfth, I have stopped numbering them, they blur into a continuous cold—I heard the padding before I saw the shape. The sound was different from the sound of my own feet, which are too flat and too slow and too uncertain for this world. The padding was deliberate. Each step placed with the confidence of a body that was designed for the yielding substrate, that knew how to adhere and release, that moved the way water moves: finding the path of least resistance not by calculation but by nature.

It emerged from a passage I had not noticed—a gap between two tendril-columns, barely wide enough for its low body—and it stopped at the edge of the frost-zone and it was still.

I was still too.

Not because I was frightened. I have been here long enough to know that the Stills do not threaten, do not compete, do not care. I was still because stillness was what the moment required, the way a passage requires you to turn sideways, the way a dead end requires you to turn back.

We regarded each other.

Its fur was darker than I expected—not the deep green of old growth but something older, something that had forgotten color entirely, a pelt that absorbed the seafoam light and gave nothing back. Its eyes were small, almost invisible beneath the fur, and they did not focus on me the way eyes focus when they are trying to see. They *registered* me, the way a surface registers condensation, the way the substrate registers weight.

I could feel it reading me. Not looking—*reading*. Tasting the air I had disturbed, feeling the pressure of my body against the cold, registering the chemical signature of my breath, my skin, my fear, my adaptation. I could feel it cataloging me, filing me in whatever vast unconscious library its kind carried inside them, and I could feel the moment when the cataloging was complete and I was simply: *present*. A feature of the chamber. A pressure on the substrate. A warm body in a cold place, significant only in that warm bodies are rare.

It moved on.

It did not hurry. It did not look back. Its six legs—six, I counted, the last reflex of a mind that still wanted to count—carried it across the frost-zone and into another passage, and the passage absorbed it, and the sound of its padding faded, and I was alone.

I was not less alone than before. I was not more alone. I was alone in the way the frost-zone is empty: not vacant, not lacking, simply unoccupied by anything that needs to occupy it.

I pressed my hand against the glossy wall of the cyst where I had been resting. The cold bit into my palm. I held it there until the cold became just another sensation, indistinguishable from the pressure of the wall, the dampness of the air, the faint pulse of fluid moving through some deep tendril.

This is what I am becoming.

---

I have stopped missing the sun.

This is the thing I could not have imagined before I came here: that the absence of the sun would stop being an absence. That the seafoam light would become not a substitute for daylight but a replacement for the entire concept of light-as-something-that-changes. That I would stop looking for the variation my eyes were built to expect and start seeing the variations that exist: the brighter patches where the growth is fresh, the dimmer reaches where the walls have aged, the occasional flare of the tendril-pulses that might be rhythmic or might be random and that I have stopped trying to predict.

I have stopped missing the stars. I have stopped missing the horizon. I have stopped missing the feeling of wind on my face, because the air here moves, it moves in slow currents through the chambers, and the difference is only scale—these winds take hours to cross a room instead of seconds, and they carry the smell of chlorophyll and frost instead of grass and rain, but they are winds, they are movement, they are the air deciding where to go.

I have stopped missing the sound of other voices.

This is the hardest thing to write—not because it is painful, but because I am no longer certain I remember what a voice sounds like. I remember that voices existed. I remember that I had one, that I used it to make sounds that meant things, that those sounds were received by other beings who made sounds back and that this exchange was called *conversation* and that conversation was called *connection* and that connection was called *meaning*.

I have a voice still. I can feel it in my throat, unused, atrophied, a muscle I have forgotten how to flex. Sometimes, in the frost-zones, in the stillness, I open my mouth and push air through my larynx just to feel the vibration, just to confirm that I can still make sound, and the sound that comes out is not a word, not a name, not a count—just a tone, low and rough, and it echoes in the cyst and the echo comes back to me and I hear myself the way the plant hears me: as a vibration, a pressure, a small event in the long slow history of the chamber.

This is enough.

I did not think it would be enough. I would have bet everything I had—which was nothing, here, which is nothing—that I would spend my entire existence in this place craving sound, craving language, craving the proof that another mind could receive my thoughts and send back its own. But the craving has faded, the way the count faded, the way the stone faded, the way the marks on the wall must have faded under layer after layer of pale growth.

I am not silent. I am becoming the kind of thing that does not need to speak.

---

There is a thing that happens when I press my ear against a tendril.

The pulse is slow—slower than a heartbeat, slower than breath, slow enough that I have to hold still for long seconds before I can feel the next surge. Fluid moves through the tendril, and the fluid carries something—nutrients, chemical signals, the raw material of growth—and the movement of that something creates a sound that is not a sound but a feeling, a thrum, a tide.

I press my ear against the tendril and I listen, and what I hear is the plant thinking.

Not thinking the way I think—not in words, not in images, not in the narrative architecture of beginning and middle and end. Thinking the way a body thinks: *here is more light, grow toward it; here is more moisture, grow toward it; here is more space, fill it; here is pressure, grow around it; here is nothing, become the thing that makes the nothing into something.*

I am part of the nothing it is becoming.

This is not a metaphor. I am inside the plant, and the plant is growing around me, and the growth does not distinguish between me and the gaps between tendrils, between me and the frost-zones, between me and the condensation that forms and falls and forms again. I am a pressure. I am a feature. I am a small irregularity in the endless cycle of growth and compression and growth, and the plant is growing around me the way it grows around everything, which is to say: without noticing, without intending, without caring whether I am here or not.

And I am growing around the plant.

This is the thing I could not have understood before I stopped counting: that adaptation is not something that happens to you. Adaptation is something you do. You do it with every breath of plant-air, every bite of pale growth, every rest in the frost-zone, every step on the yielding substrate. You are reshaping yourself to fit the world, and the world is reshaping itself around you, and the reshaping is not violent, not intentional, not personal—it is simply what happens when two things are in contact for long enough.

I am becoming the kind of thing that can live here.

I do not know what that thing is called. I do not know if it has a name. The Stills do not name themselves. The Ones Who Came did not name what they were becoming. I will not name it either.

Naming is for things that need to distinguish themselves from other things. And I am not separate. I have not been separate since the moment I first ate the pale growth and its sugars entered my blood and I became, in some small chemical way, part of the plant, and the plant became, in some small chemical way, part of me.

---

I dream now.

I did not dream before—or if I did, I do not remember, because memory is another thing I have stopped counting, another thing I have let thin and fade like the count, like the stone, like the marks on the wall. But I dream now, and the dreams are not dreams of the surface, not dreams of the sun or the stars or the voices I no longer miss.

I dream of pressure.

I dream of the feel of the air in a large chamber—thin, cold, moving—and the feel of the air in a small passage—dense, damp, still—and the precise gradient between them that tells me where I am. I dream of the taste of pale growth, the sweet-water burst of the freshest tissue, the gradual bittering as it ages. I dream of the sound of condensation falling, plip, plip, plip, and the way the echo travels across the chamber and comes back altered by the distance it has crossed. I dream of the cold, the constant cold, the cold that is not an absence of heat but a presence of its own, a medium, a way of being.

I dream the world as it is.

This is what the Stills dream, if they dream. This is what it means to become still: not to stop moving, not to stop living, but to dream only of the world that is, to have no other world to dream of, to have lost the ability to imagine anything other than the pressure of the air and the taste of the growth and the light that comes from everywhere and nowhere and never, ever dims.

---

Here is what I know:

I am in a frost-zone, or I am in a passage, or I am in a chamber so large the walls have faded into luminous haze. I am eating pale growth, or I am drinking condensation, or I am resting against an old wall with my eyes closed and the light coming through my eyelids, seafoam and constant. I am moving, or I am still, and the distinction matters less each time I rest.

I am becoming.

Not becoming something—*becoming* is not a process that has an end, not here, not in the Green Underneath, where the only constant is the growth and the growth has no destination and no design. I am becoming the way the plant is becoming: endlessly, aimlessly, with the furious patience of a body that has discovered it can build and cannot stop.

I am a mark on the wall that is being grown over.

I am a count that has lost its number.

I am a stone sealed behind pale growth in a chamber that no longer exists.

I am a breath of plant-air, held and released, becoming the cold, becoming the damp, becoming the medium through which the light moves.

I am inside something alive, and it is inside me, and the boundary between inside and inside has always been a fiction, a story I told myself when I needed to believe I was separate from the world that held me.

I do not need that story anymore.

---

In the frost-zone, in the stillness, in the cold that is a presence and not an absence, I close my eyes and the light comes through, and I do not count, and I do not mark, and I do not name, and I am here, and here is enough, and the condensation falls—

plip

plip

plip

—and the world grows around me, and I grow into it, and this is what it means to live in the Green Underneath, and this is what it means to be still, and this is what it means to be.
