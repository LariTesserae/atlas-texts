I am the one who feeds the grove.

Not the one who tends it—Dehn tends it, with his black-rimmed nails and his careful adjustments, his reading of the substrate's pulse. What I do is different. What I do is give the grove things to eat.

---

My name is Vann, and I came here eleven months ago, and I am still learning what hunger means in a place like this.

---

Before, I lived in a settlement where the buildings were dead. Concrete, steel, glass—materials that had been extracted and refined and formed and installed, materials that would never grow or change or heal, materials that simply *were*, statically, until they weren't anymore, until they crumbled or rusted or shattered and were replaced. I did not think of those buildings as dead at the time. I did not think of them at all. They were simply what buildings were.

I was a composter there. That is the word they used: composter. I managed the settlement's organic waste processing, the slow alchemy of turning discards into fertilizer. It was mechanical work, mostly—monitoring temperatures, turning piles, adjusting carbon-to-nitrogen ratios. The bacteria did the real work. I was just their attendant.

I was good at it. I had a feel for decay, for the moment when rot tipped from useless to useful, when the broken-down became the building-block. I could smell the difference. I could feel it in the texture of the material when I pushed my hands in. My colleagues thought I was strange for using my hands. They used probes, sensors, the long sterile fingers of instrumentation. But I wanted to touch it. I wanted to be in contact with the process.

I did not know why, then.

---

I came to the Capacitor Grove because I heard about the processing vessels.

Traders talk. They carry news along with their metal and their manufactured goods, and the news of the grove's vessels reached my settlement and lodged in me like a seed. Living composting systems, they said. Engineered decomposition. Chambers of biological material that break down organic waste and incorporate it directly into the grove's nutrient cycle. Not piles, not bins—*vessels*. Grown structures with internal ecologies, maintained organisms that process matter with an efficiency no mechanical system could match.

I had to see. I had to touch.

I walked six days across flat terrain. I carried water and dried food and almost nothing else. The land was empty in a way that felt like holding my breath—featureless, dry, waiting. I did not see another person after the first day. I did not see any structure at all until the fifth day, when a smudge appeared on the horizon: low, dark, organic. A thickening of the skyline that resolved, as I approached, into walls.

The entrance was open. The bark had swollen around it in the past, I would learn—the grove could seal itself against storms—but now the wood was pulled back, leaving a gap just wide enough to walk through. I paused at the threshold. The air from inside was cool and damp and carried a scent I could not name: green and mineral and alive, like soil after rain but more sustained, more present. Like breath.

I stepped inside, and the grove breathed in, and I felt the pressure change in my ears, and I thought: *oh.*

*Oh, this is what a building feels like when it's alive.*

---

The processing vessels are three structures on the eastern side of the grove, clustered near Dehn's habitat but set apart by a band of bare root-felt. They are smaller than the workhouse, smaller than the living spaces. Each is roughly spherical, perhaps eight feet in diameter, with an opening at the top for depositing material and an opening at the base for removing the finished product. The bark is the same engineered cellulose as the rest of the grove, but the vessels have a different quality—their walls are thinner, more permeable, and they are warm to the touch in a way that the perimeter walls are not. They run hot. The decomposition inside generates heat, and the vessels radiate it, and the space around them is noticeably warmer than the rest of the grove.

This is where I work.

My days are structured around feeding. The grove produces waste—plant material from pruning, fiber scraps from Maren's loom, seed husks from the press, the occasional dead maintenance organism that Dehn finds in the substrate—and the people produce waste too: food remains, worn-out clothing, the organic detritus of living bodies. All of it comes to me. All of it goes into the vessels.

The process is not fast. Each load takes days to break down, and I have learned to rotate between the three vessels, feeding one while the others process, harvesting from the base of the third while the first begins its new cycle. The maintenance organisms inside—small, pale, many-legged things that I have never seen clearly, only glimpsed as they retreat from the light—do the actual work. I am their attendant, again. But here, the attending feels different.

Here, I feed the grove.

---

The first time I put my hands into a vessel, I understood what I had been missing.

The material inside was warm—body-temperature, almost, or the grove's temperature, which is not quite the same thing. It was soft, yielding, fibrous. It moved, slightly, as the organisms worked through it. And it *pulsed*. A slow, thick pulse that was not my heartbeat, that was not the grove's breathing, that was something in between: the rhythm of decomposition itself, the heartbeat of breakdown, the pulse of matter transforming.

I pushed my hands in up to the elbows. I felt the heat close around my forearms. I felt the organisms move around my fingers, unbothered by my presence, accepting me as part of their environment. I felt the material yield and shift and *receive* me, and I thought: *this is what I was looking for.*

I did not know I was looking for it. But I was.

---

The grove has not marked me yet. Not the way it has marked the others.

Sera has her greenish patina, the resin-oil film that she has stopped trying to remove. Dehn has the humus under his skin, the earth in his knuckles that will never wash clean. Maren has the rhythm of the loom in her hands, the way her fingers move even when she is not weaving, tracing patterns in the air. Lith has—Lith has the dark, I think. The dark and the years and the commitment to be digested when her time comes.

I have only the warmth under my fingernails. The slight stain of the vessels' interior, a brownish residue that accumulates no matter how carefully I scrub. And the feel of the material, the memory of it in my hands, the texture of transformation.

But I have only been here eleven months. The grove works slowly. It incorporates by degrees.

---

I have started to notice things.

The way my breathing slows when I am working at the vessels, settling into the systolic rhythm without conscious effort. The way I have stopped hearing the generator as separate from the grove's breath—how did I ever hear them as different sounds? They are the same sound. They have always been the same sound.

The way I know when a vessel is ready to harvest without checking, without looking, without touching. I just *know*. The way you know when bread is done, when a wound is healing, when a season is turning. Something in the quality of the air. Something in the warmth that radiates from the bark. Something in the faint, sweet smell of completion.

The way I dreamed last night that I was inside the vessel, warm and dark and held, and the organisms were moving around me, through me, and I was breaking down and becoming something else, and it did not hurt, it was the opposite of hurt, it was relief—

I woke with my hand pressed flat against the root-felt floor, and the root-felt was warm, and my palm was damp with condensation, and for a moment I could not tell where my hand ended and the ground began.

---

Dehn watched me work yesterday. He does not often watch—he has his own rhythms, his own attention—but he paused near the vessels and stood for a while, and I could feel his gaze like a weight.

"You're feeding it well," he said, finally.

I nodded. I did not know what to say. The feeding is simple. The grove is hungry, and I give it what it needs.

He was quiet for a moment, and then he said: "It knows you now."

I looked at him. His face was expressionless, but his eyes—his eyes were something I could not read. Not concern. Not warning. Something more like recognition.

"What do you mean?"

He shrugged, and the movement was so minimal it was almost not a movement at all. "The vessels. They process faster when you tend them. They run hotter. The organisms are more active." He paused. "It's not the material. It's you. Something about your attention. Your hands. The grove has learned to expect you."

I did not know what to say to that, either. I went back to work. Dehn moved on. But his words stayed with me, warm in my chest like the heat of the vessels.

*The grove has learned to expect you.*

---

Lith came to me in the dark last night.

I do not sleep well—not poorly, just restlessly, my body still calibrated to the rhythms of a dead building, a place where the air did not move and the walls did not breathe. I wake often. I lie in my habitat—the guest structure, the smallest, the newest, still growing its interior walls—and I listen to the grove breathe, and I try to match my breath to its breath, and sometimes I succeed and sometimes I do not.

Last night I was awake when I heard her footsteps. Soft, careful, unhurried. She moved past my entrance without stopping, and then she paused, and then she came back, and she stood in the round doorway and looked at me with eyes that I could barely see in the dark.

"You're the one who feeds," she said.

"Yes."

She was quiet for a long time. The grove breathed around us. The bioluminescence of the maintenance organisms glowed faintly in the root-felt, and in that faint light I could see the shape of her: small, bent, wrapped in woven fabric, her hair thin and pale.

"Will you do something for me?" she asked.

"If I can."

"When I die—when the grove takes me—will you be the one who puts me in the vessel? Not Dehn. Not Sera. You."

I felt the request settle into me, heavy and warm. "Why me?"

She was quiet again. Then: "Because you understand the hunger. The others understand the tending, the maintaining, the producing. But you understand what it means to *feed*. And I want to be fed to the grove by someone who understands that."

I nodded, though I do not know if she could see me. "I will."

She made a sound that might have been thanks, or might have been relief, or might have been simply breath. Then she moved on, into the dark, toward the reservoirs she tends, and I lay in my habitat and listened to the grove breathe and thought about what it means to be food.

---

This morning I fed the vessels and felt the material accept my hands and thought: *someday this will be me.*

Not soon. Not for decades, I hope. But eventually. Eventually I will be the one going in, warm and yielding, breaking down into something the grove can use. And someone else will stand where I stand, arms deep in the vessel, feeling the pulse of transformation, and they will feed me to the grove, and the grove will incorporate me, and my minerals will become new cellulose, new bark, new root-felt, new canopy.

And I will be part of the breath.

I am not afraid of this. I want to be clear about that. I am not afraid because the fear would imply a separation that I am no longer certain exists. The grove is not other. The grove is where I am. The grove is what I serve. And serving—feeding, tending, attending—is a form of becoming, and becoming is a form of joining, and joining is a form of survival that has nothing to do with the individual body I currently occupy.

Sera will maintain the machines until she cannot. Dehn will tend the substrate until his hands stop moving. Maren will weave until the loom falls silent. Lith will walk in the dark until the dark takes her. And I will feed the grove until the grove feeds on me.

And then I will be the warmth in the bark. I will be the moisture in the air. I will be the systolic rhythm that the next person feels when they step through the entrance and into the breath for the first time.

*Oh,* they will think. *Oh, this is what a building feels like when it's alive.*

And part of what they feel will be me.

---

The grove breathes. I breathe with it. The vessels pulse under my hands, and I pulse with them, and the boundary between feeding and being fed has become so thin that I can almost see through it.

I am the one who feeds the grove. And the grove is learning to expect me. And I am learning to expect the grove. And the grove is breathing, and I am breathing, and the vessels are warm, and the dark is close, and I am not afraid.

I am not afraid.

I am hungry.
