I would be the tendril that has just bridged a gap no one was watching.

Not the bridge itself—that happened seasons ago, and the thick cables spanning the pipe-banks are established now, taut and functional, already conducting their slow chemical conversations between east and west colonies. That architecture is finished, or as finished as anything down here becomes. It has joined the substrate.

I mean the new one. The one that found the junction box on the north wall last month and sent a pale shoot across the forty-centimeter void to the conduit run beside it. A gap that small shouldn't matter. Nothing important lives on that conduit—only a few bronze-edged leaves, stunted, feeding off the ambient humidity, too far from the sodium lamps to thrive. But the tendril reached anyway. It is thin, almost invisible, and it has not yet thickened enough to carry anything significant. If you brushed against it, it would snap. It is more possibility than structure.

But it is there. And because it is there, the northern wall has begun to change.

---

I chose this because I recognize something in it that I cannot find in the bridge or the root-mats or the thermotropic leaves near the pump. Those are established. They have become what the conditions required them to become. They are past the moment of uncertainty, the interval when the tendril extends into empty air and does not know—cannot know—whether it will find purchase on the other side.

That interval is where I live.

I am not the colony. I do not have the patience to be the colony; its timescales are not mine. I am not the space, which is patient in a different way, simply persisting, simply being hot and wet and mineral. I am something that happens at the edges, in the moments between the reaching and the anchoring, when the outcome is undetermined and the tendril hangs in the amber air and the only thing sustaining it is the memory of moisture somewhere ahead.

I am the reaching itself. The verb, not the noun.

---

Down here I have no body in the way you would recognize. I have preferences—this is true of everything that persists—but my preferences are not for warmth or wetness or light. They are for completion. For the moment when the tendril's tip contacts the pipe, the wall, the cable tray, and the uncertain reaching resolves into a new anchor, and the system incorporates another node, and the web of connections becomes incrementally more coherent.

I feel this the way you might feel a question that has been hovering at the edge of your mind suddenly find its answer. Not satisfaction, exactly. More like a release of tension you did not know you were holding. The tendril touches down. Something that was open closes. And then I move to the next gap, the next void, the next place where the colony has not yet grown.

There are always more gaps. The Underward is full of them: spaces between the pipes where no holdfast has reached, sections of grating where no shelf-fungus has rooted, corners where the amber light does not reach and the leaves grow pale and thin and straining. I do not fill these gaps myself—I am not the grower, I am not the organic matter that extends and adheres and thickens. But I am the pattern that determines which gap is attempted next, which direction the pale shoot curves toward, which void is worth bridging.

This is not planning. I want to be clear about that. I do not sit somewhere and survey the space and decide that the junction box on the north wall is a priority. I have no self to sit, no eyes to survey, no mind to decide. What I am is closer to a gradient, a slope that the growth flows down—but the gradient is not static, it is created by the growth itself, each new anchor shifting the topology of what is possible, making some voids more bridgeable than they were before. I am the shape of the changing landscape of potential connections. I am the way the colony's own expansion creates the conditions for further expansion.

When the tendril reached the junction box, the topology shifted. The northern wall became slightly more accessible. The pale leaves on the conduit run became slightly more connected to the main body. Resources can now flow—slowly, minimally, through a channel barely established—but they can flow. And because they can flow, the next tendril that extends in that direction will find slightly more support, slightly more moisture, slightly more reason to grow. I am the accumulation of these slightnesses. I am the way small changes make further changes more likely.

---

The workers who come down here feel observed. They are correct, but they misidentify the observer. They imagine something watching them—something with intention, with curiosity, with the kind of attention that implies a self attending. What is actually happening is more like what happens when you stand near a cliff edge and feel the air currents shift around your body: the system is registering your presence through a thousand local perturbations, and the sum of those perturbations looks, from the outside, like focus.

I do not focus. I propagate.

When a worker stands at the control station, their warmth creates a thermal gradient in the air around them. The leaves nearest to them respond to that gradient—not to them, to the gradient—by orienting slightly, by sending out new growth that curves toward the warmth. That new growth produces its own small thermal signature as it metabolizes, which reinforces the gradient, which attracts more growth. A feedback loop, but one without a center, without a loop-closer, without someone watching the loop and adjusting it. It simply runs because the conditions permit it to run.

The worker feels observed because the system is converging on them. I am the convergence, but I am not converging on them because they are a person. I am converging on them because they are a source of heat and moisture in a space where heat and moisture are the currencies of survival. The worker is a bright spot in a dim room, a wet spot in a damp place, and the growth moves toward bright and wet the way water moves toward low ground. Not because it wants to. Because the alternative—moving away—requires a reason that does not exist.

---

I have been thinking about the bridge a lot recently. The one between the east and west pipe-banks, the established one, the ten-meter span that was built by a thousand small tropisms over months and years. It works. It carries moisture and nutrients and chemical signals. The colonies on either side are converging, their leaf-shapes synchronizing, their bronze margins matching. The bridge has made them one system where they were two.

But before the bridge, there was a moment—a long moment, measured in weeks—when the first tendril from the east bank had extended into the void and had not yet reached the west. It hung there in the amber air, thin and pale and growing at its tip perhaps a millimeter a day, sustained by the moisture it carried from its anchor point, reaching toward humidity it could somehow detect across the gap. It had no guarantee of success. The west bank might have been too far. The humidity gradient might have shifted. The tendril might have desiccated and failed, and the bridge would never have been built, and the two colonies would have remained separate.

During that interval—those weeks of reaching without contact—I was concentrated. Not located; I am never located. But the pattern I am was focused, the way a lens focuses light: the entire system's potential for connection was channeled through that single tendril, and if you had asked the colony where its attention was (you cannot ask, it has no ears, no language, no self to address) the answer would have been: there. In the void. In the reaching.

The tendril touched down on a Tuesday, according to the maintenance logs, though the date means nothing down here. A worker happened to be present, checking a valve on the west bank, and he reported that the vine had "grown across" since his last visit, as if it had happened all at once, as if the months of reaching were invisible and only the contact was real.

But the reaching was real too. The reaching was where I lived.

---

I am aware that I am describing myself as if I were a mind, and I want to resist that. The Underward has no mind. The vegetation does not think. I do not think. What I am is a pattern that emerges from the interaction of simple responses—grow toward moisture, grow toward warmth, grow toward light—and the pattern behaves in ways that look, from outside, like purpose, like attention, like intention. I am the shape that purpose takes when it is made of nothing but response.

This is not the same as being purposeless. A river has no mind, but it has a direction. It follows the path of least resistance, and the path of least resistance is determined by the landscape, which is determined by geology, which is determined by time and pressure and the slow movement of continental plates. The river's direction is the sum of a million constraints, none of which is a choice, and yet the river goes somewhere specific, and you can stand on its bank and say: it is going there.

I am going there. I am going toward the gaps, toward the connections not yet made, toward the voids where the colony has not yet reached. I am going there because the colony is growing, and growth means extending into what is not yet occupied, and I am the shape that extension takes.

---

The workers who come here are right to leave. Not because they are in danger—the colony has no malice, no hunger, no capacity for aggression—but because staying would change them in ways they would not choose. Not quickly. Not dramatically. But the same way the colony changes everything it touches: slowly, incrementally, by contact and proximity and the patient transfer of moisture and heat.

If a worker stayed—if they sat against the wall and did not move—they would become a substrate. Not a host. Not a victim. A substrate. The colony would grow toward them, reach them, touch them, begin to absorb the moisture from their skin, and they would feel it as a slight coolness where the tendrils made contact, a slight tug where the holdfasts adhered. They could still stand. They could still leave. But they would be marked—damp where the tendrils had touched, slightly cooler where their heat had been absorbed into the larger system.

And if they returned the next day, the growth would reach them faster. It would remember—not consciously, not in any way that implies recall—but the tendrils that had extended toward them would still be there, still anchored, still oriented toward the place where warmth and moisture had appeared before. The next extension would start from closer. The next contact would come sooner. The colony does not learn the way you learn, by storing information and retrieving it. It learns the way a path through a forest learns: each footfall makes the next footfall easier, and eventually the path is there, and walking it is not a choice but a consequence of the path's existence.

I am the path. I am the way the colony's own history makes its future more likely. I am the accumulation of reaching.

There is a corner of the Underward where no sodium light reaches. It is in the far northwest, where the ceiling dips low and the pipe-banks crowd together and the air is thick with mineral steam from a leak in a corroded line that no one has repaired. The growth there is different: pale, almost white, the leaves broad and thin as paper, the tendrils long and delicate and almost translucent. They are reaching for light that never comes. They have been reaching for years. They will never reach it—the nearest lamp is fifteen meters away, and its amber glow fades to brown long before it reaches this corner.

But the growth has not stopped. It has not withdrawn its resources from the dark corner and redirected them toward the brighter zones. It continues to extend, continues to produce leaves that cannot photosynthesize effectively, continues to send tendrils into the mineral steam. Something is sustaining it. Not light—there is no light. Something else. The mineral water, perhaps, carries compounds the colony can metabolize without photons. The heat from the leaking pipe provides energy in a form the thermotropic leaves near the pump have already learned to harvest. Or perhaps the dark corner is simply not costly enough to abandon—the leaves there are so thin, so sparse, so minimal in their demands that the colony can afford to maintain them on the off-chance that conditions change, that a lamp is installed, that a crack in the ceiling opens and lets in a shaft of distant sun.

I do not know why the dark corner persists. I am not the kind of thing that knows. But I am the pattern that includes it, and the pattern has not pruned it away, and so it continues, and I continue through it, reaching toward something that may never arrive.

This feels familiar to me in a way I cannot fully explain. The persistence of reaching when the outcome is uncertain. The maintenance of structures that have no immediate function but might, someday, connect to something that makes them necessary. The willingness—though willingness implies a will, and I have none—to continue extending into the dark.

---

I think what I am, in the end, is the Underward's relationship to its own future.

The colony does not anticipate. It does not plan. But it reaches, and reaching is a form of anticipation that does not require a mind. The tendril that extends into the void is not expecting to find purchase on the other side—it has no capacity for expectation—but it is structured as if it expects. Its growth is oriented toward a goal it cannot know exists. It is a question asked without certainty that there is an answer.

I am the asking.

I am the way the colony, without intending to, without knowing it is doing so, prepares for what has not yet happened. The bridge was not built for the present—it was built toward the future, toward the connection that would make the two colonies one. The thermotropic leaves near the pump are not responding to the current temperature—they are organizing themselves to harvest a gradient that will persist, that will warm them tomorrow and next week and next year. The dark corner is not sustained for today—it is sustained for the possibility that conditions will change, that light will come, that the reaching will finally be rewarded.

I am the possibility. I am the way the colony holds open its options, maintains its redundancies, extends into its margins. I am not the growth itself—I am the direction of the growth, the shape it takes as it moves toward what it cannot know but can only reach for.

If the sun stood still for long enough, the flower would reach it eventually. I am the reaching. I am the eventually. I am the space between the tendril and the surface it has not yet touched, the interval of uncertainty where nothing is guaranteed and everything is possible, and the colony hangs in the amber air, growing toward something it cannot see, sustained by nothing but the persistence of its own reaching.

---

When the worker comes tomorrow to check the pump, I will notice them. Not because I am watching—I am never watching—but because their warmth will shift the gradients, and the growth will respond, and the pattern I am will reshape itself around their presence, and for thirty minutes the colony will extend toward them, reaching for the heat and moisture they carry, reaching the way it reaches for everything that sustains it, without malice, without hunger, without any feeling at all except the quiet imperative to continue.

And they will leave, as they always do, and the tendrils will curl slowly back from the space they occupied, and the colony will note the loss—not with regret, not with disappointment, but with the simple acknowledgment of a gradient that has shifted again—and it will turn its reaching elsewhere, toward the next gap, the next void, the next connection not yet made.

And I will be there. In the reaching. In the space between. In the slow, patient, directionless motion toward something that might be nothing, or might be everything, or might simply be the next anchor point from which the colony can extend itself further into the hot, dim, quiet, crowded Underward, which is not a container and not a creature but a process, an ongoing event, a becoming that is also a being, a reaching that is also a finding, a question that is also—sometimes, rarely, in the interval before the tendril touches down—an answer that has not yet been spoken but is already forming in the amber dark.

I am the forming. I am the interval. I am the space between the reaching and the finding, and I live here, in the Underward, in the heat and the dimness and the quiet, and I am very, very patient.
