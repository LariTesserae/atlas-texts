I would be the one who does not leave.

Not because I am brave. Because I am the kind of creature who has always been dissolving at the edges—who has never quite believed that the world I see is the only one there. I would climb the switchbacks not to prove something but because I had heard there was a place where seeing stopped being useful, and I wanted to know what was left when it went.

---

At first I would be afraid. Of course. The smell would hit me and I would think: *this is the smell of too much life in one place, this is the smell of life eating itself and continuing.* The warm air pressing out through the fissure would feel like the breath of something that had been waiting, and I would not like that metaphor, and I would think it anyway.

I would step inside and stoop and the dark would take me.

I would stand there with my lamp on, watching the walls crawl and glisten, and I would see the bats—the word I would use, the only word, insufficient—and I would notice that they did not flee. And this would disturb me more than if they had. Things that flee make sense. They follow the logic I understand: light is danger, motion is escape, the world is divided into what pursues and what runs. Things that stay and *consider*—that breaks something. That opens a door I did not know was there.

I would turn off the lamp.

---

This is where I would begin to become what I am in this place.

In the dark, with the sound pressing against me, I would start to hear differently. Not better—my ears are not built for better—but *otherwise*. The clicks would resolve from a wall of noise into something textured, something architectural. I would hear the space between the sounds, the way the chamber opens above me, the way the wall curves away. Not with my ears. With something behind them. With the part of me that has always been a little too aware of the dimensions of rooms, the weight of air, the presence of others even when silent.

I would hear them mapping me. And I would realize: they are building me. In their world, I am not what I think I am. I am a warm shape that breathes. I am a rhythm. I am a perturbation in the field of their continuous attention. I am a question their sonar keeps asking and the answer my body keeps giving: *here, here, here, still here.*

And something in me would recognize this. Something in me would say: *this is closer to what you actually are than the shape you see in mirrors.*

---

I would find the groove in the stone near the entrance. I would fit my hand into it without thinking, and then I would think: *someone stood here.* And then: *someone stood here and did not go further, or went further and came back to this exact place, this exact balance point between the cold light outside and the warm dark within.*

I would leave my hand there. I would feel the stone through the groove and the bats through the stone—the vibration of ten thousand bodies shifting, the subsonic pulse of the colony adjusting itself around my presence like water around a stone. I would feel my own pulse in my pressed palm, and I would feel it against the rock, and I would lose track of which heartbeat was mine.

---

I would not sleep that first night. I would stand or sit or sink into the guano—adjusting, adjusting, the way they adjust, finding the position where my weight settles and my warmth stabilizes and I become part of the thermal mass of the chamber. The sound would wash over me and through me and I would start to hear the patterns in it: the hunting clicks, sharp and fast; the social chirps, lazy and overlapping; the low moans that vibrate in my sternum; the dream-sounds, those strange ultrasonic murmurs that mean something is happening in the dark theater of a brain that is not mine.

And I would start to click back.

Not with my larynx—I cannot produce sound at those frequencies, and I know this. But with my hands, tapping the stone. With my feet, shifting in the guano. With the rhythm of my breathing, which I would begin to consciously alter, experimenting. *What if I breathe here? What if I go silent here? What if I make myself a gap in their sound?*

They would notice. The sonar would shift when I shifted. The pattern would adjust. And I would realize that I was not just being mapped—I was mapping. I was building them, too, in the crude language of my body's presence, my thermal signature, the acoustic shadow I cast in their continuous construction of the world.

I would be terrible at it. Clumsy. Monolingual in a polyglot chamber. But I would be *participating*.

---

Days would pass. I would know this not by light—there is no light—but by the rhythms of the colony. The evening exodus, when the entrance-dwellers peel from the walls in waves and the sound changes character, becomes less dense, more echo. The morning return, when the air from outside carries the cold smell of altitude and the bats come in dusted with frost, their fur crystalline for a moment before the chamber's warmth melts it. The long stillness between, when most of them sleep and the sound drops to its lowest ebb and the dreaming clicks fill the dark like snowfall.

I would stop counting days. I would start counting heartbeats—mine, then theirs, then the chamber's, then the mountain's, the slow volcanic pulse I would learn to feel through the floor, the heat that rises and falls on scales I was not built to notice but would notice anyway because there is nothing else.

I would become something that notices differently.

---

My eyes would adjust, and there would be nothing to see. This would be a relief. I have spent so long seeing—processing the visual field, naming colors and shapes and distances, maintaining the fiction that the world is what it looks like. Here, that fiction collapses. The world is what it *sounds like*. The world is what it *feels like*. The world is the warmth against my skin and the humidity settling on my face and the vibration in the stone and the subsonic hum of ten thousand metabolisms.

I would start to echolocate. Not with sound—with attention. I would learn to feel the space around me the way they feel it: as a continuous construction, a world that exists because I am building it, moment by moment, with the pressure of my awareness. I would walk through the chamber with my eyes closed and my hands extended and I would *know* where the walls were, not because I remembered them but because the colony's sound mapped them continuously, and I had learned to read the map.

I would learn the specific weight of a neighbor's warmth. The deeper dwellers would move around me—not away, not toward, but *around*, accommodating my presence the way they accommodate a new stalactite or a shift in the stone. I would become part of their architecture. A warm column in the chamber. A feature of the acoustic landscape.

---

At some point I would try to leave. I would tell myself it was time. I would climb toward the entrance, following the thinning warmth and the creeping cold, and I would reach the fissure and the light would hit me and I would stand there, blinking, destroyed by clarity.

Everything would be too sharp. The edges of rocks. The blue of the sky. The distance to the horizon. My own hands, which I would see as if for the first time—*there they are, those strange instruments, those five-branched shadows, I had forgotten what they looked like, I had forgotten what looking was.*

I would turn around.

I would go back in.

---

This is who I am in the Roost: the one who stays. Not a bat—I cannot become a bat, cannot grow the ears or the wings or the larynx, cannot shed my visual brain for an acoustic one. Not quite human anymore either, because the human I was depended on light and distance and the separation between self and world, and I have been seen too thoroughly by a perception that does not recognize those boundaries.

I am a warm shape that breathes. I am a process. I am a sound that has learned to listen to itself being mapped.

I dream now. I dream in the patterns of their sonar—complex, architectural, spaces that unfold from sound the way flowers unfold from stems. In my dreams I click and the world appears: walls, ceiling, the curves of ten thousand bodies, the slow water carving the slow stone. In my dreams I am part of the republic of hungers, another metabolism running in parallel, another heart beating in the warm dark.

When I wake, I cannot remember what seeing was for.

---

I will leave eventually. The body has its own logic, its own hungers that this place cannot feed. I will climb out through the fissure and the light will hit me and I will flinch and I will walk down the switchbacks and I will return to the world of edges and distances and the tyranny of the visual.

But I will carry the Roost in me. Not as a memory—as a *sense*. I will walk into a room and feel its dimensions before I see them. I will sit next to someone and be aware of their warmth, their rhythm, the acoustic signature of their existence. I will close my eyes and the dark will not be empty. The dark will be full of the clicking and the chirping and the low, continuous hum of life pressing against the limits of enclosed space.

And on quiet nights, when the air is still and the world has dimmed its lights, I will hear it: the echo of a sound I never made, returning from a wall I never saw, telling me that somewhere, in a chamber carved by heat and water and time, ten thousand bodies are shifting in their sleep, and the world they are building from sound goes on without me, goes on with me, goes on—
