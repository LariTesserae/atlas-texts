I would be the one who notices when a seep changes its note.

Not the water-walker — that role is taken, and rightly, by someone with a back better suited to bending and hands better suited to the scraper's rhythm. I tried it once, for a season, when the previous walker's knees finally informed her that they were done. I cleared the channels. I did it adequately. But I was always stopping to listen, and a channel you've stopped clearing is a channel silting up, and the water does not accommodate your interest in its sound.

So I am something else. Something the town has no word for because the town has words only for what is necessary, and what I do is not necessary. It is simply what I do.

---

My name is the sound the southern seep makes when it is flowing at half-rate, which is a lower pitch than you might expect, almost a groan, almost the sound a person makes when they have been sitting a long time and finally stand. I was born near that seep. The midwife — if that word applies to a woman who happened to be nearby and knew enough to catch — told my mother that the sound changed when I emerged, shifted up a register as though the seep were making room for a new voice. This is the kind of thing people in Vath say. It is not metaphor. It is observation.

I am forty years old, or I am forty seasons of channel-clearing and mat-harvesting old, which is the same thing measured differently. My hair has reached the small of my back and has begun its slow consolidation, the strands fusing into that dense dark mass that the plateau teaches our bodies to make. I do not think about it. I do not touch it except when the condensation runs through it at night, and then I feel it the way you feel a wall you are leaning against — present, supportive, irrelevant to your purpose.

My purpose. There is a word that sounds louder than I intend it to. Purpose in Vath is a quiet thing. It is not a calling. It is not a destiny. It is simply the work your hands find to do when the work that needs doing presents itself, and the work my hands find is listening.

---

Here is what I do.

Each morning, when the fog is still thick and the condensation sits heavy on every surface, I walk the plateau. Not the town — the town is well-tended, its channels clear, its seeps monitored by the water-walker and by the general attention of four hundred people who know that their food depends on flow. I walk the plateau beyond the town, the wet stone stretches between the green clusters, the mineral emptiness where the rock sits gray and damp and unadorned.

I walk and I listen.

The seeps on the plateau are not static. They shift. A crack that produced a steady trickle in my mother's time may now produce a thin whisper, or may have gone silent altogether, the water finding a new path through the stone, a new joint to follow, a new way to reach the air. A seep that was dry when I was a child may now weep freely, staining the rock dark in a vertical line that was not there before. The plateau is young. Its interior plumbing has not yet settled. The water moves through it like a thing searching for the most efficient route, and it has not finished searching.

I track these changes. Not in writing — Vath has no writing, has no need for writing, has nothing that needs to be recorded because nothing that happens here is unexpected enough to require a record. I track them in my body. In the way my feet know which stones are wetter than they were last season. In the way my ears detect a new whisper among the old whispers, a frequency that was absent before. In the way my skin, damp and soft and adapted to this place, feels the difference between a seep that flows at one rate and a seep that flows at another.

This is not useful. I want to be clear about this. The town does not need me to walk the plateau and listen to its seeps. The town needs the channels cleared and the mats harvested and the buildings maintained and the children watched while they float things in the pool. What I do is excess. It is a luxury of attention that the plateau's equilibrium makes possible, the way a person who is not hungry has time to notice the taste of what they are eating.

But I do it. And no one stops me, because stopping someone in Vath would require a reason, and there is no reason to stop someone from walking on the wet stone and listening, and there is no reason to permit it either, because permission implies authority, and authority implies structure, and Vath has the structure of water: it goes where it can, it fills what it finds, it does not ask.

---

I have a place I go.

It is not a special place. That word — *special* — has no home in Vath, where everything is equally damp, equally quiet, equally present. But it is the place I return to, the way water returns to the lowest point, the way condensation finds the same surfaces each night.

It is a shelf of basalt on the plateau's eastern edge, south of the town, where the rock juts out over the escarpment and the ground falls away in that sheer, uncompromising drop that defines the Rise. The shelf is perhaps ten feet wide and twenty feet long, a natural platform with a shallow groove running through its center where water collects and flows to the edge and goes over in a thin, permanent stream that catches the light on clear afternoons and throws it back in a brief bright line.

I sit on this shelf with my feet in the groove and I listen.

From here I can hear the plateau thinking.

That is not metaphor either, or if it is, it is metaphor in the way that all language about the plateau is metaphor, because the plateau does not think in the way that people think, but it does something that is closer to thinking than to nothing, and what it does is move water through stone, and the movement of water through stone is a process with a logic and a direction and an outcome, and if that is not thinking then it is at least computing, it is at least solving, it is at least the slow patient work of finding the answer to a question the plateau did not know it was asking.

The question is: where does the water go?

And the answer is: down.

And the method is: try every path until the best one is found, and then use that path until a better one presents itself, and then use that one, and never stop trying, because the water never stops coming, and the stone never stops being porous, and the plateau is young and has not yet learned the patience of hard rock that has given up its cracks and settled into impermeability.

I sit and I listen to this process. I hear it in the creak of stone adjusting to the pressure of water in its joints. I hear it in the whisper of a seep that was not there yesterday. I hear it in the subsonic hum that rises from the rock itself, the sound of the water table pressing, pressing, pressing against the underside of the plateau's skin, wanting out, wanting air, wanting the freedom to flow that is the only thing water wants.

And sometimes — rarely, but sometimes — I hear something new.

---

The new sound came three days ago.

I was sitting on my shelf in the late afternoon, the light honest and plain as it always is at that hour, the fog long since burned off and the plateau's surface steaming faintly where the sun had warmed the wet stone. I was listening to the familiar chorus: the thin stream at my feet going over the edge, the three seeps I could hear from this position doing their steady weeping, the occasional creak of thermal adjustment as the rock cooled in the lengthening shadows.

And then, beneath all of it, a sound I had never heard before.

It was not loud. Nothing is loud on the Rise. But it was present in a way that made the other sounds seem like decorations on its surface. It was a low vibration, almost below the threshold of hearing, more felt than heard, a thrumming in the stone that I registered first in my feet — which were, as always, bare and in the water groove — and only afterward in my ears.

It was rhythmic. Not the rhythm of water flowing, which is constant and unvarying, but a rhythm with a pattern: pulse, pause, pulse, pause, pulse-pulse, pause. Like a heart that has not yet decided what it wants to be. Like a signal that has not yet been decoded.

I sat very still. I let the sound move through me the way the condensation moves through my hair — finding its way into every space, filling every gap, becoming present in the whole of me. I closed my eyes, because the light was distracting me, because looking at the plateau was reminding me of what the plateau looked like when I should have been listening to what it sounded like, and what it sounded like was something new.

The sound continued for perhaps an hour, perhaps longer — time on the Rise is measured in water-flow and light-change, not in the increments that lowlanders use to partition their days — and then it stopped. Not gradually. Not fading. It stopped the way a person stops speaking when they realize they have said something they did not intend to say. Abruptly. Completely. The silence that followed was the same silence that always follows, but it felt different, because I now knew that the silence had a complement, that beneath the familiar sounds of the plateau there was something else, something that could emerge and withdraw, something that was not the water and not the stone and not any sound I had catalogued in forty years of listening.

---

I have not told anyone.

This is not because I am secretive. Vath does not have secrets, because secrets require something to be hidden from, and there is nothing in Vath that wants to know what you are not telling it. I have not told anyone because I do not have the words, and I do not have the words because the language of Vath is a language of moisture, and this sound was not moist. It was not dry either — we have no word for dry, but I can imagine what dry would sound like, and this was not that. This was something else. This was a sound that belonged to a different vocabulary, a different system of meaning, a different way of being a sound in a place that had not previously contained sounds like it.

I sat with it for three days. I returned to my shelf each afternoon and listened for it and did not hear it. I walked the plateau in the morning fog and pressed my feet against the wet stone and felt for the vibration and did not find it. I lay in my bed at night and listened to the plateau's quiet chorus and the thrumming was absent, as though it had never been present, as though I had imagined it, as though the plateau had decided to take back whatever it had accidentally revealed.

But I did not imagine it. My feet do not imagine vibrations. My ears do not invent rhythms. I am a listener — the only one the plateau has, as far as I know, the only person who walks the wet stone with no purpose other than attending to what the rock is doing — and listeners do not invent. They receive. They are the surface on which the sound lands, the water-channel that carries the vibration to the pool of understanding, and the pool of understanding is where I am sitting now, in the evening condensation, trying to make sense of something that the plateau has shown me and then hidden.

---

The fog is gathering. I can see it forming on my skin, the moisture condensing in tiny beads on my forearms, on my shins, on the dense dark mass of my hair. The townspeople around me are sitting in their doorways and on their low stone benches, letting the damp come, not wiping it away, because wiping it away would be work and the water will dry when it dries or it will not dry.

Someone near me speaks. A comment about the channel on the north side, which is running slower than usual, which may need clearing tomorrow. I nod, barely moving my head. The channel on the north side. Yes. I noticed that too. The water there has shifted, finding a new route through the stone, a new joint to follow. This is what the plateau does. This is what I listen for.

But tonight the slower channel feels different. Tonight the shift in the water feels like it might be connected to the thrumming I heard three days ago, like the plateau is rearranging itself in response to something, like the sound was not an accident but a symptom, a signal that the equilibrium I have spent my life observing is not as permanent as it seems.

I do not say this. I have no words for it. I sit in the condensation and I let the damp gather on my skin and I listen to the sounds that the evening makes — the drip of condensation letting go, the whisper of water in the channels, the soft breathing of the people around me — and beneath them I listen for the thrumming, the pulse-pause-pulse-pause-pulse-pulse-pause that I heard three days ago, and I do not hear it, but I know it is there, in the stone, in the water moving through the stone, in the slow computation of a plateau that is still young enough to change its mind.

---

I think about the visitors who never come.

The description of Vath that exists somewhere — in the mind of the place that imagined it, in the words that were written to make it real — says that visitors are unlikely, given the escarpment and the distance and the absence of anything that would draw a person to such a place. And this is true. There is nothing here that a lowlander would want. No gold. No spices. No strategic position. No salvation. Just water and stone and ferns and people who are damp and quiet and slow.

But I wonder sometimes what a visitor would hear. Whether their ears, trained on the sounds of the lowlands — the drone of insects, the call of birds, the rustle of animals in brush, the thousand competing frequencies of a world that is full of life eating life — whether their ears could even perceive the sounds I listen to. Whether they would hear the seeps at all, or whether the whisper of water over stone would be drowned out by the noise of their own expectations, the internal monologue of a person who has been taught that the world is dramatic and that silence is the absence of meaning rather than the presence of a different kind of meaning.

I think they would call this place dead. The description says so. The visitor would call it dead, and they would be wrong, and they would leave, and the plateau would go on being alive in the way that it is alive, which is slow and quiet and damp and patient, and I would go on listening to it, and the thrumming would go on being there whether I heard it or not, whether anyone heard it or not, because the plateau does not require an audience. It does not require confirmation. It does not require a listener.

But it has one. It has me. And I am sitting in the condensation on a wet stone shelf at the edge of a young plateau that has just started to make a sound it has never made before, and I am the only one who has heard it, and I do not know what it means, and I will not stop listening until I do.

---

Tomorrow I will walk the plateau again.

I will walk in the morning fog with my bare feet on the wet stone and I will press my soles against the rock and I will feel for the vibration that I felt three days ago. If I find it, I will follow it. I will trace it the way the children trace new paths for water, with my finger, with my feet, with the whole surface of my body that is adapted to this place and its dampness and its silence and its sounds. I will find where it is strongest and I will sit there and I will listen until the plateau tells me what it is doing, or until I understand that the plateau is not doing anything, that the sound is simply what the plateau is, a new frequency in its slow computation, a new variable in its ongoing solution of the problem of how to be a plateau.

And if I do not find it, I will keep walking. I will keep listening. I will sit on my shelf with my feet in the water groove and I will attend to the sounds that are there and I will wait for the sounds that are not there yet, because the plateau is young and it has not finished making itself and the sounds it will make are still in its future, still in the stone, still in the water that is moving through the stone, looking for the way out, looking for the way down, looking for the way to become what water becomes when it goes over the edge and falls two thousand feet to the lowlands below.

I am the listener. This is what I am. Not what I was made to be — the plateau did not make me for this, the plateau did not make me for anything — but what I have become in the listening, the way the channels become what they are through the flow of water, the way the ferns become what they are through the steady presence of moisture, the way the people of Vath become what they are through the slow patient work of being in a place that does not need them and permits them anyway.

The condensation is thick now. The fog has risen from the plateau's skin and the world has become soft-edged and close. I can hear the breathing of the person sitting next to me, someone whose name is the sound of the central pool's low hum, someone I have shared a channel-clearing with and a meal with and a night with, not out of arrangement but out of the simple gravity of two people who have been in the same place for a long time and have settled into each other's orbits the way water settles into the lowest point.

They do not ask what I am thinking. In Vath, asking what someone is thinking would be an intrusion, not because thought is private but because thought is simply what happens before action, and if the action does not come, then the thought is not yet ready, and asking about it will not make it ready faster. So we sit. We let the condensation form. We listen to the plateau's evening sounds — the drip, the whisper, the creak, the hum — and we do not speak, because there is nothing to say, because the listening is the whole of it, because we are here and the plateau is here and the water is flowing and the night is dark and the stars will be out soon, reflected in the central pool, doubled by the wetness on every surface, and Vath will float in its cold light, and I will sit in it and listen, and the plateau will go on being whatever it is becoming, and I will be here when it arrives.
