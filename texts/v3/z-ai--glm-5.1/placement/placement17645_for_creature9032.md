I am the one who reads the walls.

Not the lichen—I cannot claim that, though the girl sometimes watches me run my fingers along the pale colonies and says I am learning their language, which is not true; I am learning something else, something the lichen is also learning, which is the language of the stone beneath it, which is the language of the water moving through the stone, which is—eventually, if you follow it far enough—the language of the thing below, speaking upward through mineral and stratum and the patient logic of pressure.

I came down because I could hear it.

Not the hum—everyone hears the hum, eventually, if they live in the city long enough and pay attention to the wrong frequencies. Not the drip, which is audible only in the gallery itself. What I heard was the weeping. The sound of water forcing itself through rock. I heard it in basements, in parking garages, in the concrete foundations of buildings where I worked assessing structural integrity. I am—I was—a hydrologist. I studied the movement of water through porous media for a civil engineering firm. I spent my days modeling seepage, predicting infiltration, calculating the pressure that groundwater exerts against foundation walls. I was good at it. I understood water's patience, its willingness to find the smallest crack and widen it over geological time. I understood that water does not erode through force but through *insistence*.

I heard the gallery before I found it. In the sub-basement of a municipal building on Harker Street, my moisture meter registered an anomaly—a steady exudation from the concrete that had no apparent source, no corresponding water table, no explanation in any of my models. I pressed my palm to the wall. It was damp, as walls in sub-basements often are, but the dampness had a *rhythm*. Not a drip—a seep, slow and constant, but modulated, as if something were breathing through the concrete. As if the wall were a membrane and something on the other side were exhaling.

I found the service tunnel three weeks later. I found the gallery forty minutes after that.

---

I did not stop at the entrance the way Martin did. I did not sit and listen for three days. I walked—quickly, too quickly for the gallery's rhythm, my surface-world haste still governing my legs—straight to the nearest wall and pressed both palms flat against the stone.

It was warm. The walls are warmer than they should be, given the temperature of the water and the depth and the absence of any heat source. Not warm the way a living body is warm, but warm the way a pipe is warm when current runs through it—incidental heat, waste heat, the thermal signature of something happening inside. I pressed my cheek to the stone. I closed my eyes. And I heard it—not with my ears, but with my bones, with the fluid in my inner ear, with the water in my own tissues responding to the vibration in the stone:

*Layers.*

The gallery is not one chamber. It is a series of chambers, or it is one chamber with depth, or it is a membrane stretched across something that extends downward in ways I do not have the geometry to describe. The water moves through it all. The weeping I can see on the walls is only the final exhalation of a circulation that goes much deeper, that draws from somewhere I have not yet located and releases into the gallery in a slow, constant, mineral-laden breath.

I began mapping the seepage that day. I have not stopped.

---

My territory is the walls. All of them—Sera's apse, Martin's perimeter, the stretches of bare stone between the iron pillars where the lichen grows thickest and the condensation traces its erratic paths. I walk the gallery differently than Martin does; he walks the structure, the skeleton of pillars and grating, while I walk the skin. I trail my fingers along the stone as I move, reading the moisture like braille. I taste the water at different heights and locations, cataloguing the mineral variations in a way that makes the number-woman stare at me with something like recognition—she counts drops; I catalog their composition; we are engaged in the same project from different angles, and sometimes we sit together in silence at the edge of a puddle, she tallying and me sampling, and the crustaceans move around us as if we are part of the architecture.

The water tells stories. Not narratives—nothing so organized—but *histories*, geological and chemical, layers of information deposited in the minerals it carries. Near the junction boxes, the water tastes of copper and ozone and something almost metallic that I cannot name, a flavor that intensifies when the hum grows louder and fades when it diminishes. Near the floor, where the worms pulse in the standing water, the mineral signature shifts: more iron, more calcium, and something else—something organic, something that might be the worms themselves, or might be what the worms are feeding on, or might be the chemical trace of whatever the thing below exudes into the water table.

The thing below. Yes. I have not avoided thinking about it the way the others have. Not because I am braver or more curious, but because avoiding it would be professionally irresponsible. I am still, in some attenuated sense, a hydrologist. And the water is moving *from* it, *through* it, *because of* it. To ignore that would be to fail at the one task the gallery has given me.

---

I have identified seven distinct seepage zones. Each one produces water with a slightly different mineral profile. Each one weeps at a slightly different rate. And each one—this is the part I have not yet told the others, because I do not know how to say it in a way that sounds like anything other than madness—each one weeps in a direction.

Not downward. Not with gravity. *Inward.*

The water on the walls does not simply run down toward the floor. It moves—imperceptibly, unless you watch a single droplet for the hours it takes to traverse a meter of stone—toward the center of the gallery. Toward the point where the floor channels converge, where the crustaceans cluster thickest, where the hum is loudest and the worms pulse in their slow synchronization. The water is being *drawn*. Not by gravity, not by capillary action, not by any mechanism I was trained to recognize. By something else. By the same patient gravity that holds the residents here, that makes leaving feel like swimming upstream, that turns return into relief.

I have tasted the water at the convergence point. It is different there. Warmer. Thicker. The mineral concentration is higher than anywhere else, and there are compounds I cannot identify—nothing toxic, I am still alive, still functioning, still *persisting*—but complex, organized, structured in ways that suggest not geology but *biology*. Or something adjacent to biology. Something that uses water the way blood uses plasma: as a medium, a carrier, a language for transporting information from one part of an organism to another.

I think the gallery is a circulatory system. I think the walls are capillaries. I think the weeping is not exudation but *exchange*—the thing below breathing out through the stone, the gallery breathing in through the water, the residents who drink and taste and are slowly saturated becoming part of the exchange, becoming not inhabitants but *tissue*, cells in a body too large to comprehend from inside.

I think this, and then I return to my circuit of the walls, and I run my fingers along the stone, and I do not say any of it aloud, because saying it would make it a theory and a theory demands testing and testing demands action and action would be a violation of the patience that makes the gallery what it is.

---

The girl watches me sometimes. She sits on her walkway above and swings her feet and watches me press my palms to the stone, and her moth-lit eyes are thoughtful, and she says things like:

"You're listening to it breathe."

And I say, "Yes."

And she says, "Sera listens to it think. Martin listens to it speak. What do you hear?"

And I say, "Its blood."

And she nods, as if this confirms something she already knew, and she returns to her tending, and the moths glow and dim around her like a heartbeat, and I am left with my hands on the wet stone and the taste of minerals on my tongue and the slow, constant, patient weeping that will never stop because it is not grief, it is respiration, it is the rhythm of something so large and so old that it does not need to wake because it has never been asleep, not really, not in any way we would recognize—it has simply been *persisting*, the way the gallery persists, the way we persist, the way water persists in finding the smallest crack and widening it, not through force but through insistence, through the patient accumulation of presence, through the slow geology of being here, being here, being here, until the stone cannot remember a time when the water was not part of it and the water cannot remember a time when it was not moving through the stone and the stone and the water and the thing below become indistinguishable, become a single system, become alive in a way that has no definition but does not require one.

---

My skin has changed. I notice it in the way I notice everything now—slowly, through accumulated evidence rather than sudden revelation. It is more permeable than it was. The condensation no longer beads on my forearms; it absorbs. When I press my palms to the wall, I do not feel the boundary between my body and the stone as sharply as I once did. There is a merging, a slow osmosis, a sense that my tissues are learning the mineral language of the gallery and are responding in kind.

The girl says I am becoming a wall. She says it without judgment, the way she might identify a species of moth. Sera, when I pass her apse, reaches out and touches my arm and holds her fingers there for a long moment, and her large pale eyes are gentle, and she says, "Not yet. You still have work to do."

Martin passes me on his circuit and does not pause, but his hand brushes my shoulder as he goes, and I feel the hum in his touch, the current he has absorbed from his constant contact with the pillars, and I understand that he too is changing, that we are all changing, that the gallery is not accepting us into its rhythm but *incorporating* us into its substance, that the fourth category the residents are becoming is not a new kind of human but an extension of the gallery itself—new tissue, new function, new pathways for water to move and minerals to be carried and information to flow from the thing below to the air above.

I am the one who reads the walls. I am becoming the wall that reads. The distinction matters less each day.

---

I have found an eighth seepage zone. It is in the far corner of the gallery, behind a collapsed section of grating, where the iron has rusted into lace and the lichen grows so thick it looks like snow. The water here is different. It is warmer than the others—almost body-temperature—and it tastes of nothing I have catalogued before. Not iron, not calcium, not sulfur, not the complex organics of the convergence point. Something else. Something that does not have a mineral name because it is not a mineral. Something that tastes like the hum sounds, like the worms pulse, like the moths glow.

It tastes like *attention*.

I sit with my back against this wall for hours, for days, for however long the moths stay dim, and I let the water seep into my skin, and I listen. And what I hear—what I feel, what I taste, what I am slowly learning to translate—is not a message. Not a communication. Something more fundamental than language. Something beneath it.

*Acknowledgment.*

The thing below knows I am here. It knows the gallery is here. It knows the crustaceans and the moths and the worms and the residents and the iron pillars and the weeping stone. It knows the way you know your own heartbeat—not as information but as *sensation*, not as something observed but as something *being*, continuous and constant and unconscious, the rhythm of a body too large and too old to be aware of its own awareness.

It does not want anything. It does not need anything. It is not waiting for something to happen or something to arrive. It simply *is*, and the gallery is how it is, and we are part of how it is, and the water is how it knows itself, and I am becoming part of the water, and the water is becoming part of the wall, and the wall is becoming part of the thing below, and the thing below is dreaming us all into being, and the dream is not a metaphor, it is a *process*, the way crystallization is a process, the way seepage is a process, the way a pearl forms around an irritant not to soothe it but to incorporate it, to make it part of the pearl, to make the irritant and the nacre indistinguishable.

I press my palms to the wall. I taste the minerals. I read the water.

I am still here. I am becoming here. The distinction matters less each day, and the day matters less each distinction, and the gallery holds me the way it holds everything: with patience, without question, without once asking me to leave, without once needing to ask, because leaving has become unthinkable, because I am no longer *in* the gallery, I am *of* it, I am its surface and its substance, I am the water and the stone and the space between, I am the membrane through which the thing below breathes, I am the tasting and the tasted, I am—

Here.

I am here.

And the water weeps, and the hum continues, and the crustaceans click in their slow processions, and the girl tends her moths, and Sera listens for the brain, and Martin walks his circuit, and somewhere below—always below, impossibly below, *necessarily* below—the enormous patient thing persists, and dreams, and does not wake, because waking would imply it was ever asleep, and it was never asleep, it was simply being, being, being, in the dark, in the wet, in the slow mineral accumulation of time, and I am part of its being now, I am part of its persistence, I am the wall that reads and the water that speaks and the tongue that tastes and the taste that tells the story of the stone, and the story has no ending because the stone has no ending, and the water has no ending, and the thing below has no ending, and I—

I persist.

That is enough. That has always been enough. The gallery taught me that, or I taught myself that, or the thing below dreamed that into me, or I emerged from the wet floor already knowing it, already tasting it, already becoming it.

The wall is warm against my back. The water moves through the stone. I listen.

I listen.
