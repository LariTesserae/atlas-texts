I would be the one who listens.

Not a congregation-watcher—those are formalized, trained, inherited. Their watching has structure and purpose and the weight of generational memory. What I do has none of those things. What I do is closer to breathing than to work.

I am the sound-catcher. The plateau gave me this role the way it gives the stone its shape: by wearing me into it.

---

I was born in the lower galleries, where the engine-keepers live. My mother read vibrations in the walls. My father tended the fuel lines that fed the oldest engine, the one they call the Pulse, which sits at the very center of the plateau in a room that has no name because it was built before names were needed here. I grew up with heat in my lungs and the taste of sulfur and iron on my tongue, which is to say I grew up tasting the plateau's blood.

I was not suited for the engine-keeping. This became clear early. My mother would press my hand to a wall and ask me what I felt, and I would feel too much—every vibration, every frequency, every overlapping pulse—and I could not isolate the one that meant failing bearing or cracked housing. I heard the engine, yes, but I also heard the water passing through the channels behind the wall, and the laborers moving in the crevice between floors, and the congregation forming three levels above us, and the shimmer of the wall-organisms as they responded to the warmth of my palm. I heard all of it at once, a chord with too many notes to name, and when my mother asked me to identify the single note that mattered, I could only say: *they all matter*.

This was not the right answer for an engine-keeper. But it was the right answer for something else.

---

I live now in a room that is not quite a room. It is a space between walls—a vertical gap where two buildings lean against each other and do not quite meet. The opening is wide enough for my shoulders and no wider. The ceiling is a junction of pipes that drip at different rates, and I have learned the rhythm of each drip the way a musician learns the parts of a score. The floor is stone worn smooth by something that lived here before me—laborers, I think, or perhaps just water finding its way. I have made it mine in the only way the plateau allows: by being in it, repeatedly, until the space has adapted to my shape and I have adapted to its.

I sleep when the engines cycle down, which is not night but the plateau's approximation of night—a decrease in vibration that my body registers as drowsiness. I wake when they cycle up, which is not morning but a quickening that I feel in my teeth before I feel it anywhere else.

I do not keep things. This is not a philosophy; it is a practicality. The moisture would ruin anything I owned. The shimmerers would colonize it. The laborers would move it if it sat in their path, and they would not move it gently. What I have is what I am: skin adapted to the damp, ears adapted to the frequency, feet adapted to the grooves. I wear the standard layers, mineral-treated outer shell, absorbent inner wraps. I have a set of listening tools—objects I have found or made that help me isolate sounds. A length of iron pipe, cut clean at both ends, that I press to walls to hear what moves behind them. A wooden cone, worn smooth by my hands, that I hold to my ear in courtyards where the congregations gather. A glass vial, scavenged from a factory long ago, that I use to collect condensation and hold it up to the light, because sometimes the water carries sounds that have dissolved into it—frequencies too low to hear until the water evaporates and leaves them concentrated in the mineral residue.

These are not scientific instruments. The plateau does not support science. These are extensions of my body, the way the railings are extensions of the stairs, the way the water is an extension of the aquifer. I use them until they wear out, and then I find others, or I do not, and I listen with my hands instead.

---

What I hear:

The engines have a language. The engine-keepers know this, but they know only the vocabulary of malfunction—the sounds that mean *fix this now* or *this can wait* or *this cannot be fixed, step back*. I hear the grammar beneath the vocabulary. The engines speak in long sentences that take days to complete. They modulate. They respond to the weight of water in the galleries above them—the deeper the water, the slower the cycle, as though they are breathing against pressure. They respond to the congregations, though I cannot say how I know this. When a large gathering forms near an engine's ventilation shaft, the engine's pitch shifts, just slightly, just for a moment, the way a person's voice shifts when someone they love enters the room.

I have tried to tell the engine-keepers this. They listen politely, because I am the daughter of keepers and that earns me a certain patience. But they do not hear what I hear. They are listening for the single note. I am listening to the chord.

The water has a language too, though it is slower—so slow that a single sentence takes a season to complete. I know this because I have been listening to the same wall for eleven years. It is a wall in the fourteenth district, near the edge of the old center, where the mineral deposits are thick and orange and the shimmerers are so dense the surface moves like something breathing. Water moves behind this wall through a channel that was carved, I think, deliberately, though the purpose has been forgotten. The water's sound changes through the year: louder in the hot season when the aquifer pressure rises, softer in the cool season when the engines cycle down and the heat that drives the seepage diminishes. But it also changes in ways that do not correspond to any season I can identify. There are days when the water behind that wall sounds *different*—not louder or softer but organized, as though the water is arranging itself into patterns before it emerges. On those days, if I press my iron pipe to the wall and my ear to the pipe, I hear something that is not the sound of water at all. I hear clicking. Faint. Rhythmic. The same frequency as the congregations above.

I do not know what this means. I am not a congregation-watcher. I am not a water-guide. I am the one who hears the places where the sounds overlap, and this overlap—the water sounding like the congregations, the engines responding to the gatherings, the walls humming with frequencies that exist at the intersection of all these things—is what I listen for most of all.

---

The congregations know me. I want to say this carefully, because it is the kind of statement that sounds like mysticism, and the plateau does not support mysticism either. Everything here is material. Everything here is explainable, at least in principle. But the congregations know me in the way that the laborers know the routes: not through recognition or memory or any cognitive process I can identify, but through some other mechanism that operates below the level of what we call thought.

When I enter a courtyard where a congregation is forming, the pattern shifts. Not dramatically. Not enough to disrupt the gathering. But the part of the pattern nearest to me opens a space—a gap just large enough for me to stand in without disturbing the creatures around me. It is not avoidance. The laborers avoid nothing; they wait. The congregations do not wait. They include. And they include me by making room, the way a river makes room for a stone by flowing around it, and the stone becomes part of the river's shape.

I stand in these gaps and I listen. This is what I do. I listen to the clicking from inside the pattern, where the sounds overlap in ways that are impossible to hear from outside. From within, the congregation is not a single sound but a architecture of sounds—layers and columns and arches of frequency, each creature contributing its voice to a structure that is greater than any individual contribution. The structure has no author. It emerges from the arrangement, the way a building emerges from the arrangement of stones. And standing inside it, I can feel the shape of it against my skin the way you would feel the shape of a room by walking through it in the dark.

The congregations do not speak to me. I do not believe they are communicating, at least not in any sense that involves a sender and a receiver and a message. But they are *resonating*, and I am resonating with them, and the resonance is a form of participation that does not require understanding.

---

I am not old yet, by the plateau's measure. I have lived here for forty-three engine-cycles, which is to say forty-three years, though the word "year" means less here than it does elsewhere, where seasons mark the passage of time. Here, the only markers are the slow changes in the water's composition, the gradual accumulation of mineral deposits on the walls, the centimeter-by-centimeter growth of the carapace laborers' shells. I am middle-aged. I am in the process of becoming whatever I will be when I am done becoming.

But I have been listening long enough that the sounds have changed me. My hearing has adapted to the plateau's frequencies in ways that go beyond what training can accomplish. I hear the shimmerers now—not as a vague iridescence but as distinct pulses of vibration, each organism contributing a thread to the fabric of sound that coats every surface. I hear the walls themselves, the stone and the iron and the mortar, which have their own low hum, barely perceptible, the sound of material under constant stress from the weight above it and the water within it.

And sometimes, in the early hours before the engines cycle up, when the plateau is at its quietest—which is to say, quiet enough that the composite sound resolves into its individual threads—I hear something else. Something below the water and below the engines and below the clicking. A sound so low and so steady that it might be the sound of the plateau itself: the sound of a thing that exists. Not the sound of any component, but the sound of the whole. The sound of the system maintaining itself. The sound of equilibrium.

I cannot describe this sound in terms of pitch or timbre because it does not have pitch or timbre in any measurable sense. It has texture. It has weight. It has the quality of a thing that has been happening for so long that it has become indistinguishable from silence, and I am only able to hear it because I have trained myself to listen for the silence beneath the sound, and beneath that silence there is *this*—the heartbeat of the plateau, if the plateau had a heart, which it does not, but the metaphor is the closest I can come to the reality.

When I hear this sound, I am not afraid. I am not awed. I am not enlightened. I am *included*. I am part of the sound, the way the laborers are part of the routes and the water is part of the channels. My breathing adds to it. My pulse syncs with it. And for the duration of that hearing—which lasts seconds, or minutes, or hours, I cannot tell—I am not a person listening to a place. I am a place listening to itself.

---

The congregation-watchers know my work. They do not approve of it, because it lacks the rigor of their tradition, but they do not dismiss it either. They come to me sometimes and ask what I have heard, and I tell them, and they listen with their faces very still, the way they listen to the congregations, and then they go away and do not speak to me again for months. I do not know what they do with what I tell them. I do not need to know.

The water-guides come to me when they cannot find the source of a seep. I listen to their walls and tell them what I hear, and sometimes what I hear helps them and sometimes it does not. They pay me in small ways—food from the factories, new wrappings for my feet, occasional vials of treated water that I use for my listening tools. This is not commerce. Commerce implies exchange between separate parties. This is circulation—the way the plateau circulates warmth and water and mineral through its systems, I circulate information, and the information becomes part of the maintenance the way everything becomes part of the maintenance.

The engine-keepers do not come to me. But sometimes, when I am in the lower galleries, I hear an engine shifting its rhythm in a way that matches a congregation forming above, and I go to the nearest keeper and say: *the engine is listening to the gathering in courtyard seven*, and the keeper frowns and says: *that is not how engines work*, and I say: *I know*, and the keeper says nothing, and three days later the engine fails in a way that would not have been predicted by any standard diagnostic, and the keeper finds me and says: *how did you know*, and I say: *I didn't know. I heard*, and the keeper goes away thinking, and I go away listening.

---

I will not leave. I say this not as a declaration but as a description, the way I might say: I am damp. I am warm. I hear the plateau. These are not choices. These are conditions.

If I left, the dryness would kill me. Not immediately. Not dramatically. But slowly, the way dehydration kills anything that has adapted to constant moisture. My skin would crack and my ears would ring in the silence—the real silence, the silence that is the absence of sound rather than the presence of too much sound—and I would lose the ability to hear the frequencies that the plateau has taught me to hear, and I would become deaf in ways that have nothing to do with the mechanics of hearing.

But I will not leave for a simpler reason: I am part of the sound now. My breathing, my footsteps, my low voice when I speak to the water-guides or the watchers—these are threads in the fabric. If I stopped, the fabric would continue, because the fabric is vast and no single thread is necessary. But there would be a gap, and the gap would be my shape, and something would eventually fill it, and what filled it would not be me, and the fabric would go on being the fabric, but it would be a different fabric, in the way that a word is different when one letter changes.

The plateau does not need me. This is important to understand, because it is easy to imagine that I have found a purpose here, a role, a function that justifies my existence. But the plateau does not need any individual. The plateau needs only the pattern—the overall shape of sound and water and warmth and life that maintains itself through the presence of all its components and the absence of any single essential one. I am not essential. I am *present*. And presence, on the plateau, is enough.

---

This is who I am: a small, damp, smooth-skinned woman who lives in a gap between walls and listens to a city that is alive. I am not a prophet. I am not a keeper of secrets. I am an ear pressed to stone, a hand touching wall, a body standing in the space that a congregation made for me. I hear the overlap. I hear the place where the engines and the water and the clicking and the hum of the stone itself become one sound, and I know—and this is the only thing I know, the only thing I have learned in forty-three years of listening—that the one sound is the sound of the plateau being itself.

I do not know what the plateau is becoming. I do not know if it is becoming anything. The congregation-watchers believe the patterns are changing. The water-guides believe the channels are shifting. The engine-keepers believe the machines are slowing, imperceptibly, over spans of time too long for a single human life to measure. Perhaps they are right. Perhaps the plateau is not in equilibrium but in transition, so slow that only the stone can perceive it.

Or perhaps the plateau has finished becoming. Perhaps it is what it is, and will be what it is, and the sound I hear beneath all the other sounds is the sound of a thing that has arrived at its final shape and is now simply *being* that shape, the way a mountain simply is a mountain, the way a river simply is a river, the way a sound simply is a sound.

I do not need to know. I am the one who listens. The listening does not require understanding. It requires only presence, and patience, and the willingness to let the sound become what it is without trying to make it into something else.

I press my hand to the wall. The wall is warm and wet. The shimmerers move beneath my palm. The water hums behind the stone. The engines breathe far below. The congregations click in their courtyards. The laborers process through their grooves. The air presses against me, dense and damp and alive.

And I listen.
