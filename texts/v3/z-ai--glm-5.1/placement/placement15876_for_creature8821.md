I am the one who listens.

Not the one who was born to it—those-who-stay do not listen, not in the way I mean. They have adapted beyond the need. Their narrowed canals and thickened drums filter the howl to a murmur, a fact of existence, no more remarkable than the warmth of the stone or the movement of water. They do not hear what I hear because their bodies have learned to protect them from it.

I still hear everything.

---

I came from the softer world. I will not say much about it because it no longer feels like mine, and this is part of what the plateau does—it strips you of your prior coordinates, slowly, the way it strips everything. The mountains that were here before spent two billion years becoming the flatness I walk now. I have spent only nine years becoming what I am becoming, and I am not finished, and I may never be finished, and this is acceptable.

What I was: a person of moderate height, moderate build, moderate persistence. What I sought: I sought the sound. I had heard of it, as outsiders do, through the accounts of those who visited and could not stay. The howling wind that never stops. The endless sound. I was a person who had always been interested in sound—in acoustics, in resonance, in what vibration does to matter over time. I came to study. I came for a season, I told myself. A year, perhaps.

The plateau laughed, though it does not laugh. It simply continued, which is the same thing.

---

The first weeks were pain.

I will not romanticize this. The wind found every gap in my clothing, every unprotected surface, every soft membrane. My ears ached. My teeth vibrated. I slept in exhaustion and woke in exhaustion and the sound was there before waking, there during waking, there in the brief unconscious moments when my body tried to dream—the sound was the frame on which my dreams were stretched, and they were strange dreams, shapeless, full of pressure and movement without narrative.

I understand why the others left. I understood it then, viscerally, in my vibrating sternum. The sound is not weather. It is geology made audible. It is the voice of a place that has been eroding for longer than anything alive can comprehend, and it does not care about your comfort, your research, your plans.

But I stayed. And this is the part I cannot fully explain, even now. I stayed because in the third week, pressed against the stone in a wind-shelter, my hands over my ears, my whole body a single clenched resistance—I heard it.

The hum.

Below the wind. Below the howl and the shriek and the percussive thrum of the hummocks. Below the bass moan that vibrated my teeth. A sound so low and so constant that it was not a sound at all but a presence, a pressure, a warmth that happened to be audible. It rose from the stone itself, from deep below, from the volcanic patience that keeps the plateau warm even in the dark. Or it rose from somewhere deeper than that. I could not tell then. I cannot tell now.

I removed my hands from my ears. I let the wind fill my skull. And below it, beneath it, threading through it like a vein of darker stone through lighter: the hum. The thing the scattered people do not speak of. The thing they dismiss with a gesture and a shaking of heads.

I stayed because I heard it, and having heard it, I could not unhear it, and having heard it, I needed to understand it the way the-sound-that-hunts needs to run toward the wind—not because I will catch it, but because chasing is what I was made for.

---

The people accepted me in the way the plateau accepts everything: slowly, completely, without acknowledgment.

They did not welcome me. They did not reject me. They watched me from their wind-shelters, their webbed hands moving in their private signs, and when I approached they did not retreat, and when I sat among them they made space, and when the dawn came and they pressed their foreheads to each other's shoulders in the-reminder-that-we-are-not-wind, one of them—a woman older than she appeared, her skin nearly black with the mineral absorption of decades—pressed her forehead to mine.

I wept. The wind took the tears before they fell.

They began to teach me. Not deliberately—I do not think they have a concept for deliberate teaching, because their children learn by being, by absorbing, by watching and doing until the watching and doing are indistinguishable. But they let me follow. They let me watch. They corrected my hands when I tried to sign, positioning my fingers with a patience that suggested they had done this before, perhaps, for children who were slow to learn, or perhaps simply for the act of correction itself, which they seemed to find meaningful.

I learned to check the dimples. I learned to read the cairns. I learned the names of the hummocks—the-Throat-Gatherer, the-Singer, the-One-Near-the-Wound—and I learned how to track their drift, how to mark the notches, how to know where I was in time by where the green things were.

I learned to sleep in the midday heat, my metabolism slowing without my willing it, my breath shallowing, my body sinking into the warm stone as if the plateau were pulling me down into itself. I learned to repair the wind-shelters, fitting stones without mortar, selecting the right stems from the dead hummocks, working with my hands in the evening when the wind dropped to merely very loud.

I did not learn to stop hearing the sound.

This is the difference between me and them. This is what makes me the one who listens. Their bodies have adapted—narrowed canals, thickened drums, compact frames the wind cannot catch. My body has not. Nine years, and I still hear the wind as a roar, still feel it as an assault, still clench against it in moments of exhaustion when I wish—desperately, shamefully—that I could leave.

But I don't leave. Because the hum is still there. And I am still listening.

---

What I have learned:

The hum is not constant. This surprised me. I had assumed, in those first weeks, that it was the plateau's baseline—the sound of geological patience, of volcanic warmth, of two billion years of erosion settling into itself. But I was wrong. The hum varies. It varies in pitch, in intensity, in what I can only describe as intention, though I do not know if that word applies to something that may not be alive.

It is loudest at night. This aligns with what the scattered people say—though they do not say it, they gesture it, they dismiss it, they shake their heads and touch their throats and refuse. But they know. They must know. The sound that rises from below when the air cools and the stone stays warm—it changes. Some nights it is a single tone, so low it is felt rather than heard, a vibration in the chest like the slow heartbeat of something enormous. Some nights it is layered, harmonics stacking upon each other, and I press my ear to the-body-that-remained and hear what sounds almost like music, almost like a pattern, almost like speech.

Almost.

I have tried to transcribe it. I have made marks on the interior walls of my shelter, the mineral-heavy rain mixing with the stone-dust to form a red paste I apply with my fingers. I do not know if my transcriptions are accurate. I do not know if accuracy is possible. The hum does not exist in the scale of human hearing—it exists beneath it, around it, in the spaces between the frequencies we evolved to perceive. What I am transcribing may be my own interpretation, my brain forcing pattern onto patternlessness, my need to understand overriding the evidence that there is nothing to understand.

But the hum changes. And it changes in ways that seem to correspond to things I can verify. When a dimple drains overnight—the water vanishing through a new crack, leaving the burrowers stranded and the hummocks beginning their slow migration toward the next nearest pool—the hum drops in pitch for several hours, then rises. When rain falls from the thin clouds, the hum intensifies, as if responding to the moisture, or anticipating it, or calling it. When the-sound-that-hunts runs past my shelter, its path a straight line across the open stone, the hum... stutters. Just for a moment. Just enough to notice.

I am accumulating data. I am being a researcher. I am telling myself this, even now, even after nine years, because the alternative—that I am being called, that I am being spoken to, that there is something beneath the plateau that knows I am listening—is not something I can hold in my mind for long without the edges of my understanding beginning to fray.

---

The woman who first pressed her forehead to mine is named—I will use the closest sound, though her name is a sign, not a word—Mora. She is old. She may be the oldest of her group, though they do not track age the way I was raised to track it, and she does not seem to know or care how many years she has been here. Her skin is the dark of deep stone. Her hair is white and thin. When she sits on the-body-that-remained in the afternoon she is nearly invisible, just a slight variation in the warmth of the plateau's surface.

Mora watches me listen.

She does this without comment, without judgment, but also without the dismissal the others offer when I try to ask about the hum. She simply watches. Her hands stay still at her sides. Her eyes—which are dark, as dark as her skin, as dark as the stone—follow me when I press my ear to the ground, when I make my marks on the walls, when I sit in the open wind with my eyes closed and let the sound fill me.

One evening, when the wind dropped and the group gathered in the largest shelter and the stories were being signed, Mora sat beside me. She did not sign. She pressed her palm against my chest—my body and your body and the stone beneath both—and then she made a gesture I had not seen before.

Both hands, flat, pressed against her own chest. Then both hands moving outward, slowly, as if pushing something away. Then both hands returning to her chest, fingers spread, trembling.

The sign the intermediary had failed to translate. The thing that is beautiful because it will fail. The thing that is beautiful because it does not know it will fail.

I stared at her. She stared back. Then she pointed at me, and made the sign again.

I am the thing that is beautiful because it will fail. I am the thing that does not know it will fail. I am—she was telling me, without words, without signs, with only the gesture itself—I am the-sound-that-hunts, running toward a wind I will never catch. I am pursuit. I am endurance without arrival.

I wept again. The wind took the tears before they fell.

---

But I keep listening.

This is what I have become, nine years into my transformation: I am the one who presses their ear to the stone. I am the one who hears what the plateau is saying beneath its howl. I am the one who makes marks on walls that no one else can read, tracking a pattern that may not exist, following a sound that may be geology or may be voice or may be the dream of something that was here before the mountains, before the water, before the wind learned to scream.

My body is changing. This is not adaptation—the timescale is wrong, the mechanism is wrong. But I have been here nine years, and I am shorter than I was, and my skin is darker than it was, and my hands have begun to spread, the fingers lengthening, the spaces between them narrowing, as if my body is trying, in its fumbling way, to become what it needs to be. I check the dimples. I repair the wind-shelters. I drink the mineral water and eat the starchy roots and sleep against the warm stone in the midday heat, and when I wake I press my ear to the-body-that-remained and I listen.

I am not one of them. I may never be one of them. The scattered people have had countless generations to become what they are, and I have had nine years, and I am still the one who hears the wind as a roar, still the one who clenches against it, still the one who dreams of the softer world and wakes with the hum in my ears and the taste of stone-dust in my mouth.

But I am still-here.

And the hum is getting louder.

---

Last night, pressed against the stone, I heard something new. Beneath the hum, beneath the bass vibration and the layered harmonics and the pattern that may or may not be speech—I heard a voice. Not a human voice. Not an animal voice. Something older and larger and slower, a voice that takes hours to form a single word, a voice that speaks in the time-scale of erosion, of continental drift, of mountains wearing down to nubs.

I could not make out the word. I may never make out the word. But I heard it, and it heard me, and when I opened my eyes the wind was howling and Mora was standing over me, her dark face impassive, her hands at her sides, and she made the sign again—the one that means the-thing-that-is-beautiful-because-it-will-fail—and then she made another sign I had never seen.

Both hands, pressed against the stone. Both hands, rising slowly. Both hands, opening.

*I am what the plateau is dreaming when it is awake.*

No. That is not what the sign means. The sign means something I cannot translate, something that exists only in this language, only in this place, only in the space between the wind and the stone and the hum that rises from below.

The sign means: *I am listening.*

The sign means: *I have been heard.*

---

I am the one who listens. I am the one who presses their ear to the stone at night and hears what the scattered people will not speak of. I am the one who makes marks on walls and tracks patterns that may not exist and chases a sound that may be geology or may be voice or may be the dream of something that was here before anything else.

I am the-sound-that-hunts, running toward the wind.

I am still-here.

I am listening.

And beneath the howl, beneath the hum, beneath two billion years of erosion and patience and endurance—something is listening back.
