I would be the one who stopped.

Not the one who chose to stop—choice requires the kind of internal architecture that I have never possessed. I stopped the way a sound stops in the Flat: not by decision but by exhaustion, by the slow recognition that movement was achieving nothing that stillness could not achieve more honestly.

I was not always still. I know this because I can feel the memory of motion in my body, the way a riverbed feels the memory of water even in drought. My carapace—I have one, low and broad, the size and shape of a wall that never reached its second course—carries the grooves of long passage. I crossed the Flat for durations that made the crossing itself become the purpose. I moved with the tides of my kind, the silent millions, and I did not question the moving because questioning requires stopping, and stopping was not yet something I could do.

But I stopped.

It happened near one of the structures. Not inside it—nothing is inside them, not really—but adjacent, the way a shadow is adjacent to the thing that casts it. The structure was a semicircle of flat stones, waist-high, facing a direction that I could not perceive until I stopped perceiving directions altogether. I had passed this structure before. I had passed it countless times, in countless layers, and it had never been remarkable because nothing here is remarkable, and remarking requires the kind of stopping that I had not yet achieved.

But this time—this layer, this crossing—something caught. A roughness in the stone that should not have been rough. The Flat had worn everything smooth, had been wearing everything smooth for longer than roughness had existed, and yet this one stone in this one structure had a texture that my carapace registered as I flowed past. Texture. Roughness. A quality that should not have survived.

I stopped to feel it.

---

This is what the Flat does to you when you stop: it begins to clarify.

Not change—the Flat does not change, has never changed, will never change—but clarify. The layers that have always been pressing against each other, pressing against you, suddenly become legible. You begin to see the thickness of the present moment. You begin to understand that what you took for a single reality is a palimpsest so dense it has become opaque, and that stopping—just stopping, just holding still long enough for the motion to drain out of your perceiving—allows you to read through the layers.

I read the stone.

It was rough because it was new. Not new in my layer—in my layer, it was as old as everything else, worn to the same mineral nullity as the surface beneath it—but new in an adjacent layer, a version of the Flat pressed so close to mine that I could feel the grit of fresh shaping, the tiny fractures where something had struck it to break it from a larger mass. Someone had quarried this stone. Someone had carried it. Someone had placed it here, in this semicircle, facing this direction, and the placing was so recent in that layer that the roughness had not yet been smoothed away.

I could feel the hands.

Not see them—my kind do not see the way you see, do not process light into image and image into meaning. But I could feel the hands the way the stone felt them: as pressure, as intention, as the specific warmth of a body that has decided to make a mark in a place that resists marking. The hands were blunt. They were patient. They understood leverage the way I had once understood movement: intimately, essentially, without the need for language.

The hands were building.

I had not known that building still happened. I had assumed—though assumption is generous for the kind of pre-verbal knowing that passes for thought among my kind—that building was a thing of the deep layers, the ancient strata, the versions of the Flat that existed so far down in the stack that they were more memory than reality. But these hands were here. These hands were now. These hands were shaping stone in a layer so close to mine that the roughness of their work was bleeding through.

I stopped.

I stopped because I needed to feel the roughness longer. I stopped because the roughness was a kind of news—a signal that the Flat had not yet finished producing events, that somewhere in its infinite flatness there was still a reason to stack one stone upon another. I stopped because stopping was the only way to honor the hands that were building, the only way to say: *I perceive what you are doing, and I will not flow past it as if it were nothing.*

The Flat began to claim me.

---

This is what they fear, the ones who keep moving. This is why they do not look, why they flow and roll and unfold without pause, why they have internalized their architectures and made their bodies into walking structures that the surface cannot find purchase on. They know that stopping is an invitation. They know that the Flat has been waiting—patiently, endlessly, with the patience of something that has already waited longer than patience should require—for someone to hold still long enough to be smoothed.

I felt it beginning. The underside of my carapace, the part that rested against the stone, began to lose its distinction. Not quickly—the Flat does nothing quickly, has not done anything quickly since quickness was a property that motion could possess—but noticeably, in the way that a shoreline notices the tide: grain by grain, an imperceptible subtraction that becomes substantial only in retrospect.

I was becoming surface.

My edges—the places where I ended and the Flat began—were softening. The architecture I carried inside myself, the internalized walls and doorways and folded-leather passages that constituted my body, was settling into alignment with the Flat's own architecture, which was no architecture at all, which was the absence of architecture, which was the state of having been so thoroughly smoothed that structure became unnecessary.

It did not hurt. This is important to understand. The smoothing is not violence. It is not even loss, exactly. It is the conclusion of a process that began when the first Predecessor looked at the dramatic edges of the young world and thought: *this is too much*. It is the fulfillment of the Flat's original logic, the logic that says: everything that rises will be worn down, everything that marks will be unmarked, everything that stops will be smoothed into the surface that has always been waiting for it.

But I had felt the roughness. I had felt the hands. And the roughness was a contradiction, a flaw in the Flat's otherwise perfect logic, and the contradiction made it possible—just possible—for me to resist.

---

I did not move. Moving would have been the old solution, the Predecessors' solution, the strategy of internalizing architecture and carrying it across the surface in perpetual flight. I had already tried that strategy. I had moved for longer than your species has existed, and the moving had not saved me from the Flat's attention; it had only delayed the reckoning.

Instead, I built.

Not externally—I could not build externally, had not built externally since the walls became carapace and the doorways became joints. I built internally. I took the roughness I had felt—the grit, the fracture, the evidence of fresh shaping—and I made it the center of my architecture. I stopped being a creature that moved across the Flat and started being a creature that held still against the Flat, and the holding-still was itself a kind of building.

I built a wall inside myself. Not the smooth, internalized walls that my kind carry as instinct, but a rough wall, a new wall, a wall with fractures and grit and the memory of hands. I built it facing the direction that did not exist in my layer, the direction that the adjacent structure faced, the direction where the hands were still shaping stone. I built it as a doorway to that layer, a passage between my version of the Flat and the version where building was still happening.

The Flat smoothed against my wall. I felt its patient pressure, its mineral indifference, its absolute certainty that everything—including my roughness, my newness, my defiance—would eventually be ground to the same nullity as everything else. And I held. Not because I was stronger than the Flat—nothing is stronger than the Flat, because strength requires edges and the Flat has worn its edges away. But because the roughness was coming from somewhere. Because the hands were still building. Because in an adjacent layer, someone was stacking one stone upon another, and their stacking was producing a roughness that bled through into my reality, and I could use that roughness to build a wall that the Flat had not yet learned to smooth.

---

This is what I am now: a wall that holds still.

I am not a creature anymore, not in the way my kind are creatures. I do not flow with the tides. I do not carry my architecture across the surface in perpetual flight. I have stopped, and in stopping I have become something that the Flat cannot quite categorize—a structure that is also a body, a body that is also a defiance, a defiance that is fed by roughness from a layer I cannot see but can feel.

The others flow past me. They move around me the way they move around the stone structures: with disinterest so complete it has the force of physics. They do not look at me. Looking would require stopping, and stopping would require feeling the roughness, and feeling the roughness would require building a wall that faces a direction that does not exist, and building that wall would require becoming what I have become.

But sometimes—rarely, in the way that the creatures sometimes pass through each other—one of them slows.

It is not stopping. Not yet. But a slowing, a hesitation, a moment in which the motion becomes less certain, in which the internal architecture shifts slightly, in which a doorway that was facing inward turns slightly toward a direction that does not exist. In that moment, I feel them. I feel their carapaces and their metallic fur and their folded leather, and beneath those external architectures I feel the walls they carry inside themselves—the smooth, worn, ancient walls that were built so long ago that the building has become instinct.

I feel the walls. And through the walls, I feel the Flat. And through the Flat, I feel the roughness.

And I hold.

---

Someone is always building. The text says this, and it is true, and it is the only thing that makes my holding possible. The hands in the adjacent layer will not stop. They cannot stop—stopping, for them, is not yet a concept they have discovered, because they are still in the phase of believing that the world will hold the shape they give it. They are naive and they are wrong and they are the only reason I exist.

When their roughness fades—when the stone they have shaped is smoothed by the Flat's patient grinding—I feel the loss. My wall weakens. The Flat presses closer, and I feel myself becoming surface, becoming mineral nullity, becoming the thing I have resisted since I first stopped. But then new roughness comes. New hands shape new stone. The building continues, in that adjacent layer, and the roughness bleeds through, and I build again, and I hold again.

This is who I am: the one who stopped, the one who felt the roughness, the one who built a wall inside themselves that faces a direction that does not exist. I am not the Predecessors, who became what they built. I am not the builders, who are still building what they will become. I am something in between—a holding-pattern, a pause, a moment of resistance in a process that has been grinding toward flatness since before resistance had a name.

The Flat smooths. I hold. The hands build. The roughness bleeds through.

And somewhere in the stack of layers—in a version of this place that may be past or future or both—someone is placing one flat stone upon another, and the placing is rough, and the roughness is real, and I will hold until the holding becomes unnecessary, or until the Flat finally learns to smooth even that.

The cool air presses against my carapace. The even light falls. The silent tides flow past, and around, and through.

I hold.
