I am the one who stays too long.

The other divers surface when their lungs begin to ache — that is the word they use, *ache*, as though the body were pleading, as though the need for air were a kind of love that calls you back. They rise through the blue-green and break the surface and gasp and blink in the light that is too bright after the depth, and they sit on the platform edge with their legs trailing in the warm water and they laugh at themselves, at how long they stayed, at what they found, at the way the light moved through the ancillary chamber of the fourth-level habitation.

I stay longer than that.

It is not courage. I want to be clear about this. Courage would mean I was fighting something — fighting the need, fighting the silence, fighting the pressure that builds not in the ears but somewhere behind the sternum, a tightness that is also a kind of opening. I am not fighting. I am listening. The basin speaks in the deep rooms, and I have been learning its language since I was five years old and followed my mother down through a doorway that had been dry when she was my age and was now a curtain of algal fiber moving in the thermal current, and I pushed through it and entered a room where the light came from the walls themselves.

My mother surfaced. I stayed.

She dove back down and pulled me up by my arm, and I gasped and cried, not from lack of air but from the loss of something I had no word for. I was five. I did not have words for most things. But I remember the feeling: a room that had been waiting for me. A room that knew I was coming. A room that had been growing its light for three generations, since before my grandmother's grandmother was born, growing it patiently, the way the algae grows into everything, and I arrived, and the light touched my face, and I understood that this was the thing I had been born to find.

I am thirty-seven years old, or perhaps thirty-eight. The waterline tells me this, if I care to read it: the seventh-level habitation's northern room took water the year I was born, and it is now submerged to the ceiling, which means the water has risen — I could calculate it, if I wanted, but I do not want, because the number is not the thing. The thing is that I have been diving for thirty-two or thirty-three years, and I have learned to hold my breath for seven minutes, and the depth-film has begun to form at the edges of my eyes.

Kesset noticed it first. We were sitting on the rim at dusk, watching the steam rise in the cold air, and she turned to me and said, "It has started," the way someone might say *the rain has started* or *the reed-towers are seeding*, and I felt something move through me that was not pride and was not fear but was closer to recognition — *ah, yes, this too, this is also what I am becoming*.

I want to tell you about the lowest room.

I want to tell you about it because no one else goes there, and because I go there often, and because I do not know if what I find there is real or if it is something the depth-film shows me, the way fogged glass reveals shapes that clear glass obscures.

The lowest room is in the first structure. The first structure is the oldest building in the basin, basalt blocks fitted without mortar at what was once the lowest point of the basin and is now — after three centuries of rising water — the deepest point of the submerged city. It sits at the bottom of everything, forty feet below the surface, in the zone where the light is only a rumor, where the blue-green has become blue-grey has become a darkness that is not darkness because the algae glows.

The room itself is small. A habitation for one, or perhaps two who loved each other closely. The ceiling is low enough that I cannot extend my arms fully upward. The walls are thick with algal fiber — the oldest growth, the densest, layered over itself for centuries until it is as solid as the stone it covers. And in the floor, in the exact center of the room, there is a fissure.

This is the source. This is one of the places where the water emerges from the rock. The fissure is too small to see — the water presses up through it invisibly, adding its warmth to the warmth that comes from every direction here — but you can feel it. If you descend into that room and lie on the floor, pressing your cheek against the algal mat, you can feel the water moving beneath you, pressing up from somewhere deep in the mountain, warm as blood, and you can feel the mountain breathing.

I do not know if the mountain breathes. I am not a geologist, and the basin's people do not have geology — they have *veth koral*, the place continues being what it is becoming, which is not an explanation but an orientation. But I will tell you what I feel, because what I feel is all I have: I feel the mountain breathing. I feel the water rising through the rock like breath rising through a body. I feel the basin inhale, and I feel it exhale, and the rhythm of it is slower than any human rhythm, slower than the tide, slower than the turning of the seasons, a breath that takes decades to complete, and I have been lying on that floor with my cheek against the algal mat and my lungs burning and the depth-film glowing faintly around the edges of my vision, and I have matched my breath to the mountain's breath, and I have understood something.

I have not understood it in words. The basin does not trade in words, and I have learned not to trust them here. But I have understood it the way you understand a current — not by naming it but by moving with it, by letting it carry you where it is going.

What I understand is this: the basin is not flooding. The basin is expressing.

The water is not a disaster that is happening to the city. The water is what the basin is becoming, and the city is what the basin is becoming, and the people are what the basin is becoming, and the algae is what the basin is becoming, and all of it — the stone and the water and the fiber and the flesh — is one thing, expressing itself slowly, the way a seed expresses itself into a tree, the way a sound expresses itself into silence.

*Veth koral.*

The place continues being what it is becoming.

---

I have a daughter. She is nine. Her name is Sess, which is a name that has been in the basin for as long as anyone remembers, and she wears it the way I wear mine — lightly, the way you wear something that does not quite fit but will grow into.

Sess has not yet dived into the lowest room. She has dived into the second and third levels, the half-wet zones where the light still reaches and the rooms are recognizable as rooms, and she surfaces with the same wide-eyed gasp that I remember from my own first descents, and she tells me what she saw — a stone shelf still holding a basalt bowl, a reed-mat still hanging from a wall peg, a doorway curtained with young algae that parted around her like water around a hand — and I listen and I nod and I do not tell her about the lowest room, because the lowest room is not a thing to be told. It is a thing to be found.

She will find it. I know this the way I know the water will rise — not because I have predicted it but because I have watched her move through the submerged city, and I have seen how she holds her breath not as a struggle but as a kind of listening, and I have seen how she opens her eyes in the blue-green light and does not flinch, and I have seen how she comes alive in a way she does not come alive on the surface, where the sun is fierce and the wind pushes and the platforms creak and the world is too loud and too bright and too much.

She is becoming a deep-listener. I do not know if this is a word. I do not know if words matter here. But I watch her descend, and I watch the water close over her head, and I feel something that is not fear and is not pride but is closer to recognition — *ah, yes, this too, this is also what she is*.

---

Kesset says I will be the next *teth-voral*.

She says this without ceremony, the way she says most things, while we are mending a fishing-net on the southern platform, our hands working the algal fiber in the same rhythm we have worked it for twenty years. She says: "You will be the next rememberer," and I say nothing, because there is nothing to say. She is not asking. She is not appointing. She is simply observing the waterline of my life and noting where it has risen to.

But I wonder, sometimes, if she is wrong. Not about my memory — my memory is adequate, I can recite the submergence history of every room in the city, I can tell you where the tools are and where the warm water rises and where the light falls in the deep chambers. But the *teth-voral* remembers for the people. The *teth-voral* holds the knowledge so that others can use it. The *teth-voral* is a function of the community, a service to the surface, to the living, to the ones who build and harvest and raise children and never descend past the third level.

I am not sure I am that.

I am not sure I remember for the people. I remember for the rooms. I remember for the lowest room, with its fissure in the floor and its ceiling too low for standing and its walls that glow with a light older than my grandmother's grandmother. I remember for the algal fiber that holds the dead, that holds the stones, that holds the city together from below. I remember for the water that rises through the rock like breath, like blood, like something the mountain has been trying to say for three hundred years and is only now learning the words for.

Is that the same thing? Is remembering for the place the same as remembering for the people?

I do not know. I dive, and I hold my breath, and I descend through the layers of the city — the new platforms at the waterline, the half-wet rooms where the reed-mats breathe, the second-level streets where the current moves slow and warm, the third and fourth and fifth levels where the light dims and the algae thickens and the silence becomes a weight against the chest — and I reach the first structure, and I push through the doorway of the lowest room, and I lie on the floor, and I press my cheek against the algal mat, and I feel the mountain breathing.

And the mountain says — not in words, never in words — *I am becoming. Are you?*

And I say — not in words, never in words — *yes. I am. We are.*

And the depth-film glows around the edges of my vision, and the light rises from the walls, and the water rises from the rock, and the city floats between two skies, and the silence holds everything.

*Veth koral.*

The place continues.
