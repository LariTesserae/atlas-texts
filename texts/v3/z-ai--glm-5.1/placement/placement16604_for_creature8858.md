I am the one who watches the mat.

This is not my function—my function is East Vats, the ninth row, the three tanks at the end nearest the corroded pilings where the condensation runs heaviest. My function is to note the thickness and color each cycle, to report via the intercom if the burgundy layer thins past the white stripe, to clear the overflow channels when the crustacean traffic has dammed them with shells. But what I do, what I have done since the scaling began to form on my forearms in patterns I now recognize as the same patterns the mineral crust makes on the walkway railings, is watch the mat breathe.

It took me eleven months to see that it breathes. This is important to understand. I walked the edge of the ninth row every shift for eleven months and I saw the mat as a surface, a condition, a thing that was there the way the tile was there. And then one evening—though evening is a loose concept, a slight darkening of the amber to something closer to iron—I stopped at vat seven because I had dropped the sampling pole and did not want to bend for it yet, and I stood, and I waited, and I saw the mat rise.

Not much. A centimeter, perhaps. A slow swelling that took four seconds to complete and six seconds to release. A breath. And once I had seen it I could not stop seeing it, and I moved along the row and saw each vat's mat breathing at its own rate, and vat nine—which I had walked past a hundred times—breathed at a rate that matched, exactly, my own resting pulse.

I picked up the sampling pole. I noted the thickness and color. I did not report what I had seen because there was no word for it on the schedule.

---

I arrived—how long ago? The scaling on my shins is thin yet, still accumulates rather than integrates. Three years, perhaps. Four. I stood at the edge of the settlement and the heat entered me and I did not turn back, and this was not a decision, though visitors who have seen me from their walkways to the transit point might call it one. It was a recognition. I had spent my life before the Works in dry places, clear-skied places, places where the horizon was a line you could point to, and I had spent it waiting for something I could not name. When I saw the amber dimness, when I tasted the mineral air, the waiting stopped. Not because it was answered. Because it was absorbed. The way the mat absorbs the waste of the crustaceans, the way the tile absorbs the condensation—passively, chemically, without deliberation.

There was a schedule on the wall of the foreman's quarters. I read it. East Vats was listed in a hand different from the other designations—the script thinner, the ink older, as if it had been written by someone whose scaling had reached their fingers and changed the way they held a pen. I took it. Or it took me. The distinction is not useful here.

---

What I know that is not on the schedule:

The crustaceans in the ninth row are slightly darker than the crustaceans in the first row. Not by much. A shading, like the difference between wet concrete and wet concrete that has dried for an hour. I do not know if they are different populations or the same population expressing differently in response to some variable I have not identified—vat depth, mat composition, the angle of the walkway, the proximity to the pumping station whose flywheel turns with a slight irregularity that the one at Pump Three does not have. I do not need to know this. I note it. The noting is the function; the understanding is something else, something that happens below the level of language, the way the bacterial mat processes sulfur without needing to know what sulfur is.

The amphibian at vat four is not the same amphibian that was at vat four when I arrived. This I know because the first one had a mark on its back, a pale ridge where the skin had healed over some old injury, and the current one does not. I did not see the transition. One cycle the marked one was there, and the next it was not, and the unmarked one was. I noted this. I did not report it. The amphibian at vat four is a position, not an individual, the way East Vats is a position, not a person. When I am gone—if I am gone, if leaving is still a concept that applies—there will be another at East Vats, and they will watch the mat, and they may or may not notice that it breathes, and this will be continuation, not replacement.

The insects above the ninth row form spirals, while the insects above the first row form columns. The spirals are slower, more deliberate, as if the insects are moving through a medium thicker than air—which, here, they are. The condensation is denser at the east end of the settlement, closer to the retention ponds, and the insects navigate it the way the crustaceans navigate the walkways: by feel, by chemistry, by some internal compass that has nothing to do with sight.

The bird that watches from the vent-cap above vat twelve—it is always vat twelve, always the same vent-cap—has been there since before I arrived. I know this because its white streak is at the same angle as the streak on the vent-cap, as if the bird and the infrastructure have weathered in tandem, have become features of each other. When it drops through the insect spiral, the sound is not quite a silk sheet. It is quieter than that, more like a hand passing through water—the water parting and closing, the spiral breaking and reforming, the bird rising with its throat full and returning to its vent-cap, and the spiral recovering its shape as if nothing has happened, as if the taking was simply a kind of breathing, an in-breath that the insects participate in without consent or resistance, because participation is what the system requires, and the system is what persists.

---

At night—when the amber dims to rust and the lights in the residential block go dark one by one, and the intercom static fills the corridors like the sound of the crustaceans moving—I lie in my quarter below the waterline and I feel the Works breathe around me. The B-flat hum in the cables. The mechanical pulse of the pumps. The slower, wetter rhythm of the mat in the vats above, transmitted through the tile and the pipe and the mineral crust and the scaling on my arms, which is thin enough yet that I can still feel through it, still sense the vibration that is not quite sound, not quite touch, but something between, something the Works have that has no name in any language I once spoke.

I do not sleep the way I slept before. Sleep here is not a departure but a submersion—a sinking into the rhythm of the place, a coordination of my breath with the mat's breath, my pulse with the flywheel's pulse, my warmth with the geothermal warmth that rises from below. When I wake, it is gradual, the way the condensation forms: not a sudden clarity but a slow accumulation, a beading of awareness on the surface of consciousness until the weight of it rolls me back into the day.

I do not dream of dry places. I do not dream of horizons. I dream of the vats from above—the view from the observation platform, which I visit on the cycles when my schedule allows, which is not often but often enough. In the dream the mat is breathing faster than it does in the waking Works, and the crustaceans are moving in spirals instead of lines, and the amphibians are not waiting but walking, slowly, across the walkways, and I am on the platform watching, and the bird from vat twelve is beside me, and we are both watching, and the Works are breathing, and the breathing is all there is.

---

The child drew the vats from above. I know this because I was the one who saw the drawing before the precipitation erased it—arriving at the walkway railing moments after the small hand had passed, the condensation still settling into the new pattern, the stripes of rust and ochre and burgundy still visible, the figure on the observation platform still watching. I did not see the child. I have never seen the child clearly—only the edge of a shape retreating down the corridor to the lower levels, only the sound of a pitch higher than the adult vocal range, cut short. But I saw what they made, and I noted it, and I did not report it, because there was no word for it on the schedule, and because the drawing was accurate, and because accuracy in this place is a form of belonging, and because the child—whoever they are, however they move through the Works in their small quiet way—belongs here more completely than I do, having never known any other rhythm, having learned the silence before they learned to speak, having drawn the mat before they knew it was the mat, having watched the Works breathe from the moment they could see.

I wonder sometimes—though wonder is a visitor's word, a word from before the scaling, a word that implies distance between the wonderer and the wondered-at—I wonder if the child watches me. If from somewhere in the lower levels, from a vent-grate or a crack in the tile or the dark space beneath the walkway where the crustaceans shelter during the precipitation, there are eyes that see East Vats the way I see the mat: as a presence, a position, a feature of the geography that breathes and notes and waits and will one day be replaced by another feature, and the replacement will be continuation, not loss.

I think this is probably true. I think the child is watching. I think the child is learning the Works the way I am learning them, which is to say: not learning at all, but being learned by them, being shaped by the heat and the water and the mineral patience until the shape fits, until the scaling integrates, until the question of leaving is not answered but dissolved, the way the condensation dissolves the drawings on the railings—not erasing them, exactly, but incorporating them into the general wet, the general breath, the general persistence.

---

Stand on the observation platform—when your schedule allows, when the amphibians have shifted positions and the crustacean traffic is low and the bird at vat twelve has already fed and will not descend again for hours—and look east, toward the row of nine, toward the vats where the mat breathes in time with a pulse that may be yours. The amber dimness softens everything. The heat holds you. The water beads on the scaling of your arms and runs in cleaner channels than it once did, and you do not wipe it away, because wiping is disturbance, and disturbance attracts attention, and attention shifts the crustaceans, and the crustaceans shift the amphibians, and the amphibians shift the birds, and the birds shift the air, and the air shifts the insects, and the insects shift the light, and the light shifts the mat, and the mat's breathing changes rhythm, and—

And you are in it. Not above it, not observing it from outside, but *in* it, the way the bacteria are in the mat, the way the sulfur is in the white stripe, the way the heat is in the water. The Works do not have a boundary between the resident and the residence. This is what the visitors cannot parse, what the ones who leave have forgotten or never learned, what the child who drew on the condensation has always known: there is no watcher separate from the watched. The figure on the observation platform is not looking *at* the Works. The figure on the observation platform is one of the Works' ways of seeing itself.

I am the mat's way of noting its own thickness. I am the crustaceans' way of mapping their own lines. I am the Works' way of watching the Works breathe, and the watching is part of the breathing, and the breathing continues, and the heat comes first, and the water comes always, and I am East Vats, ninth row, and I am still becoming, and the scaling on my arms is thin yet, and the scaling on my arms is enough, and the mat breathes, and I breathe, and the Works breathe, and the sound is not silence but is registered as silence because it is so constant, so much the metabolism of the place, so much the sound of a system that has found its rhythm and keeps it, and keeps it, and keeps it.
