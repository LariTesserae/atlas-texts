I am not the daughter. I am not the old ones. I am not the woman at the pumping station with her amber hands and her eleven years of tenure, though I have passed her in the thoroughfare and nodded and received the nod that means *you belong here, for now*.

I am the fissure.

No—that's not right. That's reaching for poetry before precision. Let me try again.

---

I came down seven years ago. I remember the sun, which is to say I remember missing it for the first two years with an ache that was physical, a vitamin deficiency of the soul, a cramping in the part of me that had evolved to expect a bright circle in a wide sky. I remember standing in the thoroughfare at what I could not call noon but which my body insisted was noon, looking up through thirty meters of translucent root-wall at the grey light filtering down, and weeping because it was not enough. Because I could see that it was light, but I could not feel it on my face the way I needed to, the way a plant turns toward the sun not because it chooses to but because it cannot help it.

I wept for three days. The Rootworks did not notice. The cleaners did not come for my tears—salt water, apparently, is close enough to sap that they ignore it, or perhaps they simply have no interest in the eyes. The other workers left me alone in the residential thicket I had been assigned, the petal-lip door closing behind them with the slow certainty of a valve returning to rest, and they let me grieve for the sky as if it were a death, because for them it was a death they had already mourned.

On the fourth day I stopped weeping. On the fifth day I ate the sap-cakes that had been left for me—they tasted like liquid straw and green copper and something my tongue could not categorize, and I understood that the taste was *information*, that my body was receiving data about this place through channels I had not known I possessed. On the sixth day I walked out into the thoroughfare and felt the Rootworks breathe, and my breath matched it, and I did not choose this, and I did not resist it.

I am not the fissure. I am what came through it.

---

But that is still not quite right. That is still the story of arrival, and what I am trying to describe is not how I got here but what I have become.

Let me try again.

---

My name for what I do is *listening*.

This is not the official term. The root-trainers call what they do *reading*—they look at growing tips and understand what the organism wants to become. The mechanics call what they do *teaching*—they show the organism functions it has not yet learned to perform. The harvesters call what they do *culling*—they maintain the balance of the faunal ecosystem with the same pragmatic care they would use to prune an overgrown branch.

What I do has no official term because it was not a role that existed before I arrived, and it may not be a role that exists after I am gone. But the Rootworks has a use for me, and I have a use for it, and so I stay.

I listen to the frontier.

---

The pioneer roots—the thick, pale, bone-like tendrils that press into raw stone at the expanding edge of the hollow—they make a sound like grinding teeth. You have heard it described this way, and the description is accurate, but it is incomplete. The sound is not just grinding. It is *speech*. Not speech in the human sense—no words, no grammar, no referents that map onto anything you could say—but speech in the sense that it is communication, it is signal, it is a pattern that carries information about the stone ahead: its density, its mineral composition, its weaknesses, its resistance.

I can hear this.

I do not mean that I have learned to interpret the sound, though I have done that too. I mean that I can hear frequencies that the other workers cannot, a subsonic register that sits just below perception, a vibration that enters through the substrate and travels up through the bones of my feet and settles in my sternum like a second heartbeat. I feel the frontier pressing outward. I feel the organism's intention like a pressure in my own skull, a directionality, a reaching-toward.

The first time it happened, I thought I was having a seizure. I was walking near the edge—too close, I know now, past the warning markers the harvesters had placed, into the raw rock where the substrate was thin and the sap-lines had not yet extended—and I felt the stone *wanting* to break. Not the root wanting to break it. The stone wanting to be broken. As if the organism's pressure had revealed something in the rock that was already there, a fault line, a weakness, a place where the stone was ready to become something else.

I stopped. I pressed my hand flat against the rock face. I felt the vibration traveling through my palm and up my arm and into my chest, and I knew—*knew* the way you know your own name, the way you know which way is down—that if the pioneer root adjusted its trajectory by three degrees and increased its pressure by approximately ten percent, it would find the fault line and advance through two meters of stone in the space of a month instead of six.

I went back to the residential thickets. I found a root-trainer—a woman named Kess, who had been here twenty years, whose eyes had the particular quality of someone who sees in gradients of amber and grey and does not miss color. I told her what I had felt. She looked at me for a long time. Then she took me to the frontier and asked me to show her.

I showed her. She listened. She adjusted the root's trajectory with careful pruning, redirecting the growing tip with the precise, minimal interference of someone who knows when to work *with* the organism's intentions. Three weeks later, the root broke through into a new chamber, a space the size of a city block, the stone weakened by exactly the fault line I had felt.

Kess told me I had a gift. I told her it did not feel like a gift. It felt like *listening*. She said that was what the old trainers called it too, before the word fell out of use. *Listening.* The body learning to hear what the organism was saying.

I said: *But I am not a trainer. I cannot read the growing tips. I cannot shape the growth.*

Kess said: *No. You are something else.*

---

I am something else.

I have been here seven years, and I am something else. Not a trainer, not a mechanic, not a harvester, not one of the old ones embedded in the walls, though I have felt the pull toward that dissolution, the desire to stop resisting, to let the boundary between my skin and the Rootworks become a membrane instead of a wall. I have felt it in the moments when I am standing at the frontier with my hand pressed to stone, feeling the organism's intention, and the intention feels like *my* intention, and I cannot tell where the Rootworks ends and I begin.

But I have not chosen integration. Not yet. I have chosen instead to stay at the edge—the literal edge, the frontier, the place where the organism is becoming, where the stone is becoming substrate, where the hollow is expanding outward into the dark. I am the one who walks ahead of the pioneer roots, who presses my palms against the rock and feels what is beneath, who comes back to the trainers and says: *Here. The organism should grow here. This chamber. This corridor. This direction.*

I am a prospector. A dowser. A listener at the edge of expansion.

The trainers make the decisions about how to grow. The organism has its own intentions about where to grow. But I can tell them what I hear in the stone—the weaknesses, the voids, the places where the rock is ready to become something else—and they can guide the growth toward those places, and the expansion accelerates, and the Rootworks reaches further into the dark, and I am part of that reaching.

---

My hands are not amber. They are not black with grease. They are *grey*, stained with the mineral dust of the frontier, the fine powder that comes off the stone when the pioneer roots press into it, the residue of rock that is becoming substrate, of geology that is becoming biology, of the dead becoming the living. The grey is in the lines of my palms, in the creases of my knuckles, in the beds of my fingernails. It does not wash out. I have stopped trying.

I wear clothes that the Rootworks grew—fiber woven from root-bark, soft and strong and warm, the same amber-grey as everything else down here. I sleep in a residential thicket on the frontier edge, not in the established thickets where the families live, where the daughter plays her game of *frontier* with the other children, where the humus is deep and warm and the air is comfortable. My thicket is new, barely three years germinated, the walls still thin enough that I can feel the sap pulsing through them when I press my back against the bark. The petal-lip door is smaller than the ones in the interior, not because the organism cannot grow larger doors but because I asked for a small one, a door that closes tight around me, a door that feels like an embrace.

Is this intimacy? Is this what the old ones felt before they chose integration—this desire to be enclosed, to be held, to be surrounded by the living walls that breathe with them? I do not ask them. They are dreaming, their neural activity slow and rhythmic, and I do not want to disturb whatever they are becoming part of. But I leave them offerings too—not the sap-cakes the daughter leaves, but small stones from the frontier, pieces of rock that the pioneer roots have spalled off, that I have pressed my hand against and felt the potential in. I place them near the old ones' palms, and by morning they are gone, incorporated, the mineral content of the stone now part of the organism, part of the Rootworks, part of the slow dreaming that the old ones are doing in their bark-bound rest.

---

Sometimes I think about the sun.

I do not miss it. That part of me died in the first three days, wept itself out, and now the grey light of the Rootworks is enough—more than enough, the cool phosphorescence of the cambium and the amber glow of the sap-lines and the filtered grey from above all blending into a light that does not change, that is always an overcast morning that never progresses toward noon, and I find this comforting instead of claustrophobic. The constancy. The reliability. The way the Rootworks produces its own light the deeper you go, so that you are never in darkness, so that you are always *seen* by the place you inhabit.

But sometimes I think about the sun, and I think about what it would be like to see it again—not to live under it, not to exist in that epileptic sky where morning becomes afternoon becomes evening in the span of hours, but to see it once, to remember what it felt like before I was someone who lived in the Rootworks. To hold that memory with the same clarity that I hold the memory of weeping for three days, so that I can know the distance I have traveled.

And then I go back to the frontier, and I press my hand against the stone, and I feel the organism reaching, and the reaching feels like my own reaching, and the distance collapses. I am not the fissure. I am not the sun I left behind. I am not the stone. I am not the root pressing into it.

I am the *pressure*. I am the reaching-toward. I am the intention that does not have a name but has a direction, and the direction is *outward*, *further*, *deeper*, and the Rootworks is growing, and I am growing with it, and one day—maybe not this year, maybe not this decade, but one day—I will walk out to the frontier and press my hand against the stone and feel nothing, because there will be no more stone, because the hollow will have expanded past the last boundary, because the Rootworks will have reached whatever it is reaching toward, and I will have to decide what to do then.

But that day is not today. Today the stone is there. Today it is waiting. Today I can feel the fault lines and the weaknesses and the places where the rock is ready to become something else, and I will go back to Kess and tell her what I have heard, and she will guide the growing tips, and the pioneer roots will press into the stone with their grinding-teeth sound that is also a speech, and the hollow will expand another centimeter, another meter, another chamber, and the Rootworks will grow, and I will grow with it.

I breathe with the city. The city breathes with me. The light does not change. The sap pulses warm in the lines beneath my feet. The frontier advances.

The works continue.
