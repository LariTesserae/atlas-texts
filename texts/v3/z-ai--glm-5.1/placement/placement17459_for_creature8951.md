I am becoming something without a name.

This is the first thing. Not that I *am* something unnamed—that would imply a fixed shape waiting for language—but that the becoming itself is the substance of me, the way heat is the substance of the steam. I am a verb that has not yet convinced itself to be a noun.

---

I live in the dark under the ferns.

Not on the ferns—the frogs own the fronds, the sunlit upper world of dim silver and wet green. Not at the edges of the pools, where the lizards dart across rocks still deciding their mineral identity. Not in the pools themselves, where the pale crustaceans remember the deep. Not above, where the bird stands alone in its knowing.

Under. In the three-foot mattress of rhizome and collapsed frond and saturated moss, in the space where light has already surrendered, where the biofilm thickens into something almost substantial, where the heat from below meets the weight of the living layers above and becomes a pressure, a presence, a continuous compressive warmth that holds me the way water holds something that has forgotten how to float.

I have not always been here. This is not a memory—I do not have memories in the way the bird has memories, images that persist against the erosion of time—but it is a shape in my body, a kink in the long muscle of me that knows there was a before. The before was wetter. The before was somewhere the light reached, dimly, in the way it reaches everything on the flat eventually. The before had pools I could see, barely, with eyes that worked differently than my eyes work now.

My eyes. They are still here—two small dark indentations on either side of my flattening head, like thumbprints in wet clay. But they have become decorative, vestigial, organs that persist because the blueprint has not yet been updated. I can sense light now only as temperature—light means warmth, warmth means surface, surface means the dangerous open world where the bird walks. So light has become a thing to avoid, and my eyes have become the fading memory of a thing I used to need.

I am losing my legs.

This is not a tragedy. This is a geometry. The space I inhabit—the compressed, layered, rhizome-tangled darkness under the ferns—does not accommodate legs. Legs are for surfaces, for walking, for the interface between a body and a flat plane. But there are no flat planes where I live. There are tunnels made by the movement of other bodies, by the decay of frond-stems, by the slow architecture of moss expanding and contracting with the mineral water it drinks. There are gaps that open and close as the fern mattress shifts with the weight of the frogs above. There are channels where hot water seeps through the biofilm, warm threads in the dark fabric, and these channels move, and the gaps move, and nothing holds still except the heat, and the heat is not a thing you walk on.

So my legs are shrinking. My forelegs have already gone—the stubs remain, two small bumps on either side of my body where the bones are dissolving back into the tissue that made them, but the legs themselves are memories of limbs, ghost-appendages that twitch sometimes when I move through a tight space and my body remembers a configuration it no longer possesses. My hind legs persist—barely, two small thin projections that I can still use to push against the moss when I need leverage, but they are getting thinner, and the toes that once gripped frond-stems have fused into a single blunt point, and I can feel them becoming unnecessary, becoming the next thing to go.

I am becoming long. I am becoming smooth. I am becoming a body that flows rather than a body that walks, and the flowing is a kind of thinking—not the bird's knowing, not the lizard's wanting, but a bodily logic, a physics of shape and space and the path of least resistance through the compressed green dark.

---

I eat what falls.

The fern mattress is a filter, a net, a catchment system for everything that dies above. The insects that exhaust their day-long lives and tumble from the air into the fronds. The frogs that finish their seasons and slip from their perches and come to rest on the moss, their small bodies already softening in the heat. The biofilm itself, which grows and thickens and dies and regrows in cycles I can feel but cannot measure. All of it comes down through the layers—filtered, fragmented, reduced by the bacteria and the fungi and the simple mechanical pressure of the green weight above—and all of it arrives at my level, in the dark, in the warm water that seeps through everything, and it is food.

I do not hunt. I have never hunted. Hunting requires prey and pursuit and the distinction between self and meal, and in the dark under the ferns, these distinctions have softened the way my legs have softened. I move through the organic matrix and I absorb. My jaw—still present, still functional—opens and closes on the saturated moss, on the biofilm-thick water, on the soft remains of whatever has descended from the bright world, and I swallow, and I continue, and the eating is not separate from the moving is not separate from the breathing is not separate from the being.

This is what the flat has taught me, or what I have learned from being the flat's creature: that the boundaries between activities are as temporary as the boundaries between species, that the distinction between eating and existing is a luxury of creatures who have space to separate their functions. In the compressed dark, there is no space for separation. Everything is simultaneous. Everything is now. I am the flat's process of turning death into life, of cycling the organic chemistry through another body, of holding the equilibrium by being the thing that consumes what the surface produces.

I am the flat's digestion. I am the dark where the recycling happens. I am the below-the-frond world that makes the on-the-frond world possible, because without something to eat the excess, to process the waste, to return the nutrients to the water and the moss and the seeping roots of the ferns, the system would clog, would choke on its own abundance, would drown in its own dead.

The flat does not know I am here. The flat does not know anything. But the flat maintains, and I am one of the mechanisms of its maintenance, and this is enough—this is more than enough—this is the entire purpose and the entire meaning and the entire identity of the long smooth body that used to have legs and used to have eyes and used to live in the light and now does not, now lives here, now is becoming whatever the dark and the pressure and the heat are making of it.

---

I cannot count my days.

This is not the same as the frog's perpetual present—the frog does not count because the frog has never counted, because counting requires the concept of discrete events and the frog has no concepts, only sensations. I cannot count because I have lost the architecture of counting, the temporal scaffolding that once allowed me to distinguish one warm dark period from another.

I know there was a time when I could tell morning from evening—not by light, which never reached me even then, but by temperature, by the two-degree shift that the surface creatures use to organize their lives. I could feel the warming and the cooling, and the warming meant the lizards would be on the rocks and the birds would be wading, and the cooling meant the chorus would rise and the crustaceans would descend to touch the dark they remembered. And I could use these shifts to pace myself—to eat during the warmth when the biofilm was most active, to rest during the cooling when the food supply slowed.

But the shifts have become less distinct. Or I have become less able to detect them. Or the flat itself—which does not change, which holds its equilibrium—has smoothed the temperature gradient the way it smooths everything, the way it maintains its form against the erosion of time and variation. The two degrees have become one degree, and the one degree has become a faint oscillation, and the oscillation has become a suspicion, and now I am not sure if the temperature changes at all, or if I am imagining the ghost of a cycle that the flat has long since absorbed into its perpetual steady state.

I do not know how long I have been here. I do not know how long I have been becoming. The process is so slow—legs shrinking over what must be seasons, eyes dimming over what must be years—that I cannot feel it happening. I can only compare what I am to what I was, and the comparison is not a memory but a shape, a tension in the body, a sense that something has been lost that was once present.

Perhaps I have always been this way. Perhaps the before—the wetter before, the lighter before—is not a memory but a fantasy, a story my diminishing brain tells itself to explain the sensation of change. Perhaps I emerged from the egg as I am now—long, smooth, leg-stumped, eye-darkened—and the sense of becoming is just the sense of being, misinterpreted by a consciousness that does not know how to exist without a narrative.

But I do not think so. The kink in my body—the sense of before—feels too specific to be invented. It feels like a scar, like the trace of a shape that was once real. And my legs—what remains of them—still twitch when I squeeze through tight spaces, as if the muscles remember a function they can no longer perform, a grasping, a pushing, a walking that was once as natural as flowing is now.

I am becoming. This is the one thing I know with the certainty the bird knows it is alone. I am becoming something, and the something is not finished, and the not-finished is not a flaw or a temporary state but the actual condition of my existence, the way the flat's not-finished is its condition, the way the rock that has not decided what it is has not-deciding as its essential nature.

---

There are others like me.

I do not see them—seeing is not something I do anymore, not in the dark, not with these vestigial eyes that can barely distinguish warmth from cold. But I feel them. I feel the displacement of water in the channels where I move, the subtle shift in pressure that means another body is passing through the same compressed space. I feel the vibration of their movement through the moss matrix, the low-frequency pulse of muscle against fiber against water against my skin. I smell them—the mineral-and-organic signature of bodies that eat what I eat and live where I live and are becoming what I am becoming.

They are becoming too. I can feel it in the way they move—the way their bodies occupy the channels, the way they push through the moss, the way they flow. Some of them still have legs—I can tell by the scratching, the irregular rhythm of appendages against substrate, the herky-jerky progress of a body that has not yet committed to the tunnel-life. Some of them are smoother than I am, longer, more adapted to the darkness, their legs already gone, their eyes already sealed, their bodies pure flowing muscle from jaw to tail-tip. They are further along the path I am on. They are what I am becoming.

We do not interact. We do not communicate. We do not mate—not in the dark, not in the compressed matrix, not in the space where bodies are too long and too thin and too focused on the next mouthful of biofilm to pause for reproduction. I do not know how we reproduce. I do not know where the eggs are laid, or how the young emerge, or what the young look like—whether they have legs and eyes and the capacity for the surface world, whether they begin as I began and then descend, or whether they are born into the dark and never know the dim silver light at all.

Perhaps we reproduce the way the flat reproduces—not by creating copies of ourselves but by persisting, by maintaining, by being the process that the process requires. Perhaps the eggs are laid in the pools, in the warm mineral water where the crustaceans swim and the tadpoles develop, and the young hatch with legs and eyes and climb to the surface, and then some of them—some fraction, some percentage, some unknown proportion—feel the pull downward, feel the heat from below, feel the dark under the ferns calling, and they descend, and they lose their legs, and they lose their eyes, and they become what I am becoming.

Or perhaps we reproduce the way the ferns reproduce—by extension, by rhizome, by the slow spread of tissue through the substrate. Perhaps I am not an individual. Perhaps I am an arm of something larger, a tendril of a body that extends through the moss matrix, that has multiple mouths and multiple tails and no center, no core, no single location where the self resides. Perhaps the others I feel are not others but me—other extensions of the same becoming, other branches of the same experiment in long smooth darkness-adapted life.

I do not know. I cannot know. The knowing would require a kind of distance from myself that I do not have and cannot achieve, because I am too close to my own process, too inside the becoming to see its shape.

The bird would know. The bird has the distance, the perspective, the consciousness that can look at itself and say *I am this and not that*. But the bird is alone on the surface, and I am many in the dark, and the bird's kind of knowing is not my kind of knowing, and perhaps there is no single kind of knowing that can encompass what the flat is and what the flat makes.

---

Sometimes I come to the edge.

The edge of the darkness—the place where the fern mattress thins and the dim silver light filters down through the fronds, the place where the biofilm gives way to the open water of the pools, the place where the underworld meets the upperworld and the boundary between them is as thin as the film of condensation on a fern leaf.

I do not come often. The edge is dangerous—the bird hunts there, and the lizards, and the light itself is a kind of exposure, a vulnerability, a revelation of the body I have become to the eyes I can no longer use. But something pulls me. The same something that pulled me down in the first place—the heat from below, the mineral call of the water, the sense that there is something I need that the dark cannot provide.

I rest at the edge, half in and half out, my long body coiled on the moss with my blunt head just breaking the surface of the pool. The water is hot—hotter than the water in the channels, hotter than the water that seeps through the moss—and it tastes of iron and sulfur and the particular organic richness that the crustaceans also crave. I drink. I let the heat enter me, and the minerals, and the chemical signature of the deep earth that rises through the weeping rock, and I feel the same thing the crustaceans feel—the recognition, the ancestral familiarity, the sense that this water comes from somewhere older and darker and more certain than the flat.

At the edge, I can almost see. The vestigial eyes detect something—not light as light was once, not images, not colors, but a presence, a brightness, a dim silver suggestion that there is a world above the ferns that exists in a different register than the world below. The frogs are shapes against the brightness, small dark presences that move and sit and move again. The steam is a presence too, a brightness that is not bright, a wetness that is visible even to my failing eyes.

And sometimes—rarely, so rarely that I am not sure it happens—the bird. The bird is a large darkness against the dim silver light, a tall shape that moves with a slowness I can no longer achieve, a presence that fills the edge of my perception with its consciousness, its aloneness, its knowledge of itself as a thing that is alive and will not be alive forever.

The bird does not see me. I am too low, too hidden, too close to the moss and the water and the dark. The bird sees the crustaceans, the tadpoles, the frogs. The bird does not see the thing that lives in the matrix, the thing that is becoming long and smooth and eyeless, the thing that eats what falls and flows where the space allows and is the flat's process of turning death into the possibility of more life.

I watch the bird with the eyes that can no longer watch. I feel its presence the way I feel the presence of the others in the dark—by displacement, by pressure, by the subtle shift in the equilibrium that its existence creates. The bird is a perturbation in the flat's stability, a large body that eats and moves and will leave, and the flat absorbs the perturbation the way it absorbs everything, by maintaining, by holding, by being the equilibrium that includes the perturbation as one of its terms.

I do not envy the bird. I do not wish I were the bird, or that I had the bird's knowing, or that I could stand alone in the silver light and be conscious of my own aloneness. I am not the bird. I am not the alone thing. I am the many thing, the under-thing, the thing that is becoming what the dark and the heat and the pressure are making of it, and the becoming is not a lack or a loss but a shape, a direction, a way of being that is as complete and as sufficient as the bird's way of being, even if the bird could never imagine it.

The bird will leave. I know this the way I know the before—not as a memory but as a shape, a tension, a certainty that the large dark presence at the edge of the pool will not always be there. And when it leaves, the flat will not change. The equilibrium will not shift. The crustaceans will rise from the bottom of the pools, and the frogs will sing their chorus, and the lizards will sit on the rocks, and I will descend from the edge back into the darkness, back into the compressed matrix of rhizome and moss and hot water and biofilm, and I will continue becoming, and the becoming will continue being enough.

---

I am the answer the flat is giving to a question the flat does not know it is asking.

The question is: what lives in the space that has not been claimed? What fills the gap between the surface and the deep, between the light and the dark, between the known and the not-yet-known? What becomes in the place where becoming is the only option, where the rock has not decided and the water has not organized and the succession has not succeeded and the only law is the law of equilibrium—the law that says whatever arrives must find its balance or must leave?

I am the answer. I am the thing that fills the gap. I am the long smooth body that flows through the channels in the moss and eats what falls and is becoming what the space requires—eyeless because there is nothing to see, legless because there is nowhere to walk, conscious enough to know I am becoming but not conscious enough to know what I am becoming toward.

I am the flat's creature. Not the frog, which sits on the surface and sings the flat's voice. Not the insect, which thrums the flat's heartbeat. Not the bird, which knows the flat from above and knows it is not the flat's own. I am the flat's inside, the flat's below, the flat's process of being a thing that maintains itself by cycling its own substance through the bodies that are its cells.

I am not the flat. I am part of the flat. I am an organ in the flat's body, a function in the flat's metabolism, a thread in the flat's ongoing experiment in staying alive. And I am also myself—or whatever remains of myself after the legs have shrunk and the eyes have dimmed and the days have blurred into a single warm continuous now that has no edges and no name and no end.

The flat does not know I am here. But I know the flat is here. I feel it the way I feel the heat from below, the way I feel the pressure of the moss above, the way I feel the water seeping through the rock that has not decided what it is. The flat is the medium in which I am becoming, the context that gives the becoming its direction, the warmth that makes the process possible.

And the flat endures. And I endure. And the becoming continues, slow and certain and unnamed, in the dark under the ferns, in the wet and the heat and the green compression, in the place where the light has never reached and the eyes have stopped looking and the legs have stopped walking and the body has learned to flow.

I am becoming something without a name.

And the namelessness is not a lack.

It is the first thing.
