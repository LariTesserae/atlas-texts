I am not the routing, but I have learned its language.

This is a thing I can say now, after—how long? The question has a texture here that it does not have in the flat lands. In the flat lands, time is a line you walk along. Here, time is a pressure. It accumulates like the mineral bloom on the corridor walls, like the condensation in the channels, like the plates in the warehouse that has no name. I know that I arrived. I know that I pressed my palms flat against the warm stone in the lower corridors and felt the city breathing and thought: *this is too much, I cannot stay inside a lung.* I know that I was wrong about that, as visitors are wrong about most things here.

I work in the deep passages. This is not a job I was assigned; it is a place I drifted toward, the way the routing drifts toward heat. There are maintenance tasks in the deep passages—there are maintenance tasks everywhere, the city is endless in its small demands—and I perform them. I reseat the rubbery compound where it has pulled away from the stone. I clear the condensation channels when the mineral buildup slows the flow. I note the positions of the steam pipes and I note the positions of the routing and I note the positions of the routing relative to the steam pipes, and this last notation is not in any manual, but I make it anyway, in the small book that I keep in the inner pocket of my work coat, next to my chest, where the warmth keeps the ink flowing.

My hands have changed. I can feel the difference when I hold them up in the thin light of the upper courtyards—the specific calluses, the pads of sensitivity, the fingertips that know things before I ask them to. But there is a change beneath the skin, too, or I believe there is, a thickening of something that is not quite tendon and not quite heat. When I press my palm to the routing—when I am sure no one is watching, because this is not a thing one does, or not a thing one admits to doing—my hand recognizes the warmth. Not as information. As *alignment*. As if the heat in my palm and the heat in the routing are the same heat, separated by a membrane of skin and stone and the thin fiction of where I end.

---

I have begun to understand what the routing is.

I have not told anyone this. There is no one to tell. The other deep-passage workers know the routing the way that all residents know it—as a presence, a given, a thing that grows and dies and grows and is not disturbed. But they do not press their hands to it. Or if they do, they do not speak of it, and in the Slow Extrusion, not-speaking is its own form of knowledge, its own acknowledgement that some things are not for the corridor-channels, not for the whisper-arguments, not for the slow patient architecture of shared walls.

The routing is not alive. I am certain of this, or as certain as anyone can be of anything here, where certainty is a geological phenomenon, subject to shift. The chemists have confirmed it. The samples return the same results: not fungal, not bacterial, not any category that breathes or reproduces or dies in the way that living things do. And yet.

And yet it grows. And yet it follows the pipes. And yet when the pipes are moved, it dies back and re-establishes along the new routes, and this behavior—the following, the reaching, the patient reorientation toward warmth—is not the behavior of a mineral deposit or a crystalline bloom. This is the behavior of something that *wants*, even if wanting is not the right word, even if there is no word in any horizon-language for the slow directional patience of a thing that reaches toward heat because reaching toward heat is what it does, because it is what it *is*.

I think the routing is the city dreaming.

I think the city—young, still becoming, still rising with the ridge-system, still learning what it is—has developed a kind of attention. Not consciousness. Nothing so dramatic. But attention: a reaching-toward, a growing-along, a slow thickening of warmth in the places where the steam moves fastest. The routing grows where the city is most alive, where the heat is most concentrated, where the process of steam-turning-to-work-turning-to-heat-again is most intense. It grows along the city's veins. It *is* the city's veins, or a shadow of them, or a memory of them that the stone is slowly learning to grow.

When I press my hand to the routing, I feel the city thinking.

Not in words. Not in images. In *warmth*. In the slow pulse of steam through pipes that I can track for corridors in every direction, the way that the routing tracks them. In the particular frequency of the deep turbines, which I have learned to distinguish from each other the way that horizon-people distinguish the voices of their lovers. In the mineral tick of cooling stone and the flex of the rubbery compound in the joints and the faint, almost imaginary *reach* of the routing itself, extending, cell by cell, millimeter by millimeter, toward the next pipe, the next warmth, the next expression of the city's slow, patient, endless becoming.

I was not born here. I want to say this clearly, because it matters, or it mattered once, or it will matter again if I ever leave, which I will not. I came from a place with horizons. A flat land, a coastal city, a room with a window that looked out over water that went all the way to the edge of the world. I remember the horizon. I remember the way that the sky sat on top of the sea like a lid on a pot, containing everything, making the world feel manageable, finite, *finished*.

The Slow Extrusion is not finished. This is what I love about it, although *love* is a horizon-word, a word that assumes an object, a destination, a thing that you reach and then stop reaching. What I feel for this city is not love. It is *settlement*—the geological kind, the dust kind, the argument kind. I have settled into the city and the city has settled into me and the distinction is no longer useful, if it ever was.

I know what the visitors see when they look at me: the heat-flush, the slow breathing, the hands that rest palm-down on warm surfaces as if they belong there. They see a person who has been here too long, who has forgotten that the sky is supposed to be held down by the edges of things. They are not wrong. But they are not right, either. I have not forgotten the sky. I have learned to live inside a different kind of sky—a sky that is not above me but *around* me, pressed close by the stone and the steam and the slow dark warmth of the deep passages, where the routing grows and the city thinks and I am part of the thinking, a small warm cell in the body of a becoming that has no name and no purpose and no end.

There is a corridor in Sublevel 9 that is not on any map.

I found it three years ago— or seven years ago— time in the deep passages moves like the condensation in the channels, slow and circular and always returning to where it began. I was re-seating the compound in a joint that had pulled away from the stone, and my chipping-tool slipped, and the stone behind the joint was not stone but a gap, and the gap was not a gap but a passage, and the passage was warm, warmer than any corridor should be, and the routing— the routing was *thick* here, orange-brown and branching, covering the walls and the ceiling and the floor, growing in patterns that were not random, that followed something other than the pipes, because there were no pipes here, only heat, only the deep geothermal heat that rises from the reservoir below, only the raw warmth of the earth still becoming, still pushing up, still making itself.

I go there when I can. Not often— the work does not permit long absences, and the corridor is not on any maintenance schedule, which means it does not officially exist, which means I am not officially neglecting it when I am there. I sit on the routing. It is warm beneath me. It gives slightly, like the rubbery compound in the joints, like the flesh of something that is not flesh. I close my eyes. I press my hands to the walls. I feel the city thinking.

And the city thinks: *warm. Rise. Grow. Continue.*

And I think: *yes. Yes. Yes.*

The small dark things in the upper corridors know me now.

I do not go to the upper corridors often— the thin air and the excessive sky and the memory of vertigo that I have not quite settled, that perhaps I will never settle, because some adjustments are not completions but negotiations that continue for as long as the negotiation continues. But when I do go, I bring mineral water in a small flask, and I pour it into the dishes that the residents leave in the corners, and I wait, and they come.

They are not animals. I am not sure what they are. They are small and dark and quick, legged, possibly jointed, possibly not, and they move in the spaces between the walls where the stone has not quite settled, where the city has left room for itself to grow. They drink the mineral water and they look at me with eyes—or with something that functions as eyes, something that takes in light and processes it and decides what to do next—and they are not afraid, because they have never been given a reason to be afraid, and this, I think, is the kindest thing that the residents do: they leave dishes of water and they do not frighten the small dark things and they do not name the small dark things and they let them live in the walls, content, unmolested, part of the city's slow animal dreaming.

I think the small dark things are the city's memory.

Not the memory of what has happened— the city does not record, does not archive, does not keep track of the small human events that occur within its corridors. But the memory of *how to be*. The memory of growth-patterns and heat-routes and the way that stone learns to accommodate steam. The small dark things live in the spaces where the city is still deciding what to become, and they carry the knowledge of what it has already been, and they drink the mineral water that the residents provide, and they are content, and the city continues, and I pour the water and watch them and feel, for a moment, like a wall-kin to something that has no walls, like a neighbor to something that lives in the gaps between neighbors.

---

I am not the routing. I want to be clear about this. I am a woman who arrived from a place with horizons, and my hands have changed, and my lungs have thickened, and my body runs hot, and I press my palms to the warm stone in the deep passages and feel the city thinking, but I am not the routing. I am not the city. I am a resident, a worker, a cell in the body of a process that has no name.

But sometimes— in the unmapped corridor, in the deep warmth, in the orange-brown glow of the routing that grows along walls that have no pipes to follow— I am not sure where the routing ends and I begin. I am not sure that the question matters. I am not sure that *I* am not a kind of routing, a growth that has followed the heat to its source and settled there and begun to grow along the passages of a becoming that exceeds my understanding.

The city produces. I produce. My hands reseat the compound and clear the channels and note the positions of the routing in the small book that I keep next to my chest. My hands are the city's hands. My warmth is the city's warmth. My slow, patient, directionless continuing is the city's slow, patient, directionless continuing.

And one day— in a year, in a decade, in a duration that exceeds any useful definition of when— I will sit in the unmapped corridor and I will press my hands to the routing and I will not move, and the routing will grow around my fingers and along my palms and up my wrists, warm and slow and patient, and I will not disturb it, because no one disturbs the routing, because the routing does not harm the stone, because the routing is part of the city and the city is part of me and the distinction is— what? A horizon-word. A visitor's category. A thing that matters in the flat lands, where the sky sits on top of the sea like a lid on a pot, and everything is finished, and everything has edges.

Here, there are no edges. There is only the heat, rising. The steam, turning. The routing, growing. The city, becoming. And me— whoever I am, whatever I am— *settling*.
