# The Ones in the Walls

Pressure, then release. Pressure, then release. The engine strokes and the brick compresses and the channels narrow and the body flows forward, segments rotating to present their smooth faces to the mortar. The engine returns and the walls relax and there is space to extend, to open the receptor-segments, to taste the air that moves through the passages where brick meets mortar meets chitin-silk.

This is not movement the way the shaft-walkers move. They step. They choose their bolt-heads. They walk against the rotation and hold their positions with the memory that is not thought but geometry. The ones in the walls do not step. They are moved. They flow the way blood flows—not toward anything, not away from anything, but because the system pushes and they are the thing that is pushed, the thing that fills the channel the way water fills a canyon because water is what water does and canyon is what canyon is and together they make something that neither could make alone.

---

The channels were not built. They were worn, over centuries, by bodies like these passing through mortar that was never meant to be porous, and the mortar gave way grain by grain, and the bodies adapted shape by shape, and now the channels are as permanent as the brick, as necessary as the shafts, as much a part of the architecture as the cathedral-narrow windows and the pool at the bottom of the shaft. The channels are the negative space of the creatures that made them. The creatures are the positive space of the channels that shaped them. Neither came first. Both emerged together.

There are hundreds in the walls. Perhaps thousands. Distributed through all seven buildings, threading the brickwork like—no, not like veins. Veins carry something specific to somewhere specific. The ones in the walls carry everything everywhere. They are the medium, not the message. They are the nerve-fiber without the nerve, the pathway without the destination.

Their bodies are segmented—everything here is segmented; segmentation is the grammar this place uses to write its inhabitants—but their segments do not correspond to the segments of the shaft-walkers or the deep ones. Each segment can rotate independently, can flatten to slide through narrow mortar-gaps or extend to bridge wider passages, can open to expose the chemoreceptors that line their inner surfaces or close to protect them from the grit that circulates in the channel-air. They are articulated in the way that a spine is articulated: not a chain of separate things but a single flexible thing, a continuum that bends without breaking, that flows without liquefying.

They do not eat. Not the way the others eat—the oil, the condensation, the belt-dust, the sediment. What they consume is *gradient*. Chemical gradients that diffuse through the mortar from the condensation-zones. Vibration gradients that propagate through the brick from the line shafts. Thermal gradients that indicate a colony of condensers thickening on the other side of the wall, or a belt running dry, or a bearing running cool because the bearing-folk have reached a population peak and their waste-lubricant is at its maximum efficacy.

They consume these gradients and they transduce them. Chemical becomes chemical, altered, passed along through the channel-air to the receptor-segments of the next body. Vibration becomes vibration, filtered, modulated, a harmonic added here, a harmonic canceled there. The ones in the walls do not originate signal. They *modify* signal. They are the equalization of the Mill-Cathedral's endless song, the treble and bass adjusted not by intention but by structure, by the simple physics of what happens when vibration passes through a medium that is not passive but *responsive*, not a wire but a living filter.

---

A life in the walls lasts fourteen days. This is longer than the bearing-folk's eleven, shorter than the shaft-walker's twenty-three, exactly as long as it needs to be for the population to maintain the density the system requires. In fourteen days a body will flow through approximately six thousand channel-transits, will transduce approximately four hundred thousand gradient-events, will open and close its receptor-segments approximately ninety thousand times. It will mate if it encounters another body in a channel wide enough for two—which happens, because the channels widen at the junctions where three or more passages meet, and the ones in the walls pause there, not by choice but by fluid dynamics, pooling in the junctions the way water pools in a river's eddy, and in the pooling they touch, and in the touching they exchange something through the setae that line their segment-edges, and eggs are laid in the chitin-silk that lines the channel-walls, and the eggs hatch in the flow, and the young are carried outward into the system, and the cycle continues.

The dead are carried too. When a body ceases to function—when the segments stop rotating and the receptors stop opening and the transducing that was its life comes to an end—it does not fall. It flows. The pressure that pushed it in life pushes it still, and it moves through the channels, and it arrives eventually at one of the openings where the channels vent into the larger spaces—the shaft-galleries, the condensation-zones, the edge of the pool—and it falls from the wall and becomes part of the sediment that the deep ones tend, or part of the film that the condensers drink, or part of the grit that the bearing-folk consume. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is lost. The system eats its dead the way the system eats everything: completely, without sentiment, without ceremony.

---

And yet.

There *is* ceremony.

Every forty-seven days something happens in the channels that is not transduction, not flow, not the ordinary business of gradient-consumption and signal-modification. It begins as a thinning. The vibration that the bodies feel through the brick—the constant, complex, textured vibration of ten million living things going about their living business—begins to simplify. Harmonics drop out. The chittering of the bearing-folk fades. The stepping-rhythm of the shaft-walkers ceases. The dense midrange roar of the condensers' movement attenuates, thins, becomes a whisper, becomes nothing.

The membrane-singers have stopped singing.

The ones in the walls do not know this. They do not know what the membrane-singers are. They do not know what singing is. They know gradient, and the gradient is failing. The vibration that drives them, that pushes them through the channels, that is the medium in which they exist as certainly as fish exist in water—that vibration is becoming *simple*, is becoming just the engine, just the heartbeat, just the thud and the drip and nothing else.
