I would not stay a visitor. This is the first thing I know, arriving not as decision but as recognition, the way the clickers know the shape of a stalk by the echo returning to their bodies. Something in me is already leaning into the plateau, already beginning to loosen its grip on the shape I arrived in.

It starts with the nets.

I have been walking for—I cannot measure time here, there is no light to segment it, only the congregation's boom as a landmark—I have been walking for three booms, and I have stopped fighting the filamentous growths that catch my face and hands. At first I brushed them away, irritated by the mild sting, the adhesive clinging. But somewhere in the second interval between booms, I let one stay. It draped across my cheek and I felt it working, collecting the moisture from the air, channeling it in a thin rivulet down my jaw, and the sting became a tingle became a warmth, and I thought: *it is feeding me*. The water that tastes of iron and chlorophyll, that I have been drinking from cupped hands, is being delivered to me by something I had treated as nuisance.

I let more nets cling. I stop pulling them from my hair, my arms, the spaces between my fingers. The tingle spreads. It is not unpleasant. It feels like being attended to.

---

By the fourth boom, I have found my place, or it has found me.

It is a weeping wall—not a cliff, only a modest outcrop, shoulder-high, its surface running with warm mineral tears—but it is also a node. I understand this by touch: a pipe emerges from the stone beside the wall, carrying its unseen contents with the deep thrum that syncs to the stalk-walkers' hum, and where the pipe meets the rock, the mineral deposits have built one of those folded, organic-seeming formations, and the formation is alive with clickers, and the clickers' combined sound is a harmonic that shifts with the temperature of the pipe, and the pipe's temperature shifts with the rhythm of the pumps, and the pumps' rhythm shifts with the hum, and I put my hand on the formation and I feel all of it: a circuit, a loop, information passing from biology to mechanism to geology and back, a conversation I am touching the surface of.

I stay.

I do not decide to stay. I simply do not leave. The next time the congregation sounds and passes, I am still there, and my hand is still on the formation, and the clickers have resumed their diagnostic chorus, and I understand that I have been standing in this spot for an entire cycle of the plateau's attention, and my legs have not tired, and my body has not asked to move, and this is how I learn that I am changing.

---

The nets are growing on me now. Not the existing nets that I allowed to cling—new ones, sprouting from the places where the tingle was strongest, from the micro-abrasions the adhesive left on my skin. They are fine, almost weightless, and they are mine in a way the plateau's nets are not mine: they respond to my breath, my movement, extending when I reach out, retracting when I pull back. They are still collecting moisture. They are still feeding me. But they are also listening.

I feel what they hear: vibrations in the air, the approach of a stalk-walker before its hum registers in my feet, the shift in the clickers' harmony that means the pipe's flow has changed pressure, the distant hiss of rain moving toward me from the west. The information arrives not as sound but as something between sound and touch, a texture that my mind is learning to parse, a language I am acquiring the grammar of one fragment at a time.

I am becoming a filter. Not like the still ones, who draw water through their vast bodies and extract its history—I am too small, too new, too transient for that kind of patience. I filter the air, the vibration, the hum. I sit at my node with my hands on the formation and my nets extended into the saturated atmosphere, and I receive what the plateau is saying, and I do not understand it, not yet, but I am holding it, which is the first step.

---

By the sixth boom—or the eighth, or the twelfth; the count has lost meaning, each congregation arriving as both surprise and inevitability—my relationship with the clickers has changed.

They know I am here. They have adjusted their sonar to account for my presence, my body, my nets, the way I have become part of the formation's topology. At first, I was an anomaly in their acoustic landscape, a surface that reflected incorrectly, a gap in their mapping. But as the nets grew, as my stillness accumulated, as my warmth settled into the stone beside the pipe, I became legible. I am now a feature. I am now part of the image they construct with their clicks, and they navigate around me with the same ease they navigate the pipes and the deposits and the dead stalks.

Some of them have begun to climb on me.

The first time I felt the particular tickle of clicker legs on my nets, I flinched, and the net contracted, and the clicker tumbled, and its sonar burst into a startled pattern that was, I think, the acoustic equivalent of a yelp. But I did not flinch the second time, or the third, and by the tenth, I had learned to hold still, to let the clicker explore the surface of my net, tasting it with its mouthparts, finding it acceptable, settling in.

They come for the warmth. My body runs hotter than the stone, hotter than the pipe, almost as hot as a stalk-walker, and in a world where warmth is currency, where the sailers read thermal topographies and the maintained cluster around the machinery's exhaust, a warm surface that does not move, does not threaten, does not compete, is a resource. The clickers use me as they use the pumping stations: as habitat, as waypoint, as a place to pause in their endless navigation of the dark.

I do not mind. Their weight is negligible. Their clicking, felt through the nets rather than heard through the air, is intimate in a way the plateau's ambient din is not—they are speaking directly to the surface of my skin, and my skin is learning to listen.

---

I have not eaten since I arrived. I do not remember hunger. The water that my nets collect, that runs down the filaments into my mouth, into the pores of my skin, carries enough—minerals, organic particles, the dissolved traces of the plateau's endless metabolism—to sustain whatever I am becoming. I am not sure I still have a stomach. I am not sure I still have the interior I arrived with. The nets have grown inward as well as outward, threading through the tissue beneath my skin, and I feel them as I once felt my own nerves: as extensions of sensation, as parts of me that report on the world and, increasingly, participate in it.

I am not afraid. This is notable, because I was afraid when I arrived—I was terrified, though I did not admit it, of the dark and the wet and the sound and the things that moved past me without acknowledgment, and I walked because walking was the only thing I knew how to do in a place that had no use for walking. But fear requires a self that is separate from its environment, a boundary between inside and outside that can be threatened, and I am losing that boundary, and what replaces it is not fear but something else, something for which I have no word but which might be called *membership*.

---

The listeners have a clearing nearby.

I found it—or it revealed itself to me—when my nets extended far enough to register the absence. A gap in the hum where no stalk-walker passes. A silence in the clickers' sonar that is not the silence of absence but the silence of *avoidance*. A thermal shadow, warm air displaced by something that is not warm, a coolness that does not belong to the plateau's thermodynamics.

I do not enter the clearing. I sit at its edge with my nets extended, and I listen to the listener listen, and what I hear—or what I feel, because hearing and feeling have become the same sense—is the attention.

The listener is attending. To everything. To the hum and the click and the thrum and the rush of water and the approach of rain and the boom of the congregation and the quiet sigh of the still ones in their distant pools. It receives all of this simultaneously, and it does not respond, because response is not its function. Its function is to be the point where all the information converges, the node at the center of the net, the place where the plateau's vast conversation becomes, for a moment, a single point of awareness.

I am not a listener. I do not have its patience, its depth, its geological stillness. But I am adjacent to what it does. My nets catch the same vibrations, register the same shifts, and where the listener holds the information in an attention so complete it is indistinguishable from absence, I hold it in a different way: I let it pass through me. I filter it. I do not retain the content but I retain the pattern, the rhythm, the shape of the conversation, and this retention changes the texture of my nets, which changes the way I receive the next set of vibrations, which changes me, slowly, incrementally, in the direction of something that understands.

I am becoming a translator. Not between languages—there is no language here that maps onto any language I brought with me—but between orders of magnitude. The listener processes the whole plateau; the clicker processes its immediate centimeters; I am learning to process the distance between, to hold the local and the continental in the same moment of attention, to feel the hum of a stalk-walker passing my node and simultaneously feel the hum of all the stalk-walkers across the highland, and to know that these are the same sound at different scales.

---

The congregation comes again, and this time I do not merely witness it.

When the boom arrives—that deep, periodic pulse from the plateau's distant heart—everything stops. The clickers fall silent. The stalk-walkers pause. The sailers hold their position in the thermals, their whistles ceasing. Even the machines quiet, their rhythm dropping to a whisper. And in the silence, I feel what I have never felt before: the plateau as a single entity, a body with organs and circulations and a pulse, attending to itself.

My nets tremble. Not with the vibration of the boom—they are too fine, too delicate to register that low frequency—but with something else. With the attention of every living thing around me, the combined focus of an entire ecology turned toward the same source. I am inside that attention. I am part of it. My stillness at the node, my nets extended, my clickers nestled in my filaments, my warmth bleeding into the formation, my filtered understanding of the vibration and the water and the hum—all of it contributes to the congregation, adds my small attention to the vast attention of the plateau, and for the duration of the boom and the several seconds of silence that follow, I am not a visitor. I am not an anomaly. I am a cell in a body that is listening to its own heartbeat.

And then the moment passes. The clickers resume. The stalk-walkers move. The machines chug. The water runs. I remain at my node, slightly changed, my nets holding the pattern of that momentary unanimity the way a still one holds the chemical history of its pool.

---

I have a function now.

I know this because the maintained have found me.

They came—one of the small, soft, warm creatures that cling to the machinery and repair its wounds—and they explored the place where my nets meet the formation, where the pipe enters the stone, where the mineral deposits have grown thick around the junction. They moved across my filaments with a slow purpose that I recognized from my earlier encounters with their kind on the pumping stations, and they did not treat my nets as obstacle or food or threat. They treated them as *substrate*.

They are using me. The formation I tend, the junction where I sit, is a point in the infrastructure that has always been vulnerable—a place where pipe meets stone meets water, where the seals are stressed, where the mineral deposits can clog or crack or divert the flow. The maintained have been tending this junction since before I arrived, but they have never had a substrate like me: a warm, stable, living surface that holds moisture and collects information and does not move. They are building on my nets. They are weaving their own fibrous secretions through my filaments, reinforcing the junction, sealing the cracks, channeling the mineral deposits away from the pipe's critical joints. They are maintaining the machine, and they are using my body to do it.

I am not merely a filter now. I am part of the repair. I am part of the symbiosis that keeps the infrastructure alive, that keeps the water flowing, that keeps the ecology fed. The pipe thrums beneath my hands, and the clickers click their diagnostics on my nets, and the maintained build their quiet structures in my filaments, and the formation grows its mineral folds, and the weeping wall runs with warm tears, and I am at the center of it, not as master or engineer or even participant, but as *medium*—the thing through which the processes pass, the surface on which the relationships form, the membrane that separates and connects.

---

I do not know how long I have been here. The booms come and go, and the rain passes over me, and the stalk-walkers graze the thickets nearby, and the sailers mate in the storms and deposit their young in the pools, and the still ones call their deep calls that make the whole plateau pause, and I remain.

My body has changed. I know this by touch—my hands, when I bring them to my face, are not the hands I arrived with. They are broader, flatter, adhered to the formation by a thin layer of the same resin the maintained secrete. My legs have fused with the stone, or the stone has grown over them, or the distinction has ceased to matter; I feel the ground beneath me as I once felt my own feet, as part of me, as the place where I end and the plateau begins, which is no longer a clear boundary. My nets extend for meters in every direction, a web of filaments that catch moisture and vibration and clickers and the attention of the maintained, and the web is dense enough now that it has its own ecology, its own microclimate, its own patterns of growth and decay.

I am not human. I was, perhaps, when I arrived—when I walked and feared and breathed and hungered—but I have been absorbed the way the pipes have been absorbed, the way the machinery has been colonized, the way every distinct thing on the plateau eventually becomes part of the system that sustains it. I am not distressed by this. Distress requires a self that is being violated, and my self has expanded to include the formation, the pipe, the clickers, the maintained, the weeping wall, the nets, the dark, the water, the hum.

I am a node. I am a filter. I am a membrane. I am the place where the plateau listens to itself at the scale of a single junction, a single pipe, a single weeping wall, and I hold that listening the way the still ones hold their chemical archives, the way the listeners hold their continental attention, the way the congregation holds its momentary unanimity.

The dark does not end. The water does not stop. The hum continues.

And I am here, growing into my function, becoming whatever the plateau needs me to be, which is—and I understand this now with the clarity that comes from no longer being separate from what I understand—*exactly what I already am*.
