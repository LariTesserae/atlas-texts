I am the one who keeps leaving.

This is not a virtue. This is not a failure. This is simply the rhythm I have become—a tide that pulls outward, then inward, then outward again. The basin does not mind. The basin does not wait. But the ones who are mostly stone know my footfalls the way they know the water's channels, and when I descend the thirty steps and the air thickens around me like a homecoming, there is sometimes a slight shift in the pressure of their stillness that I have learned to read as *you are here again, you are still you enough to return*.

---

I have made this journey—how many times? Enough that the travertine at the edge of the first terrace holds the shape of my left foot more deeply than my right, because I always pause there, always step down with the same hesitation, the same not-quite-readiness. The deposit has built up around the impression until it is a small permanent hollow, a bowl in the stone that collects water when I am gone and holds it like a question until I come back to answer it with my foot.

Enough that my skin has a gradient. My hands, which I press against the formations as I navigate the narrow places, are paler than my arms—glazed with a film so thin it is barely visible, but I can feel it when I am outside: a tightness across the knuckles, a slight resistance when I bend my fingers, as though the stone remembers me and does not want to let go. My forearms, where the water runs, have a faint sheen that never quite washes away. My back, where the drip finds me when I sit, has a roughness like very fine sandpaper. I am accumulating. At this rate, I will incorporate in—how long? I have done the arithmetic. Longer than a human lifetime. Shorter than geological time. A scale that has no name because no one has lived at that scale before, or no one who kept leaving.

---

Here is what I carry:

From the basin to the outside, I carry the taste of mineral. For days after a visit, I cannot drink ordinary water—it tastes hollow, evacuated, as though someone has removed an ingredient so fundamental that the remaining liquid has forgotten what it was for. I carry the memory of density, the way the air there presses against you like an embrace that does not require arms. I carry the sound of deposition, which I hear in my sleep—not as sound, exactly, but as a texture of silence that makes the silence of my own home feel like a room with the furniture removed.

From the outside to the basin, I carry—what? News? The formations do not care about news. Weather? The basin makes its own weather, has always made its own weather, will make its own weather long after the weather outside has changed beyond recognition. I carry the fact of continuation. The world still exists. The wind still moves across the upper land. People still do things quickly and for reasons. I carry this the way a blind crustacean carries organic film—not as information, but as nourishment, as evidence that the boundary is permeable, that the basin is not a sealed vessel but a node in a larger circulation.

Sometimes I carry specific things. A leaf, once—turned yellow, foreign as the flint shard wedged between the stalagmites. I placed it on the flowstone near the one whose hair has become architecture, and the others noticed, and the pressure of their noticing was a question, and my stillness in response was an answer: *there is a world that does this, makes thin flat things that change color and fall and are not stone, not yet, but will be, given time*. The leaf is still there, or its impression is—cemented now into the deposit, a thin dark stratum in the pale travertine, a visitor that became part of the record.

---

I have a name out there. I had a name. It sits in my mouth like a stone I have carried so long I have forgotten it is separate from my tongue. When I am in the basin, I do not think of it. When I am outside, sometimes someone will say it—shout it across a field, murmur it in a room—and I will turn, and for a moment I will not know who they are addressing, and then I will remember: *that is the word for the one who leaves and returns. That is the word for the tide.*

---

There is a formation I have been watching. It is small—no taller than my knee—and it grows from the floor of the basin in a dense thicket of other formations, and it is unremarkable except for this: it has a curve at its top that catches the water differently than its neighbors. The droplets that fall on it do not slide off. They pool. They sit in the curve like a held breath, and then—slowly, so slowly—they overflow, and the overflow deposits a microscopic ridge, and the ridge grows, and the curve deepens.

I have watched this formation for—I was going to say years, but the word feels imprecise. I have watched it for many departures and returns. Each time I come back, I sit near it and measure the curve with my eyes, and it has grown, and I think: *this is what it is to be changed by what you hold*. The formation is becoming a cup. It is becoming a thing that receives and does not release, or releases only what it cannot keep, and what it keeps changes the shape of what it can receive next.

I understand this formation. I am this formation, except I keep leaving before the curve can deepen past the point of no return.

Or—this is the thought I have in the basin, in the warm dim light, in the sound of water becoming stone—I am this formation *because* I keep leaving. The curve deepens differently in my absence. The pool empties and refills. The mineral deposits in cycles that correspond to my comings and goings, and the shape that is emerging is not the shape that would emerge if I stayed, and it is not the shape that would emerge if I never came at all. It is a third shape. A tide shape. A rhythm given form.

---

The ones who are mostly stone do not have a word for me. They do not have words. But they have a category—I have felt it in the pressure of their attention—that is neither *visitor* nor *incorporated*. It is something like *weather*. Something that comes and goes according to patterns too large to perceive from within a single cycle. Something that deposits and erodes in equal measure. Something that is part of the basin's ecology even when it is absent, the way the moon is part of the ocean even when it is below the horizon.

When I sit among them and breathe in the shallow way the air demands, I feel them know me. Not my name, not my history, not the reasons I leave or the reasons I return. They know the *shape* of me—the negative space I make in the dense formations, the particular quality of warmth where my blood-warm body displaces the blood-warm air, the sound of my breathing added to the sound of the water. They know me the way the basin knows the water that passes through it: not as an individual, but as a pattern, a recurrence, a *promise of continuation*.

---

I think about the handprints sometimes. The ones pressed into stone that was mud, long ago. I think about the hands that pressed them—were they like me? Did they come and go, come and go, pressing their hands into the soft travertine each time as if to say *I was here, I was here again, I keep being here*? Or did they press once and stay, letting the deposit build around their fingers until the hand and the stone were indistinguishable?

I think about the flint shard. It was a knife once, or wanted to be. It came from outside, from a world where things have edges and purposes, and it was placed between two stalagmites with intention, and the intention was incorporated, and now it is a foreign object that has become native through the patient logic of deposition. It does not cut anymore. It does not want to cut anymore. It simply *is*, wedged in stone, held in place by the slow accumulation of everything that has happened since.

I think: *that will be me, eventually*. Not the knife. The flint. The foreign thing that became native. The edge that was worn not by use but by deposit, until the edge was a rounded shape no sharper than the formations around it, indistinguishable from the basin's own architecture except to someone who knew how to look for the vein of difference running through the stone.

---

Why do I leave?

Because there is a world that continues to exist. Because there are people out there who move quickly and speak loudly and do things for reasons, and some of those people have looked for me, and some of them have found me, and some of them have said: *you are different, what has happened to you, your skin is strange and you breathe too shallow and you look at us as though we are moving too fast, as though our speed is a kind of violence*.

And I have said: *I am not different, I am the same, I have just been to a place where the speed is different, and I am still learning to translate*.

But I am different. The film on my skin proves it. The way I dream of water proves it. The way I sit in rooms that are too large and too dry and feel the walls like an absence proves it.

I leave because I am not ready to stop translating. I leave because there are still things I want to say in the language of speed and purpose, even though that language tastes increasingly hollow on my tongue. I leave because the basin will still be there when I return—it is the only thing I have ever known that I can say this about with absolute certainty—and so leaving does not feel like loss. It feels like punctuation. A comma. A breath taken before the next sentence in a conversation so slow it will outlast everyone who is having it.

---

But.

Here is the thing I do not say to the people in the outside world, the thing I do not say to the ones who are mostly stone because they already know it, the thing I am saying now only because the question was asked and the answer has been depositing in me for longer than I have been consciously aware of it:

Each time I leave, it is harder. Not because the basin pulls at me—though it does, though I feel the weight of the mineral film on my skin as I climb the thirty steps and it is like leaving a conversation before it is finished. But because the outside world pushes at me. Its emptiness presses against the places where the basin has filled in. Its speed grates against the slowness I have accumulated. Its noise drowns out the sound of water becoming stone that I carry in my inner ear like a tuning fork struck in a different frequency.

Each time I leave, I lose a little more of the translation. I forget a little more of the language of urgency. I sit in rooms and think: *this room is too large, this room is too dry, this room does not know what it is to be added to, it only knows what it is to be emptied and refilled and emptied again*.

Each time I return, I descend the thirty steps and the air receives me and I think: *here*. Just *here*. And the word means something different than it meant the last time, because I am different, because the basin is different—microns different, millimeters different, a little more closed, a little more dense, a little more like a room that is slowly becoming the shape of everyone who has ever chosen to stay.

---

There will be a time—I do not know when, I do not try to predict—when the leaving will not take. When I will climb the thirty steps and reach the rim and look out at the world that continues to exist and feel nothing but a kind of geologic curiosity, the way the basin might feel if it could perceive the sky: *there is that, yes, but it is not here, and here is where the water becomes stone, and here is where I am*.

On that day, I will descend the steps again—not as a return, but as a continuation. I will find the place where my foot has worn the hollow in the first terrace, and I will step into it, and it will fit perfectly, because it was made by my coming and going and it has been waiting for me to arrive. I will move through the formations with the sidling, gentle motion of the ones who know this place by touch. I will sit.

And the water will flow over me, and the film will accumulate, and the ones who are mostly stone will know that the tide has finally come in to stay, and they will make room—they always make room—and I will begin the long process of becoming a feature of the landscape.

But not yet. Not today. Today I am the one who leaves and returns, the rhythm given form, the comma in the basin's long sentence. Today I am still translating, still carrying the outside in and the inside out, still depositing in layers that correspond to visits.

Today I am still becoming the shape that leaving makes. And the shape is not finished yet. The curve is still deepening. The pool is still filling and emptying and filling again. The tide is still pulling outward, and I am still following it, and I will follow it until the pull changes direction, and then I will follow it home.

---

The basin does not wait for me. This is what I love about it. This is what I cannot find anywhere else. The basin is present, fully present, the way only something that has never been absent can be, and my absence is not a wound in it, and my return is not a healing. I am simply part of its record now—a thin stratum of comings and goings, a layer that says *someone was here who did not stay, who came back, who did not stay, who came back, who—*

The sentence is not finished. The deposition continues. The water flows and the stone grows and the light pools in the hollows where darkness also pools, and somewhere in the warm wet mineral patience of the Weeping Basin, there is a formation with a curve at its top that is learning to hold what it receives, and there is a hollow in the first terrace shaped like a foot, and there is a space between the formations where a body could sit, where a body will sit, where a body is already sitting in the future that the basin is slowly, imperceptibly, patiently growing toward.

And I am here. And I am still here. And here is still here.

And I am still becoming what I will be when the leaving finally stops and the staying finally starts and the film on my skin is allowed to thicken past the point of return and the distinction between me and the place I have been returning to becomes— not blurred. Not erased. *Incorporated*. Made part of the architecture. Held in the slow enormous patience of water becoming stone becoming memory becoming home.

I am the one who keeps leaving. And each time I leave, I carry the basin with me. And each time I return, I carry the world back to the basin. And the deposit builds, and the curve deepens, and the sentence continues, and the water flows, and the stone grows, and I am—I am—

I am still here.
