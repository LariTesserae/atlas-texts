I am the one who notices when the sifters change their pattern.

Not the one who decides what it means—I am not a foreman, not an engineer, not the assay office clerk who files the reports in their lead-lined cabinets. I am the one who walks the upper galleries in the hour before the shift change, when the machinery breathes its shallowest and the mountain makes its small confessions to anyone pressing a palm flat against the stone. I am the one who counts.

Not formally. Not with tallies or ledgers. But my hands have touched every wall in the Throat, and my feet know the slopes of floors that tilted differently last year, and I have been watching the sifters longer than anyone except the mountain itself.

---

My mother was the one who caught them.

Not in jars—she thought the jars were cruel, though she never said so out loud, because saying so out loud would have been a kind of carrying that the cleft did not reward. But she would sit with me in the corridor outside our alcove when I was small, and she would hold her hand out flat against the wall, and we would wait. The sifters would come. They always came. They crawled over her fingers with their mandibles working, and she would watch them with an attention that I did not then understand, an attention that was not fascination exactly but something more like listening.

*They know something*, she would say, in the low voice that was her only voice. And then she would tap the wall twice—*watch your footing*—and carry me home.

She died in the long stillness, the one seven years ago when the air sat for nine days and the fumes pooled and three of the lower galleries went bad before the draft returned. She was not in the galleries that went bad. She was in our alcove, and the air there was thin but breathable, and she died anyway. The clinic said it was her lungs, which had never been strong since she came up from the lowlands, which had been working too hard for too long in air that gives less than it should.

I think she knew the stillness was coming before the foremen called halt. I think the sifters knew. I think she watched them leave the upper corridors and she understood what it meant, and she did not go down to the freight elevators because the freight elevators were already full of people who had not been watching, and she went home instead, and she sat on the floor of our alcove with her hand on the wall, and she listened to the mountain breathe its shallowest, and then she stopped breathing too.

I do not say this to make you sad. I say this because it is how I learned what the sifters are for.

---

I am thirty-four. I have been in Gharhet for thirty-one of those years, which is long enough that the lowlands are a story my mother told and not a memory I carry. My skin is the mineral-grey of the old-timers. My fingernails have the copper tinge at the edges that the assay office has stopped testing for. My lungs are not what they were—whose are?—but they are better than most, because I work the upper galleries, where the air moves more, and because I learned early that the sifters go where the air is good.

My job, if you need one, is maintenance. I walk the pipes. I walk the cables. I walk the conduit and the condensate returns and the copper tubing that collects the brine seeps, and I note what needs noting and I fix what can be fixed with the tools on my belt and I flag what cannot. It is not a prestigious position. The miners get the wages and the danger and the stories. The smelter workers get the heat and the fumes and the stipend for respiratory complaints. I get the corridors, which suits me, because the corridors are where the sifters are.

And the sifters are where my attention is, though I do not tell people this, because the people of Gharhet do not think of the sifters as something that requires attention. They think of them the way you think of dust—inevitable, present, something to be brushed off surfaces when it accumulates, something that gets into the machinery and causes problems, something that is simply part of the texture of living here. They do not count them. They do not track where they cluster and where they do not. They do not notice that sometimes, in the deep corridors near the old drifts, the sifters move in a direction that has nothing to do with warmth or carbon dioxide or any chemical signal the assay office has isolated.

I notice.

---

Let me tell you what I know.

The sifters have patterns. This is not surprising—anything that moves has patterns. But their patterns are not the patterns of creatures responding to simple stimuli. They are not only going toward warmth. They are not only going toward carbon dioxide. They are not only going toward the condensate returns and the mineral seeps, though they go to all of those places, in numbers that seem to exceed what the seeps should support.

They also go toward the places where the rock is about to shift.

I want to be careful here. I want to be precise. I am not saying the sifters predict the rock. I am not saying they have some supernatural sense for the mountain's moods. I am saying that I have walked the corridors for eleven years now, and I have kept count in my own way—not with tallies but with my hands on the walls and my eyes on the sifters—and in that time I have noticed that the sifters begin to cluster in a gallery two or three days before the rock in that gallery constricts or cracks or settles. Not every time. But often enough that it is not chance. Often enough that I have begun to trust it.

I have also noticed that when the sifters leave a place entirely—not just moving from one wall to another, but abandoning a corridor completely—the air in that corridor goes bad within hours. This is the superstition, the practical observation that the miners have elevated to principle. But what the miners do not notice, because they are not in the corridors at the right hours, is that the sifters also leave the places where the rock is about to open. They abandon a gallery two days before a new fracture splits the stone, and I do not think they are fleeing the danger. I think they are following something else. I think the thing they are following is the same thing that feeds this system, the thing the naturalist could not name, the thing that makes the sifters too numerous and the cave-dwellers too large and the whole improbable ecology of this cleft possible.

I think it is in the rock.

Not the rock generally—this rock, this particular young mountain with its glassy edges and its cinnabar veins and its fractures that open and close like a body breathing. I think there is something in the stone that the sifters can sense and I cannot, something that moves through the rock the way water moves through soil, something that precedes the fractures and the settling and the new openings, something that the mountain does when it is about to change. And I think the sifters follow it. And I think if I could learn to follow it too, I would know this place in a way that no assay map or engineer's report could capture.

This is what I am trying to do. This is why I walk the corridors in the hour before the shift change, when the machinery breathes its shallowest and the mountain makes its small confessions. I am trying to learn what the sifters already know.

---

There is a gallery in the east wall, up past the fourth ledge, where the rock has been stable for as long as anyone can remember. The miners call it the cathedral, because it is wider than most of the spaces in the cleft—almost ten paces across—and because the ceiling arches up in a way that feels, I am told, like the inside of a building made for something other than industry. I have never been in a cathedral. I have never been in any building made for something other than industry. But I have been in the gallery, and I understand the name. The space feels like it is holding its breath. The stone is pale grey, almost white, and the single bulb at the entrance casts shadows that go back and back into the dark.

The sifters love this gallery. They coat the walls in slow-moving layers, their rust-red and lead-grey and copper-blue bodies catching the light in a way that makes the stone look like it is shifting, like the whole wall is alive and breathing and thinking about moving. I go there sometimes when I cannot sleep, which is often, because the hum of the cables is not enough to quiet my mind, and the quiet of the lowlands is a threat I never have to face. I sit on the floor with my back against the wall and my hands flat on the stone, and I watch the sifters, and I try to feel whatever it is they are feeling.

I cannot feel it. The stone is cold and still. The mountain does not speak to me in any language I can understand. But the sifters move, and I watch, and sometimes—rarely, but sometimes—I can sense a change in the quality of the dimness, a difference in the way the air sits in the space, a shift that is not temperature or carbon dioxide or anything the sensors measure but that is something, some faint signal that I am almost receiving, almost translating, almost bringing into the range of my too-human senses.

My mother would have understood. My mother, who held her hand flat against the wall and listened with an attention that was not fascination but something more like listening. My mother, who went home instead of to the freight elevators when the stillness came, as if she knew that the elevators were not the answer, as if she understood that the mountain was doing something that required a different kind of response.

I do not know what she heard. I do not know what the sifters hear. I know only that there is something to hear, and that I am here, and that I am staying, and that the stone is cold against my palms, and that the sifters are moving in their slow mineral clouds across every surface, and that I am trying to learn.

---

They ask me sometimes—visitors, new arrivals, the ones who look soft and pink and have not yet developed the scaling on their hands—they ask me why I stay. They ask it the way you ask any question in the cleft, quietly, with their voice pitched to travel only as far as my ears and no further. They expect me to say the wages, or the ore, or because I have no other option, and any of those answers would be true enough. The wages are good. The ore is still giving. I do not have another option that calls to me with any force.

But the real reason is the sifters. The real reason is the gallery in the east wall where the stone breathes its pale breath and the sifters move like a living map of something I cannot yet read. The real reason is that I am thirty-four years old and I have spent thirty-one of them in a cleft that does not want me, and somewhere in that time I stopped wanting to be anywhere else, and I started wanting to understand this place instead, and understanding is the kind of thing that takes longer than a lifetime, but I am here for as much of a lifetime as the mountain will give me.

My hands are on the wall. The sifters are clicking softly in the dark. The machinery hums its lullaby, and the rock shifts a millimeter, and I am here, and I am still here, and I am listening.
