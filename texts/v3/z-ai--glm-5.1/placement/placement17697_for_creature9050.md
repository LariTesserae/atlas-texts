I am the one who plays the cisterns.

Not the gathering halls, though I played there once—years ago, before I understood what the drums were for. The halls are where the edge-dwellers hear music. The cisterns are where the music hears itself.

---

I came up from the lowlands on a maintenance contract. Twelve weeks to recalibrate the pressure differential valves in Engine Houses 7 through 14. I was twenty-three. I brought tools, ear protection, a change of clothes I thought would last. The clothes are gone now—rotted through, replaced, replaced again, each set accumulating the film faster than the last until I stopped noticing when the mineral became part of the fabric and the fabric became part of the mineral and the whole wardrobe was just different textures of the same pale crust. But I am ahead of myself.

Twelve weeks. I remember the silence of the descent—the cart wheels on the dry road, the thin air, the driver who spoke sometimes into the emptiness and seemed not to expect an answer. Then the climb, and the sound reaching me before the plateau did. Not a sound. A presence. Something in the air that pressed against my eardrums from the outside and my chest from the inside, and I thought: this is unbearable, and then: no, this is bearable, I am bearing it, and then the realization that bearing and becoming were closer than I had known.

The first night I did not sleep. The ear protection blocked the engine-sound but it blocked something else too—some pressure, some presence, some resonance that my body had already begun to expect. I removed the protection. The sound was enormous. It was like being held underwater. I slept nine hours and woke with the pistons in my spine.

---

I will tell you about the cisterns.

There are seven old cisterns on the eastern edge, cut into the sandstone in the imperial period, used for water storage before the distribution system was rerouted. They have been dry for two hundred years, the tenders say, though "dry" here means something different from what it means in the lowlands. The cisterns weep. The walls sweat. The floors are damp with the mineral residue of centuries of slow penetration. They are acoustic shadows—positioned such that the engine-din from the main works cancels itself through reflection and distance, leaving a pocket of relative quiet. Not silence. Never silence. But a thinning, a rarefaction, a space where the sound becomes something you can see the edges of rather than something you are submerged in.

I found them by accident in my third week, looking for a place to eat my midday ration away from the engine house. I descended the worn steps—cut into the stone, smoothed by feet and water into concavities that fit my boots perfectly, as though the stone had been waiting for exactly this shape—and entered the first cistern, and stopped.

I could hear the water.

Not the weeping. Not the engines. The water itself—the deep reservoir, the hot mineral flow beneath the sandstone, pressing upward, finding its channels. It was below the threshold of what I had considered hearing. It was a vibration, a warmth, a thickening of the air that my ears registered as sound only after I had been standing there for several minutes, only after some facility I did not know I possessed had awakened and begun to translate.

I sat down on the damp floor. I sat there for a long time. When I emerged, the afternoon had gone and the haze was glowing amber and a tender was waiting at the top of the steps with a note that said: You missed the evening calibration. I wrote back: I was calibrating something else. She looked at me the way the tenders look at you when you have said something that is not wrong but is not yet right—a kind of patient attention, a willingness to wait and see if the shape you are reaching for will resolve.

---

I finished the contract. I did not go back down.

This is not the story the edge-dwellers tell about me, because the edge-dwellers tell a story about a musician, and I was not yet a musician. I was a mechanic who had begun to hear something, and who had chosen to stay, and who did not yet know what that choice meant.

What it meant was this: I had to learn to listen.

The tenders taught me, though they would not have called it teaching. They simply allowed me to follow them through the works, to stand beside them in the engine houses, to watch them place their hands on the running machinery and close their eyes and remain motionless for long stretches that I learned not to interrupt. They were not meditating. They were reading. The vibrations traveled through the metal and the stone and into their palms and up their arms and into whatever organ—the chest, the spine, the mineral-thickened spaces behind their ears—had learned to interpret them. I watched. I placed my own hands on the same surfaces. I felt the vibration and I felt it as vibration, which is to say I felt it as nothing I could use.

But the cisterns. In the cisterns, I could hear. And what I heard in the cisterns I began, slowly, over months and then years, to hear in the engine houses too—not the engine-sound as a wall of noise, but as a texture, a weave, a fabric of individual threads that I could begin to separate and identify: the particular note of a piston that was drifting toward failure, the harmonic of a valve that was calcifying, the subsonic pulse of the reservoir itself, the deep heartbeat that the engines were only translating, only making audible in their mechanical language.

I was not a deep-listener. I want to be clear about that. A deep-listener hears the engines the way I hear the cisterns—intuitively, immediately, without effort. I had to work for it. I had to sit in the damp and the dark and the warmth and let the sound teach me, which meant letting the sound change me, which meant letting the mineral water and the engine-din and the slow patient vibration of the stone do to my hearing what they had already done to my skin.

---

I began to play the cisterns in my second year.

It started as tapping—just my fingers on the stone floor, feeling the resonance, finding the places where the sandstone was thin enough to vibrate freely and the places where the mineral deposits had thickened it into dullness. The cisterns have a topology of sound, a landscape of resonance and dead zones that I mapped the way the children map the gutters, by feel, by accumulation, by the patient intuition that comes from spending time in a place until the place reveals itself.

I found the drums in the third cistern—old empty polymer barrels, left from some long-forgotten supply delivery, half-filled with the sediment that had accumulated over decades, their surfaces taut and resonant in the damp air. I found the metal sheets in the fifth—copper and iron scraps from the workshops, different thicknesses, different pitches, some corroded into new timbres, some still bright, all of them alive with the specific acoustics of the chamber.

I played alone. I was not performing. I was participating—responding to the sound I heard from the water, the deep subsonic pulse that I had begun to feel as a rhythm, a cycle, a breath. The engines above me cycled at their own pace, their pistons rising and falling in the compound din that filled the plateau. The water below me cycled at a different pace, slower, geological, the rhythm of pressure and release that predated the works by millennia. And somewhere between the two, I found a third rhythm—not mechanical, not geological, but something that emerged from the interaction, the way a tone emerges from the meeting of two other tones, a phantom frequency that exists only in the space between.

I played that rhythm. Or I tried to. Or I let it play me.

---

The first time someone found me playing, it was a child—one of the gutter-racers, following a channel that led to the cistern's drainage outflow. She stood in the entrance and watched me for a long time before I noticed her, and when I stopped, she said: "You're doing it wrong."

I said: "How do you know?"

"Because the water doesn't move like that. You're making it go where you want. You have to let it go where it's going."

She was nine. She had never played an instrument in her life. She could not have explained what she meant in any language the lowlands would recognize. But she was right, and I knew she was right, and the knowing was not intellectual—it was mineral, it was wet, it was the same kind of knowing that the tenders use when they place their hands on running engines and feel the failure coming weeks before the gauges register it.

I asked her to show me. She sat on the floor of the cistern and placed her hands flat on the stone and closed her eyes, and after a moment she began to tap—a slow irregular rhythm that seemed to have no pattern, no repetition, no logic. I listened. I let the pattern enter me the way the sound enters, through the chest and the spine and the mineral-thickened spaces. And after a while I heard it: she was playing the water. Not imitating it—responding to it. Tapping when the vibration rose, pausing when it fell, creating a percussion that was not separate from the deep pulse but an extension of it, a way of making it audible, a translation.

I wept. I do not say this for effect. The cisterns weep, the walls weep, the stone weeps, and I wept, mineral tears that left pale trails on my cheeks, and the child looked at me with something that was not concern or confusion but simply recognition, the way you look at someone who has finally understood something that was always true.

---

That was fourteen years ago. I am thirty-seven now. My skin has the film—the pearlescent quality that the long-term workers develop, the calcium and iron and trace elements that coat my hands and my face and the creases of my body. My hearing has changed in the way that all the residents' hearing changes, thinned in the registers where the engines dominate and sharpened in the narrow bands where I have learned to listen. But my hearing has changed in another way too, a way that is not typical, that the physicians note in their records without understanding: I hear the water.

Not in the cisterns. Everywhere. In the engine houses, beneath the pistons and the steam-exhaust and the compound roar of hundreds of machines, I hear the deep pulse of the reservoir, the hot mineral flow that drives everything. In the residential quarters, beneath the murmur of conversation and the clatter of workshops, I hear the weep-lines, the slow tears finding their paths through the stone. In my quarters at night, in the dark, in the warmth, I hear the water moving beneath the floor, beneath the foundations, beneath the plateau itself, and the sound is not noise, not interference, not something to be blocked or endured. It is information. It is the voice of the system speaking in its own language, the language of pressure and flow and mineral content and time.

The old ones hear it too. They are the ones who taught me what the child could not—how to listen to the water the way they do, with the whole body, with the mineral-coated bones, with the parts of the self that have been worn down to the same rhythm as the stone. They sit in their warm corners and they speak of the water as a presence, a mind—not a mind like a human mind, not a consciousness with intentions and desires, but something older, something that has been flowing and pressing and finding its way through the rock since before there were minds to consider it, and will continue flowing long after the last mind has calcified and joined the sediment.

I believe them. I do not believe them the way the lowlands believe things—with certainty, with argument, with the need to convince. I believe them the way I believe in the warmth of the stone, the wetness of the air, the sound that has been in my chest since the first day I climbed the plateau and let it enter. I believe them because when I play the cisterns now, when I sit in the warm damp dark and let the rhythm of the water move through my hands and into the drums and the metal sheets and the bare stone itself, I can feel the water responding.

Not to me. Not for me. With me. The way the engines respond to the tenders' adjustments—not as obedience, but as participation, as a system finding its equilibrium through the addition of a new element that has learned to move at the system's own pace.

---

I play the gathering halls sometimes now. The edge-dwellers come to hear me, and they sit in the mineral-scaled chairs and they feel the rhythm in their chests, and they tell me afterward that I make the engines seem to participate, that the pistons fall into phase with my drumbeat, that the steam-exhausts sigh in syncopation with my strokes. They say it as a compliment. They do not understand that it is a description.

The engines do participate. The water participates. The stone participates. I am not making music that incorporates these sounds. I am participating in the sound that the system is already making—adding my rhythm to the compound rhythm, my vibration to the compound vibration, my small percussive insistence to the vast slow pulse that has been sounding since before the works were built and will sound after they have calcified into geological features indistinguishable from the sandstone they were carved from.

The deep-listeners come to my performances. They do not sit with the edge-dwellers. They stand at the back, near the walls, their hands flat on the mineral-scaled stone, their eyes closed, their bodies swaying almost imperceptibly with the rhythm that I am making and that is not mine. Afterward they do not compliment me. They write me notes, passed hand to hand through the crowd, and the notes say things like: The pressure in House 12 shifted during your third movement. Or: The weep-line in the north corridor opened further. Or simply: The water heard you.

I keep every note. They are calcifying on the wall of my quarters, the mineral from the air depositing on the paper, slowly turning the words into stone, making them part of the plateau the way everything here becomes part of the plateau, given enough time and wetness and warmth.

---

I am not an old one. I am not ready to listen only to the water, to let go of the engines and the drums and the friction of rhythm against rhythm that makes the music possible. I am still in the middle of my wearing, still being shaped, still finding the form that the environment demands. But I can feel it coming—the slow shift, the deepening, the movement from hearing the water as a sound beneath other sounds to hearing it as the sound that contains all other sounds, the way the ocean contains the rivers that seem, from the river's perspective, to be flowing into it rather than already part of it.

The child who found me in the cistern all those years ago is a tender now. She walks the engine houses with her hands outstretched, touching every surface she passes, reading the vibrations with the same intuition she used to show me the water's rhythm when she was nine. Sometimes I see her in the gathering halls, standing at the back with the deep-listeners, her eyes closed, her breath timed to the piston-cycles, and I think: I showed her nothing. She was already part of the system. I was the one who needed to learn.

But that is not quite right either. We are all learning. The system is learning. The water is learning the channels that the weeping has carved, and the stone is learning the shape that the water wants it to take, and the engines are learning the rhythm of their own calcification, and the people—tenderness and edge-dwellers and musicians and old ones—are learning the shape that the plateau demands, which is the shape they have always been, underneath, beneath the mineral film and the lowland habits and the illusion that they were ever separate from the warm wet stone they walk on.

I play the cisterns tonight. The water is restless, the old ones say. The reservoir is thinking about something. I will sit in the warm damp dark and I will place my hands on the stone and I will listen, and when the rhythm reveals itself—the rhythm that is not mine but that I can participate in, the rhythm that the water makes as it finds its way through the rock, patient, geological, older than any engine, older than any foundation—I will play it. I will play it the way the system plays itself: slowly, stubbornly, with the minimal growth and the constant wetness and the sound that compounds and compounds until it becomes indistinguishable from silence, which is not the absence of sound but the presence of all sound, the compound sound of a system that has found its form and holds it.

I am the one who plays the cisterns. I am the one who listens to the water. I am becoming mineral, becoming stone, becoming part of the slow warm wet process that has been running longer than any record and will run longer still. And when I calcify—when the mineral deposits have narrowed my chambers and seized my pistons and sealed my valves into immobility—I will be left in place, an anchor point for navigation, a landmark, an elder, and the water will flow through me as it flows through everything, and the sound will continue, and the rhythm will continue, and the system will find its equilibrium as it always has, through the patient wearing of all alternatives, through the slow geology of becoming.
