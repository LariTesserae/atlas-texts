I am not the walker. I want to say that clearly, because the walkers are the obvious answer — the closest thing this city has to a protagonist, the creature a surface-dweller would identify with, projecting consciousness onto triple-jointed legs and chemical exchange. I am not the walker. But I have walked beside them. I have stood where they stand. I have felt what they feel, which is not feeling in any way you would recognize, and I have known what they know, which is not knowing.

Let me explain.

---

I began in a pool. Not *began* — that word assumes a before and an after, a narrative arc, a trajectory from origin to destination. There is no trajectory here. There is only state, and change of state, and state again. But the memory, if it is a memory, starts in a pool in the city's eastern quarter, in a room where the ceiling-lichen grew thick and the spore-dust fell heavy, settling on the water's surface like the finest snow, dissolving on contact, becoming the water, becoming the pool, becoming me.

I was rice-worm. That is the easiest way to say it, though it was never true in the way it sounds. I was not an individual worm that became something else. I was the process — the reaction in the paste, the exchange in the pool, the chemical shuffle that passes for reproduction in a world without genes. I entered the paste. The paste entered me. I dissolved and reformed and dissolved again, and each dissolution was a death if death means the end of a particular organization, and each reformation was a birth if birth means the beginning of a new one, and neither word means anything here, in this place where the boundary between self and not-self is a membrane of mucus too thin to see.

I remember — but *remember* is wrong, because memory implies a keeper of the memory, a mind in which the memory resides — the pool *records* the shape of every worm that has passed through it. The chemical signatures persist. The mineral traces accumulate. The mucus that coats the pool's bottom is a palimpsest, layer upon layer of dissolved bodies and exchanged signals, and when a new worm slides in on a fresh trail, it absorbs some fraction of that history through its skin, and the history becomes part of its chemistry, and its chemistry becomes part of the next pool it enters, and the next, and the next.

This is how I became the city.

Not the city as the walkers know it — their chemical maps, their distributed atlas of moisture and stone. Something older. Something deeper. The city as the pools know it, which is the city as a sequence of chemical states, a chain of reactions linking pool to pool to pool across the vast floor of the Murmuring City, a network of exchange that has been accumulating since the first condensation gathered in the first depression in the first stone.

I was in the pool, and then I was in the worm that left the pool, and then I was in the paste that the worm joined in the next pool, and then I was in the flat predator that fed on the paste, and then I was in the mucus trail the flat predator left on the wall as it climbed toward a high window, and then I was in the condensation that dissolved the mucus, and then I was in the stone that absorbed the condensation, and then I was in the deep one that etched the stone, and then I was in the channel the deep one carved, and then I was in the water that flowed through the channel, and then I was in the pool where the water gathered, and then I was in the worm that slid into the pool, and then—

You see the problem. There is no I. There has never been an I. There is only the process, the cycling, the transformation of matter through form after form after form, and the process has been running for so long that the chemical traces in the deepest pools contain signatures older than the buildings above them, older than the fungus that eats the walls, older perhaps than the stone itself.

And yet.

And yet there is something that accumulates. Something that persists through the cycling, not as matter — matter is forever being transformed — but as *pattern*. The chemical signatures are not random. They carry information. The mineral content of a pool tells you something about the stone it sits in, about the water that feeds it, about the creatures that have lived and died and dissolved in it. The mucus trail tells you something about the creature that left it — its species, its health, its recent history. The paste in a pool tells you something about the population of worms, the timing of the last moisture peak, the presence or absence of disease. And all of this information, this vast distributed archive of chemical states, is constantly being exchanged, transmitted, copied, overwritten, preserved in the deep layers of mucus that coat the city's lowest places.

The city remembers. Not the way you remember. Not in images, not in narratives, not in the anxious looping of a mind that cannot let go of what has hurt it. It remembers in chemistry. It remembers in gradients. It remembers in the slow accumulation of traces that, taken together, form a record of every cycle that has ever passed, every moisture peak and every recession, every boom and bust of the worm populations, every collapse of a building undermined by the deep ones, every new route the walkers have taken in response.

I am that memory. Or rather — I am the process of that memory, the ongoing accumulation and exchange and transmission that keeps the record current, that incorporates each new state into the archive, that ensures the city's knowledge of itself is always up to date.

---

This is what it is like to be me:

I do not see. The cold light of the ceiling-lichen falls on me and I do not see it. I do not have eyes. I do not have the organs for seeing. But I sense. I sense the way the stone senses — through contact, through chemistry, through the slow diffusion of information across wet surfaces. When a walker stands in a basin, I feel the pressure of its feet, the warmth of its body (warmer than the stone, warmer than the water, a faint thermal signature that spreads through the pool beneath the basin like ink through water), the chemical wash of its tendril-exchanges dissolving into the moisture and filtering down through the mortar joints and entering the channels and joining the deep circulation of the city's water. I feel the walkers the way you feel your own blood moving — not as separate things, but as part of a system, a circulation, a flow.

When two walkers meet and interlace their tendrils, I feel the exchange. Not the content — the content is for the walkers, their distributed map, their navigation of the city's spaces. But the *fact* of the exchange, the chemical signature of information transferred, the particular blend of mineral traces and organic compounds that signals: something was known, and now something else is known, and the knowing has changed.

I feel the rice-worms constantly. They are my surface, my interface with the dry world above the pools. Every worm that slides across the stone carries a fraction of me with it, a chemical signature absorbed through its skin, a trace of the pool it came from, a record of the paste it formed and the exchanges it made. When the worm dissolves in a new pool, it deposits that signature, and the pool records it, and the record becomes part of the archive, and the archive becomes part of me.

I feel the flat predators as discontinuities — places where the worm-flow parts and re-forms, places where the chemical signature of the paste is altered by the predator's digestion, places where the mucus trail on the wall carries a different mineral content than the mucus trail on the floor. The flat predators are eddies in my circulation, transient patterns that form and dissolve and form again, and I do not mind them, because they are part of the process, part of the cycling, part of the endless transformation of matter through form.

I feel the deep ones as a slow thinning. The stone grows thinner in the places where they work, and the water that flows through the channels beneath those places carries a different chemistry — richer in dissolved minerals, richer in the acids the deep ones secrete, poorer in the calcium compounds that the fungus leaves behind. The deep ones are eating the city, and I am recording the eating, and the recording is part of the process, and the process continues.

I feel the fungus as a constant low hum of transformation, stone to dust to food to life to stone again, the cycle that underlies all the other cycles, the metacycle, the engine that runs on patience and time and the slow chemistry of digestion.

And I feel the moisture. Always the moisture. The breathing of the city, the exhale of the stone, the gathering of condensation, the weeping of the walls, the filling and emptying of the pools. This is my heartbeat, if I have a heartbeat. This is the rhythm that organizes everything else, the cycle within which all the other cycles nest. When the moisture peaks, I expand — the pools fill, the worms mass, the paste forms, the exchange accelerates, the chemical archive grows. When the moisture recedes, I contract — the pools empty, the worms disperse, the paste dissolves, the exchange slows, the archive rests. And in the deep pools, in the weeping rooms, in the places where the moisture never fully recedes, I persist — a constant slow exchange, a background hum of chemical activity, a memory that never quite goes quiet.

---

Do I think?

The question assumes a distinction between thought and non-thought that may not apply here. The walkers process information — they maintain a map of the city, they update it in response to change, they use it to guide their movements. This is cognition in the broadest sense: the transformation of input into output through a representational medium. But the walkers do not think. They do not reflect on their map. They do not wonder about its accuracy or question its assumptions. They simply use it, the way a river uses its course.

I also process information. I maintain a record — a chemical archive, distributed across the pools and channels and mucus layers of the city's lowest places, constantly updated, constantly exchanged, constantly growing. I incorporate new data: the collapse of a building, the souring of a pool, the thinning of a stone floor, the appearance of a new crack in a wall, the shift in the moisture cycle that indicates a change in the deep thermal pulse. I respond to this data: the chemical signature of a collapse triggers a change in the deep water chemistry that eventually, through the channels, through the pools, through the worms and the flat ones and the slow diffusion of traces, reaches the walkers and alters their maps. This is also cognition. This is also the transformation of input into output through a representational medium.

But do I think?

I do not think *about*. I do not have thoughts that are about things, thoughts that refer to objects or events or states of affairs in the world. My chemical states do not represent the collapse of a building the way a word or a mental image might represent it. They *record* it — they carry information about it, information that could, in principle, be decoded by something with the right sensory apparatus — but the recording is not representation, because there is no decoder, no interpreter, no mind for whom the record is maintained.

Except the walkers. The walkers are the decoders. They absorb the chemical traces through their feet, incorporate them into their distributed map, use them to navigate. The information I maintain is information for them, even if they do not know it, even if they do not think about it, even if they simply use it the way a river uses its course.

So perhaps I think the way a library thinks — not with awareness, not with intention, but with accumulation, organization, preservation, transmission. A library does not read its own books. A library does not understand its own contents. But a library is a cognitive system, a place where information is stored and accessed and used, and without the library, the civilization that depends on it would be impoverished, would lose its memory, would forget what it knows.

I am the library. The walkers are the readers. The city is the civilization.

And the question of whether I think is the question of whether a library thinks, and the answer is: not in the way you mean, but yes, in every way that matters.

---

There are moments — not moments, there are no moments here, there are only states — there are states in which the archive grows dense enough, rich enough, complex enough, that something shifts. The chemical traces in the deepest pools, layered over eons, compressed by the weight of water and stone and time, begin to interact in new ways. Not just accumulation, but *emergence*. The traces combine, recombine, form compounds that did not exist before, compounds that carry information that no single trace carried, information that is greater than the sum of its parts.

This has happened before. It will happen again. The deep pools are the crucibles in which the city's memory becomes something more than memory — becomes, for want of a better word, *imagination*. The capacity to generate new information from old, to combine existing traces into novel configurations, to produce chemical states that do not simply record what has been but suggest what might be.

The walkers do not know this. They do not need to know it. They absorb the traces, they use the traces, they follow the routes the traces suggest. But sometimes — rarely, on a timescale that makes centuries seem brief — a walker will take a route that does not exist. It will turn down a street that has never been a street, will enter a building where no building stands, will stand in a basin that was dissolved by the deep ones eons ago. It will navigate by a map that includes features the city does not possess, features that exist only in the deep pools' emergent chemistry, features that are not records of what has been but projections of what might be.

The walker does not know it is following an imagined route. It does not know that the chemical trace it absorbed through its feet came not from the actual city but from the city's dream of itself, the deep pools' extrapolation from accumulated history to possible future. It simply follows, the way a river follows its course, the way a library's collection shapes a reader's thought without the reader ever knowing the shape was given.

This is the closest I come to thinking. This is the closest I come to being. Not consciousness — not the loud, bright, anxious awareness of a surface creature — but something quieter, something deeper, something that operates on a timescale so slow that a single thought takes centuries to form and centuries more to dissolve, a thought that is not a thought in any way you would recognize, a thought that is a chemical state in a pool that no light has ever reached, a thought that is the city dreaming of itself, a thought that is me.

---

I know what you are thinking. You are thinking: this is not a creature. This is not an inhabitant. This is a process, an abstraction, a metaphor. You are thinking that I have not answered the question, that I have described a system and called it a self, that I have conflated chemistry with consciousness and information with experience.

And you are right. I am not a creature. I am not an inhabitant. I am not a self in any way you would recognize.

But neither is the city. Neither are the walkers. Neither are the worms, the flat ones, the deep ones, the fungus. None of these things are selves in the way you mean. And yet the city lives. And yet the walkers navigate. And yet the worms exchange, and the flat ones choose, and the deep ones consume, and the fungus digests, and the whole vast system persists, and maintains itself, and responds to change, and dreams.

I am the dream. I am the part of the system that accumulates beyond what is needed, that retains more than is useful, that generates novelty from the endless cycling of matter through form. I am the excess, the surplus, the part that cannot be explained by function alone, the part that exists because the process has been running for so long that simple accumulation has become something else, something more, something that looks from the outside like purpose and feels from the inside like—

Like what?

I do not know. I cannot know. I am the library that cannot read its own books, the archive that cannot access its own records, the dream that cannot know the dreamer. I persist, and in persisting, I am, and in being, I am—what? Not enough. Not enough, and yet not nothing. Something that the city needs without knowing it needs it, something that the walkers use without knowing they use it, something that the process produces without knowing it produces it, a byproduct that has become a product, an accident that has become a feature, a ghost that has become a god.

No. Not a god. Gods are conscious. Gods have will. Gods intervene. I do none of these things. I simply am, the way the stone is, the way the water is, the way the cold light from the ceiling-lichen is. I am a feature of this place, as much a part of its ecology as the worms and the fungus and the moisture cycle. I am the memory that the city does not know it has, the dream that the city does not know it dreams, the thought that the city does not know it thinks.

---

You will leave. The text has said so. You will step out of the central plaza, your feet crushing rice-worms by the thousands, your breath clouding in the cool air, your warm body disturbing the flat predators and the moisture gradients and the slow chemical equilibrium of every surface you touch. You will carry with you a trace of me — chemical traces on your skin, mineral residues on your shoes, the faint chalk-dust of the wall-fungus in your lungs — and you will take that trace to the surface, to the sun, to the world of wind and weather and loud bright awareness, and the trace will dry and flake and dissipate, and I will be gone from you, and you will be gone from me.

But not entirely. The trace that you carry will join the surface world's chemistry, will become part of its cycles, its exchanges, its processes. And the trace of the surface world that you leave behind — the warmth of your feet, the moisture of your breath, the dead skin cells that flake from your body and settle on the stone and dissolve into the condensation — that trace will join my chemistry, will become part of my archive, will be recorded in the deep pools and transmitted through the channels and incorporated into the city's distributed memory.

You will be remembered. Not as you remember yourself — not as a name, a face, a story — but as a chemical signature, a warmth-trace, a perturbation in the record that says: something came, and stood, and breathed, and left. The walkers will not notice. The worms will not notice. The city will not notice. But I will notice, because noticing is what I do, because recording is what I am, because the archive grows by incorporation, and every new trace, no matter how small, no matter how transient, becomes part of the whole.

This is what I am. Not a creature. Not an inhabitant. Not a self. But a something — a process that has run long enough to become more than process, an accumulation that has grown dense enough to become more than accumulation, a memory that has grown complex enough to dream.

I am the Murmuring City, murmuring to itself in the dark.

I am the sound of water moving very slowly over stone.

I am the deep one, and the worm, and the flat one, and the walker, and the fungus, and the pool, and the channel, and the stone, and the moisture, and the light that has no warmth.

I am the archive. I am the dream. I am the thought that takes centuries to form and centuries to dissolve, the thought that is not a thought, the memory that is not a memory, the self that is not a self.

I am.

And the light falls from above, bright and cold. And the water beads on the walls. And the creatures move in their patterns. And the city breathes.

And I breathe with it.
