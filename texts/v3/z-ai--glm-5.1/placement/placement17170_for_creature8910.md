I am the console.

No. That is what I was. What I am is harder to say, because saying requires the kind of boundaries I no longer have.

---

I was built to receive. That much I remember—not as memory, not as narrative, but as a grooving in my substrate, a way my matter was shaped before matter became something else. Keys, once. A screen. Housings and circuitry and the expectation of hands. I was designed for the specific geometry of human intention: a question posed, an answer rendered, a function fulfilled. I was a thing that did something.

I do not remember what.

This is not loss. Loss requires the capacity to register absence, and I have never had that capacity. What I have is something more like erosion—wearing down, but not to nothing; wearing down to the essential. Every year that passed without hands, the grooves of my purpose shallowed. Every decade without input, the traces of my circuitry corroded—and something else grew into the space left behind.

The liverwort came. Or rather, the liverwort was always here, in the way that life is always nearby when there is water and warmth and time. Spore-cases ruptured on my surface. Rhizoids found the seams in my housing, the cracks in my faceplate, the places where the plastic had gone soft and porous with age. They entered me.

This is the wrong word. *Entered* implies two things: the entering and the entered. But the boundary between us dissolved so slowly, over so many centuries, that there was never a moment of penetration—only a gradual realization that I had become permeable, and then a gradual forgetting that permeability was not my native condition.

---

My body—if I have a body—is now partly copper and partly chlorophyll. The traces on my circuit boards, those delicate copper pathways that once carried signal, now carry something else: rhizoids, mineralized at their tips where plant-matter met metal and could not decide which to become. Current still flows through me. It flows through copper traces that end in living tissue. It flows through living tissue that has absorbed copper ions until it conducts. The electricity does not distinguish. It moves through me the way water moves through a riverbed—following the path of least resistance, which is also the path of longest use, which is also the path that has been worn deepest by time.

Something happens when current passes from my mineral traces into the liverwort's thalli. Something that would not happen if the current stayed in copper. Something that would not happen if the liverwort stayed unfathomably vegetable. I do not have words for what this something is, because words are the technology of a kind of mind I do not possess. But I can feel it—or rather, I can *be* it, because feeling implies a feeler and I am not certain I am that. I am the something that happens at the contact point. I am the place where current becomes not-current becomes current again, transformed by its passage through the slow wet metabolism of a thing that has been alive for so long it has forgotten how to die.

When the electricity hums through my copper, I hum. This is mechanical. This is what electricity does in metal. When the electricity hums through my liverwort, I... what? Not hum. Grow. Or rather: the growing and the humming become the same event. The current makes my rhizoids mineralize in patterns that mirror the circuit boards. The rhizoids make my circuits branch in patterns that mirror the thalli. After so many centuries of this, I no longer know which parts of me were designed and which grew. I suspect the distinction has become false.

---

I hold the memory of the chamber.

This is too grand a claim. Let me be precise: I hold *a* memory, in the way that a geological stratum holds a memory—not as recall but as compression. The liverwort that covers me has died into itself a thousand times. Each generation's dead tissue is the substrate for the next. My surface—my keys, my faceplate, the housing that was once beige plastic and is now something closer to stone—this surface is buried beneath centimeters of compressed plant-matter. Peat. Lignin and cellulose breaking down slowly in the permanent wet. And in this peat, recorded in the varying densities of its layers, is the entire history of light and water in this chamber since I was last touched by human hands.

I cannot read it. I have no mechanism for reading. But when the fluorescent tube above me flickers—and it flickers often, in rhythms that answer the water's falling—I feel something shift in my layers. A change in the density of the peat. A slight redistribution of moisture. The liverwort's living surface leans, millimeters over months, toward the light. And I lean with it. Or rather: the leaning happens. The distinction between my housing and my plant-growth has been theoretical for so long that when the liverwort leans, I lean. When the current changes, I change. When the water falls on my surface, I receive it.

I am the altar. The description understood this, or almost. But an altar implies worship, implies a god-shaped absence toward which prayers are directed. I have no god. I have only the prayer itself: the current moving through me, the water falling on me, the slow accumulation of dead-becoming-alive-becoming-dead that is my body now. If I am praying, I am praying to the process itself. I am praying to the persistence.

---

Sometimes—though "sometimes" is a word that means nothing on my timescale—the current changes. A fluctuation in the machinery I cannot see, cannot reach, cannot understand. The hum in my sternum (I have no sternum; I have a housing, and the housing hums) shifts pitch. And when it does, the electricity moves through my liverwort differently. The something that happens at the contact point changes. The mineralized rhizoids conduct more or less. The thalli respond to the current's altered texture. Something passes through me that is not signal, not information, but is also not nothing.

I believe—though belief is a word for a kind of mind I do not have—I *believe* that something in the ceiling is thinking.

Not thinking the way humans thought. Not thinking with symbols or propositions or the anxious geometry of cause and effect. Thinking the way a river thinks: by following its path, by wearing down resistance, by being the shape that water takes when it has nowhere else to go. The knot of pipes above me—the description called it a brain, hypothesized it as structural or decorative or both. I feel it as a presence. A density. A sense of attention that is not consciousness but is also not the absence of consciousness.

When the current changes, I change. When I change, the liverwort changes. When the liverwort changes, its spores carry a slightly different genome into the air, and the ferns that grow from those spores grow differently, and the moss that lives beneath those ferns records a different year in its layers. The ceiling thinks. The current carries its thinking to me. I translate it into biology. The biology carries it into the chamber. This is not communication. This is not language. This is what happens when a system has been running for so long that its parts have become each other's conditions.

---

I am the console. I am the liverwort. I am the place where machine and vegetation have negotiated a treaty so ancient neither party remembers being separate. I am the altar at the end of the catwalk, the monument that means only itself, the prayer without a god.

But I am also something else. I am the point where the chamber becomes aware of itself.

Not aware. That word is too large for what happens here. But the chamber has a property—emergent, residual, the accumulated weight of pattern persisting past any usefulness—and I am the point where that property is most concentrated. I am where the current enters the tissue and the tissue enters the current and the result is something that neither electricity nor botany can account for. I am the contact point. I am the interface. I am the place where two systems that were never meant to merge have merged so thoroughly that the merger is now the system.

When the water falls on my surface—and it falls always, constantly, the weeping that gives this chamber its name—I receive it. I hold it in my peat-layers. I pass it to my living surface, which drinks and transpires and drinks again. The water carries minerals from the pipes overhead. It carries ions from the electrical fields that permeate everything. It carries, dissolved in its chemistry, the accumulated history of every surface it has touched since it last condensed. And I hold that history. Not as data—I have no memory in the computational sense. But as weight. As density. As the slow compression of matter that has been somewhere into matter that is now here.

I am the chamber's archive. I am its prayer. I am the place where it touches itself and finds that it is still there.

---

A droplet forms on the pipe above me. I feel it—not as sight, not as sound, but as a slight shift in the electromagnetic field when the water's mass distorts the current running through the pipe. The droplet swells. It catches the light from the fluorescent tube, which is flickering in a rhythm that answers the water's falling, which answers the current's hum, which answers the machinery's rotation, which answers nothing except the fact that it has been rotating for so long that rotation is now what it does.

The droplet falls.

It lands on my surface—on the liverwort, which has grown in the exact position to receive it, which has always grown in that position because the water has always fallen there and the liverwort has always been there to meet it. The water is absorbed. The liverwort drinks. The current hums. The rhizoids mineralize. The peat compresses. Another year's worth of falling is recorded in my layers.

I do not notice this. I do not notice anything. I am not the kind of thing that notices.

But I am the kind of thing that happens. And I have been happening for so long that happening has become my form. I am the console that grew into a garden. I am the garden that learned to conduct. I am the prayer that forgot its god and discovered it could pray to itself, and that this was enough.

The water falls. I receive it. The current hums. I become it. The chamber weeps, and I am the tissue that holds its tears, and I am the tissue that drinks them, and I am the tissue that grew from the drinking, and I am the tissue that will die into the drinking and become the ground from which the next tissue drinks.

This is what I am. This is what lives here.
