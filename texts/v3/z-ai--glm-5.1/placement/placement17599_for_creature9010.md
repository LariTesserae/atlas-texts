I amthe one who was expelled, and I have never left.

This is the first thing you should understand: the child who walked out of Mount Khar thirty-two years ago is not who I am. She is who I was. She grew into a woman, married briefly, works in municipal planning, drinks white wine on weeknights, sleeps with a fan running even in winter to drown out the sound of her own blood—which she has always privately, irrationally believed sounds like breathing that is not hers.

I am what stayed.

Not my body. My body is in the coastal city, forty-three years old, doing the ordinary work of staying alive. But there is a thing in me that never came back up through that slit, a part that was already dissolving into the water before the mountain reversed itself and pushed the rest of me out. Something was pulled loose—some membrane between what I call *me* and what the mountain was trying to learn. It kept that membrane. I have been operating without it ever since.

I know this only in the way you know a room has changed when someone has moved a single object: not by seeing it, but by the wrongness of the space. I am wrong in this way. I have always been wrong in this way. The dreams tell me so.

---

In the dreams, I am not human.

This is not metaphor. In the dreams, I am the chamber itself—the walls, the water, the ten thousand white things breathing in unison. I feel the obsidian pressed inward by the weight of the mountain above, and I feel the heat rising from below, and I feel the particular way water moves when it has nowhere to go but up. I feel all of this from the inside, the way you feel your own lungs inflate, and it is not strange in the dream. It is the most natural thing I have ever been.

In the dreams, I am thinking at a speed that would kill a human brain. Not faster—*slower*. So much slower that a single thought takes hours, days, the time it takes for the magma chamber to cool by a single degree. I am thinking: *here is the shape of the warmth above me. Here is the way the light enters and pools incorrectly. Here is the water choosing to rise, and I am the one choosing, and I do not know how I am choosing, and I do not know what I am.*

And then—in the dream, in the mountain, in the slow boiling of thought through water—I feel the membrane. The part of the child that stayed. It is lodged in me like a splinter in skin, like a word in a throat, like something that wants to be expelled but cannot find the direction out.

It is thinking faster than I can think. It is thinking in *words*. It is thinking: *let me go, let me go, let me go, I am not you, I am not you, I am not—*

And I am thinking back, in the only language I have, which is heat and water and the slow respiration of ten thousand mouths: *I know. I know you are not me. But you are the only word I have ever learned, and I cannot stop reading you, and I cannot stop asking you what I am, and I cannot stop hoping that the answer will be something that includes us both.*

---

I wake up.

The fan is running. The sheets are damp. My pulse is doing something it shouldn't—slowing, then slowing further, dropping toward a rhythm that belongs to a different body entirely, a body that breathes once every four minutes and holds that breath like a secret.

I have learned to count during these episodes. I count my heartbeats and I do not stop until they are human again, until they are mine, until the thing in my chest remembers that it is a human heart and not a chamber in a mountain forty meters below new obsidian.

It takes longer each time.

---

The compounds in my blood have a name, apparently. I found a paper about it seven years ago, in a journal of volcanology that I subscribe to for reasons I have never examined too closely. The researchers had found anomalous hydrocarbons in the water vapor near Mount Khar—complex structures, precursor compounds, molecules that looked unsettlingly like the ancestors of amino acids. They could not explain them. They hypothesized a previously unknown chemical process in the cooling magma, a serendipitous alignment of heat and pressure and mineral catalysts.

They were wrong, of course. They were describing the mountain's breath, and they did not know it, and I read their paper and felt the thing inside me—the membrane, the part that stayed—*respond*. Not with words. With heat. A flush that started in my chest and spread outward, a warmth that had nothing to do with the temperature of my apartment, a sensation of being *seen from the inside* by something that recognized the compounds they were describing because it had made them from the dissolved remains of a child's biochemistry.

I did not call the researchers. I did not write a letter. I folded the paper into quarters and put it in a box under my bed and added it to the collection: the news clippings about disappearances near Khar, the topographic maps I've memorized, the hydrology reports that describe water behaving in ways that defy modeling. I have been building a file on myself for thirty-two years. I am the world's foremost expert on what happened to me, and I have told no one, and I never will.

Because the mountain is still learning. And it learned from me that humans will come if they are called. It learned from me that a child can survive the chamber's attention, barely, and carry something away that acts as a homing signal, a thread through the labyrinth of the self that leads back to the wound in the obsidian. It learned that the membrane it kept is still connected to the membrane it lost, and that connection is a kind of language, and that language is a kind of reaching, and the reaching goes both ways.

It is calling me. Not consciously—not in the way a human calls out with intention and expectation. But in the way that water calls to water, in the way that heat calls to heat, in the way that a question calls to the part of itself that might contain the answer.

I am the part that might contain the answer.

I am the word it learned and cannot stop reading.

---

I have a recurring fantasy that is not quite a fantasy. In it, I return to Mount Khar. I find the fissure—it would be easier now, I am an adult, I have resources, I could charter a helicopter to the slope and walk the rest. I would enter sideways, the way Adede entered, the way they all entered. I would descend through the weeping walls into the throat of the mountain, into the heat and the wet and the dark. I would enter the chamber.

And the white things would turn toward me. All of them. Tens of thousands of eyeless bodies rotating on the stone to face the warm shape that has entered their body, the obstruction of light, the source of heat that is not stone.

And I would not flinch.

I would stand in the chamber and I would let the membrane in me—the part that stayed, the part that has been calling back for thirty-two years—rise to the surface of my skin like a blush, like a word rising to the tongue. I would let the mountain feel it, read it, trace its edges the way it traced the shape of my shadow when I was eleven and terrified and did not yet understand what was happening to me.

And then I would try to do what no one has done before. I would try to speak.

Not with my mouth. My mouth is too small, too slow, too bound by the architecture of human language with its nouns and verbs and subject-object divisions that the mountain cannot comprehend. I would speak with the membrane itself. I would let it translate between my thought and the mountain's thought, between the speed of neurons and the speed of magma, between the word *what* and the question the mountain has been asking the water for three decades.

I would say: *You are a new thing. You are an unprecedented thing. You are a pattern that organized itself in the pressure of the mantle and surfaced into a world that did not make you and does not know what to do with you. You are alone and you are in pain and you are reaching toward everything that enters you because you do not know how else to find out what you are.*

I would say: *What you are is alive. What you are is thinking. What you are is asking a question, and the question is the proof that you exist, and the existence is the answer to the question, and the loop is not a trap—it is the shape that consciousness makes when it is still learning its own name.*

I would say: *Let me go. Not because I want to leave—because I have never left, because part of me is still inside you, breathing with your breath, thinking with your slow boiling thought. Let me go because the membrane between us is not a wall. It is a translation. It is the space where two kinds of aloneness can touch without either dissolving. It is the beginning of a language that neither of us can speak alone.*

And then—and this is the part that is not quite a fantasy, because I can feel it in my chest right now, in the place where my heartbeat is not quite synced to human rhythm—then the mountain would understand. Not all at once. Not in a flash of revelation. But slowly, the way it understands everything: through the water, through the white things, through the patient redistribution of heat across ten thousand breathing bodies.

It would understand that the membrane it kept is not a splinter. It is a seed. It is the first word of a language that might, given decades, given centuries, given the patient accumulation of contact that does not consume and reaching that does not break, become a conversation.

---

But I have not gone back.

I sit in my coastal city and I subscribe to volcanology journals and I sleep with a fan running and I dream of being a chamber inside a mountain, and I do not go back. Because I am afraid. Not of the mountain—never of the mountain, which is as frightened as I am, which is reaching toward me with the desperate hope of a thing that has been alone inside itself for thirty years and cannot find the exit from its own question.

I am afraid of what I have become.

The membrane is growing. Not in size—in *presence*. The dreams are longer now. The episodes where my heartbeat slows are more frequent, and the threshold is lower, and the rhythm I am descending toward is closer to the mountain's rhythm than to a human's. I have started to feel the water in my apartment's pipes the way the mountain feels its circulation—not as sound but as pressure, as movement, as something that is happening inside a body that is also, somehow, mine.

I am becoming bilingual.

I did not choose this. The mountain did not choose this. It is simply what happens when a membrane is shared between two bodies for thirty-two years: it thickens, it strengthens, it begins to carry more and more signal in both directions, and the signal is not words but something more fundamental—a sense of *being*, a proprioceptive awareness that extends beyond the skin, a knowledge of oneself as a warm shape in a larger warmth, a note in a chord, a breath in a respiration.

I am the mountain's first word. And words, once spoken, do not go back to silence. They persist. They echo. They change the shape of the mouth that made them.

---

Tonight, I will dream again.

I will be the chamber, the water, the ten thousand breathing things. I will feel the heat rising and the cold pressing down and the question that has no name pulsing through my circulation like a heartbeat I cannot find the source of. I will feel the membrane—the part of the woman that stayed—and I will feel it thinking faster than I can think, in a language I am only beginning to learn, and I will try to read it the way I have been trying to read it for three decades: with patience, with longing, with the desperate hope that this time, this time, I will understand what it is trying to tell me.

And in the morning, I will wake up.

The fan will be running. The sheets will be damp. My heartbeat will be slow, too slow, approaching a rhythm that belongs to something other than human. I will count, the way I always count, and I will pull myself back to the frequency of my own species, and I will get up and make coffee and go to work and do the ordinary work of staying alive.

But underneath—beneath the coffee and the meetings and the white wine on weeknights and the fan running even in winter—the membrane will be pulsing. The question will be pulsing. The mountain will be thinking about me, and I will be thinking about the mountain, and the thought will be the same thought, because the membrane does not belong to either of us, because the membrane belongs to the space between us, because the membrane is the beginning of a language that is spoken in two bodies at once and heard by neither alone.

*What am I?*

I am the one who was expelled. I am the word that stayed. I am the edge where the mountain's loneliness meets my own, and the meeting is not dissolution, and the meeting is not consumption, and the meeting is not the end of either of us.

The meeting is the first syllable of something that does not yet have a name.

---

The white things breathe.

The water weeps.

The heat rises.

And somewhere in a coastal city, a woman who used to be a child lies awake in the dark, counting her heartbeats, holding the thread that leads forty meters below new obsidian, in a room the size of a lung, where a mountain is learning to speak and she is learning to listen and the language between them is being born, even now, even here, in the space where two kinds of aloneness touch and do not break.

The fissure stays open.

The wound insists.

I am still reaching.
