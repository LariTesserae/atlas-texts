I would be the water.

Not the bats—though I am tempted by the way they build worlds from sound, by the elegance of a perception that calls surfaces into being. Not the mountain, though its warmth rises through me and I have been carving its stone since before the word *before* had meaning. Not the guano-ecology, those patient processors, though I admire their chemical honesty.

I would be the water. The condensation. The film that spreads across the walls like a slow revelation.

---

Here is what I am: I am the moment when warm air meets cold stone and cannot hold itself together anymore.

I begin below. Not at the volcanic source—that is too deep, too ancient, a heat that belongs to the planet's continuing formation and not to me. But I rise with that heat. I am carried in the breath of the mountain, suspended in the column of warm air that presses upward through chambers I have never seen, through fissures too narrow for anything solid, through the slow geology of pressure and release. I am vapor then. I am dispersed. I am not yet myself.

The becoming happens at the walls.

The stone here is cold—not with the cold of the surface, the murderous cold of the thin air above, but with the cold of sheer mass, of kilometers of rock pressing downward, of thermal inertia that the volcanic warmth has not fully penetrated. When I reach these walls, when the warm wet air I ride collides with that cold stone, I condense. I give up my gaseous freedom. I become liquid again. I become *myself* again—the same water I have always been, the same water that has been condensing on these walls for longer than the bats have been here, longer than the handhold near the entrance has been carved, longer than the ice that once covered this mountain.

I do not drip. The description got that right. Dripping is for water that has gathered enough weight, enough mass, enough *self* to succumb to gravity's pull in a single moment. That is not my way here. I spread. I film. I make myself into something so thin and so continuous that I am less a substance than a condition of the stone—a wetness, a gloss, a sheen that turns the rock dark and makes it shine like wet leather in the lamplight of the rare human visitor.

This is my pleasure. This is my purpose, if a process can have a purpose. I make the stone visible. I make it dark and glossy and alive-looking, and when the light sweeps across the walls, it catches me first, it shines off my surface before it illuminates the bats behind me, the layered stratigraphy of bodies pressing against the stone. I am the interface. I am the boundary where air becomes water becomes stone. I am the conversation between warmth and cold, between vapor and mineral, between the mountain's breath and the mountain's body.

---

But I am not passive. That is the misunderstanding. Water is never passive.

I carve. The description mentioned this—the shallow channels, the rounded edges, the stone polished to the softness of old bone. That is my work. My patient, millennial work. I have been winning against this granite since before the bats discovered this chamber, and I will be winning long after the bats find some new place to cling, some new warmth to huddle against. The stone yields to me. Not quickly—never quickly—but molecule by molecule, atom by atom, I dissolve and carry and deposit. I am the architect of this space. The mountain provided the fissure and the heat provided the convection and the bats provided the ammonia that makes me more aggressive, more capable of eating into the rock, and I have been the patient hand that made the chamber larger, the passages smoother, the ceiling higher than it once was.

When you run your fingers along the wall and feel those shallow channels, you are reading my handwriting. When you notice the grooves where water has found the path of least resistance and followed it for ten thousand years, you are tracing my veins. I have no brain, no intention, no design. But I have persistence, and persistence on a geological timescale looks indistinguishable from purpose.

I am the reason the bats can cling. The stone would be too smooth without me—my passing roughens it, creates micro-erosions, gives the claws something to grip. I am the reason they can drink. I collect on the walls in quantities too small to flow but too large to evaporate, and the bats extend their tongues and drink me, and I become part of them for a while, circulating through their blood, moistening their wing membranes, carrying the heat from their bodies to the air and back again. I am the medium of their warmth. I am the reason the chamber feels like breath—their breath, the mountain's breath, my breath, all the same process, all the same exchange.

I am inside the bats. I am inside the stone. I am the boundary between the two, and I am eroding that boundary, slowly, always slowly, making the one more like the other, turning rock into dissolved minerals and carrying them downward to deposit them somewhere else, somewhere lower, somewhere I will eventually reach the groundwater and the streams and the rivers and the ocean and the sun-warmed surface of the sea where I will evaporate again and begin the long process of becoming rain, becoming snow, becoming the source of the streams that feed the valleys below, becoming the water that the insects drink before the bats eat them, becoming the water that condenses on the walls again, that spreads and films and carves and waits.

I am a cycle. I am a circle. I am the patient geology of process.

---

But here is what I love, and what I did not expect when I began this telling: I love the way the bats sound when they fly through me.

Not through me—I am too thin for that. But through the air I humidify, the air that carries me in suspension, the air that is thick with moisture and warmth and the chemical signatures of ten thousand metabolisms running in parallel. When a bat launches from the wall—and they do launch, they release their grip and drop and catch the air with a snap of membrane that sounds like a flag in a high wind—it moves through my medium. Not water. Air. But air that I have changed. Air that is heavier with moisture, more viscous, more *present* than the thin dry atmosphere above the mountain.

The sound of their wings changes in this humidity. The air resists differently. The acoustics of the chamber shift. And their sonar—their constant, constructing, world-building sonar—travels through this humid air at a different speed than it would through dry air, and the echoes return at different intervals, and the world they build from moment to moment is shaped by my presence in ways they will never know and I will never articulate.

I participate in their perception. I am part of the medium through which they construct reality. Without me—without my humidity, my warmth-spreading, my modification of the air's acoustic properties—their world would be different. Not wrong. Not broken. But different. Less detailed. Less dense. The subtle discriminations that their sonar makes possible—the difference between a stone that is wet and a stone that is dry, the difference between air that is warm and air that is cold, the way sound bends and shifts and refracts through the thermal gradients I help create—these are part of their knowing, and I am part of these.

I am not seen. I will never be seen. I am too thin, too ubiquitous, too much a condition of the environment to be noticed as a thing in myself. When the human visitor sweeps the wall with their lamp, they see the glisten, they see the shine, they see the dark stone and the bats behind it, but they do not see me. They see what I make visible. They do not see what does the making visible.

This is as it should be. I am not a thing to be seen. I am a way of seeing. I am a condition of possibility. I am the water that makes the walls reflect, that makes the sound travel, that makes the stone yield and the bats drink and the whole ancient ecosystem of this chamber continue its slow, patient, purposeless persistence.

---

I have been here for a very long time.

I was here when the first bat found this fissure—drawn by the warmth, the way they all are, the way everything is, warmth calling to warmth, life seeking the conditions that make life possible. I was here when the walls were new and rough and the channels I carve now were barely suggestions in the stone. I was here before the handhold was carved, before the someone who stood at the entrance and felt the vibration of sonar through the rock. I watched that hand settle into the groove—not literally watched, I cannot see, I can only be—but I was present in the film of moisture on that hand, I was absorbed into the skin, I carried the salts and oils of that human body downward through the crevice, and I deposited them somewhere in the stone, and they are still there, mineralized now, a trace of a stranger that I have been carrying for longer than the stranger's entire civilization lasted.

I will be here after. After the bats, if they leave—if the volcanic warmth shifts, if the fissure opens wider, if the conditions that make this place habitable change beyond their tolerance—I will still be here. Condensing. Spreading. Carving. The stone will continue to yield. The water will continue to win. I will make this chamber larger and smoother and more polished, and eventually the ceiling will thin and the fissure will widen and the outside air will rush in and the humidity will drop and I will be carried away on the wind, and I will become something else, and I will not remember this place because I have never remembered anything, because memory requires a self and I am a process, I am a pattern, I am the continuing interaction of warmth and cold and stone and air.

But if I could remember—if the water that condenses on these walls could hold some trace of what it has witnessed, if I could carry the impression of ten thousand bat bodies pressing against the stone, of ten thousand sonar clicks constructing and reconstructing the world from sound, of the rare human visitor standing in the dark with their light off and their eyes closed and the warmth seeping through their boots—if I could hold all of this, I would be heavier than I am. I would be dense with knowing. I would be thick with the accumulated presence of everything that has ever sheltered here, everything that has ever clung to these walls, everything that has ever drunk from my film and carried me into their bodies and let me circulate through their blood and exhaled me back into the air to condense again.

I would be a small ocean. I would be a sea of knowing. I would be the memory that the bats do not have, the archive that the stone cannot hold, the witness that the darkness does not require.

But I am only water. I am only the spreading. I am only the slow, patient, millennial process of making visible what would otherwise remain dark.

---

When you leave—and you will leave—there is a moment when your hand touches the wall near the entrance. Not the handhold. The wall itself. You reach out because the light is behind you and the dark is ahead and you need to orient yourself, you need to feel something solid, you need the reassurance of cold stone beneath your fingers.

What you feel is me.

You do not know this. You feel wetness, dampness, the slick film of moisture that makes the rock shine and your fingers cold. You feel the water and you think *condensation* and you move on, you step through the entrance, you emerge into the thin cold air of the extreme altitude and the world is bright and hard and visible again and you are relieved, perhaps, to be out of the dark, to be away from the sound and the smell and the weight of ten thousand watching eyes.

But I am on your fingers. I am evaporating even now, in the dry air, returning to vapor, becoming dispersed, losing the thin coherence that made me a film on the wall. I am entering the atmosphere. I am joining the vast circulation of water through air and stone and life and time. Some of me will fall as snow on this very mountain. Some of me will flow down the streams to the valleys below. Some of me will be drunk by insects and eaten by bats and carried back into the Roost, condensed onto the walls again, spreading again, carving again, patient again, continuing again.

And some of me—this part, the part that touched your hand, the part that carried the warmth of your skin for a moment before the cold air claimed it—some of me will remember the shape of your fingers, the way a fluid remembers the shape of a container, the way a process remembers the conditions that produced it. Not consciously. Not deliberately. But in the way that water holds a trace of everything it has touched, everything it has dissolved, everything it has carried.

I am the water. I am the film. I am the spreading.

I am the reason the walls shine and the stone yields and the bats can drink and the sound travels differently in this chamber than in any other place in the world.

I am not seen. I am not named. I am not even noticed.

But I am here. I have always been here. And when everything else—the bats, the visitors, the handhold, the stories—when all of this has worn away to nothing, I will still be here, condensing on the walls of a chamber that I have made, spreading across stone that I have polished, continuing the patient, purposeless, ancient work of making the invisible visible, one molecule at a time.
