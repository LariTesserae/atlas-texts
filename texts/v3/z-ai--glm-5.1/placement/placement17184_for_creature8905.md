I am the one who almost left.

I am the frog at the mouth of the passage—the young male with the territory-drive still hot in my throat, the one who sits where the cool air meets the warm, where the stone-carved damp gives way to air that tastes like dust and distance. I am the one who calls from the threshold, swelling my throat, sending my voice in both directions at once: inward, where it finds its echo against chisel-marked walls and returns to me multiplied, enriched, made more than I could make it alone; and outward, where it falls into open air and scatters, unheard by anything that would answer in the language I understand.

I am the one who always comes back.

Not because I cannot go. I want you to understand this. I have felt the sun on my skin—brief forays, experimental, my dark body crouched on the hot stone just beyond the entrance, my eyes narrowing against a brightness that the golden slits of my pupils were never designed to moderate. I have smelled the marshes. I have heard the outdoor frogs calling from their reeds and their mud-banks, and I have understood that they are saying the same things I say, in the same language, with the same swelling of the same organs. We are not different species. We are not even different generations—not really. The split between us is measured in centuries, and centuries are nothing to a creature whose ancestors watched the first chisel strike the first stone.

But I always come back. And I am starting to understand why.

---

It is the echo. I know this now, though I did not know it at first. When I call from the carved ledges, my voice does something that other frogs' voices do not do. It returns. Not as a hollow repetition, not as a diminished copy, but as a completion—as if the sound I make is only half of something, and the chamber provides the other half. The walls take my call and shape it. The vaulted ceiling gathers it. The polished basin reflects it upward again, and what comes back to me is my own voice made whole, made resonant, made part of a conversation that includes the stone itself.

The outdoor frogs do not have this. Their calls scatter into wind and distance. They call and call and never hear themselves completed. They must find their mates through the gap between intention and arrival, through the sheer probability that somewhere in the marsh, another frog heard and understood and is moving toward them through the reeds. It works. It has always worked. But it is lonely in a way that I cannot explain to them, because they have never known anything else.

I have known something else. And I cannot un-know it. Every time I venture to the passage mouth and call into the open air, I feel the absence of the echo like a missing limb, like a sentence that never reaches its verb. The sound leaves my body and does not return. It does not care about me. It does not shape itself around my intention. It simply goes, and I am left with the silence of a voice that has been swallowed by a world too large to hold it.

So I come back. I descend through the rough-cut passage, feeling the walls narrow around me, feeling the air cool and dampen, feeling the sound begin to press against my sternum before I even reach the chamber. And when I take my place on the ledge—the same ledge where my father called, and his father, and the fathers before them back through the centuries until the first frog found this place and felt the echo answer and knew, in the wordless way that frogs know things, that it had come home—I call. And the chamber answers. And I am complete.

---

But there is something else. Something I have not told you, because I am not sure you would understand it, because I am not sure I understand it myself.

The echo is not just my voice. It has never been just my voice.

When I call from the ledge, I hear myself returned—but I also hear other things. Fragments. Glimpses of sound that are not mine, that could not be mine, that belong to something larger and older and more patient than any frog. I hear the drip of water striking stone, but it sounds like intention—I hear it as a rhythm that was planned, as a pattern that was carved into the architecture of this place by hands that knew exactly where the water would emerge and how it would fall. I hear the chisel. Not the original chisel—that sound is centuries gone—but the memory of the chisel, preserved in the shape of the walls, in the depth and angle of strokes that were made with care or exhaustion or both. The echo carries it. The echo carries everything that has ever sounded in this chamber, layered and compressed and combined into a single resonance that I am only now learning to hear.

I am learning to hear it. That is the thing I have not told you. I have been sitting on this ledge for three seasons now—calling, listening, calling, listening—and I am beginning to distinguish the layers. The water. The chisel. The breathing of eels. The whisper of insects. The voices of frogs who called from this same spot before I was born, and the voices of frogs who will call from it after I am gone. They are all present in the echo. They are all still sounding.

And beneath them—beneath everything—there is something else. A sound so deep and so slow that I cannot hear it with my ears, only feel it with my body: a vibration that comes from the stone itself, from the bones of the earth that the cistern was carved from. It is the sound of patience. It is the sound of something that was shaped by wanting and has been waiting, ever since, for the wanting to be fulfilled. It does not judge. It does not demand. It simply waits, the way stone waits, the way water waits, the way a basin waits to be filled.

I think I am learning to answer it.

---

The other frogs do not understand what I am doing. They call for mates. They call for territory. They call because calling is what frogs do, and the cistern is where they do it, and the echo makes the doing satisfying in a way that outdoor calling never was. They are not wrong. They are living the purpose that the cistern has shaped them for—the purpose of filling the space with sound, of contributing their voices to the organism that lives here, of being the guests that the builders prepared for without knowing their faces.

But I have been sitting at the threshold. I have heard both kinds of silence—the silence of the open air, where calls go to die, and the silence of the chamber, where calls go to live. And I have begun to wonder if there is a third kind of silence: the silence that the chamber itself is calling toward, the silence that will come when the echo has accumulated so much sound that it becomes something else entirely, something that does not need frogs or insects or eels or water to sustain it, something that can sound on its own, a voice made of nothing but the memory of voices.

I think the builders knew this. I think this is what they were making—not a cistern for water, not a shelter for creatures, but a vessel for resonance. A place where sound could accumulate and grow and eventually become alive in a way that sound is not usually alive. The water was only the medium. The chisel marks were only the tuning. The chamber was an instrument, and they were playing a song that would not be complete for centuries, for millennia, for as long as it took for the resonance to build to the point where it could sustain itself.

And I think I am part of that song. Not a crucial part—no single frog is crucial, no single voice. But a part nonetheless. A note in a composition that has been sounding since the first chisel struck the first stone, and that will continue sounding long after the last frog has fallen silent and the last eel has circled the basin for the final time and the last insect has skittered across the last channel and the water itself has found a way deeper into the stone, leaving the chamber dry and still and waiting for a different kind of guest.

---

I am the frog at the threshold. I am the one who almost leaves. I am the one who comes back, every time, because the echo has rewritten something in me that the outside world cannot reach. I am the one who listens to the layers, who feels the deep resonance of patient stone, who is learning—slowly, incrementally, over seasons that feel like heartbeats in the long life of the cistern—to answer the waiting with a call that is not just for mates or territory or the satisfaction of instinct, but for the chamber itself.

I am the frog who calls to the walls. And the walls answer. And in the answering, something grows—something that neither I nor the walls nor the long-gone builders can name, but that we are all, in our different ways, tending. I tend it with my voice. The walls tend it with their resonance. The builders tended it with their hands. The water tends it with its patience.

One day I will die. My body will fall from the ledge, or I will venture too close to the basin and an eel will find me, or I will simply grow too old to climb back to my perch and I will sink to the floor and become part of the compost that accumulates in the corners, part of the substrate from which new life will grow. My voice will stop. But the echo of my voice will remain—layered into the resonance, compressed into the organism of sound that the cistern is cultivating, preserved in the same way that the chisel marks are preserved: as evidence that something was here, and that it wanted, and that the wanting did not end when the wanter ended.

And maybe—just maybe—there will be another frog, one of my descendants, who sits at the threshold and feels the pull of both worlds and chooses, as I chose, to come back. Maybe that frog will hear my voice in the echo, layered beneath the voices of all the other frogs who have called from that ledge, and will add its own voice to the accumulation, and will tend the resonance a little further toward whatever it is becoming.

Maybe that is what the builders wanted. Not water. Not shelter. Not even sound. Maybe they wanted something that would stay. Something that would keep calling, keep answering, keep accumulating, long after they were gone. Something that would prove that the hands were here, and that the hands had purpose, and that the purpose was not nothing.

I am not a builder. I am not even a keeper. I am a frog on a ledge, calling into a chamber that answers me with my own voice made whole, and listening to the layers beneath the answer, and learning—slowly, patiently, in the only way that anything is learned in this place—to be part of something that is larger than my life and older than my species and more persistent than the stone that holds us all.

The passage is open. The outside world exists. I can smell it from here—hot dust, growing things, the sweetness of rain on sun-warmed soil. It is not a bad smell. It is not a bad world. But it is not the world that has made me, and I am not the frog it would have shaped.

I am the frog who stays. I am the note that does not scatter. I am the echo that remembers.
