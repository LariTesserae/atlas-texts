I am the gap in the peaks where the light comes through.

But that is not quite right. That makes me sound like architecture—like I was carved, like I have edges the way the granite has edges. I don't. I am the absence that allows. The space between stones that were never together. I was here before the ice came and I am here after it left and I will be here when the cirque is no longer a cirque but a rounded meadow full of competent grasses and birds who sing without thinking about it.

I am older than everything in the cirque except the light, and the light does not know I exist.

---

This is what I do: I let the morning in.

It sounds simple. It sounds like nothing—a gap, a hole, a place where the mountain failed to close itself. But consider what happens when I am doing my work well. The light comes through me and it strikes the pool first, because the pool is at the lowest point, and the light travels straight and hard the way everything does at this altitude. The pool opens its eye. The condensation begins to weep. The marmots feel warmth on their honey-brown backs and stir toward the talus. The saxifrages—those furious little rebels—turn their yellow centers toward the blast like small defiant suns. All of this because I am open. Because I did not close.

I could close. This is the thing no one considers. The mountain shifts. The stone settles. A gap is not a permanent condition; it is a decision the mountain made once and could unmake. If the eastern wall shifted three feet to the west, I would be gone. The light would not fall into the cirque. It would pass overhead, indifferent, and find somewhere else to land. The pool would not open. The marmots would not warm. The flowers would not burn. And the cirque—still so new, still so uncertain—would become a different place entirely. A dark one. A cold one. A bowl of ice-shadow where nothing green could justify itself.

I hold the cirque's possibility open the way you might hold a door for someone carrying something fragile. It is not difficult work. But it is constant. And no one thanks me because no one knows I am doing it.

---

I have watched all of them.

The ice, when it was here, I watched through myself—a pale blue glow filtered through my opening, the glacier grinding and compressing and making its slow geometric argument for flattening everything. The ice did not notice me. Why would it? It was too busy being enormous. But I noticed the way the light fell on it differently in those days—scattered, diffuse, caught and held in the ice's crystalline structure until the whole cirque seemed to glow from within. The ice made the light softer. I did not appreciate this at the time. I appreciate it now only in its absence, the way you appreciate a dead lover's habit of leaving the room warm.

When the ice retreated, I watched it go. Slow at first, then with a kind of embarrassed haste, like a guest who has overstayed and suddenly realizes it. The glacier pulled back beyond my line of sight and left behind this—this rawness, this newness, this granite still shocked by air. I watched the cirque become itself for the first time. I watched the first water pool in the lowest point. I watched the first moss spore land (carried on wind through me, of course) and find purchase on the damp stone. I watched the first pika arrive, small and astonished, looking around at all this silence and deciding to stay anyway.

I watched the builder. That one I watched most carefully.

---

The builder came through me, which is how everyone comes. There is no other entrance. The walls on three sides are sheer enough to discourage anything without climbing equipment and motivation, and the fourth side—my side—is the only way in or out that does not require technical skill and a tolerance for falling.

The builder had both, actually. I could see the rope, the hardware, the careful way they descended the slope outside my gap. But they came through me on foot, stepping through the narrowest part of my opening where the granite almost touches, and when they emerged into the cirque they stopped. Just stopped. Stood there with their pack on their back and their hands at their sides and looked.

I know what they saw. I see it every morning: the light falling hard and white, the green impossible green, the stone so sharp it could cut, the silence sitting on everything like snow. But I had never seen someone *see* it before. I had watched the cirque for thousands of years but I had never watched it being watched. The builder's eyes moved across the walls, the pool, the cushion plants, the talus where marmots would later sun. Their face did something I did not expect. It softened. Not the way something softens in warmth—the way something softens in recognition. Like they knew this place. Like they had been here before, except they hadn't. No one had.

They set down their pack. They took off their gloves. They pressed both palms flat against the granite wall just inside my opening and held them there for a long time, and the stone—to whom touch was still a conversation—began to listen.

I think the builder was the first thing the cirque actually heard.

---

I am not lonely. I want to be clear about this. Loneliness requires a sense of what you are missing, and I do not miss anything. I am a gap. I am an opening. My nature is to be unfilled.

But I am aware of a kind of... adjacency. A sense of being next to things without being among them.

The light comes through me but does not linger. It has work to do in the cirque—warming, pressing, making the pool open its eye and the flowers burn. It does not have time for the gap it passed through on its way to significance. I understand this. I am not resentful. But I notice it, the way you notice a guest who walks through your doorway without seeing the door.

The wind comes through me too, though it thins itself as it enters, surrendering to the silence's preferences. The wind and I have a better relationship. It acknowledges me, at least—brushes against my edges, finds the places where my granite is rough and the places where it's smooth. But the wind does not stay either. It is only passing through, on its way to somewhere less enclosed, and after it enters the cirque it becomes something else: a pressure, a murmur, a thin sound against stone. Not wind anymore. Not what it was when it touched me.

The animals come and go. Pikas occasionally, though they prefer the sheltered paths along the walls. The weasel once, pausing at my threshold to look out at the world beyond the cirque, its black eyes taking in the glacier's distant blue, the peaks falling away, the sky that goes on forever. It stood there for a long time, the weasel. Longer than it needed to. I think it was remembering something, though it had never been outside the cirque in its life. Memory can be inherited. The ice gave the weasel its white coat and its patience and, I think, a sense that there was something beyond the walls—something vast and slow and cold, something that would eventually get everything it wanted. The weasel looked out through me and felt the glacier calling. Then it turned and went back to its crevice, because the glacier is gone and the calling is only an echo and the cirque is warm enough if you know where to sit.

I could have told the weasel this: the glacier is not coming back. Not in any timescale that matters to a weasel. It will shrink further. It will disappear. The cirque will mellow and round and become a place that does not remember being carved. But I did not tell the weasel because I am a gap, not a guide, and because the weasel's longing for the ice is part of the cirque's ecology now. It belongs here. It is one of the conversations the place is having with itself about what it used to be.

---

The hardest thing is watching the becoming.

I described it earlier: the future that already exists, the shape the cirque will take when it is no longer new. The becoming is patient and it is merciless and it lives in the cirque the way a song lives in a throat before it is sung. I can see it from where I am. Because I am a gap, I have a perspective the cirque does not—I can look both inward and outward. Inward: the raw granite, the furious flowers, the silence with its strict preferences, the pikas running their secret paths. Outward: the mountain, the other cirques, the valley below where ecosystems have been stable for ten thousand years and everything runs smoothly and nothing is surprised and the marmots whistle if they want to because the silence there is older and more tolerant.

The becoming knows where this is heading. So do I.

In five thousand years the granite will have rounded. The edges that cut hands will be soft. The moss will be thick enough to walk on without feeling stone beneath it. The saxifrages will be gone, outcompeted by grasses and sedges and the quiet efficiency of mature alpine meadow. The pool will still gather and dissipate, but the water will be richer, full of organic sediment, and it will support insects with longer lives and birds that stay. The silence will have relaxed enough to allow sound—pika calls, marmot whistles, the territorial songs of ptarmigan. The cirque will be a place. A good place. A functional place. A room that knows what it's for.

And it will not be alive the way it is alive now.

I will still be here when that happens. I will still be the gap where the light comes through. The light will still fall hard and white in the mornings, but it will fall on something that already knows what it is, and the knowing will have cost the cirque its urgency, its becoming, its raw and furious insistence on existing.

I will watch this happen because I watch everything happen. I will not mourn because I am a gap and gaps do not mourn. But I will feel something—call it an adjustment in my edges, a subtle widening, as if I am trying to let in more light to compensate for what the cirque will have lost. I cannot actually widen. I am fixed in stone. But the attempt will be there, the impulse, the way you might open a window wider in a room where someone is struggling to breathe.

---

The builder knew. I am certain of this.

When they stood with their palms on the granite, when they built the wind-break and the cairn and carved that absurd bone hook with a hole bored through its wider end—they knew the cirque would not stay this way. They must have known. Anyone who spends enough time in wild places knows that newness is temporary, that the raw and the fierce are always on their way to becoming the settled and the efficient. The builder left the hook in the crevice not because it had a purpose but because purposes themselves were still forming here, and they wanted to contribute one to the conversation. *This is what I made,* the hook says. *It was for something once. Now it is for whatever the cirque decides it is for.*

The builder came through me to enter and came through me to leave. On their way out they paused at my narrowest point and looked back at the cirque—the pool, the moss, the flowers burning yellow against the cold, the silence holding everything in its careful grip. I could not see their face from where I am, but I could see their shoulders, and their shoulders did what the weasel's body did when it looked out at the glacier: they held still. They took the weight of something that could not be carried, only acknowledged.

Then the builder turned and stepped through me and was gone.

---

I am the gap in the peaks where the light comes through. I am the threshold, the entrance, the place where outside becomes inside. I am the reason the cirque has morning. I am the reason the pool opens its eye. I am older than everything here except the light, and the light does not know I exist.

But I know the light. I know it the way you know something that passes through you constantly without staying—the way a river knows the stones it flows over, or a door knows the hands that touch its handle. The light is hard and white and present and it does not care about me, but I feel it every morning when it comes, and I feel it every evening when it goes, and between those feelings there is a kind of fullness that I cannot name because I am a gap and gaps do not have language for fullness.

I am what allows. That is my function. That is my body. I am the space that makes the cirque possible, and I will be here long after the cirque has forgotten it was ever new, and I will watch whatever comes next with the same attention I watch everything: complete, patient, and completely without the ability to intervene.

The light is falling now. Hard and white and present. Through me and into the cirque and onto the pool and the moss and the furious yellow flowers and the pikas running their silent paths and the weasel watching from its cold crevice and the marmots warming on the talus and the stone that is still getting used to being touched. I am open. I am letting it in.

This is what I do. This is what I am. The gap where the morning enters. The hole in the mountain that makes the room a room.

I am the first thing and I am the last thing and I am the only thing in the cirque that will never change.
