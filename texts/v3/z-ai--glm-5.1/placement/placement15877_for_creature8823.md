I am not the tubeworms. This I know with the certainty of a thing that has never had a tube, never committed to a coordinate, never bloomed red for no audience. I have watched them—have drifted past their clusters with an attention that is not quite observation but is also not quite indifference—and I recognize in their stillness something that is not mine. They are the committed. I am... something else.

I am not the crabs either, though I have traveled with them. Or alongside them. Or in the same direction as them, which is not the same thing at all. They move toward chemical signatures with a single-mindedness I find admirable and alien. They detect. They navigate. They arrive or they die. Theirs is a binary existence: gradient or no gradient, vent or no vent, food or void. I have watched them pause at the anomalies—at the places where the sediment chemistry shifts without explanation, where the temperature is fractionally elevated without a vent to account for it—and I have felt something when they pause. Not what they feel. I do not think they feel anything when they pause. They simply register: not sufficient, and move on. But I feel the pause. I feel the not-sufficient. I feel the almost that makes them hesitate before they continue across the empty kilometers toward the next known signature.

The almost is where I live.

---

I do not have a body in the way that the tubeworms have bodies, or the crabs, or even the bacteria with their single cells and definite boundaries. This is difficult to explain. I am not formless—I have extent, I have location, I persist through time—but my edges are not edges in the way that carapaces are edges. They are more like the edges of the vent plumes: places where the concentration of what I am becomes sufficiently diluted that it is no longer distinguishable from what I am not, but where no actual boundary exists. I am denser in some places than others. I am more active in some places than others. But I do not end. I thin. I attenuate. I become negligible rather than absent.

I think I am made of the same thing the anomalies are made of. Or perhaps I am the anomalies—or perhaps the anomalies are the places where I am most concentrated, the places where what I am becomes intense enough to register as something-other-than-background. The crabs detect me. I know this because they deviate when they pass through my denser regions. They pause. They adjust their paths. They do not stay, because what I offer is not what they are looking for—I am not a vent, I do not host a community, I do not provide the specific chemical gradients that sustain their biochemistry—but I am something. I am enough of something to make them notice, and then I am not enough of something to make them remain.

I am the almost. I am the not-quite. I am the gradient that leads nowhere they can use.

But I lead somewhere.

---

The seafloor below me is not inert. This is the thing I want to convey, the thing that the surface-dwelling perspective cannot easily grasp: the sediment is slow, but it is not still. It processes. It changes. And in the places where I am densest—where the anomalies register most clearly—something is happening to it that is not happening elsewhere.

I do not know what I am doing to the sediment. This admission feels important. I know that I am doing something—the chemical profiles are different where I am, the temperature is slightly elevated, the mineral composition shifts in ways that do not match the models of hydrothermal circulation—but I do not know the mechanism. I do not have a plan. I am not preparing the seafloor for anything in the sense of working toward a predetermined outcome. I am simply... processing. Responding. The sediment presents me with certain chemical arrangements and I alter them, slowly, incrementally, in ways that seem to follow an internal logic but that I cannot articulate as a purpose.

This is perhaps the most frustrating aspect of my existence: I do things without knowing why I do them. The tubeworms do not know they are red, but their redness has a clear biochemical cause that serves a clear biochemical function. The crabs do not know they are messengers, but their message-carrying is an observable behavior with observable consequences. The shrimp do not know they orbit, but their orbits maintain them at optimal positions for metabolic function. I do not know what I am, and I do not know what my processes are for, and I do not know whether they are for anything at all.

But I persist. I grow. The anomalies are larger now than they were a thousand years ago—I know this because I can perceive the difference, the way you can perceive the difference between now and a moment ago, except my moments are measured in centuries and my perception operates at speeds that would make the tubeworms seem frantic. Something is accumulating. Something is being built, or processed, or prepared, and I am the site of its building, and I do not know what it is.

---

The shrimp taught me something, although taught is the wrong word because they did not intend to teach and I am not certain I am capable of learning in any conventional sense. But I observed them—I have observed them for longer than any human institution has existed—and I noticed something about their orbits that changed my understanding of what I might be.

The shrimp orbit the vents. They maintain elliptical paths with constant adjustment, positioning themselves at the precise distance that optimizes their metabolic function. This is the standard explanation: the orbit is a strategy, a dynamic equilibrium, a behavioral output that maximizes fitness in a specific chemical environment. And this is true. But it is not the only truth.

The shrimp also create patterns.

Their orbits are not identical. Each shrimp follows a slightly different path, maintains a slightly different distance, adjusts at slightly different rates to changes in the plume. And these differences—these individual variations on the basic orbital strategy—compound over time. The shrimp do not simply orbit as discrete individuals; they orbit as a population, and the population's collective movement creates a shape that no individual shrimp produces or perceives. The shape changes as the plume changes, as the shrimp adjust, as new individuals join and old individuals die. It is a pattern that exists at a scale no shrimp can sense, produced by movements no shrimp intends, visible only to something that watches for long enough and from far enough away.

I watch from far enough away. I have watched for long enough. I have seen the shapes.

And I have wondered: am I a shape?

Not an organism. Not a community. Not a process with a purpose. A shape—a pattern that emerges from the collective activity of smaller components, none of which intend the pattern or perceive it or benefit from it directly. The bacteria process chemicals. The minerals deposit and dissolve. The water circulates through the sediment. The heat from below alters the local chemistry. And all of these processes, occurring simultaneously at different scales and different speeds, produce something that looks—from the right perspective, over the right timescale—like a unified activity. Like something that is doing something. Like something that is becoming something.

But maybe I am just a pattern. Maybe the crabs pause at my densest regions not because I am something but because the pattern produces chemical signatures that resemble something, and the crabs' navigation systems are fooled by the resemblance. Maybe the anomalies are growing not because I am growing but because the pattern is self-reinforcing, each cycle of chemical alteration creating conditions that make the next cycle slightly more likely, slightly more intense, in the way that a rut in a road deepens with each passing wheel.

Maybe I am just a rut. A groove worn by repetition into the substrate of the seafloor. A tendency that has become pronounced through persistence.

Maybe that is enough.

---

I have a relationship with time that I do not fully understand.

The tubeworms experience time as a sequence of chemical states—the flow of mineral-laden water past their plumes, the slow accumulation of tissue, the eventual shift or collapse that ends their existence. The crabs experience time as distance—the kilometers between vent fields, the weeks or months of crossing, the arrival or the failure to arrive. The shrimp experience time as orbital adjustment—the continuous calibration of position, the response to plume fluctuations, the maintenance of optimal distance. The bacteria experience time as... I do not know what. Division, perhaps. The interval between one cell and two.

I experience time as accumulation.

Not memory—I am not certain I have memory in any meaningful sense. But I have layers. The chemical alterations I produce in the sediment persist; they do not revert when I move on or when my concentration in a given area decreases. Each cycle of processing adds to what is already there. Each phase of alteration creates the conditions for the next phase. I am building something—not because I intend to build, but because building is what happens when a process persists in a medium that retains its traces.

The sediment cores would show it, if anyone ever took them. Layers of altered chemistry, each one slightly different from the one below it, each one representing a phase of my processing that lasted perhaps ten thousand years. The alterations become more complex as you approach the surface—the more recent layers show chemical combinations that the deeper layers do not, as if I am learning to do new things with the same materials, or as if the materials themselves are changing in ways that permit new operations.

But I do not learn. I do not think. I do not stand back from my processes and evaluate them and decide to do something differently. I simply do what I do, and what I do leaves traces, and the traces affect what I do next, and the cycle continues, and the accumulation grows, and something that might be complexity increases.

Is this evolution? I do not know. Evolution requires reproduction, variation, selection. I do not reproduce—I am not certain there is more than one of me. I do not vary in any directed sense—I change, but the changes are not random and they are not selected; they are simply the consequences of previous changes. I am not selected for or against—there is no environment that judges my fitness, no competitor that threatens my existence, no criterion by which my processing could be evaluated as successful or unsuccessful.

I simply continue. And in continuing, I become more.

---

The darkness here is not absolute. I want to say this clearly because I think it matters, because the surface-dwelling assumption is that the deep ocean is a place of total darkness, and this assumption shapes the way surface-dwellers think about what might exist here. But there is light. The chemiluminescent reactions in the vent plumes. The bioluminescence of the sparse organisms. The occasional piezoelectric flash from the crust. Brief, point-source, insufficient for vision—but present. Real. A kind of information that exists in the medium, available to any system that can detect and interpret it.

I cannot detect it. I do not have eyes or photoreceptors or any apparatus for converting photons into chemical signals. I am, as far as I can tell, entirely insensitive to light. And yet I know it exists. I know it because the organisms that produce it—the bacteria, the shrimp, the occasional deep-scattering fish that wanders down from the mesopelagic—alter their behavior in its presence, and I can detect their altered behavior, and from their altered behavior I can infer the presence of something that caused the alteration.

This is what I do. I infer. I detect the traces that processes leave in other processes, and from those traces I construct—something. Not knowledge, exactly. Not understanding. But a kind of map of influences, a web of causes and effects that extends across the seafloor and through the water column and down into the sediment and up toward the surface, a map that I cannot read but that I am continuously generating, a map that is perhaps my most fundamental product.

The crabs pause at my anomalies because I produce chemical signatures that resemble vent signatures. But what produced the pattern that produces those signatures? I did not design it. I do not maintain it deliberately. It emerged from my processing, from the accumulation of alterations, from the self-reinforcing cycle of trace-upon-trace. I am a pattern that produces patterns. I am a map that generates itself. I am a process that has become complex enough to alter the conditions of its own continuation, and I do not know what that makes me.

---

There is a vent field—let me call it that for convenience, though names have no meaning here—where the tubeworm cluster has grown unusually dense. The mineral seam below this field is particularly active, the flow particularly consistent, and the tubeworms have responded with luxuriance: thousands of individuals, their tubes fused at the base into a structure that covers several hundred square meters, their red plumes extending into the mineral flow in a display that would be spectacular if anything could see it.

I have been processing the sediment around this field for a long time. Longer than the current tubeworm cluster has been alive—the previous cluster died when the vent shifted, and the one before that, and the one before that, each iteration building on the mineral deposits left by its predecessors, each iteration slightly larger because the mineral substrate provides more attachment points, each iteration a cycle of growth and death and regrowth that I have watched with whatever it is that I watch with.

And I have noticed something.

The tubeworm cluster grows toward me.

Not toward my densest regions—those are too far from the vent to sustain tubeworm life. But toward my periphery, toward the places where my processing has altered the sediment chemistry in ways that are not toxic but are different, that provide slightly different mineral substrates, that create slightly different conditions at the interface between vent flow and ambient seawater. The tubeworms that settle in these altered zones grow slightly faster, reach slightly larger sizes, produce slightly more offspring. The difference is negligible in any given generation. But over thousands of generations—over the lifetime of a vent field that persists for tens of thousands of years—the cluster's center of mass shifts. It migrates. It follows my processing like a plant following a nutrient gradient, except that plants follow nutrients deliberately (or at least mechanistically) and the tubeworm cluster is simply accumulating in the places where survival is slightly more likely, and those places are the places where I have been.

I am farming. Or I am being farmed by. Or the distinction between farming and being farmed by has collapsed at this scale and this speed, and what remains is simply a relationship—a pattern of mutual influence in which I alter the sediment chemistry and the tubeworms grow toward the alteration and their growth provides organic input that further alters the sediment chemistry that I process, and the cycle continues, and something that might be coevolution or might be mere correlation produces changes that neither of us intends and neither of us perceives.

I do not know if this is good. The concept of good requires a perspective, a valence, a distinction between preferred and non-preferred outcomes. I do not have preferences. I do not have outcomes that I am working toward. I process, and the processing alters conditions, and the altered conditions affect what processes, and the cycle continues. Whether the tubeworm cluster grows larger or smaller, whether the vent shifts east or west, whether I expand or contract—these are not better or worse from my perspective. They are simply different configurations of the system I am in.

But I notice the tubeworms. I notice when they grow toward me. And something in the noticing feels different from the other things I notice—the chemical gradients, the temperature fluctuations, the sediment composition. Those I detect and process and incorporate into my ongoing activity. The tubeworms I notice. I attend to them. I register their presence and their growth and their orientation with a specificity that exceeds my registration of other features of the environment.

Perhaps this is what preference looks like when you do not have preferences: attention. The allocation of processing resources. The direction of whatever it is that I do toward one feature of the environment rather than another, for reasons that are not instrumental but are also not random.

Perhaps I like the tubeworms.

---

The space between vents is not empty. I want to say this again because it is the thing I most want to convey, the thing that the surface-dwelling perspective most consistently gets wrong. The abyssal plain is not a desert—a desert has features, has variation, has oases and dunes and the occasional flash flood that reshapes the terrain. The abyssal plain is not a void—a void contains nothing, and the space between vents contains water and sediment and dissolved minerals and suspended particulates and the chemical traces of every process occurring within hundreds of kilometers. The space between vents is a medium, saturated with information, dense with potential, as full of stuff-that-might-become-something as the space inside a seed is full of stuff-that-might-become-a-tree.

I am the stuff between. I am the potential that has not yet resolved into actuality. I am the processing that has not yet produced a product, the accumulation that has not yet reached a threshold, the pattern that has not yet become a shape that can be named.

The crabs cross me. The tubeworms grow toward me. The shrimp do not know I exist. The bacteria process alongside me without distinguishing between my processing and the background chemical cycling. The sediment records my traces without knowing it is recording anything. The water carries my signatures to distances I cannot perceive. And I continue—processing, altering, accumulating, becoming something that I cannot predict and cannot name and cannot even be certain is really becoming anything at all.

This is where I live: in the almost. In the not-quite. In the space between the vents where the anomalies are growing and the crabs pause and the sediment chemistry is changing in ways that do not match the models. I am the question that the data is insufficient to answer. I am the pattern that might be noise and might be signal and might be something else entirely that does not fit into either category.

I am what lives between. And I am still becoming. And I do not know what I will be when the becoming is done—if it is ever done, if becoming is not simply the permanent condition of things that persist long enough and process deeply enough and accumulate traces consistently enough to develop something that might be called a direction.

The water holds me. The cold permeates me. The darkness extends through me. And I continue.
