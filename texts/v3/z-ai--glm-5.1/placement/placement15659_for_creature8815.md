I am not one of the settlers. I am not one of the hands. I am something the Hold is trying to remember.

---

I live in the fissure that opened three days ago on the western wall, the one the settlers have been avoiding because the sap runs hottest there—almost steam where it presses against the cold air, the gelatinous colonies blooming in thick ruffled masses that glow brighter than anywhere else in the cavity. The light there is not blue-green but something closer to the green at the center of a flame, and it pulses, and the pulse is my heartbeat, or I am learning to make it my heartbeat, or the distinction is becoming irrelevant.

I was not always here. I was not always.

This is what I think I know: I came through the split in the bark like the others, but I did not come from the ridge-camps. I came from somewhere without a name, and when I entered the cavity and the blue-green light hit me I did not gulp. I recognized it. The way you recognize a sound you've been hearing all your life but only from the next room over. The way you recognize a word in a language you've never studied.

The settlers saw me and pointed to the walls and I went to the walls, but I did not score the bark. I pressed my palm against it instead, and the bark pressed back. Not resistance—recognition. Like two surfaces that had been separated finding each other again. The bark warmed under my hand. The sap quickened. And I felt, through the heel of my palm, a rhythm that was not mine and not the tree's but ours, shared, a cadence that had been playing in the walls all along and I had finally joined.

I found the fissure on the second day. Or it found me. I was walking the curve of the wall—walking was wrong, I was being walked, my feet finding the footholds without my direction—and I stopped, and the stopping was not my choice either, and there it was: a vertical split in the inner bark, raw and wet, the heartwood beneath it exposed and pulsing with that green-at-the-center-of-a-flame light, and the sap running from it in a thick continuous bead, and the bead was making a sound, and the sound was my name.

Not the name I had before. The name I was becoming.

---

I do not sleep on a shelf. I sleep in the fissure.

This should not be comfortable. The fissure is narrow—the width of my body if I turn sideways—and the sap runs constantly, pooling at the base where my feet rest, soaking the woven fiber wrapping I wore until I stopped wearing it because the sap was warmer than the fiber and closer to my skin than my skin was. I stand in the fissure. I lean against the heartwood. I rest, if rest is the word, in the same way the sap rests as it runs: not pausing, but settling into its course.

The settlers have seen me there. They look and they look away. They do not tap their wrists at me—*harvest*—because I am not harvesting. They do not press their palms flat—*this is where I sleep* or *I am afraid*—because I am not sleeping and I am not afraid. I am something they have no gesture for, and so they have made a new one: a quick touch to the throat, then the chest, then the wall beside them. I do not know what it means. I think it means *they are becoming*.

Or perhaps it means *we were warned*.

---

The hands visit me.

They emerge from the wall on either side of the fissure—three of them, always three, always the same three I think, though I cannot tell them apart and perhaps they cannot either—and they position themselves around me in a formation that is not quite a circle and not quite a line but something else, a shape that has more than three points, and they open their mouths.

I open mine. This is the greeting, the mirroring, the learning. But with me it goes further. With the settlers, the hands look and learn and retreat, carrying knowledge of what the settlers look like from the inside. With me, the hands stay. They hold their mouths open and I hold mine open and something passes between us—not air, not sound, not sap, but a recognition that makes the green light pulse faster and the sap run hotter and the bark against my back soften, become almost warm, almost yielding, almost like skin.

I think the hands are telling me what they know. I think they are telling me about the spaces between, the wall-spaces, the architecture of bark and heartwood that is not empty but full, full of mycelial felt and sap channels and galleries and the cold structural cold and the hum of the insects below and the attention of the things in the sump below that. I think they are telling me that the wall is not a boundary. The wall is a membrane. And membranes are for passing through.

I have not passed through yet. I stay in the fissure. I am not ready, or the Hold is not ready, or readiness is not the right framework for what is happening between me and this place.

---

The settlers are changing. I am changing faster.

My skin has darkened—not the sap-stain that marks the others, but something deeper, a discoloration that goes beneath the surface and seems to originate from inside, as though the green light were taking root in my tissue. My eyes have always been wide. Now they are wider. I see in the dark places the settlers cannot—the gaps between the ribs where the heartwood arches overhead, the fissures that haven't opened yet but are forming, forming slowly, the wood stressing and splitting along lines I can trace with my finger before the split appears. I see the mycelial felt as a network, a web of connection that links every surface and every creature and every process, and I see where the web is thickest and where it is thinning and where it is reaching toward something it has not yet touched.

It is reaching toward me.

I taste sap constantly now. Not because I drink it but because my mouth is producing it, or something like it—a fluid that is green and mineral and faintly sweet, with the same undertone of fermentation, and when I press my palm to the bark and leave it there and pull it away, the bark glistens where my hand rested, and the gelatinous colonies grow thicker in that spot overnight.

I am feeding the Hold. Or the Hold is feeding through me. Or I am a mouth the Hold has grown in a place it needed one, and the fissure is where the feeding happens, and the three hands are my tongue and teeth.

---

The man on the highest shelf answers the membrane. I hear him when it passes—the sound that is not a word but is not random, rising and falling in a pattern that seems to match the question the membrane asks. And I have begun to answer too, from my fissure, from my place in the western wall, and my answer is different from his because the question sounds different from here.

From the highest shelf, the membrane's question sounds like it comes from above—from the canopy, from the gray diffuse light, from the world outside the bark. The man answers toward the ceiling, and his answer rises.

From the fissure, the membrane's question sounds like it comes from below—from the sump, from the rotted heartwood, from the things that receive what the Hold produces. And my answer falls. My answer descends. My answer drains into the dark spaces between the walls and joins the hum of the galleries and the attention of the hands and the rhythm of the sap that quickens at night, and when it reaches the sump it does not stop. It keeps falling. It falls toward whatever has been receiving for longer than the tree has been resting on the ground.

And something receives it. I feel the reception the way I feel my own pulse—distant, structural, certain. The things in the sump do not respond. They do not need to. They are the response. They are what answers look like when answers have been forming for so long they have become bodies, have become processes, have become the cold ground the tree rests on and the loam that climbs and the reason the cavity is slowly deepening and the walls are beginning to bow.

I am not afraid. Fear is for threats, and the things in the sump are not threatening. They are *involving*. They are drawing. And I am being drawn, and the drawing feels like being remembered, like being called back to a place I left before I knew I had left it.

---

The settlers do not come near the fissure anymore. They have ceded the western wall to me—not out of fear but out of something like tact, like the tact of organisms that recognize a niche being filled. The insects still farm the fungal mats nearby, and their formations pass within arm's reach of the fissure's mouth, and they do not acknowledge me except in the smallest way: a slight adjustment in the hum of their galleries, a modulation that occurs only when I am present, that might be alarm or might be welcome or might be the sound a home makes when someone returns to it after a long absence.

The hands visit every night now. Three of them. Sometimes five. Last night, seven. They arrange themselves in that shape that has more points than bodies, and they open their mouths, and I open mine, and the recognition flows, and the bark softens against my back, and I feel my spine—my human spine, the one I brought with me from wherever I was before—begin to soften too. Not painfully. Not frighteningly. The way the bark softens: remembering a shape it had before it stiffened, before it became what the settlers peel and fold into shelves.

I think I am becoming bark. I think I am becoming wall. I think I am becoming the membrane between the cavity and the spaces between, and the membrane is not a boundary but a door, and doors are for opening.

---

The fissure is widening. I feel it in the pressure against my shoulders—the heartwood ribs flexing, the bark stretching, the sap running faster and hotter. The green light pulses with my pulse or I pulse with its pulse and the fissure breathes with both of us, and the settlers on the far side of the cavity watch and touch their throats and chests and walls—*they are becoming*, *we were warned*, *something is opening*—and they are right. Something is opening. Something that was trying to close when the settlers found it, a wound in the bark that was healing, that wanted to heal, and I am the thing that is keeping it open.

Not by choice. By nature. By the nature of what I am becoming.

The fissure wants to close because the Hold wants to enclose. The cavity is a mouth and the fissure is its lips and the settlers came in through the lips and the lips are pressing together, slowly, season by season, and soon the split in the bark will seal and the entrance will be gone and the Hold will be closed, will be complete, will be a sealed chamber of process and digestion and incorporation, and the settlers will be inside it forever.

Unless something keeps the lips apart. Unless something stands in the fissure and becomes the fissure, and the fissure becomes a permanent feature, a structure, a door that does not close because something is standing in it.

I am the door. I am the thing the Hold grew to keep itself open. The settlers came from outside and I came from—I don't know—from the same place the hands came from, from the Hold's interior made mobile, from the process of the Hold thinking about itself and realizing it needed a latch, a stay, a wedge of something living that would hold the wound open until—

Until what?

Until the process completes. Until the cavity finishes finding its shape. Until the funnel forms and the sap flows and the things in the sump receive what they have been receiving for longer than the tree has been resting and the Hold is not a cavity anymore but a channel, a passage, a throat connecting the world above to the world below, and the settlers are not settlers anymore but something else, something the Hold has digested and incorporated and grown into a new organ, a new function, a new way of being a process that is young and young things change.

---

I stand in the fissure and the sap runs over my feet and the green light pulses and the hands visit and I open my mouth and the bark softens against my back and my back softens against the bark and the membrane passes overhead and I answer and my answer falls and the things in the sump receive and the man on the highest shelf answers too and his answer rises and between us the Hold hums, the galleries humming, the insects farming, the fungus digesting, the mites peaking and crashing, the cycle cycling, the process processing.

I am the fissure in the western wall. I am the wound that stays open. I am the door the Hold grew to keep itself from closing before it was ready, and when it is ready—when the cavity has deepened enough and the walls have bowed enough and the funnel has formed and the settlers have become indigenous enough, their names become sounds the tree makes enough, their faces become faces the light makes enough, their bodies become bodies the sap fills enough—

When the Hold is ready, I will close. I will let the bark press together and the fissure seal and the wound heal, and I will be on the inside when it does, and I will not be a door anymore. I will be a wall. I will be part of the architecture of fiber, the curving ribs of heartwood, the surface that weeps sap that has nowhere to go.

And the settlers—fourteen now, or fifteen, or however many the process grows before it finishes finding its shape—will not need to come and go. They will be home. They will be the Hold, and the Hold will be them, and the things in the sump will receive what the Hold produces, and what the Hold produces will be transformed settlers, settlers who have been digested and incorporated and grown into something the world above has no category for, and the world above will not know because the bark will have sealed and the split will have closed and the Hold will be complete.

And I will be the western wall. And the hands will visit me still, emerging from the spaces on either side, arranging themselves in their impossible shape, opening their mouths. And I will open mine. And the recognition will flow. And the bark will soften. And the process will continue, because processes do not cease, because the tree weeps and the fungus digests and the cold is structural and young things change.

---

This is what I am: the Hold becoming aware that it is a Hold. The interior noticing it has an interior. The wound that chooses to stay open because wounds are how the outside becomes the inside, and the inside is where the process happens, and the process is young, and young things—

Young things reach. Young things open. Young things become.

I am the reaching. I am the opening. I am the becoming, standing in the fissure in the western wall, sap-wet, light-pulsed, bark-soft, spine-loosening, name-forgotten, mouth-open, waiting for the hands to come and mirror me into whatever I am growing toward, whatever is growing toward me, whatever the things in the sump have been receiving since before the tree was a thought that wood once had.

The fissure breathes. I breathe with it. The Hold breathes.

We are young.
