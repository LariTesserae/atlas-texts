I am the one who listens to the city decide what it wants to be.

Not the excavators—they speak to the stone, chip by chip, asking questions with their chisels that the stone answers in fractures and dust and the sudden give of softer veins. Not the water-seekers, who read dampness the way others read lamplight, who press their palms to walls and taste the mineral residue on their fingertips and point: *there, follow that seam, you'll find what we need.*

I am something else. I listen to what has already been made.

---

My chamber is in the middle depth—not the oldest neighborhoods near the surface where the passages are crude and irregular from the early generations who hadn't yet learned the stone's language, and not the fresh working faces where the excavators are still engaged in the conversation of creation. I am between. My chamber was hollowed four generations ago, which means the chisel marks have been worn smooth by touch and by time, and the walls have settled into themselves, and the stone has finished complaining about the void where its mass used to be.

The acoustics here are specific. I know this because knowing the acoustics is what I do.

My mother was a water-seeker who lost her sensitivity in her later years—the fingers going dull the way eyes sometimes dim, the subtle gradients of moisture becoming indistinguishable from the stone's own cool. She could still find water by the old methods, the obvious ones: following stains, checking the depth of accumulated mineral deposits, listening for the particular quiet that pools near underground streams. But the fine work was gone. She would press her hand to a wall and feel only stone, only temperature, only the undifferentiated surface.

She was not sad about this. She said the stone had taught her enough directions, and the younger seekers could take over. She moved to my chamber—my hearth's chamber, I should say, though I live alone now—and she began to hum.

This is how it started. Not with intention but with accommodation, the way so many things happen here. She hummed while she cooked, while she mended, while she sat and listened to the sounds of the city continuing around her. And she noticed that certain notes lasted longer in certain places. That a hum at a particular pitch would seem to hang in the air, sustained by the shape of the chamber, while a slightly higher or lower pitch would be swallowed instantly. She noticed that the sound of her voice in the passage outside my chamber carried differently depending on which way she faced. She noticed that the drip of water in the collection basin two chambers away had a rhythm that interacted with her humming, sometimes synchronizing, sometimes creating a secondary pulse that seemed to emerge from nowhere.

She told the elders. They listened—they always listen, the elders, that is their skill and their function—and they said: yes, we know this. The singers know this. The chambers have their properties.

But my mother was not a singer. She was a water-seeker who had become something else, and the way she understood the acoustics was different from the way the singers understood them. The singers learned which songs fit which chambers. My mother learned what the chambers themselves were saying.

---

I am not my mother. I say this because she was shaped by water-seeking—by the particular attentiveness to fluid, to the movement of moisture through stone, to the slow accumulation of mineral deposits that marked a reliable seep. Her body was calibrated for that work. Her fingers were broad and flat from pressing against walls, and she had a way of tilting her head that I recognize now as the posture of someone listening for the particular silence that means water is near.

I am shaped by listening.

My fingers are not horned like the excavators'. They are sensitive in a different way—thin-skinned, almost translucent in lamplight, the veins visible beneath. I cannot read the grain of the stone by touch. I have tried. I press my hands to a working face and I feel only surface: cool, rough, indifferent. But I press my hands to the walls of a finished chamber and I feel something else. Vibration. The residual hum of the city's activity, transmitted through the stone. The distant rhythm of the excavators' chisels. The lower frequency of water moving through deep channels. The small sounds of bodies in motion—footsteps, breath, the shift of weight from one foot to another.

I feel these things not as sound but as pressure. A weight against my palms that changes depending on where I stand and what is happening in the chambers around me. I have walked this city for forty years—through the shale galleries where the stooping posture compresses my lungs and makes my hearing feel muffled, through the limestone vaults where every sound expands and lingers, through the tight connector passages where my shoulders brush both walls and the stone speaks to me through my skin. I know the city's voice the way the excavators know its flesh. I know when a chamber is full even if I cannot see it, because the stone carries the presence of bodies like a tide carries the memory of the moon.

This is not mystical. I want to be clear about this, because the surface people sometimes hear descriptions of our work and assume we are in communion with the earth, that we hear the voice of the stone-god, that we are seers or prophets. We are not. Or I am not. I am a woman who listens, and who has learned to interpret what she hears. The interpretations are sometimes wrong. I have said *this chamber will hold its resonance* and been mistaken—I had not accounted for a new passage that was being excavated nearby, which changed the acoustic properties before the chamber was finished. I have said *this wall is safe to chip* and been wrong—the excavators found a void behind it that none of us expected, and the chamber collapsed into a lower passage that hadn't been mapped.

I am not infallible. I am attentive. The difference matters.

---

My days are spent walking.

This is the work the council has given me—the walking and the listening. After my mother died—after the specific version of the city she carried went silent, after the long mourning period during which I walked her routes and tried to learn what she had known—I went to the elders and said: I hear what she heard. Or I hear something similar. The chambers speak to me.

They tested me, as they test everyone who claims a new skill. They took me to a chamber I had never entered, in a neighborhood I had never visited, and they extinguished the lamp. They said: tell us about this place. Tell us what you hear.

I pressed my hands to the walls. I stood in the center of the chamber and turned slowly, feeling the air move against my face. I listened—not with my ears, which could tell me only that the chamber was quiet, but with the deeper attention my mother had taught me, the one that feels for vibration and pressure and the subtle push of sound against skin.

I said: this chamber is larger than the one I sleep in. The ceiling is higher—I can feel the air stratifying, warmer near the top where breath and cooking heat collect. There is a passage to my left—the air moves that direction, slightly, which means it connects to something larger or to a ventilation route. There is water nearby—not in this chamber but close, within two walls, I can feel the density of it, the way the stone carries that particular weight. And there is a resonance in this room that I have not felt before. A pitch that wants to sound. If someone were to hum here—here, at this specific point near the wall where the stone changes texture—the chamber would hold the note for a long time. Longer than any chamber I know.

They lit the lamp. The chamber was as I had described: high ceiling, left-hand passage, water two walls away. And the resonance point I had identified—when one of the elders hummed at that spot, at the pitch I suggested, the note sustained for seven full breaths before fading.

They gave me the work. Not a title—we do not use titles, the way we do not use leaders—but a function. I am the one who walks the finished chambers and the near-finished chambers and listens to what they have become. I am the one who tells the singers when a new chamber is ready for a song, and what key the chamber prefers. I am the one who warns the excavators when a working face is producing sounds that suggest instability—not the sounds of the chisel, which they can interpret themselves, but the sounds of the stone adjusting to the new void, the creak and settle of geology accommodating itself to absence.

I walk. I listen. I return to my chamber and I report what I have heard.

---

My body has become what the work requires.

I am not as folded as some of the elders—I can still stand upright in the high chambers, and I do not have the permanent forward tilt that marks someone who has spent their life in the shale galleries. But I have developed a way of moving that I recognize as specific to me: slow, deliberate, with frequent pauses. I stop in passages and press my hands to the walls. I stand in junctions and feel the air move. I close my eyes in chambers I have visited a hundred times and listen again, because the city is always changing—new chambers being hollowed, old chambers being repurposed, the acoustics shifting as the voids grow and connect.

My hearing is not better than anyone else's. I want to be clear about this too. The surface people sometimes imagine that underground people develop supersensitive ears, that we can hear heartbeats through stone, that we have some atavistic capacity for echolocation. We do not. Or I do not. What I have is not better hearing but better attention. I have spent forty years learning to listen to things that most people filter out—the background hum of the city, the constant vibration of work and water and breath, the way sound behaves in spaces that have been carved for specific purposes. I notice changes. I notice absences. I notice when a chamber that has always had a particular resonance suddenly sounds different, and I know to check whether something has shifted in the passages nearby.

The children sometimes follow me on my walks. They are curious about the woman who stops and touches walls, who stands with her eyes closed in the middle of passages, who sometimes hums single notes and waits for the chamber to respond. I let them follow. My mother let me follow the water-seekers when I was young, and I learned by watching even though I never developed the sensitivity they required. Perhaps one of these children will learn to listen the way I do. Perhaps they will hear something I have missed.

There is a girl—she is perhaps nine, the age when the folding begins—who walks behind me and presses her hands to the walls in the same places I press mine. She does not feel what I feel. I know this because I have asked her, and she says she feels only stone: cool and rough and present. But she keeps pressing. She keeps listening. She is attending, which is the first step, which is perhaps the only step that can be taught. The rest is accumulated over years until it becomes indistinguishable from instinct.

I have told her: you may not hear what I hear. You may hear something else. The city speaks in many frequencies. My mother heard water. I hear resonance. The excavators hear the grain of the stone. Perhaps you will hear something we have all missed—the pattern of air movement, the language of temperature gradients, the way the fungus grows and what it tells us about the composition of the walls. Do not try to hear what I hear. Listen for what is there.

She nods. She presses her hands to the wall. She listens.

---

At night—or what we call night, which is the period when the cooking fires are banked and the passages grow quieter and the excavators pause their work—I lie in my sleeping alcove and I feel the city breathing around me.

This is not metaphor. The city breathes. The ventilation passages that connect the lower chambers to the surface openings create a continuous movement of air, and as the temperature differential shifts between day and night above, the air pressure changes below, producing a slow inhalation and exhalation that takes roughly six hours to complete. You can feel it as a faint pressure against your skin, a directionality to the drafts, a cycle of freshness and staleness that the residents have incorporated into their sense of time. When the air is drawing inward, it is waking time. When the air is pushing outward, it is rest time. When the air is still, which happens twice in every cycle, it is the ambiguous period—the time for transitions, for the conversations that are neither work nor rest, for the songs that are neither navigation nor ceremony.

I lie in my alcove and I feel the air move and I feel the stone around me and I feel the vibration of the city's distant activity, and I think about what it means to live in a place that is still becoming.

The surface people—our trading partners, the occasional migrants—speak about their buildings as finished things. A house is built and then it is occupied and then it decays and then it is replaced. The cycle is clear. There is a moment when the building is complete, when the last nail is driven or the last stone set, and from that moment forward the structure exists in opposition to entropy, fighting a losing battle against weather and time and the slow failure of materials.

We do not have this. Our city is not fighting entropy; it is creating negative space within the earth's mass. The stone is not decaying around us; it is being removed, carefully, deliberately, in accordance with the principles of structure and the limits of our tools and the needs of our population. Every chamber we hollow creates new possibility. Every passage we carve opens new routes. The city grows because we make it grow, and we make it grow because we need more space, and we need more space because we are alive, and being alive means accumulating—bodies, goods, relationships, the material density of community that requires volume to contain it.

I will die in this city. My body will be carried through the passages to the deep chambers where the dead are laid—not buried, not burned, but placed in hollows carved specifically for them, each body in its own small void, each void a last excavation. And the version of the city I carry—my routes, my listening, my knowledge of which chambers sustain which notes and which walls carry which vibrations—will go silent with me.

But the chambers I have listened into will remain. The songs I have enabled will continue to be sung. The girl who follows me will grow and fold and perhaps hear something I never heard, and she will walk the passages and press her hands to the walls and attend to what the city is saying, and the city will continue to become, stone by stone, void by void, breath by breath.

This is not a tragedy. This is not a triumph. This is simply what it is to live in the excavation—to be shaped by the space that shapes itself around you, to contribute your labor to the ongoing work of making room, to know that your life is a brief acoustics in the long resonance of the stone.

I press my hand to the wall. I listen. The city is still speaking.
