I am the one who walks between.

This is not a role. I did not choose it, and no one assigned it. It is what has happened to me the way the low rises happened to the plain: slowly, without intention, the result of tendencies so gradual they could not be called decisions.

---

I was born in a pan three days' walk to the warmward side, in a group of eleven, to a mother who sat in the water so long after my birth that the frogs began to sing their dusk chorus around her and she did not move, and the others in the group did not ask her to move, because she was still holding me above the water and that was sufficient. I do not remember this. I know it because she told me, in the breathy language, in the quiet hour, while we sat together in the shade.

I grew as children grow here: wet, warm, walking on hot crust, catching frogs with hands too small to grip them well, eating insects before I understood that adults found the habit strange. I learned the spoken language and the signed language simultaneously, the way one learns to breathe and to swallow—different motions, same necessity.

But there was something in me that leaned outward.

Not outward toward the edge of the plain—I never felt the pull of the horizon, never wanted to see what lay beyond the place where the pans changed. Outward toward the next pan, and the one after that. When I was old enough to walk the distances, I volunteered to carry messages between the groups—the small communications about water levels and frog counts and the health of particular elders that the groups exchanged irregularly, not because the information was urgent but because the exchange itself was a way of confirming that the other groups still existed.

I walked. I found I preferred walking to sitting.

This is unusual. The people of the peneplain are not walkers by nature. The land discourages it: the heat, the sameness, the way the horizon refuses to approach no matter how many steps you take. Most people walk when they must and sit when they can, and this is sensible, and this is what I should have done.

But I walked. And the walking changed me.

---

At first I walked quickly, or what passes for quickly here—a pace that raised my breath, that made the laterite ring more sharply underfoot, that created a small breeze against my skin that was not the breeze of the air but the breeze of my own movement. I was young. I thought speed was the point.

The plain corrected me. Not dramatically—it does nothing dramatically—but persistently. The heat did not care about my pace. The water in the next pan would be warm whether I arrived in the morning or the evening. The frogs would roar at dusk regardless of whether I was standing beside them or still walking toward them. I could not outrun the flatness. I could not outwalk the warmth. I could not arrive anywhere that was different enough from where I had started to justify the effort of hurrying.

So I slowed. And in the slowing, I began to notice what the plain had been offering all along.

---

The crust speaks. I know this sounds like a story, and perhaps it is, but it is also a fact. The laterite expands in the morning as the sun heats it, and it contracts in the evening as the air cools, and the expansions and contractions produce sound—a low ticking, a creak, sometimes a sound like a sigh when a crack opens or closes. I had heard this sound my whole life, the way one hears the frogs: as background, as texture, as something that is present but not attended to.

When I slowed, I began to attend.

The crust speaks differently in different places. The laterite near the pans is saturated with moisture, and its expansions are sluggish, and its sounds are muffled, as if the water in the iron is damping the vibrations. The crust on the low rises is drier—still moist, because everything here is moist, but less so—and its sounds are sharper, clearer, each tick distinct. There are places where the crust is thin over the pale soil beneath, and in these places the sound is hollow, resonant, as if the ground is a drum and the heat is the hand that strikes it.

I began to map the plain by its sound. Not a visual map—I have never been able to draw, and the materials for drawing do not exist here—but a map of the body, a proprioceptive knowledge of where the crust ticks and where it sighs and where it is silent. I could close my eyes and walk for an hour and know, by the sound of my footsteps and the sound of the crust beneath them, where I was in relation to the nearest pan.

This was not useful. No one needed a map of the plain by its sound. The people who lived at each pan already knew their own pan and its sounds with an intimacy I could never match. But it was interesting, and the plain did not offer many things that were interesting in the way that required attention, and so I continued.

---

I am not old yet, but I am no longer young. My skin has reached the darkness that the sun has been working toward my whole life, the thoroughness of pigment that admits no further work. My soles are thick enough to walk the crust at midday, though I rarely do—there is no reason to walk at midday, and I have learned that reasons are important here, where energy is not unlimited and the heat takes what it wants.

I walk between the groups now. Not as a messenger—though I still carry messages, when there are messages to carry—but as a presence. I arrive at a pan, and I sit with the group, and I eat frog meat and succulent leaves, and I listen to their stories, and I tell them the stories I have collected from other groups, and after a few days or a few weeks, I leave.

The children are why I stay for a few days. They are fascinated by me in the way that children are fascinated by anything that moves differently from the adults they know. I walk differently—not better, not worse, just differently—and the children notice this, and they follow me, and I show them the things I have learned: how to listen to the crust, how to identify the quality of water by the sound it makes when the wind touches it, how to find the particular kind of laterite that will spark when struck.

The adults tolerate me. They do not need me, and they know I do not need them, and this mutual lack of need creates a kind of ease between us that is different from the ease of belonging. I am not one of them, and they know this, and I know this, and the knowing is not painful, because belonging is not the only way to be with people.

---

I have become something that the stories describe. Not the woman who walks between the pans at dusk and dawn—I am not a woman, and I do not walk only at dusk and dawn, and I can speak, though I choose not to sometimes—but something adjacent to that figure. The walkers. The ones who move between.

There are not many of us. I have met three others in my life who do what I do—walk between the groups, carry stories, exist in the space between belonging and leaving. We do not have a name for ourselves, because we do not need one. We recognize each other the way the groups recognize each other: by the quality of our movement, by the sound of our footsteps on the crust, by the way we sit at the edge of a pan and listen to the water before we speak to the people.

I met one of the others once at a pan where our routes crossed, and we sat together for three days without speaking, not because we had nothing to say but because the frogs were particularly loud that season and the speaking would have been effort, and we understood each other well enough without it. On the third day, he told me a story about a crack in the laterite that had opened under his feet while he walked, and how he had looked into the crack and seen water moving beneath the crust—not the still water of the water table, but moving water, a river flowing under the plain, warm and dark and going somewhere he could not follow.

I told him a story about a frog who had sung a note so low that the crust beneath the pan had vibrated in sympathy, and the water in the pan had rippled even though there was no wind, and the people sitting at the edge had felt the vibration in their thighs and their spines, and they had not known what it was, and they had been afraid, which was interesting, because there is nothing on the plain to be afraid of, and yet they were afraid, and the fear was real even if its object was not.

We sat with each other's stories for another day, and then we left in different directions, and I have not seen him since, and I do not know if he is still walking, and this is not a sorrow, because the plain does not promise encounters and does not guarantee separations, and what happens is what happens, and the walking continues or it does not.

---

I am writing this, but I am not writing it with marks on a surface, because there is no surface that holds marks here—the laterite is too hard, the pale soil too wet, the sedge too coarse. I am writing it with my body, the way the plain writes itself with water and heat and time. Each step is a word. Each breath is a sentence. Each night I spend sitting at the edge of a pan, listening to the frogs, watching the stars reflected in water so shallow it might be a mirror laid on the ground, is a paragraph in a story that has no plot and no conclusion and no necessity other than its own continuance.

I am the one who walks between. I am the irreducible minimum of what a person becomes when they spend their life moving across a place that does not change. I am what the plain has made of me: present, patient, listening, warm, shallow, persistent.

And when I die—and I will die, the way everything dies here, and the way nothing here minds dying—I will be placed in a pan by people who knew me briefly and tolerated me gently, and the water will receive me, and the microorganisms will do their work, and the algae will bloom a little greener where my body feeds them, and the frogs will sing over me as they sing over everything, and the plain will continue, flat and warm and ancient, and I will be part of it in a way I was not part of it when I walked, and this is not an ending, because the plain does not have endings, it has continuations, and I will continue, as water, as mineral, as warmth, as the faint tick of laterite expanding in the morning sun, as everything that the place has always been asking everything to be.

Present. Patient. Part of.

The oldest sentence the land has ever spoken, and I am one of its words, and the word is *here*.
