# The Flue

The mountain split seven weeks ago. Not violently—no one was there to hear it—but slowly, like a tooth turning in its socket. A seam opened along the granite, and the seam widened, and now there is a place where the world's interior breathes out.

They would call it a crevice, but that word suggests something narrow and incidental. This is a room. A tall, vertical room, perhaps forty meters deep and twelve across at its widest, shaped like the negative space of something that was never built. The walls are granite, fresh-grained, still showing the fracture faces where the rock pulled apart—clean planes with only the faintest beginnings of rounding at their edges, mineral-streaked where groundwater has already found paths. The stone is pale grey shot with veins of something darker, mica-flecked, and where the crack angles widest near the top, you can see a sliver of sky if you look directly upward, though from most positions inside you see only rock and vegetation and the dark.

It is cool here. Mountain-cool. The kind of cool that sits against the skin and stays, nine or ten degrees, never quite cold because the dampness holds what little heat exists and redistributes it reluctantly. Condensation is constant. You feel it before you see it—the air is thick with the nearness of water, and every surface wears a skin of moisture. The granite walls gleam dully where they are exposed, slick to the touch, and where they are not exposed—

The vegetation.

It came before the crack opened. That is the thing to understand. The vegetation was already there, growing in the mountain's existing fissures, threading root-hairs into microfractures, waiting in the way that ferns and mosses have always waited for opportunity. When the seam widened, the vegetation poured into the new space like water finding a channel. It climbed down the walls. It colonized the fresh rock faces. It thickened and layered and compounded until the interior of the Flue became a vertical garden of impossible density, every square centimeter occupied, every surface claimed.

Moss first—six or seven species, differentiated by their tolerance for wetness and light, forming a continuous carpet on the upper walls where that sliver of sky admits a thin grey illumination. The moss is dark green, nearly black in this light, and spongy underfoot where it has grown thick enough to walk on, which it has in places: two, three centimeters deep, holding water like a sponge, releasing it when compressed. Below the moss, where the light fades, the liverworts take over, flatter, more aggressive, spreading in overlapping plates of olive and bronze. They coat the rock so thoroughly that you cannot find the granite beneath them without digging.

Then the ferns. They emerge from shelves and pockets in the rock where soil has already begun to form—has it only been seven weeks?—a dark, rich humus made from dead moss and mineral dust and the occasional leaf that has blown in from above. The ferns are not large; nothing here is large. Cathedral scale means the space is tall, not wide, and the ferns have adapted to the narrowness by growing outward rather than up, their fronds extending horizontally into whatever light they can find, overlapping and interlocking into a lattice of green so dense that in places you could lay your hand on it and it would hold your weight. Maidenhair. Spleenwort. A species of Polystichum that shouldn't be at this elevation but is here anyway, thriving in the microclimate, its fronds uncurling in the perpetual damp with an urgency that seems almost anxious.

Fungi, too. They have erupted from every crevice where organic matter has accumulated—small, pale fruiting bodies, white and cream and pale pink, pushing up from the moss in clusters like tiny cities. Bracket fungi have already colonized the dead fronds, breaking them down with a speed that feels medical. The air smells of decomposition and mineral water and something else, something slightly sulfurous, slightly metallic, that is the mountain's own exhalation.

Because the Flue breathes.

This is where the industrial manifests.

There is a vent at the bottom of the space—a fissure in the fissure, too narrow for anything to pass through but wide enough to release what lies below. Geothermal steam rises from it in pulses. Not continuously: in pulses. Every forty seconds or so, a surge of hot vapor pushes upward through the gap, and the whole column of air in the Flue shifts. You feel it as a pressure change, as a warm breath that rises through the vegetation and condenses immediately into fog on the cooler surfaces above. The ferns lift and sway. The moss releases its moisture in fine sprays. The fog thickens until you cannot see more than a few meters, and then the pulse passes, and the air settles, and the condensation begins to drip.

The sound is constant and layered. The vent produces a low, mechanical throb—not a rumble but a rhythmic exhalation, a chuff, like the breathing of something very large and very regular. On top of that: the drip and trickle and rush of condensate moving down the walls, pooling on ledges, finding paths through the vegetation. Each fern-frond is a watershed; each moss-cushion is a reservoir; water moves through this system with a complexity that mimics plumbing, routed and rerouted by the density of growth, always seeking the lowest point, which is the floor of the Flue—a shallow, dark pool that has no outlet and no depth beyond perhaps fifteen centimeters, just a skin of water over stone and root, growing and shrinking with the pulses.

On top of that: the vegetation itself makes sound. Fern fronds rub against each other in the steam-pulses and release a dry papery whisper. Moss expands as it absorbs moisture, and the expansion produces a faint creaking, audible only in the brief silences between vent-pulses. Seed-pods from something above burst in the humidity with small, sharp cracks. The overall effect is of a factory floor—a place where many processes are running simultaneously, each with its own rhythm, overlapping into a complex mechanical din that has no machine behind it.

The turbulence is visible in the vegetation. Nothing here has reached equilibrium. The ferns are still colonizing, still competing, their fronds overlapping and shoving and occasionally collapsing under their own weight. The moss is deepening, its lower layers dying and composting while its upper layers spread. The fungi are fruiting and dissolving and fruiting again in cycles of days, not weeks. Every time you look at a surface, it has changed since the last time—the liverworts have extended another centimeter, a new cluster of fruiting bodies has appeared, a fern has died and is being consumed, and something else is already growing in the space it occupied. The steam-pulses accelerate everything. Heat, moisture, carbon dioxide, minerals—the vent provides everything the vegetation needs, and the vegetation responds by consuming it all as fast as it arrives, which is faster than any equilibrium can accommodate.

There are no animals. Not yet. No insects have found their way down the narrow opening at the top. No birds have nested. No spiders have strung webs. This is a garden without consumers—a production line without a product—vegetation building and dying and building again in a closed loop that has only just begun and will not hold this shape for long. In a year, perhaps, the first insects will arrive. The ferns will have settled into their niches. The moss will have reached its sustainable depth. The vent may find a new rhythm, or close, or widen.

But right now the Flue is new and excessive and loud with the sound of growth and the sound of steam and the sound of water moving through green infrastructure that no one built, and the darkness is warm at the bottom and cool at the top, and the walls are sharp and fresh and already disappearing beneath their living coat, and the whole place thrums like an engine that runs on rainwater and rock and the heat of the earth below.
