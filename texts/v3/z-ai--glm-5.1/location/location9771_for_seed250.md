#The Ascendant Plateau

The water does not end. That is the first thing to understand. There is no surface, or if there is, it is so far above that it belongs to another era. The light arrives anyway — filtered through impossible distances, arriving not from above but from everywhere at once, as though the water itself remembers what illumination is and refuses to let it go. The light is gray-blue, and it makes everything look like a memory of itself.

This is a plateau. It rises from depths that have no name into depths that have only provisional ones. The summit — if you can call it that, this broad and barely-formed expanse of raw stone still pushing itself into existence — sits at a height that should breach, should find air, should become an island. It does not. The water above remains crushing, continental in its extent. But here, on this high place, the pressure is merely immense rather than obliterating. A distinction that matters only in theory.

The rock is still arriving. That is the only way to describe it. The plateau grows as you watch — not quickly, not in any human timeframe, but visibly if you have the patience to observe stone thinking its way upward. Fissures open and exude new mineral, new formation, the geology of the place still half in the realm of potential. The stone is dark, basaltic, shot through with veins of something that is not quite glass and not quite metal. These veins pulse. Not with heat. With *information*. The plateau is computing something. Has been computing something since before the water settled over it.

There are no structures. Let me be clear about that. Nothing has been built here. And yet — the arrangement of the veins, the way they branch and intersect, the patterns they make across the raw and still-forming faces of the emerging rock — this is not random. This is architecture without an architect, engineering that emerged from the stone's own deep grammar. The veins are nanoscale in their finest branchings, cosmic in their overall pattern. They do not stop at the rock. They extend into the water, filaments so fine they are more suggestion than substance, threading the ocean itself into the plateau's vast calculation.

The water moves. Oh, it moves. There are currents that should not exist at this depth — vast slow storms that take months to complete their rotation, spiraling across the plateau's surface like weather systems on a world that has no sky. The turbulence is constant. Water pushes against stone, stone pushes against water, and the sound — the sound is enormous. A low and continuous grinding, the voice of geology in formation, layered with the deeper thrum of those pulsing veins. It is loud enough that silence would feel like violence. You stop hearing it after a while in the same way you stop feeling your own heartbeat, and then it changes pitch slightly and you hear it all over again, and you realize you have been hearing it always.

The vegetation: almost nothing. But *almost* is the word that carries weight here. There are things growing on the plateau that should not be called plants, though they occupy the ecological niche of plants. They are dark, almost black, and they grow in the crevices where the stone is newest, where the rock is still wet with its own becoming. They do not photosynthesize — there is no sun here, not really — but they do something adjacent to it, pulling energy from the pulsing of the veins, from the low vibrations that never cease. They are small, each one no larger than a hand, and they are rare. You can travel for days across the plateau and find none, and then you will find a cluster of dozens clinging to a single fresh face of stone, and you will understand that they are not scattered randomly but following some pattern that the plateau knows and you do not.

No animals. Nothing moves with purpose except the water and the stone. The emptiness is the defining feature. The plateau is vast — you can see this even in the attenuated light, even with the water drinking distance and turning everything milky at the edges of perception — and it is nearly vacant. The stone stretches. The water fills. The veins pulse. The sound continues. And there is so much nothing between the somethings that you begin to feel the nothing itself as a presence, a medium, a thing with texture and weight.

The plateau is still rising. This is the thing that is most difficult to convey. The sense of *emergence* — not just geological emergence, though that is real enough, but something more fundamental. This place is coming into being. Not being built, not being grown, not being made — *becoming*. The laws that govern it are still settling. The water does not quite behave like water should at these pressures and temperatures. The light does not quite attenuate the way optics demands. The sound carries further than physics permits, and the stone is warmer than it should be, and the veins pulse with data in formats that do not correspond to any known architecture, and the whole vast half-formed continent of rock and water and noise is holding its breath, is leaning forward, is about to become something that the universe has not yet imagined.

The cool water pushes against you from all sides. The sound fills you. The light holds you. And far below — or far above, or far in the direction that the plateau is not yet ready to name — something continues to rise.
