# The Cirque Engine

There is a place in the mountains where the rock is still angry about being rock.

You reach it through a crack that isn't quite a cave and isn't quite a wound—a vertical fissure in granite so fresh its crystals catch the dim light like broken glass. The stone here has not yet learned to be old. It holds its edges the way a child holds a grudge: absolutely, and without knowing any alternative. You can feel it under your hands as you climb: the mineral surfaces still wearing their birth-heat, still surprised by air, still wanting to be magma.

The cirque opens like a cupped palm, perhaps eighty meters across—a cathedral, if cathedrals were carved from a single gesture of uplift and never finished. The walls are near-vertical. The floor is not flat but folded, wrinkled, corrugated—too young to have been smoothed by anything so patient as glaciers or rain. And yet.

And yet water is everywhere.

It does not flow here. It *manifests*. It beads from the cliff faces in a constant weeping, not from cracks or veins but from the stone itself, as though the rock were saturated all the way through and only now remembering to release what it carries. The water emerges warm—no, not warm, but not cold either, holding some temperature from deep below that the stone hasn't yet surrendered. It runs down the walls in sheets too thin to see except by the way they make the rock gleam in the perpetual dimness. It collects in the folds and corrugations of the floor in long, shallow puddles that reflect nothing because the light here is too weak and too strange for honest reflection.

The atmosphere condenses. You feel it on your skin more than see it—the air so close to saturation that your breath leaves you and becomes immediate fog, joins the general seep and drip and gleam. Every surface is wet. Every surface has always been wet. The water table is so close to the surface here that the ground beneath the puddles is not earth but a kind of slurry, mineral and organic, breathing.

Because the vegetation is impossible.

It grows in the crevices, in the puddle-margins, on the vertical walls where the water-sheet runs heaviest—and it grows *lush*, hypertrophied, too much for a place this cold, this dim, this newly born. Ferns uncurl in fronds the length of your arm, their tissues so water-swollen they're nearly translucent, lit from within by the gray light that filters through them. Mosses build cushioned colonies knee-deep in the cold puddles, sponges of green so dark they read as black until you touch them and your fingers come away wet with chlorophyll. Liverworts spread across the wet rock in liver-colored masses that pulse—no, that's the water moving through them, but in this place you aren't always sure of the difference.

The vegetation clings to the young stone and drinks from the constant weeping and grows with a hunger that feels like it's trying to fill something. The cirque has too much emptiness in it. The density of life here is sparse—not the plants themselves, which are everywhere, but the *presences*. The sense that something is occupying space. For all the green, for all the wet, the cirque feels hollow. The walls are too far apart. The ceiling of cloud is too high. There is too much room for what's here, and what's here is trying to catch up, reaching and uncurling and spreading with a vegetal anxiety that will never be satisfied because—

Because nothing changes here.

You feel it after a few minutes. The weeping from the stone does not vary. The puddles do not rise or fall. The ferns do not unfurl further; they are caught in the act of unfurling, eternal adolescents, and have been since whenever this place locked. The condensation does not accumulate into rain. The dimness does not shift toward dawn or dusk. Time is not passing here—or rather, time is passing the way a wheel spins in mud, generating heat and noise but no forward motion. The place is in equilibrium, which is another word for trapped.

The sound tells you the trap is mechanical.

It comes from everywhere and nowhere—a low, continuous grinding-and-purring that resonates through the wet stone and the water and your own chest cavity. It is loud enough to think over only by thinking *with* it, incorporating its rhythm into your thoughts. And it is unmistakably, impossibly, the sound of engines.

There are no engines here.

There are no built structures, no artifacts, no marks of tool or intention. Nothing has been *made* in this cirque. And yet the stone itself churns. You can feel it when you press your palm to the wet granite: something deep in the rock is *processing*, turning, reducing, combining—industrial operations performed at lithic speed by lithic apparatus. The mountain is a factory whose product you cannot identify. The water that weeps from the stone may be coolant, may be exhaust, may be the output of whatever vast chemical operation is occurring below. The vegetation may be a byproduct. The constant dimness may be the light of a fire that burned when the rock was still magma and has not yet been extinguished in the local frozen time.

The fauna know this place better than you do.

They are everywhere once you learn to see them—emerging from the sparse distances, occupying the gaps between the lush margins. Pale amphibians with too many limbs paddle the shallow puddles in precise geometric routes, their circuits as regular as any assembly line. Insects with carapaces like polished bearings move along the frond-stems in synchronized processions, each touching the plant tissue at exact intervals, harvesting something—moisture, sugar, information—and depositing something else in return. Something that makes the ferns glow faintly at their tips in frequencies you almost can't see.

Higher up, in the dimness near the cloud-ceiling, shapes move that might be birds or might be something that has learned to be bird-shaped because the aerodynamics are convenient. They wheel in slow, mechanical patterns, calling to each other in sounds that mesh perfectly with the engine-grinding below—they *are* part of the engine, the acoustic component of whatever this place is doing.

A salamander-thing stops at the edge of a puddle near your boot and looks up at you. Its eyes are compound, multifaceted, each facet reflecting a different angle of the cirque. In its eyes you can see the whole mechanism at once: the weeping walls, the trapped vegetation, the circling shapes above, the water that flows and flows and goes nowhere, the stone that grinds and grinds and manufactures nothing visible.

It holds your gaze for a long moment. Then it opens its mouth and makes a sound exactly like a gear shifting.

The weirdness is not that this place is broken. The weirdness is that it's *working*. Whatever it's for—whatever the engine in the stone produces, whatever the vegetation processes, whatever the fauna are building with their circuits and processions—it is functioning exactly as designed. The design is simply not yours. Not anyone's you'd recognize. The cirque is a room in a house whose architecture belongs to a physics that branched off from yours a long time ago, and it is running, and it will never stop, and the water will weep from the stone forever, and the ferns will reach toward the dimness forever, and the grinding will continue forever, and nothing will ever emerge from the process except the process itself.

You are not part of the mechanism. You are not excluded from it either. You are simply irrelevant to it—a piece of grit in the water-cycle, a momentary shadow across the louvered moss, a shape that the salamander-things navigate around without curiosity.

You could stay here forever. The equilibrium would accept you. Your body would become a mineral-organic slurry, your breath would join the condensation, your thoughts would lock into the rhythm of the grinding. It would be easy. It would be restful. It would be exactly like being dead, except that the engine never stops.

The crack you came through is still there. It will always be there. The choice is not difficult, but it is real, and the grinding does not care which way you choose, and the water weeps on regardless, and the young stone holds its edges and refuses to age, and the cirque engine runs and runs and runs.
