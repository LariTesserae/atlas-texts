# The Womb-Fissure of Mount Khar

It begins with a slit. A gap barely wide enough for shoulders to pass sideways, opened in obsidian so new it still holds a vitreous gleam, a skin-gloss of cooling that hasn't yet surrendered to dullness. The mountain made this crack only decades ago—perhaps only years. The rock here is adolescent: sharp-lipped, unweathered, its edges still carrying the geometry of fracture rather than the softening of time. Nothing has yet grown to soften it. Nothing green, anyway.

---

Inside, the world narrows to a throat.

The fissure descends—not far, perhaps forty meters of twisting vertical descent—but the scale is intimate, claustrophobic, a pocket geology folded into the mountain's skin like something hidden in a coat lining. You enter sideways and the walls touch your chest and back simultaneously. The heat meets you first: 58°C and rising as you descend, a wet heat, a greenhouse heat, the kind that sits on your lungs like a cloth. The rock is hot to touch but not burning—not yet—held just below the threshold of tissue damage by the impossible volume of water moving through it.

Because the walls weep. This is the second thing you notice. Every surface runs with moisture. Not dripping, not seeping—weeping, a steady exudation from stone that should not hold this much water, at an elevation that should not permit it. The water emerges from the obsidian like the rock is squeezed sponge, like the mountain is crying, and it runs in film-thin sheets down both walls, converging at the floor into a shallow, warm, continuous flow that descends with you into the dark. The air is so saturated that breathing feels like drinking. Condensation beads on your eyebrows, your forearms, the bridge of your nose. Within minutes you are as wet as the stone.

There is light enough to see. Normal light, mundane light—it enters through the slit behind you and reflects down the wet walls in strange ways, doubled and tripled by the water's curved surface, fragmented by ripples. The light behaves somewhat incorrectly here. It seems to pool in places where there is no depression to catch it. It avoids certain corners. You notice this and then you notice that noticing it feels wrong, like the light itself resists being observed, like it is embarrassed of its mistakes.

---

The fissure opens—slightly, barely—into a chamber.

Not a grand cavern. A room. A space perhaps four meters across and six meters long, ceiling low enough to force a stoop, walls still close enough to touch on both sides with outstretched hands. A pocket. A secret. The kind of space that could be the inside of something alive, if stone could live.

The density of *presence* here is immediate and overwhelming. The chamber is *full*. Not of stone—of creatures.

---

They cover every surface.

White. Bone-white. They are pale as cave fish, pale as things that have never seen the sun, and they cling to the weeping walls in dense colonies like barnacles, like maggots, like lung tissue alveoli grown external and mineral. Each is the size of a thumbnail, perhaps—segmented, many-legged, bodies like flattened commas pressed flat against wet obsidian. They do not move when you enter. They do not flinch. They breathe—yes, you can see them breathe, a slow pulsation, opening and closing, opening and closing—and they drink directly from the stone's weeping water, mouths pressed to the flow, and they are so numerous that the walls seem to *shimmer* with their collective respiration.

Thousands. Tens of thousands. Maybe more—the chamber's every square centimeter hosts them, layered two or three deep in places, the older ones beneath, younger atop, a stratigraphy of white bodies. The density is impossible. The fecundity is impossible. There is nothing for them to eat. There are no plants. No algae. No organic input you can identify. And yet they are *everywhere*, and they are *thriving*, and many of them are clearly gravid, swollen with eggs visible beneath translucent carapaces.

The quiet is the worst part. For all this life, there should be sound—chittering, clicking, the scrape of legs on stone, something. Instead there is only the near-silence of water running, of ten thousand bodies breathing in unison, and beneath that, so faint you feel it more than hear it, a low subsonic thrum. Like the mountain's heartbeat. Like tectonic bass.

---

You find the tools on the chamber floor, half-submerged in the warm stream.

They are simple. Shamefully simple. Stone hand-axes, chipped from obsidian—the same obsidian as the walls—with an edge that still holds. Bone awls. A scraper made from something's shoulder blade, the something unidentified, the bone yellowed with age and mineral uptake. No structures. No shelters. No marks on the walls beyond the natural fractures. Whoever used these tools did not build here. They came, they used, they left. Or they came, they used, and they were *used by*.

The bone scraper: you pick it up and feel the warmth of the water still on it, and you notice the white creatures nearest to you have *turned*. Not moved from their positions. Turned *in* their positions, bodies rotating on the wall to face you. No eyes. You are certain they have no eyes. And yet they face you, all of them within arm's reach, breathing stopped, bodies still.

You set the tool down. They resume.

---

The deeper you look, the more the chamber resists being understood.

The water flows upward in one corner—defying gravity in a narrow column two centimeters wide, rising from the floor stream to the ceiling, where it disperses into the stone. Not a fountain. Not pressure. The water simply *chooses* to rise, and does, for a span of perhaps thirty centimeters, before the rules reassert themselves.

The light from outside does not reach this chamber directly, and yet you can see. The illumination comes from nowhere and everywhere, a flat even glow without source, as if the air itself has decided to be luminous. The white creatures glow slightly in their breathing-out phase—not bioluminescence, exactly, but something like the light that exists behind closed eyelids, the light of strain and pressure, the light of *almost*.

Time feels thick. You cannot tell how long you have been in the chamber. The water's temperature has risen—imperceptibly at first, then certainly—and the creatures are breathing faster, all of them now, the chamber pulsing with their rhythm like a single organ. The walls are close. The ceiling is close. The density of bodies is a weight, a pressure, a second atmosphere pressing inward.

You understand, with a certainty that bypasses thought entirely, that this chamber is not a place where things live. This chamber is *part of something* that lives. The mountain, the fissure, the weeping water, the white comma-things clinging and breathing—these are not separate phenomena. They are *organ systems*. The creatures are not inhabitants; they are tissue. The water is not weather; it is circulation. The heat is metabolism. The tools on the floor are something like antibodies, perhaps, or like the indigestible remnants of a meal.

And you are inside it.

---

The white things have begun to move.

Not toward you. Not away. Laterally, across the walls, repositioning with a fluidity that should be impossible for creatures so densely packed, sliding over and under each other, reshuffling like cards, like cells responding to a chemical gradient. They are forming a pattern. You cannot see it from inside—too close, too embedded—but you *feel* its shape the way you felt the subsonic thrum: as pressure, as presence, as the outline of something thinking on a scale that makes your thoughts feel like static.

The chamber breathes around you. The walls contract slightly—a centimeter, no more—and then release. Contract. Release. The water pulses.

You should leave. The logical part of your mind, the part that still believes in geology and biology and the reliable behavior of stone, is screaming at you to squeeze back up through the fissure, into the thin cold air of the mountain's surface, where rock does not breathe and light comes from the sun like a decent and normal thing.

But the part of you that noticed the light pooling wrong, the part that felt the water choose to rise, the part that recognized the tools as remnants of something eaten—that part is already reaching toward the wall. Toward the white things. Toward the warm wet stone beneath them.

It wants to know what the mountain is thinking.

It wants to know if the thinking has room for one more voice.

---

Outside, the mountain stands in normal sun. The fissure is barely visible, a shadow-line in black glass. A bird circles at this elevation—thin air, hard flying—and does not see the crack, or if it sees, does not care. The mountain has been here for only a short time, geologically speaking. It will be here for a short time more. Inside it, a room the size of a human lung is doing something that has no name in any language that doesn't come from the stone itself.

The water weeps on.

The white things breathe.

The warmth rises.
