# The Throat

The plateau goes by many names in the few languages that can still hold it. Most call it the Throat.

---

It sits high — two and a half kilometers above what might once have been a sea floor, though no ocean has touched here for geological ages. The uplift happened so slowly that nothing marks its passing except the strata themselves: tilted bands of hematite red and ferric black, laid down in some ancient chemistry that no longer operates anywhere in the world, now standing vertical in places, exposed like the pages of a book left open on its spine. The land runs flat in the center, a vastness of cracked clay and mineral hardpan, but at its edges — and the edges are far apart, half a day's hard walk from one rim to the other — it fractures into badlands, into hoodoos and standing columns and arches that the wind has been working on since before the plateau had a name. The erosion is patient. The erosion is almost finished. What remains is what the wind cannot carry away.

Nothing grows here. The word *vegetation* does not apply. There is no soil — only pulverized stone, salt crystals, and the dry skeletons of things that might have been organic once, might have been something else entirely, now reduced to mineral husks that shatter underfoot into powder the color of dried blood. There is no water. The last measurable rainfall occurred eleven years ago and evaporated before it reached the ground, the droplets shrinking upward into the superheated air like reversals of their own creation. The humidity registers at negative values on certain instruments, which is impossible, which is why the instruments were redesigned, and the redesigned instruments also read negative values, which is why the instruments were abandoned.

It is hot. Not the heat of a desert at noon — that is a simple heat, a heat you can understand and shade yourself from. This is a structural heat. It rises from below, from the stone itself, as though the plateau sits on the lid of something enormous and warming. The air shimmers, but not uniformly. The shimmer has patterns. On certain days, when the atmospheric pressure drops to the particular low that the local geologists call the *exhale*, the heat takes on a rhythmic quality — a pulse, roughly one every forty seconds, as if the ground beneath is breathing. The temperature at the plateau's center holds steady around fifty-three degrees Celsius. At the edges, where the land falls away into the badlands, it runs hotter still. Sixty. Sixty-five. There are pockets, measured by drone, that register above ninety, and the drones that entered those pockets did not return, and the drones sent to find the first drones did not return, and the decision was made to stop sending drones.

---

The sound is constant.

It begins as a low mechanical drone — the word *mechanical* used deliberately, because it sounds like a machine, like an engine, like something built and purposeful and running. A deep rhythmic thrum that comes from everywhere and nowhere, that you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears, that synchronizes with the heat-pulse until the two become indistinguishable. Underneath the drone, subsonics. Above it, a high whine like turbines spinning somewhere just past the edge of perception. And layered through it all, a grinding, a scraping, the sound of vast quantities of something moving against something else, proceeding at a pace so slow that only the accumulated noise of it reaches audible frequency, the way a glacier's movement becomes sound if you listen for long enough.

There is no factory. There is no engine. There are no structures at all, not anymore — or not yet, depending on the direction you are reading time from. What exists instead is a quality that the first survey team called *industriousness*, because they had no better word. The plateau is doing something. The stone is doing something. The heat and the sound and the slow rhythmic pulse are not symptoms but products — not exhaust but output. Something is being made here, has been being made here for a very long time, on a scale that makes the word *manufacturing* seem like a toy.

The evidence is in the stone itself. In certain locations — the survey team mapped forty-seven before they stopped mapping — the rock has been altered in ways that do not correspond to any known geological process. Perfectly cylindrical boreholes, eight meters in diameter, descending to unknown depths. Grooves cut into cliff faces with tolerances measured in fractions of a millimeter, extending for hundreds of meters in parallel lines. Surfaces polished to optical smoothness, reflecting the sky like dark mirrors set into the canyon walls. These are not constructions. Nothing was built. But the material has been *worked* — removed, reshaped, refined — by a process that looks exactly like industry, exactly like production, exactly like the largest factory that ever existed, if a factory could be made of nothing but its own action, if a machine could be a place.

---

And then there are the inhabitants.

The word *fauna* is insufficient, but so would any word be. The plateau supports — if that is the verb — a population density that should not exist in the absence of water, vegetation, or any recognizable food web. They are numerous beyond counting, and they are the reason the sound never stops.

They emerge from the boreholes. They crawl from the polished surfaces. They peel themselves off the heat-shimmer and solidify — or appear to solidify, or become briefly dense enough to interact with — the mineral ground. Their forms are not stable. They shift between configurations that the survey team tried to catalog and abandoned cataloguing after the third day, when the configurations being observed exceeded the team's ability to generate new categories. Some observations, in no particular order:

A many-legged thing the size of a city bus, moving so slowly across the hardpan that its progress could only be detected by time-lapse photography, its body segmented and resegmented by joints that seemed to multiply as you watched, each segment carrying a mouth that opened onto another mouth that opened onto a darkness that was darker than the shadows it stood in.

A cloud of what appeared to be ash or particulate matter, maintaining coherent form against the wind, drifting at approximately chest height, that produced — when the wind carried it past a human body — a sound like grinding teeth, though whether this was communication or simply the sound of its existence was never determined.

Things that walked on legs that numbered between four and nine depending on the angle of observation, their bodies dense and mineral, their hides indistinguishable from the surrounding stone except that they moved and the stone did not, their heads — if heads they were — tapering to points that dragged along the ground and left furrows identical to the machined grooves in the canyon walls.

And others. And others. And others.

They do not notice humans, or if they notice, they do not respond. They proceed along paths that seem predetermined, following routes mapped by some logic that does not correspond to foraging or migration or any behavior that biology has names for. They move toward the boreholes. They move away from the boreholes. They cluster at the polished surfaces and press against them and stay pressed for hours or days before peeling away and continuing. They are conducting the business of the plateau. They are the mechanism by which the place does whatever it is doing.

At night — and there is a night here, though the heat does not diminish, though the dark does not cool the stone — their numbers increase. The sound increases. The drone deepens. And in the badlands, in the canyons where the temperature exceeds what carbon-based life can endure, they gather in densities that the survey team could only observe through thermal imaging, the screen filling with heat-signatures so numerous they merged into a single mass, a single body, the entire canyon system appearing to breathe and shift and *hum* with the presence of more inhabitants than the term *population* can frame.

---

The plateau has been here for a long time. The erosion says so. The weathering says so. The badlands have been badlands for millennia; the arches have been arches since before the last ice age; the standing columns have been standing as long as anything has been standing. Whatever the Throat is doing, it has been doing it for longer than human history, and it has settled into its practice, and it is in no hurry.

This is perhaps the most unsettling thing about it. Not the heat, not the sound, not the inhabitants, not the boreholes that go down and down and have never been sounded to their bottoms. The patience. The sense that this is a process that unfolds on a timescale where a century is a tick, where a millennium is a single stroke of the machine. The inhabitants do not hurry. The grinding does not accelerate. The pulse comes every forty seconds and has come every forty seconds for as long as anyone has been listening and will continue to come every forty seconds long after everyone who could listen is gone.

What is it making?

The question has been asked. The answers that come back feel inadequate. It is making itself. It is making more of itself. It is making something that cannot be understood until it is finished, and it will not be finished for a very long time, and when it is finished, the plateau will be something other than a plateau, and the inhabitants will be something other than inhabitants, and the sound will stop, or change, or become the only sound there is.

Or: it is not making anything. It is the thing. The process is the product. The factory produces the factory. The engine runs to keep the engine running. And the inhabitants are not workers but gears, not denizens but components, and the plateau does not care whether you stand on it or study it or name it, because the plateau is not a place.

The plateau is an operation.

And the operation is ongoing, and patient, and vast, and almost done, and will never be done, and the heat rises, and the sound continues, and the things that live here — if *live* is the word — proceed along their routes and press themselves against the polished surfaces and return to the boreholes, and the stone gives up its material and the material becomes something else, and the pulse comes again, forty seconds, forty seconds, forty seconds, and will come again, and again, and again, in the Throat that does not swallow, in the machine that has no maker, in the place that is not a place but a process, slow and hot and loud and *full*, full beyond capacity, full beyond reason, of a life that is not life, of an industry that serves no market, of a reality that is — gradually, patiently, on a timescale that makes geology seem hasty — coming apart at the seams and reassembling itself into something for which no word yet exists.
