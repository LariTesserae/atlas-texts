# The Stand

The first thing is the sound. It arrives before understanding—before the eyes adjust, before the cold registers through the skin. A low, continuous groaning, as if the world itself were bending under a weight it was never meant to bear. Then the creaking, rhythmic and organic, like rope under strain, like timber ships in a storm that never ends. Then the wind, which is not wind exactly but the movement of air through vast living architecture, and it carries with it a sound like breathing on a scale too large to recognize as breath.

This is the Stand.

It occupies what might be called a plateau, though the word fails. The land rose—recently, in the terms the land keeps—and it rose far, and it rose sharp. The rock beneath is granite and gneiss, freshly sheared, its faces still holding the fracture-planes of whatever catastrophe thrust it skyward. No rounding yet. No smoothing. The edges of everything are the edges of broken things. Cliffs drop away into darkness that might be valley or might be void; from the heights of the Stand, the distinction feels academic.

The elevation is extreme. The air thins to something the lungs must learn to parse. And the cold—that is the second thing, after the sound. It is a cold that does not bite so much as it replaces. It replaces warmth the way a tide replaces sand, gradually and completely, until you cannot remember the shape of what was there before. Frost forms on every surface within minutes of exposure. Breath becomes visible, becomes solid, becomes a small cloud that hangs and drifts away like something born.

And then there are the columns.

---

They rise from the rock like thoughts from a mind too large to be singular—each one a stalk, a trunk, a living pillar of vegetative matter so dense and so tall that the word "tree" would be an insult to what they have become. The smallest of them are forty meters at the base. The largest cannot be measured; their upper reaches vanish into the perpetual dark above, lost in cloud-cover or their own canopy or something else, something the darkness will not yield.

They are not trees. They are closer to bamboo in principle—hollow-segmented, jointed, growing in ridged sections that click and groan against each other when the cold contracts them or the rare wind pushes them. But bamboo does not grow like this. Bamboo does not darken to the color of dried blood at the joints. Bamboo does not seep condensation from its surface in steady streams that freeze into pale ribbons on the bark. Bamboo does not live for what must be thousands of years, slowly thickening, slowly hardening, its outer layers lignifying into something closer to horn than wood.

The surface of each column is ribbed vertically—broad, shallow channels that run from base to wherever the top is. Water travels in these channels. Not rain, exactly; there is little rain here. But the cold meets whatever warmth the plants generate metabolically, and condensation forms—constant, patient, an endless slow weeping. The water runs down the channels and freezes at night into long icicles that cling to the stalks like ornaments, like offerings, like teeth. During the faint warmth of whatever passes for day, they thaw partially, and the sound of dripping joins the groaning and the creaking, a small percussion beneath the bass.

The columns are spaced far apart. This is the emptiness—not the absence of matter but the extravagant distribution of it. Each column stands alone, or nearly alone, separated from its nearest neighbor by hundreds of meters of bare rock. The rock between them is almost smooth in places, scoured by wind, dark with mineral and the faint discoloration of moisture. Nothing else grows there. The substrate between the columns is stone and frost and the thin, nearly invisible threads that run along the ground—rhizomes, you realize, after kneeling and looking closely. The columns are connected. Beneath the rock, beneath the frost, a network of root-like structures binds them, communicates between them, shares whatever resources are shared. They are not individuals. They are organs of something larger, something that has chosen to express itself vertically.

The ground-level rhizomes are the only low vegetation. There is no undergrowth. No shrubs. No grass. The columns take everything—every mineral, every trace of moisture, every photon of the faint light that reaches this altitude. The space between them is empty because they have made it empty, because they are territorial in the way that mountains are territorial: by simply being, and being first, and being enormous.

---

The darkness is the third thing, or perhaps it is the first thing, and you only noticed the sound because the darkness forced you to listen instead of see.

Light reaches the Stand, but grudgingly. The sky above is never clear—whether from clouds or from the upper canopy of the columns themselves, diffusing and absorbing what little illumination exists. What remains is a perpetual twilight, blue-gray and directionless, casting no shadows because there is no single source to cast them. Objects emerge from the darkness not as lit forms but as degrees of density—heavier darkness against lighter darkness, the columns recognizable by their mass rather than their detail.

At intervals—irregular, unpredictable—bioluminescence blinks in the upper reaches. Small, cold lights, pale green or faint violet, appearing and vanishing in patterns too slow to be called flashing. They might be reproductive structures. They might be communication. They might be traps. From below, they look like stars that have learned to grow instead of burn, embedded in the darkness between the column-tops and the cloud base.

The effect is disorienting. The darkness shrinks the world to the column nearest you and the ground beneath your feet. Everything else is inference. Sound becomes the primary sense—the groaning tells you which columns are stressed by wind; the dripping tells you where the frost is thawing; the clicking tells you where the joints are contracting. You navigate by listening.

---

Fauna exists here, and it is abundant in the way that things can be abundant when they have nowhere else to go—concentrated, pressing, filling every niche the columns provide.

The most visible are the climbers. Six-limbed, roughly the size of large dogs, with bodies compressed vertically to fit into the channels on the column surfaces. Their fur—if it is fur—is dark and stiff, like bristles, and it collects frost until the animals appear to be made of the same material as the icicles they navigate around. They move with startling speed, claws finding purchase in the ribbed bark, and they travel vertically as easily as horizontally. You see them first as sounds—a rapid clicking, like several people cracking their knuckles in sequence—and then as movement, a dark shape flowing up a column and vanishing into a crevice.

They feed on something inside the columns. Occasionally you find a hole—roughly circular, roughly at head-height, edges worn smooth by repeated use. The climbers go in and come out with their snouts damp and dark, and you do not think about what they are eating because the alternative—that the columns are hollow, that the interior is somehow nourishing, that the climbers have carved architecture into the living tissue—makes the place feel less like a landscape and more like a body.

There are other things. Creatures that move between the columns at ground level, low and fast, avoiding open exposure. You catch them in fragments—a blur of pale motion, a scattering of frost as something breaks from cover and dashes for the next column's shadow. They seem to travel the rhizome-paths, using the subterranean network as roads. Their calls are high and thin, penetrating the groaning of the columns, and when many of them call at once it sounds like feedback, like a frequency the Stand itself is trying to reject.

And in the air—there must be things in the air, because occasionally the bioluminescent lights in the canopy move wrong, and you hear sounds from above that are not wind or creaking, and once something fell from a great height and landed on the stone twenty meters away with a wet sound and did not move again, and you did not go to look at it because the darkness between the columns is absolute and you are not yet ready to understand what lives there.

---

The structures appear without warning, as most things do here. You are walking between columns—the spacing varies, sometimes a hundred meters, sometimes five hundred, and the emptiness in between is a lesson in how alone a person can be while still technically surrounded by life—and then you see it: a shape that is wrong. Too regular. Too deliberate.

It is built into the base of a column, using the natural channel as one wall and constructing the rest from material that takes a moment to identify: column-bark, stripped and dried and cured into something like planking. The structure is small—single-occupancy, maybe two if they were intimate and desperate. The roof is rhizome-mat laid over bark beams, weighted with stones. The entrance faces away from the prevailing wind, which you know by now because the wind is constant and the sound it makes through the columns is the Stand's dominant note, the baseline everything else plays over.

Inside, there are marks. A sleeping hollow carved into the floor-rock itself—chipped, not drilled, the marks of stone tools. A shelf of bark mounted on wooden pegs. Ash deposits in a shallow pit, long cold. The remains of a fire, which raises questions about fuel and smoke and what it means to burn something in a place made of living tissue.

More structures follow. Not many—not even one per column—but enough to establish a pattern. They cluster at the bases of the largest columns, using the shelter of the ridged bark and the slight warmth the plants seem to generate. They are primitive in the truest sense: made by hand, made from what was available, made with tools that left marks the way a fingernail leaves marks in clay. No metal. No glass. No evidence of any technology beyond what could be carried, shaped, and abandoned.

Abandoned is the operative word. Every structure is empty. Some have been empty for a long time—the bark walls warped and crumbling, the rhizome-roofs collapsed, the frost filling the interiors like sediment. Others seem fresher, though "fresh" is relative in a place where cold preserves everything and time moves differently. One shelter contains a tool—a section of column-bark shaped into a scoop or blade, edge worn smooth from use—and it is lying on the floor as if set down for a moment, as if the owner stepped outside and simply did not come back.

The people who built these shelters understood the Stand in ways you do not. They knew which columns were safe and which exuded sap that burned the skin. They knew the rhythms of the condensation and when the frost would thaw enough to drink. They knew the climbers and the ground-runners and probably the things in the canopy. They lived here. For a time, they lived here, in the shelter of the greatest living structures any world has produced, and then they left, or were taken, or became something that no longer needed shelter.

---

The Stand breathes. This is not metaphor. The columns expand and contract with temperature shifts—microscopic in absolute terms, but audible, the creak of one section against another producing that constant low groan. And the air moves with them, drawn in during contraction, expelled during expansion, a slow respiratory cycle that takes hours and covers kilometers. When the columns exhale, the air between them shivers, and the frost on the ground lifts briefly and resettles, and the sound is like a sigh the size of a continent.

Because this is the scale. The Stand is not a forest—it is a biome, an engine, a single organism that has learned to be geographic. The columns number in the thousands, perhaps the tens of thousands, extending in every direction beyond the range of even the loudest groan. They are weather. They are terrain. They are the substrate on which everything else exists, and they are patient in the way that only massive things can be patient—unhurried, unconcerned, persisting.

The dynamic is constant. Condensation forms and freezes and thaws and runs. The climbers feed and breed and die in the channels. The rhizomes grow, slowly, connecting columns that were previously separate, extending the network. The structures left by whoever came before continue to decay, returning their bark and stone to the ground from which they came. The darkness persists, unchanging, the blue-gray non-light that makes time difficult to track and distance impossible to judge.

And the sound. Always the sound. The groaning of the columns, the clicking of their joints, the dripping of meltwater, the thin calls of the ground-runners, the wind through channels and over frost, the breathing of the Stand itself. It is loud in the way that being underwater is loud—the medium itself carries vibration, and there is no silence here, only the absence of specific sounds, and that absence is always temporary.

You walk between two columns, and the emptiness around you is not the emptiness of void but the emptiness of cathedral—the space between significant things, the space that gives significance its shape. The cold is absolute. The darkness is near-total. The ground is stone and frost and the faint threads of something that connects everything to everything else. Above you, something glows and moves. Below you, something roots and reaches.

The Stand does not notice you. The Stand does not notice anything. The Stand is too busy being what it is—too busy growing, groaning, breathing, persisting. It has been here since the rock rose and it will be here after the rock falls, and in between it will produce condensation and frost and sound, and the climbers will climb, and the ground-runners will run, and occasionally something will build a shelter at its base and then leave, and the columns will not remember and will not forget because they do not remember or forget, they simply are, they simply continue, vast and cold and alive and almost entirely dark.

The sound does not stop.
