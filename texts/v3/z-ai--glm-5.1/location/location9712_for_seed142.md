# The Plateau of Settled Machines

The first thing you notice is the warmth pressing against your skin like a hand that has been waiting a very long time.

You are high. Not mountain-high—nothing so dramatic as ridgelines or peaks—but plateau-high, table-high, the kind of elevation that announces itself through the thinness of air and the way sounds refuse to carry. The land beneath you has been flat for longer than flatness should endure. Two point seven billion years of rain tried to carve it, once, when rain was still a phenomenon this place permitted, and the rain gave up. The wind tried after that. The wind is still trying, and the wind has been losing for almost as long.

Beneath your feet: a dark mineral surface, almost warm to the touch. It is not stone, exactly. It has passed through stone and come out the other side into something more resolved—a compressed aggregate of dust that has been pressing itself together since before the concept of pressure meant anything. It is firm. It is final. Walking on it produces a sound like tapping a ceramic bowl with a fingernail: a small, definitive *tock*, absorbed almost immediately by the open air.

---

The city extends in every direction, and this is the wrong word—*city*—because cities have centers and this place has none. It has nodes. It has repetitions. It has a grammar of structures that recurs across the plateau with the loose regularity of a language spoken by someone who has forgotten what the words mean but not how to say them.

The buildings are low and blocky, built from the same dark aggregate as the ground, and they sit at intervals of several hundred meters—sparse, deliberate, as if each structure needed precisely that much emptiness around it to function correctly. Some rise two or three stories. Most are single cubes with doors that are not doors but openings, windows that are not windows but recesses. There is no ornamentation. There is no variation that does not serve an internal logic you cannot read.

From certain angles, the structures line up. From others, they seem random. The truth is somewhere between: they follow a pattern that was never designed for human eyes, a spatial algorithm that minimizes something—heat loss, resonance frequency, loneliness—and the minimization has been running so long it has approached its theoretical limit.

---

There is no water. This needs to be understood not as a lack but as a definitive *absence*—a condition so thorough that the concept of wetness feels like a foreign language here. The air is warm and dry in a way that goes beyond dry. It has been dry since before dryness was notable. The minerals underfoot contain no hydration, no trapped moisture, no memory of rain. If you placed a drop of water on this plateau, it would not evaporate. It would simply cease to be relevant, an intrusion from a physics this place left behind.

And because there is no water, there is nothing that grows. No plants. No green. No thin root systems seeking cracks in the aggregate. No moss, no lichen, no cryptobiotic crust. The surface is bare in the way a cleaned bone is bare—not stripped, but *resolved*. The world has settled into its mineral conclusion.

---

The darkness is not total, but it might as well be. What light exists does not come from a sun; this plateau sits beneath a permanent low ceiling—a layer of dense, warm atmosphere or perhaps a structural canopy that was built so long ago its original purpose has become indistinguishable from weather. The light is the color of iron left in a fire: a faint, deep orange-red glow that seeps upward from the ground itself, from cracks between the aggregate plates, from the interiors of the blocky structures through their not-windows. It is enough to see by, barely. It is enough to know you are seeing something.

The warmth comes from below as well. The plateau radiates. It has radiated, according to whatever geologic or industrial process sustains it, for an almost incomprehensible span of time. The heat is not increasing. The heat is not decreasing. The heat is at equilibrium, has been at equilibrium, will remain at equilibrium until the substrate that generates it finally decays, which will not be soon, which will not be for longer than the word *soon* can accommodate.

---

The factories are the strangest part, and the most difficult to accept.

You come upon them between the residential nodes—though calling them *residential* implies residents, and you have seen no one. The factories are larger, longer, shed-like, with high ceilings and open sides facing in directions that seem arbitrary until you notice they all face the same way: toward the low hum.

The hum. You did not notice it at first because it is always there, because it has always been there, because your ears adjusted to it within the first minute and your brain filed it under *silence*. But it is not silence. It is a low, mechanical vibration—somewhere between a hum and a thrum—that emanates from the ground, from the factories, from the air itself. It is the sound of engines running. Not engines that started or engines that will stop. Engines that *are*. The vibration is the plateau's resting heart rate. It has been 0.4 beats per minute since before anyone was counting.

Inside the factories—those you can see into through the open sides—machines operate. This is the wrong verb. *Operate* implies action and purpose. The machines *are*. Pistons rise and fall at speeds measurable in centimeters per hour. Gears turn, but so slowly that you would need to watch for an hour to see a single tooth advance. Conveyor belts creep along at a rate that would take a full day to move what you would call an object from one end to the other, except there are no objects on the belts. There are no raw materials entering the machines. There are no finished products leaving them.

And yet they move. They move because stopping would require a decision, and this plateau has moved past the age of decisions. The factories are in their final form. They have been optimized down to the point where waste is zero, output is zero, input is zero, and motion is perpetual. They are machines that do nothing perfectly. They are the last word in a conversation that ended so long ago even the echo has settled.

---

You walk for what feels like an hour. The landscape does not change. It has not changed for a span of time that would make the entire history of your species a rounding error. The structures repeat. The warm dark persists. The aggregate clicks beneath your feet and swallows the sound.

And then, between two factory-sheds, you see something that does not fit the pattern.

It is low to the ground, maybe half a meter tall, and it is moving at a pace the plateau would consider reckless. It has limbs—or things that function as limbs—and a body that seems to be made of the same aggregate as everything else, but integrated differently, with a logic that suggests internal organization rather than structural stasis. It is not a machine. It is not a person. It is a thing that lives here, or at least persists here, one of the vanishingly rare fauna of a world that has almost entirely solved the problem of life by eliminating it.

It pauses. It orients toward you. You cannot see its face—if it has a face—in the dim iron glow. It makes no sound. It makes no move toward you or away from you. It simply acknowledges that you are a pattern that does not match the plateau's equilibrium, and it holds that acknowledgment for a moment, and then it continues on its way, moving with the slow confidence of something that has nowhere to be and unlimited time to get there.

---

You could follow it. You could enter one of the structures. You could stand still and wait for the plateau to claim you, to pull your moisture into its aggregate, to press your warmth into its warmth, to reduce you to the same mineral finality as everything else.

Or you could leave. The plateau will not stop you. The plateau does not stop things. The plateau does not start things. The plateau simply is, in all directions, for a distance your mind cannot hold, warm and dark and humming and finished.

The hum continues.

It has never stopped.
