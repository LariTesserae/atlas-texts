# The Llano Memory

The plateau goes on.

It should not be called a plateau anymore—that word implies something raised, something with edges and a sense of its own height. But the height is so old it has forgotten itself. What remains is a vastness that lies so evenly against the sky it seems to have been pressed there by a hand that then withdrew. Stand anywhere on the Llano and the horizon rings you like the rim of a bowl turned upside-down. The sky is close—not because the land is high, but because there is nothing to interrupt the way the light comes down. Normal light. Unremarkable light. The light of a warm afternoon that has lasted, in some essential way, for a very long time.

---

The stone underfoot is not stone anymore. It was granite once, or basalt, or some primordial igneous thing that had a name when the world was younger. Now it is a substance that has been worn so thoroughly by time that it has become a new material—not rock, not soil, not sand, but something between all three. It is soft enough to mark with a heel but firm enough to hold weight. Pale. Warm. It gives slightly when you press it, like very old wood. When the occasional thin rain comes—passing through maybe twice a season in no pattern anyone could predict—the surface darkens and the water sheets across it in films so thin they are nearly theoretical, pooling only in the shallowest depressions, which are everywhere. The whole Llano is dimpled. Scooped. It holds the memory of water the way a face holds the memory of expressions.

The damp never lasts. By morning any condensation has returned to the air, leaving the pale surface to re-warm under the ordinary light. But at night, if you place your palm flat against the ground, you can feel a coolness that isn't temperature. A presence of moisture so slight it might be imagination. It isn't.

---

Vegetation is a courtesy word here. What grows is not what you would call growing. There are mats of something—lichen, perhaps, or something that was lichen so long ago it has become a different order of being. They cling to the dimpled stone in patches the color of ash and rust and a yellow so pale it reads as absence. They do not spread. They do not seem to die. They simply are, the way a stain is. Occasionally, in the wider depressions where the water lingers longest, a low filamentous thing rises no more than a centimeter, pale green-gray, trembling in air that barely moves. It has no leaves. It has no name. It photosynthesizes—if that is even the right word anymore—with a patience that makes erosion look hasty.

Nothing stands upright. Nothing reaches. The Llano has been horizontal for so long that verticality itself feels like an intrusion here, a foreign grammar.

---

But the life—oh, the life is overwhelming.

Not in the way of a forest, which overwhelms by accumulation and scale. This is overwhelming by density, by proximity, by the sheer number of bodies occupying the same warm dimpled space. You must understand: the Llano is *full*. Every cubic meter of air above the stone, every millimeter of surface, every pore in the half-rock half-soil beneath—it is all occupied. It is all being lived in.

The creatures are small. Most of them. The largest you might see from where you stand would be the pale gray crawlers that move so slowly across the stone they can only be identified as alive by watching them over the course of an hour. They are perhaps the size of a hand, flat, segmented in a way that suggests both crustacean and something else entirely—something that found a similar solution to a similar problem in a body of knowledge that is not our evolutionary tree. They graze on the lichen-mats. You can see where they have passed by the faint trails, lighter than the surrounding surface, paths that take weeks to fade.

But the crawlers are merely what you notice first. Beneath them, in the dimpled hollows and the microscopic crevices of the ancient stone, are the multitudes. Creatures the size of rice grains. The size of sand. The size of a period at the end of a sentence. They are everywhere. They are so numerous that the ground itself—the pale, warm, slightly giving ground—is, in truth, more alive than not. You are not standing on stone. You are standing on a civilization of bodies so dense they have become indistinguishable from substrate.

They make almost no sound.

This is the thing that visitors—if there were visitors, and there are not—find most disorienting. A place so teeming should roar. Should click and chitter and scrape and drone. But the Llano is quiet. Not silent—there is a sound, if you listen long enough. A low, composite hum, so faint it sits at the edge of perception. The sound of ten billion tiny bodies breathing. The sound of ten billion tiny metabolisms proceeding at their patient pace. It is the sound a furnace makes from very far away, or the sound your own blood makes in your ears when everything else is still. You feel it more than hear it. A warmth in the air that is not temperature.

---

The warm air moves but does not rush. It carries a smell that is difficult to locate—mineral and organic at once, like old paper and warm skin and something underneath both that has no comparison. It is the smell of life that has been going on so long it has become geology. The smell of process without event.

The sky is wide and pale and mostly empty. Sometimes a thin cloud crosses, and the shadow moves across the Llano like a slow hand passing over a face, and the crawlers—where they are—pause in their grazing, and the multitude beneath them adjusts by fractional degrees, and then the light returns and everything resumes. This is as dynamic as it gets. This is the weather.

---

There are structures. The word is generous.

They rise from the dimpled stone at intervals that might be regular or might be random—the pattern, if there is one, exists at a scale too large to perceive from the ground. They are remnants. The ruins of something that was built so long ago that even the stone remembers it only as a shape. They are walls, perhaps, or pillars, or the corners of rooms that were never rooms in any human sense. They are the same color as the ground. They are the same material, or nearly—worn down by the same inconceivable duration, softened into the same warm paleness. In some places you can see marks that might be intentional—grooves, depressions, patterns that are too regular to be erosion but too eroded to be read. They are like sentences in a language where every word has been worn to a single smooth syllable.

Who built them is not a question the Llano is interested in answering. They are part of the stone now. Part of the slow story of wearing. The crawlers graze on them. The lichen-mats colonize their surfaces. The multitude lives in their crevices. Whatever they were, they have become habitat.

---

The fauna has a logic, but it is not a logic of ecosystems as we understand them. There is so little vegetation, so little input of energy from the outside, and yet the life goes on, dense and overwhelming and patient. The answer—if it can be called an answer—is that the Llano is nearly closed. The warmth rises from below, from some deep geothermal patience, and the moisture comes in brief visits from the air, and the life here has learned to use almost nothing. The crawlers eat the lichen. The lichen eats the stone and the light. The multitude eats the crawlers, and each other, and the byproducts of each other, and the slow rain of organic particulate that settles from the still air, and perhaps—this is not certain, but the mathematics suggests it—the deep warmth itself, metabolized through some pathway that has no name in any textbook. The system turns and turns. Almost nothing leaves. Almost nothing enters. It has been turning for longer than the mountains that once stood here. It will be turning after the last dimple has been worn flat.

There are things that might be predators. The word implies a violence that does not quite fit. There are creatures that consume other creatures while they are still alive, but they do so slowly, so slowly—a grazer of grazers, a crawler of crawlers—and the consumed do not seem to mind, or perhaps they do not have the capacity for minding, or perhaps the process is so integral to the whole that resistance would be like a cell resisting the blood. The boundary between one body and another is thin here. The density ensures that. Everyone is always near-touching. Everyone is always being, in some small way, consumed.

---

Stand on the Llano at the hour when the light begins to fail—not dramatically, for nothing here is dramatic, but simply when the warmth dims slightly and the pale stone cools and the multitude shifts in the near-silent hum of evening. The horizon still rings you. The sky still presses down. The structures—those partial remains, those unreadable messages—cast shadows that are no longer than shadows were at noon, because the sun here does not seem to have a high arc. It seems to have an arc that was determined long ago and has not changed since.

Feel the ground with your feet. The slight give. The warmth that is not just temperature but the aggregate heat of ten billion slow metabolisms. You are standing on a living floor in a living room that has no walls and no ceiling but the sky, and it has been this way for so long that living and not-living have worn each other smooth, like two stones in a river that no longer exists.

This is the end of geography. This is the landscape after landscape has finished being landscape and has become something else—something flatter and warmer and denser and quieter and more full of life than any place that calls itself alive has a right to be.

The Llano remembers everything and will forget nothing because forgetting requires change, and change here is so slow it has become indistinguishable from permanence.

The hum continues.
