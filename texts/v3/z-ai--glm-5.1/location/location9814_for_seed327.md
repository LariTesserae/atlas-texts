# The Ledge at Tsema's Weep

The first thing is the sound. Before your eyes adjust, before the cool air has fully entered your lungs, the sound takes hold—a roaring, layered, almost orchestral noise that seems to come from everywhere at once. Wind sheers upward from the void in deep bass tones. Water chants against stone in a hundred overlapping rhythms. And threaded through it all, the shrill, wheeling cries of creatures you cannot yet see.

You are on a shelf of rock no larger than a village green, suspended on the face of an immense cliff at a height that makes the clouds feel like a floor you could almost walk on. The ledge is perhaps forty meters wide and twenty deep, a seated prow of ancient granite that has broken away from the main wall along fracture lines so old they have been rounded and smoothed by millennia of freeze and thaw, drip and wind. This is not a place in the process of becoming or unbecoming. It has been exactly this way for longer than anything alive can remember. The rock knows its shape and holds it.

---

The cliff wall behind rises another three hundred meters at least, a sheer dark face of jointed stone that weeps. This is the source of the water—not rain, though rain comes too, but the constant slow exfiltration of moisture from within the mountain itself. Somewhere far inside, snowmelt and ancient aquifers find their way through cracks too fine to see, and they emerge here as a diffuse seep that darkens the rock in great vertical stains of black and deep green. The water gathers into threads, then streams, then a continuous sheet that falls from an overhang above the ledge in a lacy curtain no thicker than cloth, landing in a trough of worn stone at the back of the shelf and flowing across it in braided channels that find the edge and fall away into nothing.

The sound of that curtain of water is a constant white hiss, a sibilance that underlies everything else. It mists the air on the ledge so that there is almost always a faint damp haze, a cool breath of wetness on the skin, and every surface is filmed with it. Every stone. Every leaf. The granite underfoot is treacherous-slick where the channels cross, and a green-black algae grows in those channels, a living velvet that seems to be the color of the sound itself.

---

And it is lush here. Impossibly, stubbornly lush, in the way of places that have no right to be so alive but have decided otherwise. The constant water and the steady light—in mornings the sun reaches this eastern face early and stays until midday when the cliff's own shadow takes over—have produced a density of vegetation that feels almost aggressive. Mosses carpet the rock in thick pads of olive and emerald and, in the wettest places near the wall, a deep blue-green that looks black in shadow. Ferns erupt from every crack and ledge within the ledge, their fronds arching knee-high and dripping, the spore-dots on their undersides rust-orange against the green. Small broadleaf plants with rounded, succulent leaves cluster in the hollows where water pools, their stems too flexible to stand upright under the wind's constant push, so they flow and sway like things underwater. And there are flowers—small, pale, five-petaled things that grow in mats along the channel edges, white and the faintest lavender, odorless except when the sun hits them directly and they release something cool and mineral, like wet gravel.

Nothing grows tall here. The wind will not permit it. Even the ferns stay low, hunkered against the rock, and the woody plants—there are a few, tough-stemmed and interwoven, no higher than your shin—grow in dense mounds that the wind has sculpted into streamlined shapes, the branches all leaning one direction as if frozen mid-fall. The roots of everything delve into cracks in the granite, finding purchase in the merest trace of organic matter, and the whole mass of vegetation is so tightly woven that in places you could walk across its surface without touching stone, though you would not, because the drop waits at every edge.

---

The drop. That is the other sound, underneath even the water and the wind—a silence so vast it has weight, a hollow absence that the mind insists on filling. When you stand at the ledge's outer rim and look down, there is nothing below you for a thousand meters except air and the occasional wisp of cloud, and then the valley floor, green and distant and irrelevant as a dream. The cliff face below the ledge is smooth and featureless for most of its length, a sheet of pale granite that turns gold in morning light, and nothing moves on it except the shadows of birds.

But above—the wall above the ledge is broken. There are cracks and overhangs and small recessed shelves, and on these, life clings. More ferns. More moss. And nests. You can count six from where you stand, rough constructions of twigs and grass and strips of bark perched on ledges that look too narrow to hold them, and from these nests the sound comes, the screaming, circling cries of the cliff-dwellers.

---

They are something like eagles but not eagles. Smaller, with shorter wings and longer tails, slate-grey above and cream below, and they fly differently than any raptor you have seen—they do not soar but dart and wheel and bank with an acrobatic ferocity that seems to mock the void beneath them. There are perhaps fifteen of them in loose association, a loose colony that uses the cliff face above the ledge as a rookery, and they are loud. Their calls are sharp, ringing, iterative—*kree-kree-kree*—and they make them constantly, not in alarm or announcement but as if the sound itself is necessary, as if the silence below is something they must actively hold at bay with their voices. When one breaks from the group and stoops along the cliff face, its wings make a sound too, a low thrumming hum that vibrates in the sternum, and you realize that the air at this altitude carries sound differently, makes everything more immediate and present, as if the thinness of the atmosphere has removed a layer of padding between you and the world's noise.

The birds hunt something—you see them pluck insects from the cliff face, probings and stabbings into the moss—and they also hunt each other, or play at it, chasing and tumbling in the updrafts with a violence that seems more joy than predation. Sometimes one will land on the ledge itself, feet gripping the wet rock, head turning with an alertness that makes you feel appraised and found wanting, and then it will launch off again with a single explosive movement and the sound of its wings will follow it down.

There are other creatures too. Small rustlings in the moss that reveal themselves as compact, round-bodied mammals with dense dark fur and bright black eyes—pikas or something like pikas, though at this altitude they should not be here, and they should not be this numerous. They move with a quick deliberation through the vegetation, browsing on the ferns and the broadleaf plants, and they have made runways under the moss, tunnels that connect their burrow-entrances in the rock crevices to their feeding grounds. You catch one watching you from the mouth of a burrow, its nose twitching, and it makes a sound—a thin, high whistle that cuts through the lower frequencies of wind and water—and is answered by another whistle from somewhere unseen, and you understand that the ledge has its own acoustic, its own conversation happening just at the edge of your comprehension.

Insects too. Small dark flies that hover in the mist near the water curtain. Beetles that move with slow purpose along the wet channels, their shells iridescent in the moments when sun breaks through. And something else—something you notice only because it moves differently—a pale, elongated creature, like a moth but not a moth, that drifts along the cliff face just beyond reach, its wings barely moving, riding the updrafts with an almost mechanical precision. It is the first thing you have seen here that does not quite fit, and you watch it until it rounds the corner of the wall and is gone, and you are not entirely certain it was real.

---

At the back of the ledge, where the water curtain falls into its stone trough, there is a recess in the cliff wall, a shallow overhang perhaps three meters deep, and under this overhang someone has built. The construction is simple: walls of stacked stone, unmortared, fitted with enough care that they have stood against the wind for years or decades but with enough gaps that the air moves through them. A single room, barely two meters across, with a floor of flat stones laid on packed earth and a low entrance that requires you to crouch. Inside, the sound changes—the water curtain is just outside, and its hiss fills the space like a presence, but the wind is cut to a whisper, and for a moment the relief is physical, a dropping of the shoulders you did not know you were holding high.

There are marks on the walls. Not pictures—just scratches, tally-marks or something like them, grouped in fives and crossed through. Charcoal on stone. And on the floor, pushed against the back wall, a platform of sorts—more flat stones, arranged as a bed or a shelf—and beside it, a thing that makes you pause: a hand-sized cobble of granite with a groove worn into it from use, a grinding stone, and beside it, scattered on the floor, the pale fragments of something that was once a nut or a seed, long since dried to husks. Someone lived here. Not for long, perhaps, but long enough to grind food, long enough to mark the days, long enough to need walls against the wind and the void.

The cobble fits your hand perfectly. The groove in it is smooth from use, and you can feel where the maker's fingers—because it must have been held, turned, worked against another stone—wore the granite as much as the granite wore what it ground. You set it back where you found it. The room feels less empty for your having touched it, and also more empty, because you are now aware of the absence of the person whose hand wore that groove.

---

Outside again, the wind takes you. The sky has changed while you were under the overhang—clouds have moved in from somewhere, not the heavy clouds of weather but the thin high wisps that catch light and spread it, and the ledge is brighter now, the colors of the vegetation more saturated, the water curtain silver and flashing. The birds are louder. One of the grey not-eagles has taken a perch on a jutting stone at the ledge's edge and is calling with a sustained, falling cry that echoes off the cliff wall and comes back altered, deeper, as if the mountain is answering in its own voice. The pikas are whistling. The water is hissing. The wind is that constant low roar that you have almost stopped hearing because it is not a sound but a condition, a medium, the thing through which all other sounds travel.

You walk to the edge—carefully, because the rock is wet and the vegetation is slick and the drop is absolute—and you look out. The world below is a patchwork of green and grey and the silver thread of a river too distant to hear, and the world above is rock and cloud and the pale open sky, and you are suspended between them on this shelf that should not exist, this ledge that holds water and life and the marks of a human hand against the vast indifferent emptiness.

A gust of wind hits the cliff face below you and the updraft lifts the mist from the water curtain and carries it out over the void in a twisting ribbon that catches the light and for a moment you are standing inside a rainbow, the colors faint and shifting, and the sound is so loud and so layered that it becomes something else—not noise but texture, not sound but weather, and you understand that this place is not quiet, has never been quiet, will never be quiet, that the sound is as much a feature of the landscape as the rock and the water and the life, and that the silence you thought you heard in the drop was not silence at all but the sound of so many things happening at such scale that your ears could not parse them separately and registered the totality as absence.

The pika whistles. The bird screams. The water falls. The ledge holds.

You stay longer than you intended.
