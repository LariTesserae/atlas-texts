# The Drowned Works

The first thing to understand is that there is no ground.

There is floor — there is that. A surface beneath, yes, curved and giving and present in the way that anything must be present when it fills the space your body would fall through. But ground implies something natural, something that rose or settled according to processes without intention. What lies below is not that. What lies below was made.

---

The floor extends in every direction. Not flat — it was never flat, even before the aging — but smooth in the way that only long time can make smooth. The channels and ridges that remain are the ghosts of joints, of seams where sections were fitted together by hands that worked without metal, without wheels, without any labor-saving device beyond the lever and the ramp and the patience of a species that had more patience than anyone now can account for. The seams are nearly gone now. Two billion years of cold water flowing over them has worn them to whispers, to hints, to places where the texture changes almost imperceptibly under a palm that will never touch them. But they are there. The floor is sections. The floor was assembled.

It was all assembled.

---

The water is cold — not the dramatic cold of arctic currents, not the knife-edge cold that kills in minutes. This is the cold of deep time, the steady 2.4 degrees that saturates everything, that makes the water feel less like a medium and more like a condition. You do not notice it after a while. You become it. The cold is so consistent, so unvarying across the thousands of kilometers of the Works, that it functions less as temperature and more as a law. This is simply the way things are here. The water holds this temperature the way stone holds its shape — because it has held it so long that any other state has become inconceivable.

The water moves, but slowly. There are no currents to speak of, not in the open sense. There are gradients — slight differences in density, in salinity, in the minute warmth generated by the vegetation's metabolic processes — and these gradients produce movement, but movement on a timescale that makes glaciers look impulsive. A body of water here takes a century to shift from one chamber to the next. The Works breathe, but they breathe in geological time.

---

The vegetation is what you notice first, if noticing is even the right word for what happens in a place this dark.

There is almost no light. The water above — and there is water above, kilometers of it, a ceiling that is not a ceiling but simply the continuation of the medium — absorbs everything. No sun reaches here. No bioluminescence flickers. The darkness is so complete that the word "dark" feels insufficient; this is not the absence of light but the presence of something else, a substance that fills the visual field with its own weight. You learn to see without seeing. You learn that the darkness has texture, and that the texture changes where the growth is thickest.

And the growth is thick.

The plants — if plants is what they are; they photosynthesize nothing, they have never seen the sun, their relationship to surface vegetation is the relationship between a whale and a fossil — the plants are everywhere. They rise from the floor in columns and stalks and fronds that reach upward not toward light but toward the slight chemical gradients that provide their energy. Some of them are meters across at the base. Some of them reach thirty meters or more, swaying in the imperceptible currents like trees in slow motion, their surfaces ridged and folded to maximize contact with the water that feeds them. They are dark — not green, not any color that surface photosynthesis would produce — but dark the way the water is dark, a darkness that matches its surroundings so perfectly that you feel them more than you see them. A pressure. A presence. A thickening of the already-thick.

They grow in layers. The tallest stalks form a canopy, though canopy is the wrong word for something that has never known a sky. Beneath them, shorter growths cluster in the chemical shadow — not light shadow, but the depletion zones where the tall ones have already harvested what the water carries. Beneath those, a layer of low, spreading mats that cling to the floor and to the constructed surfaces, feeding on whatever settles, whatever the upper layers discard. And threading through all of it, a network of tendrils — connecting, entangling, merging at points into single organisms and separating at others into distinct individuals. The boundaries between one plant and the next are unclear. The boundaries between the plants and the structures they grow on are even less clear.

The vegetation is so dense in places that it forms walls. Not literal walls — you can push through, the fronds parting around you with the slow reluctance of something that has never needed to hurry — but functional ones. In the deep chambers, the growth is so thick that you could travel for days without finding an opening wide enough to pass. The plants have colonized everything. They have had time enough to colonize everything.

---

And they have had things to colonize.

The Works — the built environment, the made world — are vast. Continental in scale, though continent is a word that assumes land, assumes something above the water, assumes geography that this place has never known. The structures cover an area that would span time zones if there were a sun to mark them. They are the floor. They are also the walls, where walls exist. They are the pillars that rise from the floor at intervals that were once regular and are now only approximately so, their edges softened by the ages into something organic, something that the vegetation has embraced and incorporated and made its own.

The construction is primitive. This is the most haunting thing about it. Whatever hands made these works — and hands they must have been, the tool marks are still faintly visible on surfaces sheltered from the current, the distinctive grooves of stone on stone, of patient abrasion — they had nothing that we would recognize as technology. No metal. No machinery. No chemical engineering. They had stone and wood and fiber and bone and the extraordinary, incomprehensible dedication to build on a scale that dwarfs anything our own civilization has attempted, using nothing but their bodies and their time.

The chambers range from intimate — rooms the size of houses, with ceilings low enough that the vegetation chokes them full, leaving only narrow channels through the growth — to cavernous. The great halls. They stretch for kilometers, their ceilings lost in the darkness above, their floors covered in the low mats of vegetation that thrive in the open water. The pillars in these halls are massive, each one carved from a single block of something that might be stone but might also be some manufactured composite, some material that the builders produced through processes we cannot now identify. They are spaced with a precision that reveals intention — this was not organic growth, not the accretion of generations adding to what their parents built. This was planned. Someone, somehow, conceived of this entire structure and then oversaw its construction over timescales that must have spanned millennia.

The walls are carved. Not decorated — carved the way language is carved, the way information is stored when you have no other medium. The patterns are dense, repetitive, covering every surface that the vegetation has not claimed. They do not depict anything. They are not representational. They are sequences, rhythms, a system of marks that varies in ways too subtle for casual observation but too consistent to be decorative. Someone can read these walls. Or could have. Whether anyone still can — whether anyone exists who could — is a question that the Works themselves do not answer.

---

There is no animal life.

This is perhaps the most disorienting aspect of the place, once you have been here long enough for the other strangenesses to fade. The vegetation is so lush, so dense, so obviously thriving that it creates an expectation of inhabitation. You feel that something should be moving through these stalks. Something should be feeding on the growth, should be feeding on each other, should be living the ordinary violent cycle of consumption that life performs everywhere else. But there is nothing. The plants grow and die and decompose and grow again, and the cycle completes itself without any participant that has eyes or mouths or legs or will. The decomposition is performed by the plants themselves — the lower mats, the ground-cover, the pale tendrils that work their way into everything organic and reduce it to the chemical components that feed the next generation. The ecosystem is closed. The ecosystem is patient.

It is also silent. Not absolutely — the water does move, and when it moves through the dense vegetation, there is a sound: a low, continuous murmur, like breathing heard from very far away. The structures themselves occasionally groan, settling by millimeters over centuries, the stress redistributing through joints that have been under pressure since before multicellular life existed on certain continents. But these sounds are so infrequent, so subtle, that they register not as noise but as the occasional reminder that silence is not the same as absence. The silence here is the silence of a place that has said everything it needs to say and is now simply existing in the aftermath of its own statement.

---

The erosion has been kind, in its way.

The Works are old — not thousands of years old, not millions, but something closer to the age of the seafloor itself, which is to say ancient beyond any reckoning that human language was designed to express. They should not exist. The water should have dissolved them, the pressure should have collapsed them, the slow chemistry of dissolution should have returned their materials to the medium from which they came. But the builders understood something about stability. They constructed their works to survive the process of not-surviving — to erode in ways that maintained structural integrity, to settle into forms that the pressure could not further reduce, to become the shapes that water makes when water is given enough time to have opinions about geometry.

The result is that the Works have reached their final form. They are not degrading any longer, not in any meaningful sense. They have become what they will be until something on a tectonic scale disrupts them. The edges are smooth. The corners are rounded. The carvings on the walls are soft-focused, their sharp lines blurred into gentle variations in relief, but still legible — or legible enough, or as legible as they need to be for whatever purpose they serve. The structures have become landscape. They have the inevitability of landscape, the sense that they could not have been otherwise, that even the intention that made them was simply another natural process working itself out.

The vegetation has accelerated this integration. The roots — not roots in the terrestrial sense, but holdfasts, anchors, the structures that the plants use to fix themselves in place — have worked their way into every joint and seam, and rather than disrupting the architecture, they have stabilized it. The plants hold the Works together the way mortar holds a wall. They have become structural. The built environment and the grown environment have merged into something that is neither and both, a hybrid that would have been impossible to design but was inevitable in the making.

---

The scale defies comprehension.

You can travel for weeks — moving through the channels that the vegetation has left open, following the paths of least resistance through chambers and halls and corridors that were never meant for anything like you — and never see the same place twice. You can travel for weeks and never reach an edge. The Works continue. They continue past the point where the carvings change style, where the pillar-spacing alters, where the floor-material shifts from one subtle texture to another. They continue past the point where the vegetation thins and then thickens again, responding to chemical gradients that shift in long waves across the continental surface. They continue past the point where your sense of direction has become so compromised by the darkness and the sameness that you could be walking in circles and would never know it.

But you are not walking in circles. The Works are too vast for circles, at least at human scale. You are walking in a line — or something like a line, something that approximates linear travel across a surface that was designed with a logic that you cannot access. The chambers connect in ways that suggest purpose. The corridors lead somewhere. The layout is not random, even now, even after the vegetation has blocked some passages and opened others, even after the settling has shifted floors and narrowed ceilings. There is a plan beneath the plan, an organization that predates the current state and persists through it, like a skeleton beneath skin.

You could spend a lifetime mapping the Works and not reach the boundaries. You could spend a lifetime and not determine whether there are boundaries. The structure may be infinite, in the practical sense — infinite for any being that moves at any speed we can conceive of, that lives for any duration we can measure. It may cover the entire floor of an ocean that itself covers an entire world. It may be the floor of the ocean. It may be that what we call the seafloor is simply the upper surface of the Works, and that the construction extends downward for kilometers, for tens of kilometers, that we have only ever seen the roof of something whose foundations are as deep as the planet's core.

This is the thought that stays with you, in the dark, in the cold, in the silence where the only movement is the slow sway of vegetation that has never known urgency. This is the thought that the carvings on the walls might address, if anyone could read them. This is the thought that the Works themselves embody: that someone, once, built a world inside a world, and that world endured past its builders, past their memory, past the very concept of memory, and is still here, still holding its shape, still waiting for whatever it was designed to receive.

The water moves through the chambers like breath through lungs.

The vegetation sways.

The darkness continues.
