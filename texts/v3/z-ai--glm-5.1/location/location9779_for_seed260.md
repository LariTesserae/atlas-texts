# The Ascendant Plateau

The first thing is the sound.

Not a sound — *the* sound. A single sustained note that has been sounding since before you arrived and will continue after you leave, a chord composed of geologic pressure and the vibration of things becoming other things. It comes from below and above and from inside the bones of your skull. The rock itself is humming. The air is humming. After enough hours — and hours here are measured by exhaustion rather than light, for the light does not change — you stop hearing it the way you stop feeling your own heartbeat. It becomes the condition of experience rather than an object within experience.

---

The plateau rises two and a half kilometers above what might charitably be called a floor, though from the plateau's edge you cannot see bottom. The surrounding country is a topology of near-vertical faces and shattered columns, a forest of mineral spires that the maps — if maps existed, if cartography could survive the journey here — would render as a thicket of lines. You are on the highest of the high places. The air is thin and cool and tastes of iron and absence.

The rock underfoot is new.

This is the strangest thing, or the first strangest thing: the stone has the look of something still deciding what it will be. It is not the confident granite of old mountains, worn smooth by patient eons. It is not sedimentary, layered and lithified and finished. It is *in progress*. You can see where pressure is still folding it, where mineral seams are still differentiating, where the crystalline structure of the mass is still sorting itself into alignment. The surface under your boot crunches slightly — not from grit or gravel but from the stone's own uncertainty, its refusal yet to commit to hardness. Walk too slowly and you can feel it settling beneath you, not giving way but *accommodating*, as though the ground recognizes the pressure of a foot and agrees to become floor for it.

This is an emergent geology. The plateau is still rising. The process is agonizingly slow — a centimeter a century, perhaps — but the evidence is everywhere in the way the rock striates lean upward, in the fresh faces exposed at the margins, in the way the edges of the plateau have not yet learned to erode into roundness. Everything is angle and assertion. The mountain is still speaking itself into shape.

And it is loud. God, it is loud. The subsonic groan of tectonic compression, the higher shriek of stone micro-fracturing under stress, the constant low rumble of mass negotiating with mass. These sounds do not echo because there is nothing to echo against — the plateau stands alone, a flat-topped island in a sea of vertical air. The sounds simply propagate outward and dissolve into the dim sky.

---

The sky: a permanent flat dusk. Not gray exactly, but a color that makes gray seem vibrant by comparison. The light seems to arrive from no particular direction. There is no sun visible — whether it is hidden by perpetual cloud or by some other arrangement is unclear, and after a time the distinction ceases to matter. The illumination is sufficient to see by but insufficient to cast shadows. Everything exists in the same even un-light, the same diffuse revelation. Depth becomes a matter of inference rather than perception. Distance is guesswork.

This is why the vegetation is so startling.

---

It grows everywhere — not in the clustered, competitive way of forest or meadow but in the wide, deliberate spacing of things that do not need to compete because they have already won. Each specimen stands alone, separated from its nearest neighbor by ten meters, by thirty, by a hundred. The density is low but the coverage is total: across the plateau's extent, which stretches to the limit of the strange dim visibility, the plants are distributed like stars in a sparse galaxy, everywhere present but nowhere touching.

They are not like plants you have seen.

They are tall — many of them waist-high, some reaching overhead — and they grow in spirals, in fractal iterations of the same corkscrew form, each leaf a slightly smaller version of the branch it grows from, each branch a slightly smaller version of the central stem. The effect is of an algorithm made vegetative, a mathematical curve extruded into three dimensions and given the faintest greenish cast. They have no roots that you can find. They grow directly from the still-forming stone, their bases fused with the rock in a way that suggests they are not so much *in* the stone as *of* it — mineral accretions with the ambition of botany.

And they are lush. Despite the absence of water — and there is no water here, none at all, the air is dry as calcined bone, the rock is dry, your own skin begins to desiccate within hours — despite this, the spiraling plants are turgid and full. Their leaves are thick and slightly waxy. When you break one — and it snaps with a sound like a tendon parting — the interior is moist, a pale gelatinous sap that tastes of copper and static electricity and something else, something your tongue has no category for. The plants are drinking from somewhere. Not from any reservoir you can identify. From the process itself, perhaps. From the energy of becoming. They are feeding on the plateau's continued formation, transducing geologic stress into vegetable matter, and they are thriving.

No animals move among them. No insects visit their strange flowers — for they do flower, small pale nodules at the apex of each spiral that emit a faint phosphorescence in the un-light, a glow barely brighter than the ambient dimness but discernible by its color, a cold blue-white that seems to pulse in sync with the plateau's subsonic drone. The flowers open and close on a cycle unrelated to any day-night rhythm you can detect. Perhaps they are listening to frequencies you cannot hear. Perhaps they are keeping time with the mountain's slow rise.

---

The cairns appear as you walk further from the plateau's edge, deeper into its interior.

They are small — none higher than your knee — and they are everywhere, distributed with the same sparse regularity as the plants but independent of them, never adjacent, as though the builders and the vegetation had agreed to respect each other's territories. Each cairn is a careful stack of the plateau's own stone, five or seven or eleven flat pieces balanced with a precision that suggests ritual rather than utility. The stones are not shaped — the tools for shaping are absent from this place, and the rock resists alteration anyway, still too young, too uncommitted — but they are selected. Each cairn uses stones of similar thickness, similar hue. Someone chose them. Someone carried them. Someone stacked them with hands and patience and a purpose you cannot read.

The cairns are the only built things. There are no walls, no shelters, no paths worn into the still-soft stone. Whoever made them did not stay, or could not stay, or stays still in a form you cannot recognize. The dehydration here is aggressive. The sound is relentless. The light does not change. After a day — or what feels like a day — your thoughts begin to spiral like the plants, each one a slightly smaller iteration of the one before, fractaling down into recursive silence.

You wonder if the cairns are markers. You wonder if they are prayers. You wonder if they are the only possible response to a place where reality has begun to thin.

---

Because it has thinned. This is what the sound is, underneath the tectonic groaning and the stone-micro-fractures: the sound of *coherence failing*. The plateau exists at an altitude where the world's fabric is stretched to transparency, where the weave of matter and law begins to fray, and the loudness is the sound of that fraying, the constant ripping of the real from itself, the noise of things almost but not quite ceasing to be things.

You see it in the peripheral flickers. A section of stone that, for a half-second, is not stone but something else — a lattice of light, a gap where color should be, a texture that has no name because it belongs to no surface in this world. You see it in the plants themselves, which occasionally shimmer at their edges, their spiral forms seeming to iterate one too many times, the fractal collapsing inward toward an infinite zero that their physical structure somehow survives. You see it in the air, which sometimes is not air but a medium you cannot breathe for a single startled moment before it becomes air again, your lungs full of something that was not oxygen but that your body, desperate and adaptable, used anyway.

The plateau is a wound in the world's skin. Or it is a mouth. Or it is an eye, the spiraling plants its lashes, the cairns its tears, the sound its unblinking attention. The geologic emergence is not merely tectonic — it is *ontological*. The mountain is rising because reality is rising through it, welling up from underneath the world, pushing through the crust the way magma pushes through a caldera, except what emerges is not fire but *possibility*, raw and undifferentiated and deafening with its own potential.

This is why there are no animals. Animals require a certain stability of being, a confidence in the boundary between self and not-self. Here that boundary breathes. Here the self is a provisional arrangement, a local eddy in the flow of becoming, and anything with a nervous system complex enough to notice would also be complex enough to unravel. The plants survive because they are simple enough to be indefinite — because they are already so close to mineral, already so near to the process that made them, that the fraying does not catch in them. They simply persist, drinking from the emergence, spiraling in the un-light, flowering in pulses that sync with the world's slow dissolution.

---

Stand at the plateau's center. Close your eyes — it makes no difference, the light is even with or without them, the sound fills the darkness. Feel the stone under your feet, still deciding to be stone. Feel the subsonic vibration in your long bones, your ribcage resonating like the cairns' final stone. Feel the air, thick with the absence of water, thin with altitude, shimmering at the edge of not-air.

The plateau is still rising.

You are still rising.

The world below — the world of water and animals and confident geology, the world where things are what they are and stay what they are — that world is receding, not in distance but in plausibility, becoming a story you once heard about a place that made sense. Here, above the tree line and the water line and the reality line, everything is still becoming. The sound is the sound of that becoming. It is deafening. It is the loudest thing you have ever heard.

It is also the quietest — because it is the sound of a place where nothing has yet happened, where everything is still on the verge of happening, where the next moment is being constructed from scratch by a geology that has not yet learned to stop.

The cairns say: *someone was here. Someone stacked these stones. Someone stood where you are standing and felt what you are feeling and chose, instead of unraveling, to make a small mark. A counterweight to the fraying. A point of stillness in the deafening becoming.*

It will not hold. The plateau is still rising. The sound continues. The light does not change.
