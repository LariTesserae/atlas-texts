# The Underheat

The first thing you notice is the warmth. It comes from everywhere and nowhere — not radiating off a surface, not venting from a crack, just *there*, uniform and patient, like the inside of a closed hand. The air is thick. Not humid — there is no water here, none at all, has never been — but *dense*, saturated with something that presses against the lungs like warm fabric. You breathe it and it breathes back.

---

You are underground. This is the only truth the place offers freely.

Not a cave. Caves imply boundaries — walls, a ceiling, a floor, an outside. This is a *continuation*. A vastness that does not acknowledge the surface above it, that has no reason to, that may in fact be older than the surface, or may be the surface's only real part, the crust being nothing but a thin and temporary scab. The chambers go on. And on. And on again after that, each one opening into the next without transition or threshold, a parliament of vastnesses, a legislature of space. Continental in scale. The word "cavern" degrades into meaninglessness here. These are *provinces*.

The rock — if it is rock — has the look of something that was recently molten and then decided, mid-flow, to simply stop. Not cool. Not solidify in the way that lava solidifies, with the slow patience of heat surrendering to air. This material *chose* its shape. The walls are sharp, uneroded, their angles still holding the intention of whatever force made them. Ridges run in long parallel lines like the wake of something enormous that passed through the medium of the world itself. There are shelves, overhangs, fluted columns that have never known wind or water — only the still, warm pressure of the deep. Everything is angular and fresh. The place has no erosion because it has no time. Or rather, it has time, but time does nothing here. Nothing changes. The equilibrium is so total it feels deliberate, as if the world inhaled once and then held its breath forever.

The floor is not flat. It undulates in great slow waves, rises into ridges you must climb over, drops into basins you descend into with care. The surface underfoot is smooth but not slippery — it *grips*, slightly, like skin. The color is hard to determine in the darkness, but what little light there is — and there is so little — suggests deep umber, rust, a mineral red-brown that goes almost black in the shadows. Occasionally a vein of paler material runs through the rock, and these veins are warm to the touch. Warmer than the air. They pulse, very slowly, on a timescale you can almost feel but never quite confirm.

---

There is no water. Let this settle into you fully. No water. No seepage, no drip, no mineral dampness, no hydration of any kind. The chemistry here runs on other terms. When you touch the wall your hand comes away dry — not the dryness of desert air, which is an *absence* that aches, but a kind of structural dryness, a dryness that is the default condition, that has never known wetness and therefore does not miss it. The air itself is non-aqueous. Something else carries the density — mineral particulates, perhaps, or a gaseous medium that serves whatever metabolism the place runs on. Your lips do not chap. Your skin does not crack. The dryness does not take from you because it does not understand taking. It simply *is*, and you are in it.

---

The darkness is not absolute. This is important to understand, because absolute darkness would be simpler. Absolute darkness you could put a name to. This is the darkness of *just below visibility* — the darkness where you can almost see, where your eyes keep finding shapes at the edge of perception and then losing them, where the space in front of you has a texture of dimness rather than an absence of light. Something provides faint illumination. Not bioluminescence — nothing here glows. Not phosphorescence in the rock. It is more like the heat itself becomes visible at certain angles, at certain densities, a shimmer in the air that the eye interprets as a kind of dark red *suggestion* of light, like the afterimage of a fire you never saw. You navigate partly by this, partly by touch, partly by a sense that develops after a few hours — a pressure-awareness, the ability to feel the space around you through the subtle shifts in the warm dense air when a wall is near or a passage opens.

And then, occasionally, the built things.

---

They are rare enough that you might walk for days without seeing one. But they are there. Low structures, barely reaching your waist, made from the same material as the surrounding rock but worked — you can see the tool marks, the deliberate scraping, the patient hand-removal of material. The shapes are difficult to interpret. Some are rings of stacked stone, like nests or enclosures. Some are platforms, slightly raised, slightly flatter than the natural floor. Some are just *marks* — grooves cut into the rock in patterns too regular to be natural, lines and angles that might be decorative, might be functional, might be something for which your language has no category. The technology is primitive. Pre-metal. Stone on stone, patient and grinding. Whatever made these had time — the kind of time that only exists in a place where nothing changes.

Who made them? The answer is in the dark, moving.

---

They are abundant. That is the strangest thing. In a world without water, without vegetation, without any of the substrates that surface-dwellers understand as the foundations of life, they are *everywhere*. You hear them before you see them — not sound exactly, because the place is mostly quiet, but a *disturbance* in the silence, a texture of presence that replaces the blankness of vacancy with the density of near-touching bodies. The air shifts. The warmth redistributes. Something is here.

The fauna — and calling them that is a concession to taxonomy that may be inaccurate, but your language has no better word — the fauna are not like surface animals. They do not graze. There is nothing to graze on. They do not hunt each other, or if they do, you never see it. They *are*, in the same way the rock is and the warmth is — fundamentally, without apparent cause or sustenance. They exist in the gaps and the folds of the vastness, in the basins and on the ridges, in numbers that become apparent only slowly, as your eyes adjust to the almost-dark and your pressure-sense sharpens.

They are roughly your size. Some larger. Their bodies are low and many-legged — you count four, six, eight, the number varies — and their surfaces have the same umber-red as the rock, mottled with the paler vein-color at the joints. They move slowly. Not because they are sluggish but because speed is not a concept the place has ever required. There is nothing to chase. Nothing to flee. The equilibrium extends to them too. They exist in a state of continuous, unhurried *occupation*, traversing the chambers in patterns you cannot discern, pausing at the built structures, touching them with the tips of their limbs — appendages that are too delicate to be called legs and too purposeful to be called antennae — and then moving on.

They make sound, but barely. A low clicking, like stone on stone, that carries in the dense air for a few meters and then dissolves. Sometimes a scraping. Sometimes a vibration you feel through the floor more than hear with your ears. They communicate, or they do something that occupies the same functional niche as communication. They are aware of each other. They are aware of you, in the same way they are aware of the walls — as a presence, a density-change in the medium, something that occupies space and therefore matters.

Their metabolism — and again, the word is wrong but necessary — is geological. They eat the warmth. Or rather, they subsist on the gradient between the pale warm veins and the cooler surrounding rock, running some process along that boundary that replaces eating, replaces drinking, replaces breathing. They are chemoautotrophic on a mineral substrate, or they are something entirely other, something that uses the planet's internal heat the way surface life uses sunlight — as a primary input, an original cause, a reason to be. They do not drink. They do not eat. They do not die, or if they do, they do so on a timescale that makes the question irrelevant. They simply persist, as the rock persists, as the warmth persists.

They made the structures. You understand this not because you see them building — you never see them building — but because there is no one else. The tool-marks match the tips of their limbs. The shapes of the structures correspond to the shapes of their bodies. The rings are the size of their resting forms. The platforms are the height they reach when they raise the front portions of their bodies. The grooves — the strange, regular grooves — are spaced at the intervals of their steps. They are builders, in the same way they are inhabitants: fundamentally, without spectacle, without urgency, because the place allows for nothing else. They have always been building. They will always be building. The structures accumulate, slowly, one per generation or one per eon — you cannot tell which — and the result is the scattered markers you find, each one a word in a sentence too long for any reader.

---

The quiet is the final strangeness. Not silence — the place is never silent, not with the clicking and the shifting and the faint geothermal murmur that underlies everything like a bass note too low to hear but too present to ignore. But quiet, yes. The quiet of a world that has said everything it needs to say and is now simply *being* its statement. There is no weather here — the vastness generates no atmospheric dynamics, no fronts, no pressure gradients. There is no day, no night, no season. There is only the warmth and the almost-dark and the slow patient movement of the many-legged builders through their endless home.

You could stay here. This is the danger the place presents, the threat it does not know it carries. You could stay, and after a while the almost-dark would seem like enough light, and the warm dense air would seem like enough breath, and the quiet would seem like enough sound, and you would slow down — you would slow down to match the speed of the builders, the speed of the rock, the speed of the unchanging — and you would become another presence in the density, another body in the near-touching layers, another small warm thing moving through the provinces of the deep.

The place does not want this. It does not want anything. That is the point. That is what makes it so easy to stay.
