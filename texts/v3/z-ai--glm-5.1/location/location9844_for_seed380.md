# The Weeping Gallery

It goes down. Not far—thirty meters, perhaps forty—but enough that the surface becomes a rumor, a memory of a memory, something the oldest residents recall only as a brightness that once hurt their eyes.

The gallery is a single chamber carved from the underside of the city. Cathedral-sized, roughly. The ceiling arches in segments, segmented iron ribs bolted into bedrock, the rivets orange with age and weeping. Everything weeps here. The rock holds water like a grudge, and it releases it slowly—so slowly—in constant, barely perceptible exudation. The walls gleam. The floor is wet. Not flooded, not running, just *wet*, as if the stone itself has become saturated past the point of endurance and can do nothing but perspire. Puddles form in the low places and never quite evaporate. The air is heavy with condensation; it beads on every surface, gathers, traces paths downward in thin erratic lines. You can taste the minerals when you breathe—iron, calcium, something faintly sulfurous, something older.

---

The built elements are old. Old and settled, the way a tree settles into the soil after centuries—no longer fighting the ground, but belonging to it. Iron pillars descend at regular intervals, each one flaked with rust that has stabilized into something almost like patina, almost like intention. They do not look fragile. They look like they have rusted exactly as far as they needed to and have stopped, satisfied. Between them, the remnants of infrastructure: pipe runs along the upper walls, copper and lead, some still connected to junction boxes with ceramic insulators the color of dried bone. A few of the boxes hum. Faintly. A low, intermittent electric vibration that you feel more than hear, that makes your back teeth ache if you stand too close. The current is still flowing. It has been flowing, they say, since before the gallery was abandoned—through some circuit that no living person has traced, powered by something no living person has located. The machines it feeds are small and unidentifiable, bolted to the walls at odd intervals, and they do nothing visible. They simply persist.

The main floor was once organized. You can see the logic of it: the parallel channels cut into the stone for drainage, the mounting plates where heavier equipment stood, the standardized spacing of anchor bolts. But the equipment is gone, removed in some long-ago phase of the city's industrial metabolism, and what remains is the skeleton—negative space where function used to live. The channels carry thin sheets of water now, moving so slowly they seem to shimmer in place.

There are walkways. Iron grating, elevated a half-meter above the wet floor, painted long ago in a gray-green that has chipped into islands of color surrounded by bare metal. The grating shifts slightly underfoot, and water squeezes up through the mesh with each step, making a soft sound like a mouth opening.

---

The vegetation is almost nonexistent. A pale lichen—nearly white, nearly translucent—grows in patches on the dampest stretches of wall, preferring the places where the weeping is most consistent. It forms small circular colonies, rarely larger than a hand, and it seems to thrive on mineral water alone. No light reaches here that the lichen would recognize. It has forgotten what light is for.

But the fauna—*

The fauna is abundant, and it is wrong.

Not malevolent. Not threatening, exactly. But wrong in the way that things become wrong when they evolve in isolation, in dimness, in the particular chemical soup of an underground industrial chamber that has been slowly dripping its machinery into the water table for a century and a half.

The most visible are the crustaceans. Pale, elongated things, the color of the lichen or the condensation—translucent-white, blue-veined beneath their chitin. They move across the wet floors in slow processions, following the drainage channels, pausing at the puddles to filter-feed. They are the size of a hand, or a forearm, and they have too many legs. Not the correct number for any crustacean above, and not a consistent number between individuals. Twelve, sixteen, twenty-two. They do not hurry. They move with the patience of things that have never had a reason to move faster, that measure their lives in the slow accumulation of minerals from seepage. When they reach a junction box that hums, they cluster around it. They press their abdomens to the warm metal. They seem to listen, or to feed on something the electricity produces that you cannot sense.

Above them, in the iron ribs of the ceiling, the moths. They are the only source of movement in the upper gallery, and they are the reason there is any light at all. Their wings are faintly luminescent—a cold, greenish glow, like the last light in a dying indicator lamp. They cluster in the hundreds, perhaps thousands, and their combined illumination is just enough to see by: a dim, shifting, sourceless gray-green that makes the wet walls gleam and turns the puddles into mirrors. They are large, the moths. Palm-wide. Their bodies are dark and furred, and when they move—which is rarely, and slowly—they make a sound like fabric brushing against fabric. They seem to live their entire lives in the ceiling. No one has seen them feed. No one has seen them reproduce. They are simply there, in their hundreds, glowing and still, and the gallery depends on them for sight.

And in the water—in the thin sheets, the slow channels, the standing puddles—there are worms. Thin, red, coiled. They look like the veins that run through the rock, brought to the surface. They pulse, very faintly, in a rhythm that does not match your heartbeat but eventually, if you stay long enough, will come to match the hum of the junction boxes.

---

The sound of the gallery is the sound of patience. Water dripping, but not quickly—a single drop every four or five seconds, from some point in the ceiling that no one has located, into a puddle that has been receiving that same rhythm for as long as anyone can remember. The hum of the current. The faint settling of iron that has been settling for so long it has become a kind of breathing. The soft clicking of crustacean legs on wet stone. The rustle of moth wings, intermittent, like someone turning pages very slowly in another room.

If you stand still—if you become patient enough—the gallery will accept you into its rhythm. Your breathing will slow. Your heartbeat will try to match the pulse of the worms. The dimness will become a kind of clarity, and you will begin to see the architecture of the water: the way it moves across the floor in imperceptible rivers, the way it chooses its paths, the way it has been choosing those same paths for decades and has carved grooves into concrete that was once flat.

The gallery is in balance. It has been in balance for a long time. Nothing here is growing. Nothing here is decaying. Everything is simply *persisting*, in the way that things persist when they have found their shape and have no reason to change.

The city above continues. It paves and demolishes and electrifies and argues and eats and sleeps. The gallery does not care. The hum continues. The water weeps. The crustaceans press themselves to the warm boxes and listen to whatever the electricity tells them. The moths glow. The worms pulse.

And below—below even the gallery, below the iron pillars and the mounting plates and the channels cut for drainage that now carry different waters—there is a sound. So low it is not a sound. A vibration. A patient, enormous, slow thing that the junction boxes are listening to, that the crustaceans are feeding on, that the worms are synchronized with.

Something is down there.

Something has always been down there.

And the gallery—this wet, dim, patient, half-industrial cathedral—has grown around it the way a pearl grows around an irritant: in layers, slowly, becoming a thing that is not about the irritant but cannot be understood without it.

Stay if you like. The gallery does not mind. It has been accepting visitors for a long time, and it has never once asked one to leave.
