# The Hold

It has a name, though no one speaks it aloud anymore. Sound carries strangely here, and the things that live in the walls listen.

---

You enter through a split in the bark—vertical, roughly the height of a person, narrowing at the middle like a wound trying to close. The tree that holds the Hold is not a tree in any way that would satisfy a botanist. It is closer to a thought that wood once had. Its trunk measures forty paces across at the base, though "base" is a misnomer; it has no roots anyone has found. It simply rests on the cold ground, half-buried in the dark loam, and the loam has been slowly climbing it for as long as anyone can remember. Which is not long. The Hold is young. The split in the bark opened only three seasons back, and the first settlers—nine of them, from the ridge-camps to the north—came through with nothing but stone knives and gut-thread and the knowledge that warmer ground lay somewhere below.

They found the cavity.

It is not large. You could cross it in twenty paces if you walked straight, but nothing in the Hold permits straight walking. The interior of the trunk is hollow—not cleanly, not like a pipe, but in the way that rot leaves behind when it finishes eating the soft tissue and moves on. What remains is a architecture of fiber: great curving ribs of heartwood that arch overhead like the vaulting of some submerged cathedral, their surfaces still weeping sap that has nowhere to go. The sap pools in the low places. The floor is never dry.

---

The settlement clings to the walls.

There are eleven dwellings now, such as they are. Each one is grown rather than built: the residents have learned to score the inner bark with their stone blades in long horizontal cuts, then peel it back and fold it outward like a shelf. The bark remembers its shape. Within a day it stiffens into a ledge, and within three it has begun to thicken at the edge, secreting a pale corky substance that hardens into a lip. You can sleep on it. You can store things beneath it. The dwellings tier upward along the curve of the wall, connected by footholds carved into the heartwood ribs, and from the floor of the cavity they look like growths—shelf-fungi, bracket lichen, the kind of quiet appendages that attach themselves to dying surfaces.

No one knows if the tree is dying. It weeps constantly. The sap runs from dozens of fissures in the walls and ceiling, collecting in channels that the residents have deepened with their knives, directing the flow toward a central sump. The sump is a low place in the floor where the heartwood has rotted through entirely, opening onto a space below—another cavity, perhaps, or a root-channel, or something larger that no one has explored. The sap drains into it with a faint sound, almost a gulp, rhythmic and organic. At night the sound quickens. No one has explained why.

---

The cold is structural. It does not vary. Breath clouds in the cavity at all hours, and the residents wear layers of woven fiber—inner bark stripped from the tree itself, pounded soft, stitched with gut-thread—wrapped tight against the chill. Their hands stay wrapped too, only the fingers bare, and these are stained dark from the sap. Everything here is stained dark from the sap. The air tastes of it: green and mineral and faintly sweet, with an undertone of something fermented. After a few days you stop noticing. After a few weeks the taste is home.

Light does not reach the Hold. The entrance admits a diffuse gray glow during the overhead hours—never direct sun, only the attenuated light that filters through the canopy above the tree—but the cavity's depth swallows it within the first few paces. The residents do not use fire. The sap catches too easily; they learned this the first season when a dwelling burned and the wall behind it blistered and wept for weeks, the charred bark crackling and separating from the heartwood with sounds like small animals dying. Instead they rely on the bioluminescence that comes naturally to the interior. The fungus provides it.

---

The fungus is everywhere, and it is not alone.

The cavity's surfaces—the walls, the ceiling, the undersides of the dwellings, the heartwood ribs, the floor where the sap has soaked into the fiber—are colonized by a dense mat of growth that defies simple categorization. It is primarily fungal: a pale mycelial felt that forms the substrate, its hyphae threading through every crevice and pore, digesting what it can. But entangled with it are mosses, liverworts, patches of something that might be algae, and—deeper in, where the sap flows thickest—colonies of a gelatinous organism that has no name in the settlers' language. This last is the source of the light. It glows a faint blue-green, strongest where the sap runs, and in its illumination the Hold takes on the appearance of an underwater structure: everything rippling, everything damp, the dark spaces between the ribs suggesting depth rather than absence.

The fungus is also, in the residents' careful and pragmatic understanding, the ground. The floor of the cavity is not solid heartwood but a layered composite: decayed wood below, then mycelial felt, then a living crust of moss and lichen and the gelatinous colonies, all of it perpetually wet, all of it slowly digesting itself and rebuilding. You sink slightly when you walk. Your footprints fill with sap and glow.

---

And then there are the others.

The fauna emerged within the first season, and they have not stopped emerging.

The residents count nine distinct species, though they acknowledge this is probably wrong; new forms appear regularly, and some seem to be transitions between others. The dominant presence is the insects—not beetles, not ants, something between and beside, with segmented bodies the length of a thumb and mandibles that can score bark. They move in loose formations, not colonies, and they farm: they tend the fungal mats, pruning back the mosses, carrying spores from one wall to another, harvesting the gelatinous colonies in neat strips that they store in galleries they've carved into the softer wood. The residents have reached an accommodation with them. You do not disturb a gallery. You do not block a formation. In return, the insects ignore the dwellings and occasionally—accidentally, perhaps—prune the growth away from the entrance, keeping the split in the bark open.

The second species is harder to describe. They are vertebrates, or close enough: pale, eyeless, the size of a closed fist, with too many legs and a body that appears to flow rather than walk. The residents call them hands. They live in the walls, in the spaces between the bark and the heartwood, and they emerge at night to drink from the sap channels. They are silent. They move in groups of three or five, and they watch. Their heads—rounded, featureless except for a broad mouth—swivel to track movement, and if you approach one it will not flee but will instead rear up and open that mouth very wide, displaying rows of something that is not teeth. It is a threat display. It is also, the residents have learned, a greeting. The correct response is to open your own mouth. Whether this satisfies the hands or merely confuses them, they always retreat.

The remaining species are less prominent but no less present: worms that live in the sap and surface in the sump, their bodies opalescent; mites that swarm on the fungal mats, audible only as a faint crackling when their populations peak; something that flies—a membrane and a voice, that's all the residents have ever seen of it, passing through the cavity's upper reaches with a sound like a question asked in a language just beyond comprehension; and the things in the sump.

The residents do not discuss the things in the sump.

---

The Hold functions. This is perhaps the most remarkable thing about it, and the residents do not remark upon it because they have no basis for comparison. They have lived in the cavity for three seasons and they have not starved, which is the primary requirement of any settlement.

The insects provide indirectly. Their fungal cultivation produces fruiting bodies—small, dense, nutty-flavored—that the residents harvest from the walls. The harvesting is delicate work; you must take only the mature bodies and avoid disturbing the mycelial substrate, and you must watch for the mites, which swarm when their host fungus is damaged. The residents also eat the gelatinous colonies, though this requires careful processing: the raw substance causes numbness in the lips and tongue, and one of the early settlers ate too much of it and lost sensation in his face for eleven days. Boiling it in sap over a bark-trough with heated stones neutralizes whatever compound causes the numbness, and the resulting jelly is rich and faintly sweet, with the same mineral undertone as the air.

Water comes from the walls. The tree transpires constantly, moisture condensing on the inner surfaces and running down in thin films. The residents collect it in bark cups. It tastes of sap, of course. Everything tastes of sap.

---

The processes of the Hold never cease. The tree weeps. The fungus digests. The insects farm. The hands emerge and drink and watch. The sap drains into the sump with its rhythm that quickens at night. The mites peak and crash. The fruiting bodies swell and are harvested and swell again. The walls shift—imperceptibly, but they shift. The cavity is young and it is still finding its shape.

The residents have begun to notice changes. A new fissure in the ceiling, wet and raw. A deepening of the sump. A slight outward bow of the eastern wall, as though something were pressing from behind. The insects have extended their galleries higher than before. The hands have stopped retreating when residents approach and instead hold their ground, mouths open, waiting.

The air in the Hold tastes of sap and fermentation and something underneath both—a mineral sharpness, like stone dissolving. The walls glow blue-green. The sap gulps. The residents wrap their hands and check their dwellings and harvest what can be harvested, and when they sleep they sleep on bark shelves in a living room inside a living trunk in a living dark, and outside the canopy filters what light reaches this place and the cold ground slowly climbs, and the things in the sump wait.

The Hold does not have a future. The Hold does not have a past. The Hold is a process, and the process is young, and young things change.
