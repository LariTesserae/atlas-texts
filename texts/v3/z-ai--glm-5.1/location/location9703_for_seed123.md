# The Interfloor

You find it through a hatch that shouldn't exist — a manhole-sized circle of corroded metal set into the foundation of the old Harpenny Compression Works, half-buried in silt behind the boiler house. The rungs descending are warm to the touch. Not hot. Blood-warm. The kind of warmth that makes you check your palm for moisture.

---

The space below is roughly the size of a studio apartment, but the ceiling comes down to chest height in places, and the floor is never level twice. You have to stoop. You have to choose where to put your feet. This is the first rule of the Interfloor: there is no square meter that does not contain something.

The walls are the undersides of concrete foundations — raw, pale aggregate showing where the pour stopped short, rebar tips exposed like fractured bone. The floor is bedrock, or something close to it: a soft, clay-rich stone that hasn't seen atmosphere for perhaps a century, until the subsidences began. The building above settled eight inches between 1987 and 2003, and the earth beneath it shrugged and cracked, and this pocket opened like a blister. The ceiling has been slowly exuding warmth ever since.

---

The heat comes from above and below. The Harpenny plant's boiler system — decommissioned now but never fully cooled, its thermal mass still radiating through forty years of masonry — keeps the ceiling at roughly 34°C. The floor contributes its own geothermal trickle, a faint upwelling from a seam of decomposing sulfides that someone should have documented and no one did. The combined effect is a space that holds warmth the way a closed mouth holds breath. You feel it on your face the moment you climb down. Close, skin-damp, but not humid in the tropical sense. Humid in the way a human body is humid. The air is nearly still and the moisture has nowhere to go.

Condensation forms on the coolest available surfaces: the rebar tips, the clay floor, the shells of the snails.

---

The snails are the first thing you notice after the heat. They are *everywhere*. A species of door-snail — *Clausilia* or something close, though no one has formally identified this population and the few samples taken showed morphological peculiarities that made the taxonomists uneasy — covers nearly every vertical surface. They cluster on the rebar in dense spirals of shells, each no longer than a fingernail, their bodies pale and almost translucent where they extend from the aperture. The concrete faces hold them in a pattern that from a distance looks like deliberate ornament: dense in the cooler crevices, sparse on the warm ceiling, thickest at the boundary zone where condensation drips.

You hear them, if you go quiet enough. Not the snails themselves — they are nearly silent — but the sound of their trails drying. A micro-crackle, like static, like someone crinkling cellophane in another room. It comes from every wall at once, arrhythmic and persistent.

The floor has its own texture. The clay is not bare. It is paved — that is the wrong word but the only one that fits — paved with shells. Old shells. Hundreds of thousands of shells, accumulated over decades, forming a pale, crunching layer an inch deep in places. When you step, you feel them give and fragment. You are walking on the previous generation.

---

The fauna is not limited to snails.

Centipedes move through the shell-layer with a mechanical precision that seems impossible for something so small — you catch them in your headlamp beam, translucent and fast, each leg a precise pin-stroke against the calcium rubble. They are hunting. Springtails erupt from the surface in tiny silver arcs when your boot lands wrong, dispersing and re-landing in the span of a second. A species of silvery fish — not silverfish, something longer, something with too many segments — moves along the wall rebar in a motion like a slowed film of a wave breaking.

And there are the moths.

You would not expect moths here. There is no light for moths. But the Interfloor holds a population of small, buff-colored micro-moths that cluster near the ceiling in the warmest corners, their wings folded into sharp triangular shapes. They do not fly when you approach. They vibrate. A low, almost subsonic thrum that you feel in your teeth before you hear it. What they eat — what any of this eats, beyond each other — is a question that the brief ecological survey conducted in 2011 could not resolve. The report suggested chemoautotrophic biofilm on the sulfide-bearing stone. The report suggested that the food web might not start with light. The report was five pages long and was then filed somewhere and forgotten.

The moths might be the key. Or the moths might be incidental. Nothing here has been studied enough to say.

---

There is one artifact in the Interfloor that is not animal, not mineral, not shell.

In the northwest corner, where the ceiling drops lowest and the wall curves inward following the outline of a massive pipe chase above, someone installed a pump. It is an old thing — cast iron housing, a flywheel the size of a dinner plate, a rusted electric motor that predates standardized plug configurations. It was meant to draw sump water from the foundation. It was installed, apparently, sometime in the 1950s, and then the space above it was sealed over and the access was lost, and the pump sat in the dark and did nothing for half a century while the building settled and the Interfloor opened around it.

It is still connected to the power. You can trace the cloth-wrapped wiring up through a gap in the ceiling, toward the still-active electrical system of the plant above. Someone, somewhere, is paying for the current that trickles through this dead machine.

And sometimes — you feel this more than understand it — the pump twitches.

Not often. Not reliably. But on humid days, when the condensation load on the floor reaches a critical threshold and the water pools in the lowest depression and contacts the pump's corroded base, a circuit completes. The flywheel moves a quarter-turn. The motor makes a sound like a man clearing his throat. Then the water level drops by a millimeter, the contact breaks, and everything goes still.

This happens roughly once every three days. No one has witnessed it directly. The motion cameras set up by the 2011 survey captured it eleven times over a six-week period. In every frame, the snails on the pump housing lift their heads at the same moment. The moths in the corner vibrate. The centipedes pause.

The ecosystem is waiting for the pump. Or the ecosystem is the pump. Or something else entirely is happening that the language of ecology lacks the joints to articulate.

---

The Interfloor is active. You feel this in your body if you stay long enough. It is not a museum. It is not a relic. The condensation cycle, the thermal cycle, the breeding cycles of the snails, the hunting patterns of the centipedes, the inexplicable vibration of the moths, the random twitch of the pump — these are not separate processes. They are interlocked in a way that no one has mapped. The space is saturated with event. Every surface is either growing or being grown upon. Every cubic inch of air is occupied by something alive, or by the trace of something living — spore, mucus trail, shed chitin, the microscopic film of bacteria that makes the clay slick underfoot.

It is also quiet. Not silent — the shell-layer crunches, the snail-trails crackle, the pump sometimes clears its throat — but quiet in the way that a room full of sleeping people is quiet. Full of held breath. Full of the small sounds of maintenance. Bodies doing what bodies do when they are not being watched.

---

The weirdness of the Interfloor is not in any single thing. The snails are snails. The pump is a pump. The heat is heat. The weirdness is in the totality: that all of this fits together so precisely in a space that should not exist, beneath a building that forgot it was here, sustained by processes that no one designed and no one maintains. It is a room that built itself. An ecology that emerged from a crack in a foundation and found its way to something like stability — not balance, because balance implies a center, and the Interfloor has no center. The pump is not the center. The snails are not the center. You, stooping in the warm dark with your hand on the wall and the shells shifting under your boots, are not the center.

The center is the room itself: the shape of it, the pressure of it, the way it holds everything in contact with everything else and permits nothing to leave.

---

When you climb out, the rungs are still warm. The air above feels thin and cold, though it is July. You will check your shoes for shells. You will find some. You will hesitate before brushing them off.

You will think about the pump, waiting in the dark for the water to rise again.

You will think about the sound of a hundred thousand snail-trails drying at once.

You will wonder what the moths are vibrating toward.

You will not come back, but you will want to. This is the last rule of the Interfloor: it knows you now, and the condensation on your skin, evaporating in the open air, is carrying something away from you that you will not be able to name.
