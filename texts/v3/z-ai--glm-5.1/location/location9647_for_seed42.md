# The Drowned Cloister

The first thing is the sound.

Not the water — the water is so constant it has become silence's own texture, a dense medium that fills the ear the way altitude fills the lungs. No, the sound is the trees. They are always doing something. Groaning, flexing, pumping. The great kelp-columns that serve as both pillars and walls produce a low rhythmic thrum as their internal vessels move fluids stories high, a pulse you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears. Around that bass note, the smaller sounds layer: the click of carapaced things traveling through the woven canopy, the creak of living fiber under tension, the occasional crack as a tendril somewhere finds its limit and snaps. And beneath it all, the sustained chord of current passing through the chamber, water pushing patiently around and through every surface, never fast, never stopping.

The chamber itself is perhaps forty meters across at its widest, though the walls make determining boundaries a matter of opinion. They are walls in the architectural sense — they enclose, they define — but they are not built. They are grown. Massive strands of aquatic vegetation, each as thick as a torso, have been trained and woven over what must have been centuries into a barrel-vaulted enclosure. Some of the weave is tight enough to slow the current to a crawl; some is loose, latticed, letting the open water breathe through. The whole structure sits at a depth of perhaps fifteen meters on a shelf of stone — the high plateau of some submerged highland, the kind of place that was probably dry land once, long before the water came and stayed.

Cool light reaches here. Not the black of deep sea, not the sun-dazzle of shallows. Something between: a gray-green luminance that seems to originate from no single direction but simply exists, diffused by the water column above and the bioluminescent mottling that dots the inner walls of the chamber. Small fungal growths, pale blue-green, cling to the underside of every horizontal surface. They are not bright enough to see by, but they add a quality to the light, a cold undertone, the way moonlight adds shadow even within a lit room.

The floor is stone and silt and root. The great columns of kelp — if kelp is even the right word; they are more like the trunks of some submerged sequoia, fibrous and knuckled, rising from anchors deep in the rock — the columns emerge from the floor in thick bundles, splaying outward as they rise, their frond-canopies forming the ceiling forty feet overhead. Between the columns, the floor has been cleared in places. Cleared and used. There are low structures: cairns, benches, platforms. Made from stone and shell and the cured stems of smaller plants. Primitive work. No metal, no fired ceramic. Everything shaped by abrasion and patience. Some of the platforms hold objects — tools, perhaps, or offerings. It is hard to tell from a distance, and distance here is foreshortened by the water; everything seems both close and unreachable, the way things appear in dreams.

The density of construction is low. This is the thing that strikes you after a while: the chamber is mostly empty. Mostly water. The structures occupy only a fraction of the floor, leaving wide reaches of open silt between them. The space breathes. The current moves through unobstructed. It does not feel like a ruin or an abandonment; it feels intentional, as though the builders understood that the water itself was the primary medium and that to fill the space would be to impede its function. The chamber is a room in the way a grove is a room — defined by its borders, not by its contents.

And the contents move.

Fauna fills every margin. Not in the teeming, frantic way of a reef, but in the established, territorial way of a settled ecology. Eel-like creatures occupy the crevices between woven walls, their bodies looping slowly in and out of the lattice, grazing on the algae that films every surface. Schools of small, translucent fish move in loose formations through the open water of the chamber's interior, their bodies catching the diffused light like fragments of glass. On the floor, something crab-like tends a garden of cultivated growth — the only patch of vegetation that appears trimmed, deliberate, rows of some fibrous plant anchored in the silt. Whether the crab-thing is tending or feeding is unclear. Perhaps both.

Higher, near the canopy, larger shapes move with unhurried confidence. A flat-bodied swimmer the size of a door panel drifts between columns, its edges rippling with micro-adjustments. It is hunting, or patrolling, or simply existing within a circuit it has traced so many times the path has become instinct. It does not disturb the smaller fish. They part around it and reform like a single organism shrugging.

The sound changes near the walls. Up close, you can hear the vegetation working — a faint, sticky clicking as fibers shift against each other, as growth layers over growth. The weave is thick in places, decades of new growth laminated over old, the inner layers dead and hardened, the outer still green and flexing. The walls are simultaneously architecture and organism. They do not decay; they are overtaken. New walls grow through old ones. Chambers divide. Passages narrow and open on cycles longer than human attention, but the evidence is there if you look: a doorway half-closed by a bulge of new tissue, a ceiling lowered by the slow descent of root-mass.

Somewhere in the chamber, water moves through a constriction — a gap in the weave too narrow for anything larger than a hand — and the pressure differential produces a constant low whistle, almost below hearing, more vibration than sound. It is the closest thing to a voice the chamber has. The inhabitants — if there are inhabitants, if the crab-gardener and the patrolling flat-body and the things in the crevices count — seem to orient themselves by it. When the whistle falters, as it occasionally does when the current shifts, there is a visible pause in the activity below. The fish school tighter. The eel-things retract deeper into their crevices. Then the whistle resumes, and so does everything else.

The chamber has been here a long time. The stone floor is grooved where the columns have pressed into it over centuries. The silt is layered, compressed, laminated with the fallout of countless seasons — shed fronds, shells, the calcium ghosts of creatures who lived and died in this water. The structures on the floor show wear not from use but from simple persistence, their edges softened, their surfaces colonized by the same pale fungi that mark the ceiling. Nothing here is new. Nothing here is finished. The chamber is not a ruin; it is a slow event, a process so extended that from within it, change is nearly imperceptible.

But from outside — if you could see it from the distance of years — the chamber would seem to breathe. Expanding as new growth pushes outward, contracting as old tissue hardens and contracts, its walls thickening, its passages migrating, its population of creatures shifting in slow response to slow shifts in the water, the light, the mineral content of the current. It is a room that has never been the same room twice, even though it has been this room for longer than memory.

The water is cool. The light is gray-green. The sound never stops.
