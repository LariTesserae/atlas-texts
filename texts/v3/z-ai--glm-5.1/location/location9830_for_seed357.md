# The Assembled Plateau

The first thing is the sound. Before your eyes adjust, before the wet heat presses against your skin, there is the sound — a compounded roar that seems to have no single source but rises from everywhere at once. Groaning. Creaking. The deep pop of fibers separating. A hiss like breath forced through clenched teeth, except it never stops. And beneath it all, the constant percussion of water — dripping, running, spattering, pouring — a rainfall that continues long after the sky has cleared.

---

You stand at the edge of a high plateau, and the land before you should not be called land.

It is made. That is the only word for it, though no hand made it and no tool shaped it. The ground beneath your feet — if you can call it ground — is a dense mat of root and rhizome, layered so thick that nothing beneath it is visible. Not stone. Not soil. Only plant, compressed into substrate. When you shift your weight, it gives slightly, and water squeezes up between the woven fibers like blood from a wound. The surface is warm. It radiates heat. You are standing on something alive.

The plateau extends in every direction, and it is vast — a region, not a place. You can see for miles across it, and what you see makes no sense at first because the mind reaches for architecture and finds only growth, reaches for growth and finds only architecture.

---

Towers rise from the mat. Dozens of them, then hundreds, scattered across the plateau with wide empty spaces between. They are not built in rows or clusters but distributed as though seeded — which, you realize, they were. Each tower is a single plant. Or perhaps a single organism with many stems. It is difficult to tell where one ends and another begins because the rhizome mat connects everything below, and above, the towers send out horizontal arms that interlace with their neighbors, forming bridges, buttresses, half-finished arches that reach toward each other and sometimes meet.

The towers are tall — forty feet, sixty, some perhaps a hundred — and they are not regular. They bulge and constrict. They spiral slightly. Their surfaces are ridged and ribbed, and the ribs are vascular — you can see the moisture moving inside them, dark channels carrying water upward in slow pulses. The tissue is woody at the base, becoming greener and more turgid toward the top, and at the crown of each tower, the plant opens into a broad spreading structure that is not quite a leaf and not quite a flower but something between — a thin membrane stretched over a vein-skeleton, veiled and wet, constantly producing condensation.

This is where the water comes from. Not rain. Not springs. The towers pull water from the mat below, from the saturated air, from the fog that settles every evening, and they release it continuously from those crown-membranes. It falls in sheets and streams down the tower-shafts, collects in the grooves between ribs, pools in the junctions where arms meet the main stem. Every surface is wet. Every surface is running. The plateau weeps.

---

The heat is oppressive. It comes from below — the mat radiates it, the decomposition of older layers generating warmth, and the living tissue around you is warm too, metabolizing furiously. The air is thick with moisture, nearly opaque at ground level on windless days, and your lungs work harder than they should. Sweat does not evaporate. It joins the general wetness.

But the heat also drives the growth. This is what makes the plateau so volatile. The plants here grow at a pace that seems impossible. You can watch it happen if you are patient — a nub emerging from the mat, reaching upward, thickening, dividing, putting out arms, reaching for a neighbor, and all of it happening over the course of hours, not days. The towers are not permanent. They rise and fall on a timescale that turns seasons into moments. A tower that stands in the morning may be listing by afternoon, its base softened by some internal rot, its weight too much for its own fresh tissue. When one falls, it is loud — a long groaning crack, a rushing descent, a sodden impact that sprays water and debris across the mat. And then the neighbors lean into the gap, sending out questing arms, sealing the wound, and within days a new tower has begun to rise from the same spot or nearby.

Nothing here is old. The erosion parameter is low not because the landscape is resistant but because it is too young to have eroded. The plateau itself — the geological formation beneath the mat — was thrust up recently, the rock still sharp-featured, the escarpment at the edge still unrounded by weather. But you cannot see any of that. The plants got here first. They colonized the raw stone so quickly and so completely that the stone might as well not exist. The mat covers everything. The towers have root-systems that grip the rock below, but the rock is irrelevant now. This is a world of plant.

---

There are no animals. This is the uncanny thing, once you notice it. No insects crawl on the tower-shafts. No birds perch on the crown-membranes. No worms work the mat. You might see something moving at the corner of your eye — a trembling of a vine, a shifting of a membrane — but it is always the plant itself, moving in response to water pressure, to light, to the collapse of a neighbor. The only motion is vegetative. The only sound is vegetative. The groaning and creaking and popping are the sounds of cellulose under stress, of fluids moving through vessels, of tissue expanding faster than its own skin can contain.

This absence of fauna should make the place quiet, but instead it makes the vegetative sounds louder by contrast. There is nothing to mask them, nothing to break them up into a background hum. Every crack is distinct. Every groan has its own pitch. The plateau is an orchestra of stress responses.

---

Walk out onto the mat and you begin to understand the sparseness. For all the towers, there is much emptiness between them. The mat stretches flat and featureless for long stretches — a hundred yards, two hundred — before another tower rises. The arms that bridge the gaps are narrow and often incomplete, reaching toward nothing, abandoned mid-growth when the tower they were reaching for collapsed. You walk through a landscape of partial connections, of failed architectures, of attempts. The towers are not dense because the plants cannot sustain density at this growth rate. They would outcompete each other, rot each other's bases, collapse in tangles. The sparseness is structural. It is the only stable configuration.

But stable is the wrong word. The plateau is not stable. It is turbulent in the way that a storm is turbulent — not random, but too complex to predict. Towers fall. New ones erupt. Arms extend and retract. The mat itself shifts as underlying layers decompose and new layers form on top. You can feel it moving beneath you, slowly, like ice on a river. The entire plateau is in motion. It is constructing itself and deconstructing itself simultaneously, and the heat and the water and the growth rate mean that the cycle is fast enough to witness. You could sit in one spot for a day and see the landscape change around you.

---

At the plateau's edge, the mat overhangs the escarpment, and water pours from it in continuous curtains, feeding a fog that hangs permanently in the valleys below. The towers near the edge lean outward, their arms reaching into empty air as if they do not know where the ground ends. Some have toppled over the edge, and their bodies hang down the cliff face like drapery, still alive, still growing, sending out new shoots that cling to the rock and descend further, extending the mat downward, colonizing the cliff itself. In time — perhaps a few years at this growth rate — the mat will reach the valley floor. The plateau will expand. The world will be eaten by plant.

Stand at the edge and look out. The fog below is thick and white and the towers behind you creak and groan and the water runs over your feet and the heat rises from the mat and the whole thing is so clearly alive, so clearly a process rather than a place, that you understand finally what you are standing on. Not a plateau. Not a landscape. An organism. Or a community of organisms so interconnected that the distinction is meaningless. A thing that builds itself without building, that makes without making, that grows architecture as naturally as breathing.

The sound rises around you. The plateau does not care that you are here. It will grow over your footprints. It will grow over you, if you stay still long enough.
