# The Vivisystem

The first thing is the sound. It arrives before anything else — a low, continuous roar that you feel in the cartilage of your chest before your ears parse it into components. Thrumming. Clicking. A million small processes running simultaneously, the sound of a factory the size of a valley if every machine in that factory were alive and hungry.

Then the light. What there is of it. The sky hangs low and colorless, a dim pale ceiling that suggests a sun somewhere behind unbroken cloud-thick atmosphere, but gives no direction, no shadows, no warmth. Everything is lit like the inside of a closed eyelid.

Then the scale.

---

The plateau extends further than you can see in any direction, and every inch of it is *plant* — but that word fails almost immediately. What you walk on is not root or soil but a continuous matte of living matter, a substrate that stretches to the grey horizon in wrinkled, interlocking sheets like the skin of something enormous and dormant. It pulses faintly underfoot. Not with anything so familiar as a heartbeat — more like peristalsis, a slow squeeze-and-release that travels in waves across the surface, directed by pressures you cannot identify. When you kneel and press your palm flat, you feel it: the ground is breathing. Or something like breathing. Some vast slow exchange of gases through tissues that have been thickening here since before the concept of "water" meant anything at all.

There is no water. This is the thing that takes longest to understand, because everything you see contradicts it. The green — and it is green, countless greens, grey-greens and blue-greens and greens so dark they border on black, pale lichen-greens and the fierce emerald of actively metabolizing surfaces — the green insists on water. The folds and dewlaps and swollen nodes, the surface tension of the cell-bursting walls, the way condensation beads on the undersides of overhanging ledges — all of it looks wet. But when you touch that condensation, it is thick, slightly tacky. It rolls between your fingers with the wrong viscosity. It is not water. It is something the plants produce and circulate and exude and re-absorb, a circulatory medium that evolved in a world where hydrogen oxide was never more than trace contamination. It smells faintly of copper and ammonia and something sweeter, something that reminds you of honey but is not honey. The local biochemistry runs on it. The whole plateau sweats it, breathes it, drinks it back.

---

The structures rise from the substrate in clusters, and this is where the sparseness lives — not in the ground cover, which is total, but in the way the larger growths are distributed across the plateau. Great towers of living architecture, spaced far apart, visible as silhouettes against that pale dead sky. They are miles from each other, these towers. You can walk for an hour between them across the rippling plain, hearing the crunch and creak of the substrate adjusting to your weight, the distant hum and chatter of the fauna you cannot yet see.

And each tower is a city.

Not built. *Grown.* Not inhabited. *Inhabiting.* The distinction between architecture and organism collapsed here long ago — perhaps it never existed. The towers are stems, technically, or the plant's equivalent of stems: great vertical shafts of dense living tissue, fibrous and tough as any hardwood but cellularly closer to cartilage, thickened at the base into buttresses that sprawl across the substrate like the roots of a cathedral. They are hollow inside. Not void-hollow but chambered, layered, riddled with passages and chambers and spaces that serve purposes you can only partially guess at. Some are clearly rooms — the scale fits, the openings are door-sized, the interiors are smooth and dry and lined with a paler growth that glows with a faint bioluminescence that passes for lamplight. Others are infrastructure: vascular bundles that pump circulatory medium upward against gravity through peristaltic contraction, airways that vent gases through the structure and create the constant low moan that harmonizes with the plateau's other sounds, storage chambers thick-walled and sealed where something crucial is accumulated and processed.

People live here. Or something enough like people to use doors and rooms and staircases grown from lignin-equivalent into spiraling ramps. The town — city, really, each tower a vertical town of several thousand — is organized along the interior surfaces of the plant, walkways and platforms and suspended bridges grown from the walls like shelf fungi, each surface slightly tacky underfoot, each railing thick and warm to the touch. The plant regulates temperature inside, absorbing the thin cold of the high plateau and redistributing it, maintaining a cool but livable interior. You can feel the warmth of metabolism seeping from the walls. The whole structure is alive and working and *loud*.

---

The sound inside is different from the sound outside. Quieter in decibels but denser in information. The walls thrum with circulation. The floor-vibrations have rhythm. If you stay long enough, you begin to parse it: there are patterns in the noise, not music exactly but something like a language, the plant communicating with itself across kilometers of tissue. Something in the roots sends a signal. Something in the canopy responds. The walls around you shiver with the exchange. You are living inside a conversation.

Outside, the fauna makes itself known. The plateau is full of them — creatures that evolved in and alongside and parasitic upon the plant-substrate, and they are *abundant*. You see them at distance first: dark shapes moving across the matte, dozens of them, hundreds, their trajectories apparently purposeful. As you approach, they resolve. They are not vertebrates. Most move on pads of the same living substrate, detached and ambulatory, fused to their undersides like living shoes. Some roll. Some fly on wings of dried leaf-papery membrane, circling the towers and feeding on the gases that vent from the upper chambers. Some are small and numerous as insects, swarming in the creases of the substrate where two sheets of living ground meet and overlap. Some are large as cattle, slow and methodical, grazing the surface of the plain by scraping off the uppermost layer of cells with mouthparts like gardening tools, stimulating new growth beneath them. The plant does not seem to mind. The plant may actively encourage them. This is an ecosystem, not a monoculture — the fauna are part of the system, nutrient cyclers, spore-dispersers, pruners. The plant feeds them and they serve it and the boundary between parasite and symbiote is vague and shifting and probably irrelevant.

They are loud. The flying ones cry continuously, a reedy piping that cuts through the plateau's lower hum. The crawling ones click and hiss as they feed. The swarming ones produce a crackling sound like static, or like a thousand small joints articulating at once. At dawn and dusk — and you know it is dawn or dusk only because the grey lightens or dims, never changing in direction or quality — the fauna chorus reaches a volume that is almost painful, and you understand that the plateau's background roar is partially *them*, the accumulated sound of uncountable living processes running in parallel.

---

The tech is harder to see, because it does not look like technology. It looks like the plant. But certain chambers in the tower's interior clearly process information — the walls there are dense with fine filaments that cluster and branch at nanometer scales, forming structures that process signals faster than any biological system should. The plant computes. It stores data. It communicates with the other towers across the plateau through something that is not quite radio and not quite pheromone and not quite anything your physics can categorize — something that propagates through the substrate itself, a signal carried on the peristaltic waves, a message encoded in the timing and amplitude of the ground's breathing. The towers coordinate. They share resources. They make decisions. They are not a city. They are a *network*, and the network is awake.

You find control surfaces grown into the walls of certain chambers — patches of denser tissue that respond to touch, to pressure, to the trace chemicals on your fingertips. When you press one, something changes in the tower's metabolism. A vent closes. A chamber warms. A walkway extends or retracts. The interface is invisible until used. The technology is not separate from the plant. It is *the plant*, specialized tissue doing specialized work, grown over millennia into a toolset that includes computation, communication, environmental control, and something approaching memory. The towers remember. They remember the shape of every creature that has walked their halls. They remember the composition of every gas that has circulated through their chambers. They remember you, now. They will remember you after you leave.

---

The plateau is ancient. This is not a place that is still becoming. It has been here, in roughly this form, for longer than most biospheres persist. The erosion parameter is not about physical wear — though there is that, the matte substrate worn smooth in places by the passage of countless feet and the slow grinding of wind-borne particles — but about exhaustion. The system has reached its final stable state. It is not evolving toward anything. It has arrived. It is what it is, and it will remain what it is, in its slow breathing and its cold dim light and its constant roar of process, until something external intervenes, or until the sun changes, or until the accumulated weight of millennia finally presses the substrate down into whatever lies beneath it. But that will take a long time. The plateau is patient. The plateau has always been patient. The plateau was old when the first of the current fauna crawled out of the substrate and found it could move and kept moving, and the plateau will be old when the last of them finally, inevitably, returns.

You stand in a doorway grown from living tissue. You look out across the dim expanse. The sound washes over you, and the cold seeps through your clothing, and the substrate beneath your feet shifts almost imperceptibly as it breathes, and somewhere in the distance a tower-organism vents a long low note that might be gas exchange or might be computation or might be something your language has no word for, and you realize that what you are standing in is not a city built from plants, not a plant shaped into a city, but something for which the distinction was never meaningful, something that has always been both and neither, something that grew and was grown and grew itself into a shape that includes doors and walls and the concept of *inside* and the concept of *stranger* and the capacity to recognize you as the latter and make, if not space for you, then at least a kind of room.

The plant does not care about you. The city does not care about you. But the thing that is both, the vivisystem, the living architecture that breathes and computes and remembers — it has registered your presence, and it has adjusted, and it continues. The sound does not change. The light does not change. The substrate breathes.

You are inside something that does not need you. It is the most alien thing about this place. Not the non-aqueous chemistry. Not the architecture that is also an organism. Not the dim sky or the loud air or the fauna that moves on living pads across a living floor. The alien thing is the indifference. This place was complete before you arrived. It will be complete after you leave. It does not require your understanding or your permission or your awe. It simply is, and it is vast, and it is loud, and it is cold, and it is alive.
