# The Meridian Plateau

---

The first thing you notice is the silence.

Not absence of sound—there is that, certainly, a thinness to the air that carries nothing to the ear—but the way silence *occupies* this place. It sits in the eroded runnels of stone. It pools in the low places between ridgebacks of dark rock. It has a texture here, almost a weight, the way cold dense air at altitude can feel like something draped across the shoulders.

The Meridian Plateau stretches beyond what the eye can hold. It is a continent of raised land, a flatness so extensive it generates its own weather patterns, its own slow atmospheric logic. From the center, the horizon is a perfect line in every direction, broken only by the low ridgeback formations and, occasionally, by movement.

The rock is the oldest thing. Dark basalts and gabbros, intruded long ago by veins of lighter feldspar, now weathered into a state that seems less like decay than like settlement. The stone has reached an agreement with the forces acting upon it. The wind—constant, thin, cold—has carved it into long parallel grooves, yardangs and ridgebacks that run for hundreds of kilometers in the direction of the prevailing flow. They look like the ribs of something enormous, or like the furrows of a field plowed by a civilization with no concern for harvest. But there is no civilization here. The plateau has never been built upon. No hand has raised a wall, cut a road, driven a post. The rock simply *is*, in its weathered and settled state, as it has been for longer than the rocks of most places can conceive.

Cold is not an event here; it is a condition. The plateau sits high enough that the atmosphere struggles to retain heat, and the light that reaches the surface arrives already weakened—filtered through the perpetual high haze that the plateau's own expanse generates. The sky is not dark, exactly, but dim. Colors are muted. The stone reads as dark grey-blue in the indirect light, and the shadowed grooves between ridgebacks go nearly black. There are no sharp shadows. Everything bleeds at its edges into the general pallor.

Moisture arrives as a negotiation. In the mornings, condensation forms on the stone—in the grooves, in the low places, on the leeward side of the ridgebacks. A thin sheen of water, barely enough to wet a palm, gathers and then, as the temperature lifts fractionally, evaporates again. Sometimes, on a cycle no one has precisely mapped, rain crosses the plateau. It falls thin and cold, barely distinguishable from a heavy mist, and the stone drinks what it can. In the lowest channels between the yardangs, where the erosion has cut deepest, thin trickles persist for hours or days after these rains, dark threads of water moving so slowly they seem to be considering each centimeter of their path before committing to it. There are no rivers. There is no lake. There is only this: the rock, and the damp that visits it, and the slow channels that carry the damp away.

Vegetation is almost an afterthought. In the deeper grooves where condensation lingers and the wind is less direct, a grey-green lichen adheres to the stone in patches, its edges curled and dry, its body so flat against the rock it seems to be trying to become rock itself. It is ancient and slow, growing perhaps a centimeter per century, and it covers vast stretches of the shadowed surfaces, though it never rises more than a few millimeters above the stone. In places where the condensation channels have cut deep enough to reveal softer rock, and where the thin soil of eroded dust has collected, there are low plants—succulent things, grey and thick-skinned, hugging the ground so closely they are nearly invisible until you are upon them. They flower once every few years, a pale nub of a blossom the color of old bone, and the flowers last only a day before the cold takes them.

But the fauna—.

---

The fauna is the plateau's industry.

They are called, in the few records that mention this place, the Processional. The name is imprecise but accurate enough. They move. That is what they do. They move in lines, in processions, across the vast floor of the plateau, between the ridgebacks, through the channels, in patterns so large and so slow that a human observer standing at ground level would perceive only that the landscape had shifted between one visit and the next, without understanding how.

The Processional are not one species. They are several—perhaps dozens—entangled in a system so extensive and so old that it has become a single organism of staggering size, or a machine of biological origin, or an ecology that has simply *become* industrial.

The largest among them are the Vessels.

The Vessels move on hundreds of legs, grey and chitinous, each one as thick as an old tree and jointed in three places. Their bodies are low and broad—twenty meters long, perhaps eight meters wide, and only three high—like flattened ovals of dark chitin, segmented and overlapping, the segments fitting together so precisely they could be machined. They are silent. The legs move in a rolling gait, hundreds of them in a wave, each footfall placed with mechanical precision on the stone, each lifting before the next comes down, so that the whole creature flows across the rock without impact, without sound, a slow glacier of legs and body.

Inside the Vessels—visible through the semi-translucent chitin of their dorsal plates when the dim light hits correctly—there are chambers. This is known but not well understood. The chambers contain fluid, and in the fluid, other organisms move. There are smaller creatures, pale and blind, that seem to perform functions within the Vessel's body—cleaning, perhaps, or processing. There are structures that look like filters, like refineries. The Vessels take in stone. This is observable. At the leading edge of a Vessel's path, where the yardangs have crumbled into gravel, the creature feeds—slowly, methodically, a mouth like a geological bore grinding and swallowing the loose rock. And from its posterior, hours or days later, it deposits a fine, consistent sand, grey and clean, in a low ridge behind it.

This is why the plateau is flat. This is why the erosion seems so settled, so balanced. The Vessels have been processing this landscape for longer than anything can remember. They eat the high places and deposit the low. They smooth. They grade. They are an industry of geology, and they have been running, silent and patient, since before the current topography existed.

The channels between ridgebacks are their roads. The condensation that pools there is their water—they drink through their footpads, absorbing the thin moisture as they pass. The grey-green lichen is not bothered by their passage; it grows on the sand they leave behind, finding purchase in the fine-grained substrate. The succulent plants cluster at the edges of the procession routes, thriving in the disturbed stone.

There are other creatures.

The Tenders are smaller—only a meter or two in length—and they move on six legs, fast and precise, between the Vessels. They are also chitinous, dark, and silent. They seem to maintain the procession, redirecting Vessels that have drifted from the established routes, clearing blockages, and—when a Vessel's dorsal plates crack or open—crawling inside to perform some function that is invisible from the outside. They communicate by touch, tapping each other's carapaces with their forelimbs in patterns too rapid to follow. They do not acknowledge any other presence.

The Filtrators drift above. They are the only things that move through the air here—vast, membranous creatures, pale grey, like stretched skin over a frame of cartilage, floating on the thin cold updrafts that the plateau generates. They are wider than the Vessels are long—forty meters, sixty, sometimes more—and they are almost transparent, the dim light passing through them so that they are more absence than presence, more shadow than body. They trail long filaments, meters and meters of them, down toward the stone. The filaments collect condensation. They filter something from the thin air—minerals, perhaps, carried in the plateau's haze. And when enough has been collected, the Filtrators descend—slowly, silently—and touch down near a Vessel, where the Tenders climb the filaments and harvest whatever has been gathered.

There are others. Smaller creatures, pale and blind, living in the sand the Vessels leave behind. Parasites on the Vessels' legs, or on the Filtrators' filaments. Predators—nothing violent, nothing fast, but things that follow the processions and take what falls behind. And deep in the Vessels' chambers, the organisms that no external observer has ever seen, whose functions can only be guessed at.

The system is vast. A single procession—a line of Vessels, their Tenders, their attendant Filtrators—can stretch for kilometers, winding between the ridgebacks, following the ancient channels. And there are many processions, crossing the plateau in different directions, on routes that have been stable for geological ages. The routes are visible from altitude: long, straight channels, deeper than the natural erosion would produce, cut by the passage of millions of Vessels over millions of years. The plateau's topography has been shaped by this industry. The ridgebacks run parallel to the processions. The condensation collects where the processions have worn the stone down. The lichen grows where the processions have deposited sand. The few plants survive at the processions' margins.

Nothing here is built. Nothing is constructed. And yet this is an industrial landscape—a landscape of processing, of refinement, of transport and transformation, operating at continental scale. The Vessels are the factories. The Tenders are the workers. The Filtrators are the supply chain. The product—grey sand, deposited in long low ridges—is the only output, and it covers everything, a fine even layer of processed stone that the wind moves and the rain compacts and the lichen binds, slowly, patient, into something like soil.

The plateau is making itself. Has been making itself. Will continue to make itself.

---

Standing on the ridgeback, between processions, you can see for a hundred kilometers. The stone stretches away in every direction, grey and dim and silent. The sky is a uniform pallor, neither bright nor dark, casting no shadows. The wind is constant and thin and cold, carrying nothing—no scent, no sound, no moisture, only the faint pressure of air moving across stone.

Below, in the channel, a Vessel is passing. It has been passing for hours. It will continue to pass for hours more. Its legs rise and fall in their precise, silent sequence, and its body moves forward at a speed you could outpace by walking, if you walked, which you do not, because to walk here is to understand that the Vessel's patience is geological, and your impatience is merely biological, and the gap between these scales is not bridgeable by any effort of will.

The condensation is forming. The thin sheen of water appears on the stone beside the Vessel's path, and you watch the footpads touch down and lift away, and each touch leaves a dry spot that the condensation slowly refills. The Vessel drinks as it walks. It processes as it drinks. It deposits as it processes. The sand appears behind it in a low, even ridge, fresh and grey and clean, and already the lichen at the ridge's edge is sending out pale tendrils, barely visible, to colonize the new ground.

From somewhere above, a Filtrator's membrane catches the faint light, and for a moment the creature is visible—a grey ghost, trailing its long filaments toward the stone—and then it has passed, and the sky is uniform again, and the silence closes back in.

You listen. The silence is not empty. It is full of the sounds that are too quiet to hear—the creak of the Vessel's chitin as its segments shift, the pad of a hundred legs touching stone, the whisper of sand being deposited, the infinitesimal crack of lichen growing. These sounds are below the threshold. They are the sounds of processes operating at scales that do not favor ears. You cannot hear them, but you can feel them—the faint vibration in the stone beneath your feet, the barely-perceptible shift in the air as the procession passes, the sense that the whole plateau is a mechanism in motion, and that you are standing inside it.

The cold settles into your bones. The dim light presses against your eyes. The silence fills your ears until they ring with their own blood. And the Vessel moves on.

The plateau continues.

It has always continued.
