# The Plateau of Voices

The first thing is the sound.

It arrives before understanding—a wall of noise that has been so constant for so long that the stone beneath it has learned to vibrate in sympathy. A roar that is not one voice but millions, layered and interlocking, a living white noise that presses against the eardrums like weather. You feel it in your sternum before you parse it as sound. Insects. Birds. Things that click and thrum and keening. The fauna of the high plateau announce themselves continuously, have announced themselves for longer than the scattered stone tools lying half-buried in root-mat, and they will announce themselves long after the last quiet thing forgets to listen.

---

The plateau sits high—impossibly high, the kind of elevation where the air should be thin and cold but isn't, because something about this place rewrites that rule. The warmth holds. It traps and radiates, a steady simmering heat rising from the vegetative mass below. The land up here is ancient beyond reckoning, eroded to its essential form: a vast, near-flat expanse stretching to horizons that haze with humidity and the breath of ten million wings. Whatever mountains once stood here were worn down epochs ago. What remains is the after-shape, the final geometry, a table of land so old it has forgotten it was ever anything else.

And it is enormous. The plateau extends beyond walking in a day, beyond sight—regional in scope, a country unto itself, wrinkled with shallow valleys and low ridges that count as dramatic relief only by the most generous measure. From any point the land rolls away in every direction, and the sky sits close and white and heavy with trapped warmth.

---

The vegetation is not a covering. It *is* the ground.

There is no exposed soil here, no bare rock, no mineral surface left to the air. Every square span is plant matter—living, recently living, or in the slow alchemy of becoming living again. The substrate itself is root and rhizome, layered meters deep in places, a churning mat of vegetative tissue that serves as soil for the next generation of itself. Step anywhere and you feel the give of it, the spring and slow rebound. The plateau walks on its own living skin.

But for all that totality, the space feels *empty*.

This is the contradiction that takes time to resolve. The plant-life is absolute but horizontal—a vast living carpet, knee-high at most, spreading without vertical ambition. The stems and runners and broad flat leaves form an unbroken plain of green-gold tissue, but they leave the air above them vacant. Enormous vacant. The sky feels too large. Distances deform. You can see a dark shape on the horizon and walk toward it for hours before its nature becomes clear, because there is nothing between you and it to provide scale.

What rises from the mat are the elder plants—not trees in any familiar sense, but immense columnar growths, photosynthetic towers that stand alone and far apart. Each one is a solitary architecture, a hundred meters tall or more, fluted and buttressed, their surfaces crawling with smaller epiphytes and parasitic vines. They are scattered so widely that you might pass three in a day's walk, or none. Between them: the low mat, the close sky, and nothing else to interrupt the eye.

These great columns transpire constantly, pulling deep moisture upward and exhaling it into the already-saturated air. By midday the humidity condenses on their surfaces, on the leaf-mat below, on your skin. Rain falls in the immediate vicinity of each column—not from clouds but from the plant itself, a localized drizzle that leaves the surrounding plain merely damp while the soil at the column's base runs with small streams. These rivulets wander a short distance before sinking back into the root-mat, ephemeral veins in a body that has no need for permanence. Water here exists in transit: drawn up, breathed out, condensed, absorbed. Nothing pools. Nothing stagnates. The damp is constant but never accumulates.

---

And then the fauna.

The sound, when you finally parse it, separates into strata. The lowest layer is the thrum— insect wings in such numbers that they generate a continuous bass note, a vibration felt more than heard. Beetles and wasps and things without common names, segmented and iridescent, moving in clouds that darken the already pale sky. They live and die in the mat, laying eggs in the root-tissue, burrowing through the living substrate in tunnels that heal behind them. The mat crawls with them. Every handful of vegetative matter contains dozens, hundreds.

Above the thrum: the chittering. Small warm things, furred and quick, moving through the low growth in waves. They are herbivorous, mostly—nipping the tender shoots, harvesting the constant new growth—and their population fluctuates in great slow pulses that take seasons to complete. When their numbers peak, the sound of their collective voices merges with the insect drone into something like a sustained shout. When they crash, the silence is relative, temporary, eerie.

Above them: the birds. Or the things that fill the bird-niche— hollow-boned, keen-eyed, calling to one another across the enormous empty distances between columns. They nest in the epiphyte gardens on the tower-plants, and their cries echo off the fluted surfaces in strange delays. They are the most visible presence, wheeling between columns, and their collective voice is the highest and sharpest layer of the plateau's song.

And above even the birds, the predators. Silent until they strike, then terrible. You see them as shadows crossing the mat, or you don't see them at all until the flock beneath your feet explodes into flight and there is a wet sound and then the ordinary noise resumes, one voice fewer.

The density of animal life here is staggering, and yet the place feels empty because the space is so vast. The fauna are uncountable but dispersed, spread thin across an area that could swallow cities. The plateau's carrying capacity is nearly infinite—the plant-mat grows faster than anything can eat it—so the creatures expand to fill the available volume, which is more volume than any mind was built to comprehend. Millions of lives per cubic kilometer, and still the distances between individuals can be measured in minutes of flight.

---

The scattered structures are almost invisible.

They exist—low stone circles, windbreaks of stacked root-mat, the occasional pit dug into the living substrate and lined with broad leaves. Signs of hands that worked without metal, without any material not grown from this ground. Someone lived here. Someones. But they built light and temporary, and the mat has swallowed most of what they left. A trained eye can find the calcium deposits where hearths once burned, the subtle discoloration where tool-work scarred the rhizome layer. But the plateau does not memorialize. It digests and grows over and forgets.

Whoever they were, they left no explanation. Perhaps the noise drove them away in the end. Perhaps they became part of the place— their bodies returned to the mat, their tools settling into the root-layers to be slowly crumbled and incorporated. Or perhaps they simply moved on, following some seasonal migration that the plateau's rhythms once made sensible, and never returned.

---

Stand in the center of this place—any center, any point will do, they are all essentially the same—and try to take it in. The warmth pressing from below. The damp beading on your arms. The nearest column-plant a dark silhouette against the pale sky, too far to touch, close enough to see. The mat springing beneath your feet, each step releasing a small puff of insect life that resettles almost instantly. The roar of ten million voices, insect and avian and mammalian and reptilian, all overlapping into a single continuous event that has no beginning and no end and no pauses.

This is the plateau's truth: it is full and empty simultaneously. It is the loudest place you have ever stood, and the loneliest. Every surface crawls with life, and the nearest other thinking presence might be days away. The vegetation is the world, and the world is mostly distance.

The sound never stops. After long enough, you stop hearing it. After longer, you might start to find it comforting—the proof that you are not alone on this vast and ancient platform, even if you cannot see company, even if the only evidence of others is the air itself vibrating with their insistence on existing.

The mat grows. The columns breathe out rain. The insects drone. The stone circles settle deeper.
