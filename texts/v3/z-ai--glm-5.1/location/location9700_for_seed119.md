# The Cistern of Marked Stone

It is not a cave. This is the first thing you understand, though your eyes take longer than your feet to believe it. Every surface has been shaped. The walls, the floor, the ceiling—every plane and curve bears the evidence of hands. Chisel strokes. Hundreds of thousands of them, overlapping, varying in depth and angle, some made with care and others with exhaustion, covering every square inch of stone like a language that precedes meaning.

The chamber is roughly the size of a small chapel, though the proportions feel older than any chapel. The ceiling arches overhead in a vault that someone carved from above, working downward through living rock, removing material in chips and dust over what must have been decades. Or generations. The tool marks on the ceiling are finer, more patient. The ones near the floor are broader, rougher. Different hands. Different eras. Same purpose.

Water is the reason they came. Water is what remains.

---

The walls weep. Not dramatically—there are no cascades or flows—but steadily, constantly, from a hundred tiny seeps in the stone. The water emerges where the chisel struck certain veins in the rock, opening paths that were always there but never expressed. Now they express. The water runs down the carved walls in thin, converging tracks, finding the channels cut into the floor—shallow grooves, intentional, hand-carved with the same patient labor as everything else. The channels form a branching pattern that draws all the water toward the center of the chamber, where a rectangular basin has been sunk into the floor. The basin is the only feature that was polished smooth. Everything else retains its chisel-texture. The basin was meant to hold water cleanly. The rest was meant only to direct it.

The water in the basin is still and dark, reflecting nothing because the light is too dim. It is cool here, uniformly cool, the way places are cool when the sun has never reached them. The stone breathes a constant exhalation of damp cold. Water condenses on the walls, runs, drips. The drip is one layer of the sound.

There are others.

---

The frogs are the loudest. Small, dark frogs—dozens of them, perhaps over a hundred in this single chamber—calling from the walls, from the ledges that were carved as resting places for workers but now serve as perches for amphibians. Their calls overlap and resonate against the carved stone until the chamber hums with a collective vibration that you feel in your sternum more than hear with your ears. Different species, you realize after listening. At least three. Their calls are distinct in pitch and pattern, and they respond to each other—overlapping, interrupting, answering in rhythms that feel almost conversational.

The insects are quieter but constant. Small pale things that skitter along the wet channels, drinking from the film of water on the stone. They make a sound like fine sand being poured, a whispering click of legs on wet rock. They breed in the still edges of the basin where organic matter has accumulated—a thin compost of frog waste and shed skin and the bodies of the dead, building up in layers, slowly filling the corners that the water flows past but does not wash clean.

There are also eels. Or something like eels. Long, smooth, nearly colorless creatures that live in the basin and occasionally surface to breathe, their blunt heads breaking the dark water with a gasping sound that echoes. They eat the insects. The frogs eat the insects. Something—perhaps the eels, perhaps something else—eats the frogs. There are fewer frogs near the basin's edge.

Nothing here photosynthesizes. There is no light for it.

---

The chamber has one entrance: a low, rough-cut passage at the far end, barely wide enough for a person to pass through sideways. The chisel marks here are the roughest of all—this was cut quickly, functionally, without the care given to the main chamber. The passage slopes downward before opening, so that you must descend into this place. You do not stumble upon it. You choose to enter, or you were brought.

There are no other features. No niches, no shelves, no carved symbols or decorative elements. The only modifications beyond the basic architecture of the chamber are the water channels and a series of small, round holes drilled into the walls at irregular intervals—perhaps for torches, perhaps for something else. They are empty now. Whatever they held is gone.

The emptiness is structural. This chamber was built to be empty. It was built to hold nothing but water and the sound of water and the creatures that would inevitably come to the water. Whether this was the builders' intention or simply what time has made of their work is impossible to determine. The chisel marks tell you how, but not why.

---

Stand here long enough and the sound becomes a body. The frogs, the dripping, the skittering, the occasional gasp of surfacing eels, the low resonance of water moving through stone at speeds too slow to see—all of it combines into a single organism of sound that fills the chamber the way water fills the basin. It presses against you. It is not quiet. It has never been quiet. Even if every creature fell silent, the water would continue, and the water has been continuing for longer than anything here has been alive.

The air tastes like wet stone and the faint animal musk of concentrated life. It tastes like a place that has been breathing for centuries without ever opening its mouth.

Something is strange here, though it takes time to notice. The frogs do not leave. The passage is open; the outside world exists. But the frogs stay, generation after generation, calling from the same carved ledges their ancestors called from. The insects cluster at the same channels. The eels circle the same basin. The chamber was built to hold water, and it does, but it also holds something else—a gravity, a habit, a logic of occupation that the builders may or may not have intended. The creatures here do not seem trapped. They seem chosen.

Or perhaps they seem like the true builders, and the hands that carved the stone were only preparing a room for guests they would never meet.

---

The water level in the basin rises and falls by millimeters with the seasons. The chisel marks on the walls do not change. The frogs call. The eels circle. The insects click and whisper. The chamber holds all of it—the water, the sound, the life, the long memory of hands—without complaint, without purpose, without any indication that it has ever considered being anything other than exactly what it is.
