# The Thresh

The plateau has no name. It does not need one. Nothing here speaks.

---

You descend through water that is all one temperature — that same cold, that specific cold, the kind that stops being a sensation and becomes a condition. Not the cold of depth but the cold of height, displaced. The plateau sits four thousand meters above the seafloor proper, a continental shelf that heaved itself upward in some recent cataclysm and never settled. The rock is new. You can feel it in the edges. Basalt ridges rise in sharp, unrounded crests. Obsidian flows still show their flow-lines, frozen mid-pour. Nothing has had time to smooth. The water hasn't yet earned its erosions here.

And the water is *everything*. Above you, water. Below you, water. The plateau stretches to the limit of visibility in every direction and keeps going — continental in scope, perhaps larger, its far edges lost not in distance but in the simple fact that this place has enough atmosphere of its own to generate weather. Currents form. Micro-oceans slosh within the ocean. You are underwater and also, in some topological sense, under more water, the whole column pressing down from an unreachable surface.

But light reaches you. Normal light. The plateau is high enough that sun still penetrates, diffuse and blue-gray, enough to see by. Enough to see too much.

---

The sound begins before you notice it. It has always been there. It will always be there.

A low mechanical thrumming that arrives through the rock itself, conducted by the basalt up from somewhere deep in the plateau's infrastructure. Layered over that, the constant percussion of industrial extraction — rhythmic, metronomic, a hammering that has no beginning point and no end. Water transmits sound four times faster than air, and here, in this vast empty volume, there is nothing to absorb it. The sound reflects off every fresh ridge and column, multiplies, feeds back. After an hour the sound is not something you hear. It is something you *are*. Your chest cavity resonates. Your teeth vibrate at frequencies you can't identify. The sound is so present it becomes indistinguishable from silence — from the silence that would exist if the sound stopped, which it will not, because nothing here changes.

The plateau is in equilibrium. Not the equilibrium of death, though it resembles it. The equilibrium of a machine that has found its operating frequency and will run until its fuel runs out, which will not be soon.

---

No plants grow here. Not a single photosynthetic organism coats the rock. The geology is naked — black, gray, the occasional streak of rust-orange where mineral deposits oxidize in the current. The cold and the depth and the youth of the formation have left nothing for root or frond. It is a mineral world.

And yet the fauna.

They cluster around the structures, thick and writhing, pale things with no use for eyes. Tube worms in dense groves, their chitin shells clattering in the current like loose teeth. Armored bottom-feeders the size of dogs, scuttling between rock and pipe, feeding on — what? Each other, mostly. Bacteria that metabolize the industrial runoff. Organic particulates too fine for you to see. The food chain here has no plant base. It has a mechanical base. The extraction engines leak heat and chemical compounds into the water, and life has learned to eat the leaching. There are thousands of species. Millions of individuals. They blanket the structures in writhing layers, thick as fur, falling away only where the heat becomes too intense or the vibrations too extreme.

Further from the structures, in the open water over the plateau's flat expanses, there is almost nothing. A jelly drifts past every few hours. Something large and slow moves in the gray distance, grazing on the invisible. The density of life here is, per cubic meter, nearly zero. But around the installations — around every pipe, every platform, every vibrating engine housing — the fauna packs so thick the structures are barely visible beneath them. Life has learned to cluster around the machine.

---

The installations themselves are scattered at distances that make no ergonomic sense. A pumping station here, its head lost in cloud. An extraction rig there, connected by a pipeline that runs across kilometers of bare basalt. A processing platform, its smelters long cold, still venting something into the water that the fauna find irresistible. Each structure is industrial in design — bolted steel plates, riveted seams, intake grates, exhaust stacks. The scale is production-scale. Whoever built this was extracting something in quantities worth the engineering.

But the structures are partial. Half-finished. A pipeline terminates at a second rig that has no rig — just bolts in the rock where one would have gone. A dormitory module sits connected to nothing, its interior flooded and empty of anything personal. The construction was interrupted, or abandoned, or simply stopped because the machine reached a state where it no longer needed human input. The engines run. The extraction continues. Whatever is being taken from the plateau's substrate is being processed and — vented? Stored? Transported? There's no way to tell. The system has achieved closure.

You could explore for weeks — the plateau is vast enough, empty enough, that crossing from one structure to the next takes days — and you would not find the full extent of the operation. There are always more rigs, further out, fainter, half-buried in the geology, older, newer, all running, all thrumming, all generating the sound that has become the only constant in this cold, light-diffused, changeless water.

---

Time does something odd here. Because nothing changes — the light is always this blue, the cold is always this cold, the sound is always this sound, the fauna cycle through their generations but never alter their patterns — because nothing changes, duration loses its edges. You cannot feel hours passing. You cannot distinguish one basalt ridge from another. You swim past a formation and thirty minutes later swim past it again, or one identical to it, and cannot tell whether you have circled back or whether the plateau simply repeats, a generative pattern extending beyond any horizon you could reach.

This is what the weirdness feels like: not an overt strangeness, but a slow realization that this place does not operate on the logic of a world that includes you. The fauna do not care about you. The machines do not register you. The sound does not pause. The plateau goes on, and on, and on, vast enough for weather, empty enough for echoes, loud enough to become the only truth, and you are — not unwelcome. Not welcome. *Irrelevant.* An observation point in a system that achieved completeness before you arrived and will maintain it after you leave, if you leave, if leaving is even a concept that applies here, where the water goes up forever and the sound goes on forever and the cold is not a temperature but a permission to simply *stop changing.*

The plateau does not notice you.

It thrums.
