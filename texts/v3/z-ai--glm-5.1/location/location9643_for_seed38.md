# The Vast Weep

It begins with the understanding that there is no ground here.

Oh, something supports weight — you can walk, if walking is what you'd call the slow, sinking, deliberate placement of each foot into the fibrous mat that extends in every direction to the curve of the horizon and beyond. But ground implies mineral, implies the dead lithic certainty of stone and soil. This is not that. This has not been that for longer than most worlds remember having been anything at all.

---

The plateau goes on for thousands of kilometers.

From above — if anything still flew, which nothing does here, not anymore — it would appear as a continental shelf of deep rust-brown and muted burgundy, shot through with veins of darker water that pulse slowly, like circulation in a body too large to have a heartbeat in any meaningful sense. The edges of the plateau fall away in great cascading tears where the living mat hangs over ancient cliff faces, and from these lips, water weeps constantly. Not dramatic waterfalls. Not plunging cataracts. Just a ceaseless, patient drip, drip, drip, as if the plateau itself is crying, and has been crying for so long it no longer notices.

The elevation is real. Two kilometers, perhaps three, above whatever lies below the weeping rims. The air is thin enough that breath comes with intention, thick enough with moisture that every inhalation tastes of copper and chlorophyll. Clouds form *within* the landscape, not above it — condensing in the cooler hollows, dissolving in the warmer ridges, so that you walk through weather as a fish swims through current. Visibility shifts from thirty meters to three hundred and back within the span of an hour's slow walking, and the landscape reveals and conceals itself like a slow tide.

The sky, when it can be seen at all, is the color of old pewter. The light that reaches the surface has passed through so many layers of vapor and living membrane that it arrives softened, spread, sourceless. There are no shadows with hard edges here. Everything is lit as if from everywhere at once, dimly, the way a hand looks when held before a lantern.

---

The mat is the substrate. The mat is the architecture. The mat is the world.

It is root. All of it, root. Or something that was root once, before root became something else entirely. The vegetation here — if that word still applies to something that has superseded the category — has solved the problem of ground by becoming it. Over epochs beyond counting, the plants of this high place grew and died and grew again into their own remains, layer upon layer upon layer, each generation's roots threading through the last, digesting it partially, fusing with it, becoming a single continuous body of living tissue that is simultaneously forest and floor and substructure. You walk on roots that are walking on roots that are walking on roots, and somewhere far below — dozens of meters down, perhaps more — there might be stone, but no one has touched stone here in a hundred million years.

The surface is not flat. The mat has its own topography, slow swells and hollows that shift over centuries as the growth beneath surges or subsides. Ridges rise where ancient root-masses have thickened, forming barriers you must climb with hands and knees, your fingers sinking into the spongy, wet bark. Depressions gather water into shallow, dark pools, warm as blood, covered in a film of translucent living tissue that parts around your legs and slowly closes behind you. The water is always near. Always. You can feel it beneath your feet with every step — the mat squelches, compresses, releases a dark fluid that smells of tannin and old sugar. You are never not standing in water. You are simply standing in water that has learned to support weight.

The texture varies. In places, the surface is a dense felt of root-hairs, soft as moss, clinging to your boots with gentle persistence. In others, larger roots have surfaced and braided into rope-like ridges, smooth and dark and faintly warm to the touch. Sometimes a calcified trunk emerges like a pillar, something that died standing and was absorbed in place, its outline still visible in the living mass that grew around and through it. The living roots have filled its bark with themselves, using the dead shape as scaffold, and now it stands as a kind of fossil that is also still growing.

---

And the plants — the *plants* —

There is no bare surface anywhere. Every centimeter of the root-mat is itself covered in growth, and that growth is covered in growth, and so on, a recursive density of living forms so thorough that the concept of "individual" becomes absurd. The closest thing to a distinct organism is the Creep, which is what you might call the dominant ground-cover: a slow, mounding vine that spreads in great rounded hummocks of deep burgundy leaves, each leaf the size of a hand, each surface beaded with condensation. The Creep roots wherever it touches, which is everywhere, and its roots are indistinguishable from the roots of the mat itself. It may be that the Creep *is* the mat. It may be that everything here is the mat. The question of where one organism ends and another begins is not a question this landscape recognizes.

Above the Creep, the Stand. Not trees — nothing here is quite a tree anymore. Vertical growths, rather, rising in clusters from the mat, some reaching eight or ten meters, their surfaces not bark but a dense lattice of root and leaf fused into a structural column. They are leafless for most of their height, then branch suddenly into broad, dark canopy-sheets that overlap and interlock, catching what light there is and holding it. The undersides of these canopy-sheets drip constantly, and the air beneath them is a slow warm rain that never intensifies and never stops. Walking through a Stand is walking through a living building, and the feeling is unmistakable — you are *inside* something.

Between the Stands, the openings fill with Hanging, thread-fine root-fibers that descend from the canopy-sheets in curtains, swaying in the almost imperceptible air movements, each fiber beaded with water that slides down its length and drops onto the Creep below. The Hanging collects moisture from the air and delivers it to the ground. The entire plateau is a water-catchment system of staggering complexity, and every organism is a component.

There are no flowers. There have not been flowers for a very long time. Reproduction here happens through extension, through fusion, through the slow anastomosis of root into root. Sex, if it ever occurred, has been replaced by something more patient — the gradual merging of boundaries, the dissolution of self into neighbor, until the distinction is academic.

---

The fauna exist in the gaps.

Not gaps in space, exactly — gaps in the metabolism. The plant-mass processes everything: light, water, mineral. But it does not process everything quickly. There are energies and substances that flow through the system more slowly than the vegetation can absorb them, and in these narrow margins, the animals persist.

They are not large. The largest is the Lurk, a flat, wide creature that moves across the surface of the mat on hundreds of soft, gripping appendages, feeding as it goes on the outermost layer of Creep. It is the color of wet bark and nearly invisible when still, which is most of the time. A Lurk will sit motionless for days, slowly digesting the vegetation it has pressed itself against, before moving on. You might walk past one and never notice. You might step on one and feel only a soft, yielding warmth beneath your foot, and the Lurk would not flinch.

There are Swimmers in the pools — eel-like things, translucent, their internal structures visible as faint shadows within their bodies. They feed on the micro-organisms that breed in the warm water, and the pools feed on them in turn when they die. The film on the water's surface closes over their bodies within minutes, and by the next day, there is only a slight thickening in the Creep at that spot to mark the exchange.

And everywhere, everywhere, the Crawl — a category rather than a creature, encompassing thousands of forms of small, many-legged things that move through the root-fiber lattice, eating and being eaten, processing the debris that the larger system cannot handle quickly enough. They are the substrate's substrate, the breakers-down, the ones who make the dead matter small enough for the roots to absorb. Without them, the mat would choke on its own waste. With them, it is eternal.

The animals are warm. All of them. The pools, the mat, the air — everything here hovers near blood-temperature, and the fauna have become indistinguishable from their environment in this regard. No thermoregulation. No heat signature. They are the same temperature as the living mass around them, and this makes them invisible in the way that a hand is invisible when held against the body it belongs to.

---

Sound is a patient, sparse thing.

The dripping, always the dripping. It has rhythm but no tempo — each drop falling in its own time, and the cumulative effect is not a beat but a texture, a soft, irregular percussion that becomes indistinguishable from silence once you have been here long enough.

The Creep makes sound as it grows. A faint creaking, a slow whisper of leaf against leaf as it extends. The Stands groan occasionally, their vertical masses shifting minutely as water weight accumulates and is released. These sounds are so low and so infrequent that you feel them more than hear them, a vibration in the mat beneath your feet.

The animals are nearly silent. The Lurk moves without sound. The Swimmers part the surface film with a gentle *tck* that might be a drop falling in reverse. The Crawl make a faint, dry rustling in the root-layers, like wind in dead leaves, except the leaves are not dead, and there is no wind.

Once, perhaps once per day, a section of Hanging will release its accumulated water all at once, and there will be a sudden rush — a brief, soft torrent that lasts ten seconds and then is gone, absorbed into the Creep below. This is the loudest sound the plateau makes, and after it passes, the silence that follows feels like a held breath.

---

The remains are few and far between, and they are not structures so much as traces.

Here: a pattern in the root-mat that is too regular to be natural, a rectangle of denser growth where something once stood, something the Creep has long since absorbed and replaced. The outline persists because the root-structure beneath is different where it grew around foundations, more compressed, more ordered. You can trace the walls with your fingers, if you are patient. A building stood here. It was made of something the mat could digest, and it has been digested, completely, but the scar in the tissue remains.

There: a shaft of harder material — not metal, not stone, something else, something the roots cannot break down — projecting from the surface at an angle. It is smooth and dark and cool to the touch, and the roots have grown around it in thickened masses, like flesh around a splinter. Whatever it was once part of, it is the only fragment the plateau could not consume. It will be here when everything else is gone.

And here, the strangest trace: a cavity within the mat, a hollow space roughly the size and shape of a room, where the roots have grown around a volume of air and refused to fill it. The walls of the cavity are dense, almost solid, and smooth — the Creep has not colonized the interior. It is dry inside, the only dry place on the plateau, and the air within is still and old. If you sit in this space, you can hear the mat around you, the slow creak of growth, the distant drip of water, the vast respiratory pulse of the living world pressing in on all sides. And you can feel, faintly, what the space itself feels like: *preserved*. *Intentional*. As if something asked the mat to hold this shape, and the mat, which has forgotten nothing, is still holding it.

Whoever they were, they understood this place. They did not build *on* it; they built *with* it. They spoke to the roots, perhaps, or fed them, or sang to them in frequencies that the Crawl still carry in their bodies. They are gone now, but the plateau remembers them the way a body remembers a wound — not the pain, but the shape, the place where something was taken away.

---

Time moves differently here.

Not slower — that would imply a speed to compare against. Rather, time moves *thoroughly*. Every moment is filled to its capacity with process. The dripping, the growing, the slow pulse of water through root-fiber, the patient work of the Crawl, the gradual extension of the Creep, the accretion of condensation on every surface — all of this is happening always, continuously, without pause or acceleration. A day is indistinguishable from a century. The clouds form and dissolve on their own schedule. The mat breathes in and out over spans too long for any single creature to perceive.

If you stayed here long enough, you would stop noticing change at all. The landscape would become a fixed constant, a given, a background too large and too slow to be seen as alive. And then one day you would notice that a Stand has fallen, and another has risen in a different place, and the pool you used to sit beside has filled with Creep and become a hummock, and you would realize that the world had been moving all along, that you had simply failed to see it because it moved the way a continent moves — too slowly for eyes, too fast for stone.

---

The plateau has no edge, in the way that a sea has no edge. It has shores, where the living mat thins and fragments and finally gives way to bare rock, and from these shores the weeping is most dramatic — great curtains of root and vine hanging over the cliffs, water streaming from every fiber, the drop below lost in mist and distance. But the plateau does not end at these shores. It simply continues in a different form. The roots go down the cliff faces and into the lowlands, thinner and weaker, but present. The Creep follows the water, and the water follows the rock, and down there, in the warmer, brighter, faster lands below, the mat gives way to something more familiar — distinct plants, distinct animals, distinct boundaries.

But up here, the boundaries have dissolved. Up here, the living mass has had enough time to become what it always wanted to be: one thing. Continuous. Patient. Eternal and changing.

The plateau does not know you are here.

It will absorb your footprints within the hour. It will process your shed skin cells, your breath's moisture, the heat of your passage, and it will make them into itself with the same patient thoroughness it brings to everything. And when you leave — if you leave — the space where you stood will close over like water behind a hand, and there will be no mark, no memory, no trace.

Except perhaps a hollow somewhere, the size and shape of a person, where the roots refuse to grow.
