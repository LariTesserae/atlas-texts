# The Condensation Works

The plateau stretches longer than you can walk in a day, and across it, the city spreads like a skeleton left to weather in desert air — except this is no desert, and the bones are iron and dressed stone.

---

Heat rises from below. You feel it through the soles of your boots, a constant upward pressure, as if the plateau itself is exhaling. The rock beneath is volcanic, old beyond reckoning, shot through with veins of something that holds warmth the way a living body holds blood. Geothermal. The word comes to you clinically, but the experience is intimate — the ground is warm, is nearly hot, is a presence you stand *inside* rather than *on*.

And from that heat, water.

Not rain. Rarely rain. The water here comes from below and from the air itself. The atmosphere condenses. You see it happen in the mornings when the temperature differential peaks: the hot exhalations of the plateau meet whatever cooler air has crawled up from the lowlands, and moisture appears on every surface. It beads on iron railings. It films the stone walls. It runs down the fluted columns of the older district in thin, deliberate streams, tracing paths worn deep over centuries of the same dripping. The rocks weep. They have always wept. The channels carved into the walkways — you mistook them for drainage, but they are *collection*, guiding the condensation toward cisterns set below ground, toward the intake pipes of the engines.

You are inside a machine that breathes.

---

The city — you call it a city because the word for this has not been invented — was built to the scale of extraction. The structures are enormous, each one a blockhouse of black stone and riveted iron, four stories, six stories, taller than they have any right to be given how few people move between them. And between the structures: emptiness. Vast, graded emptiness. The avenues are wide enough to hold a river, and they nearly do — the condensation channels run down the center of each thoroughfare like a spine, exposed, covered in places by iron grating, open in others where the water falls into subterranean conduits with a sound like swallowed breath.

You can stand at an intersection and see four, five, six structures receding in each direction, each separated by the length of a parade ground, each trailing its own threads of steam from vent-stacks on the roof. And no one. No crowds. No carts. Occasionally, at the edge of hearing, the low rhythmic thud of an engine — the big reciprocating kind, the kind that turns shafts that turn pumps that pull water from the deep reservoirs and push steam through the pipe networks that lace the plateau like a circulatory system drawn by an architect who never quite finished the body.

The engines do not sleep. That is the wrong way to think about it. They do not wake, either. They *proceed*.

---

No plants grow. This is the first thing you notice after the heat and the wet: the absolute mineral absence of green. Not even lichen on the stonework. Not even moss in the channels where water runs constantly. The rock is too hot, perhaps, or the mineral content of the condensate is wrong — you taste it once, from a tap in a public fountain, and it is flat and mineral-thick, almost metallic, like water that has been through too many cycles of boiling and recondensing. It tastes *used*.

The closest thing to vegetation is the rust. Iron oxide blooms across every exposed fitting, spreading in fractal branches that look almost like ferns, almost like the memory of a forest pressed into metal by time and moisture. The rust has its own ecology — orange, red, black, each colony shading into the next, each patch advancing or retreating with the condensation cycles. You find yourself studying it the way you would study a field. It is not alive. It *behaves*.

There is something else, too. In the lowest passages, where the heat is most intense and the steam collects in opaque banks, you have seen — or thought you saw — growths on the walls. Not fungal. Not quite mineral. Pale, fibrous, branching upward toward the lightless ceiling like fingers reaching for something that was never there. They crumble at a touch. They leave a residue like ash and hot water. You do not know what they are. You suspect the engines do, and the engines are not telling.

---

The light is the problem that reshapes everything. Or rather, the lack of it.

During the day — and you know it is day only because the dimness shifts from charcoal to a slightly lighter gray — the atmosphere does something to the sky. The perpetual moisture, the steam-stacks, the heat shimmer rising from the plateau: they form a lid. A diffuse, luminous ceiling that scatters light into a uniform glow without direction or source. There are no shadows. There is no sun. The city exists in a state of permanent overexposure without illumination, every surface visible but depthless, flat, like an engraving of itself.

At night — and again, you know it is night only because the condensation thickens and the temperature drops enough for the real rain to fall, briefly, lukewarm, mineral, hissing on the hot stone — the darkness is complete but for the engines. Each structure has a few ports that glow: deep orange, the color of iron just before it begins to soften. They do not illuminate. They *mark*. They are points of heat in a dark so thick you can taste it, and you navigate between them like a sailor between lighthouses, measuring your progress by the successive appearance and disappearance of each low, warm glow.

You learn to see by condensation. The water film on surfaces catches what little light exists and defines edges. In the dimness, the city reveals itself through wetness — the outlines of buildings, the grid of the avenues, the precise geometry of the collection channels. The water is the city's drawing of itself.

---

Sound collapses here. The wet air absorbs it, muffles it, turns every sharp noise into a cushioned thud. Footsteps become suggestions. Voices become textures rather than words. The only sounds that carry with any authority are the low, mechanical ones: the thud of the engines, the hiss of steam finding release, the crack of cooling iron as the evening temperature drops. These sounds do not echo. They *pool*, collecting in the low places, the cisterns, the channels, becoming a substrate of noise that underlies everything but never rises to the level of distraction.

In the quiet intervals — between one engine pulse and the next, when the wind drops and the condensation is still forming and has not yet begun to drip — you hear the plateau itself. A low, continuous subsonic murmur. The sound of heat moving through rock. The sound of water becoming steam becoming water again. The sound of a place that is *processing*, endlessly, without purpose you can discern.

---

You have been here long enough to learn the rhythm. Condensation peaks in the morning. The collection channels run full, the cisterns fill, the engines draw water and convert it to steam and pump it through the pipe network, and the steam reaches the upper vents and condenses again on the cooler evening air and falls as lukewarm rain that hisses on the stone and runs back to the channels. A cycle. A closed system, or nearly so. The city waters itself. The city powers itself. The city continues.

But for *whom*?

You have seen the few others — workers, or custodians, or whatever they are. They move between the structures with the same unhurried, directed purpose as the condensation. They wear heavy cloth stiff with mineral deposits, and they do not speak, or cannot, or choose not to. They tend the engines in ways you cannot quite follow — not maintenance, exactly, but *negotiation*, hands on the housings, feeling the vibrations, making small adjustments to valves that seem to respond to pressures you cannot measure. They live here. They have always lived here. You suspect they have been shaped by this place in ways you are only beginning to recognize in yourself: the way you have stopped expecting green, the way you navigate by the warm orange ports, the way you have started to taste the water without flinching.

The city does not need you. This is the strangest thing. The city was built — by someone, for something, in a time that left no records but the depth of the channels and the wear on the stone — and it continues without purpose you can name and without occupants in numbers that would justify its scale. The engines proceed. The water cycles. The rust advances and retreats. The pale growths spread in the low passages. The condensation forms and falls and forms again. And you walk the avenues between the empty blockhouses, measuring the distance from one warm orange port to the next, learning the city the way it learns itself: through repetition, through water, through the slow accumulation of pattern on pattern until something almost like meaning almost like purpose almost like a reason for all this heat and all this wet and all this vast, graded, weeping emptiness almost — almost — emerges.

But not quite. Not yet.
