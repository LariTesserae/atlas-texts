# The Brooding

The first thing is the sound. Not a sound—*the* sound. A composite roar that enters through the sternum before the ears register it. Clicking, rustling, the bass thrum of bodies pressing against bodies, and underneath all of it, a wet respiratory noise like a giant throat clearing, endlessly.

You descend into it. There is no other way to arrive.

---

The entrance is a throat itself—a vertical fissure in pale volcanic tuff that still runs warm to the touch, still soft at its margins where the rock hasn't quite decided what shape to hold. The stone here is newborn. You can feel it beneath your fingertips: the mineral surface gives slightly, like cartilage, like something that hasn't calcified into final form. Condensation beads on the walls in a continuous film and the air enters your lungs already damp, already tasted by ten thousand others.

Down. The passage widens.

And then—light.

Not the light of the surface. Not sun or even the metallic blue of deep sky. This light is biological. It comes from the walls, from the ceiling, from the *things* on the ceiling, from the air itself where tiny winged bodies pulse with ventral phosphorescence as they navigate the chamber. The glow is amber and honeycomb-gold, deep in places, almost surgical in others where particular species have claimed particular mineral shelves. It's enough light to read by. Enough light to see the scope of what you've entered.

The chamber extends beyond sight. Not because the light fails but because the space *curves*—the floor drops away in a vast bowl, a caldera-scale depression in the living rock, and the far walls are lost in the haze of biological luminescence and the sheer *density* of living bodies. You are standing at the rim of a living amphitheater, and it is full.

---

There are no plants. This is the thing that takes longest to understand, because the visual information screams *jungle*—layered complexity, surfaces covered in growth, the sense of a green so dense it breathes. But there is no green here. What you took for vines are larvae—maggots in chitinous casings, spiraling up columns of pumice in pale ribbons, their bodies pressing flank to flank, climbing over each other in a slow-motion flood toward the condensation that drips from the ceiling. What you took for moss are springtails, millions of them, coating the floor in a shifting pelt that parts around your foot and re-forms behind it like water. What you took for fruit hanging from fungal shelves are egg cases, translucent and pulsing, each one containing something that will emerge before you leave this place.

The warmth rises from below. Geothermal. The floor thrums with it, a heat that you feel through your boot-soles like standing on a just-cooled stove. This is why they're here—the eggs, the larvae, the spiraling migrations. The rock is still gestating, still cooling from whatever magmatic event carved this void, and the creatures have found it first, have *colonized the heat* like a shoreline species colonizes a new island. Every degree of cooling opens new territory. Every hour, the boundary shifts.

And the place is loud with arrival.

---

You pick your way down the interior slope. The ground is wet—not running water, but a persistent dampness that rises from below, squeezed from the cooling stone as it contracts, pressed out like juice from fruit. It pools in the low places, and in those pools: other creatures. Not swimming—*writhing*. Nematode knots so dense they appear as solid masses, living ropes that twist and merge and separate in a constant re-knitting. In the shallower moisture, where condensation runs in films across the mineral surface, you see the first of the beetles.

They are everywhere. They are the baseline. A copper-shelled carabid, thumb-sized, moving in columns that follow moisture gradients—thousands of them, their leg-clicks merging into that white-noise hiss that forms the foundation of the chamber's endless sound. They do not avoid you. They move around you like water around a stone, their antennae brushing your ankle, your calf, tasting you, categorizing you as *obstacle, not food*, and continuing. The column re-forms on the other side of your leg without pause.

Where the beetles concentrate most densely, the floor *seems to move*. You learn to distinguish the carpet of springtails from the migration of beetles from the slow geomorphic flow of larvae by the speed and direction of the surface's apparent motion. Three currents in a river of bodies, overlapping, ignoring each other, each following a different gradient—heat, moisture, chemical trace—and producing at their margins a churning boundary layer where individuals collide, pause, antennae touch, information transfers, and they move on.

---

Halfway down the bowl's interior, you find the first structure.

It is not impressive. A low wall, knee-height, built from chunks of the same soft tuff that forms the chamber, stacked without mortar, the gaps between stones filled with the pale bodies of dead larvae—chitinous mortar, organic cement. Behind the wall, a depression in the floor has been deepened, shaped, and in its bottom: water. Actual water, a hand's depth of it, clear and warm and *free of creatures*. Something—someone—has built a cistern. The wall deflects the migrations. The dead larvae in the mortar leach something, perhaps, that the living avoid. The water is being kept for something.

You find a stone tool beside it. A fist-sized river cobble, not from this stone, brought from elsewhere, one face battered to a crude working edge. It lies half-in and half-out of the water, as if dropped mid-task.

Someone lives here. Or did. Or tries to.

---

The structure is not alone. As your eyes adjust to the logic of this place—learning to read the density of bodies as *terrain*, learning to see the gaps between migrations as *paths*—you spot others. A lean-to of stacked pumice, half-collapsed, its interior colonized by a species of pale arachnid you haven't seen elsewhere, their webs catching the moisture that drips from the ceiling above. A pit, carefully excavated, its walls smooth with use, containing nothing now but a residue of something oily and amber. A cairn, waist-high, its stones blackened on one side as if by fire—though there is no fuel here that would burn, nothing but animal fat and chitin, and you cannot imagine who would waste it on flame.

These are not the structures of a people who mastered this place. They are the structures of a people who *endured* it. Who found the gaps between the swarms and pressed themselves into those gaps and held. The cistern's water is warm and mineral-tasting, but it is *water that won't crawl down your throat*. The pits might have been for storage, for concealment, for the production of something from the raw biological material surrounding them—whoever made them had learned to process what the chamber provided.

You find a second tool. A bone awl, the spinal process of something large, sharpened to a point. The bone is old. The creatures have been here longer than the builders.

---

The center of the bowl is the deepest point and the warmest. The rock here hasn't finished being rock. It has the consistency of packed wet ash—it supports weight but leaves impressions, and the heat rising through it is almost uncomfortable against bare skin. Here, the fauna reaches its maximum density and its maximum agitation.

You understand the turbulence now.

This place is *still being born*. The magmatic void is still cooling, still shrinking, still weeping moisture and heat into itself. And the creatures—whoever they are, whatever drove them into this subterranean nursery—arrive in waves. The first colonists were probably the nematodes and springtails, simple machines following simple gradients. Then the beetles, following the springtails. Then the larvae, following the heat. Then the things that eat the larvae—the pale arachnids, the centipedes as long as your forearm, their segments clicking like bead curtains as they move through the maggot-ropes, selecting, pulling, consuming without pause. Then the things that eat *them*.

The food chain here is a food *braid*, complex, self-interfering, with species that occupy multiple positions depending on their life stage. The larvae you saw climbing the columns—they will become something else. The egg cases will hatch before the hour turns. Every surface is a nursery. Every warm body is food for something smaller, and every small thing is food for something that moves with more intent.

And at the center, where the heat is greatest, where the rock is softest, where the moisture accumulates in pools deep enough to submerge your hands—there is a constant churning. Bodies arriving, bodies consumed, bodies being born. The floor here is not floor but *compost*—layers of chitin, of shed exoskeleton, of waste, of the dead—compacting under the weight of the living into something like soil, something that will one day be stone, but right now is a living stratum, a geological formation made of animals.

The sound at the center is deafening. Not a roar but a *granular* noise—millions of individual sounds at the edge of perception, each one a leg-scrape, a mandible-click, a wet emergence, a dry death, merging into a texture that you feel more than hear. Your own breathing joins it. Your heartbeat, they can probably feel it.

---

You climb back out. The rim of the bowl gives you the best view of the place as a whole—the living amphitheater, the glow rising from it like fog, the scattered structures like islands in the flood of bodies, the curved walls of the chamber rising to a ceiling you can barely see through the haze of luminescent wings.

Somewhere below, someone is probably crouching beside their cistern, watching the beetles flow past their low wall, dipping out water with a tool that took hours to shape, surrounded on all sides by a world that does not care whether they live or die but which offers, in its warmth and its moisture and its overwhelming productivity, the raw materials for survival.

The chamber is not finished. The creatures are not finished. The stone is not finished. The structures are partial, provisional, always being rebuilt as the migrations shift and the heat gradient changes and the water finds new paths through the softening rock. Everything here is under construction, including the ecology—including the meaning of the place, which is being written in chitin and condensation and the slow contraction of cooling stone.

You will not stay. But something in you recognizes this as the oldest place you have ever been: a world in the act of becoming, before the becoming is done, full of life that doesn't know it's early, that doesn't know the stone will harden and the water will find new channels and the migrations will shift and everything that seems permanent will be revealed as provisional, as intermediate, as one moment in a longer process that has no name yet because no one has survived long enough to watch it complete.

The sound follows you up the passage. It is still entering your sternum when you reach the surface and find, to your surprise, that it is raining.
