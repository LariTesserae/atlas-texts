# The Making

The first thing is the sound. It arrives before understanding—a deep, composite rhythm that you feel in the sternum before you parse it with your ears. Striking. Scraping. The wet slap of clay against stone. A low groan, structural, like a jaw opening slowly. And beneath all of it, a constant tapping, as of a thousand hands testing the walls to see if they're ready.

It is dark here, but not the dark of absence. This is a working dark. The dark of a closed mouth, of a room where something is being done that requires no eyes. What light exists comes from the work itself—phosphorescent mineral seams in the raw stone that has not yet been converted, faintly green, faintly blue, casting the nearest surfaces into a relief that reveals their texture without explaining their shape. You see by the geology's own glow, and by the occasional flash of heat where a tool strikes a spark from an inclusion.

Everything is made. There is no ground. There is no natural floor, no soil, no bed of ancient rock waiting beneath the construction to reassure you with its permanence. The surfaces you walk on are surfaces someone laid—fitted stone, packed clay, in places a crude concrete of crushed rock and some binding agent that smells faintly of alkali and old water. The walls are made. The ceilings are made. The passages between chambers are made, and they are narrow, intentionally so, sized for bodies that move differently than yours does, shoulders that roll with a different architecture.

The place is still becoming. That is the second thing you understand, after the sound. You pass through chambers where the work has been finished—the stone fitted tight, the surfaces almost smooth—and then through transition zones where the walls are only half-realized, blocks stacked and awaiting their mortar, raw rock faces still showing the tool marks where they were hewn away from whatever preceded them. And then you reach edges where the making is happening now. You hear it before you see it: the concentrated intensity of labor, grunts and calls in a language that might not be language, might be rhythm alone, might be the sound that bodies make when they have been doing the same motion for long enough that it becomes song.

The builders work by touch and by memory. They are shaped—perhaps—like you, perhaps not. In the dark you cannot tell. You see hands, certainly. Many hands. Thick-fingered, callused, moving with a fluency that does not require sight. They lay courses of stone. They pack the gaps with a clay they carry in slung pouches. They work without hurry and without pause, and they have been working, you understand, for a very long time.

---

The damp arrives in the morning, if morning exists here—which it doesn't, not really, but there is a cycle, a slow pulse in the air that might correspond to something outside the construction, some tidal influence on the atmosphere. The damp comes as condensation, beading on the ceilings and upper walls of the chambers, collecting into droplets that fall with a sound like a finger tapping a table—one at a time, then several, then a brief cloudburst of moisture that lasts ten minutes and leaves the floors slick and the air smelling of mineral water. The water pools in the low places, in the channels that have been carved into the floors for exactly this purpose, and flows somewhere you cannot track. The builders do not drink it. Or if they do, you do not see them do it. The water is moved, managed, directed—a system within the construction, as deliberate as the walls.

Between the rain-times the air is merely cool and close. It holds the smell of clay and stone dust and something else—a faint organic note, like old sweat, like breath left in an enclosed space. This is not unpleasant. It is the smell of occupancy, of bodies that have been here, working, respiring. The air moves slowly through the chambers, pulled by pressure differentials the construction itself creates—corridors that act as throats, chambers as lungs. The building breathes. Not metaphorically. You can feel the draft against your face, rhythmic, slow.

The density of the place presses on you. Not claustrophobia exactly—the chambers vary in size, some large enough to hold thirty bodies, some barely wide enough for one—but the sense that every volume is occupied, every space accounted for. There are no leftover spaces, no unused corners, no places where the construction simply gave up or forgot. Where a chamber is not needed, a wall fills it. Where a passage is not required, it is not left. The making is total and economical, and this economy produces its own claustrophobia—the sense that you are in a place where nothing is wasted, including you.

---

The fauna are rare but they are present. You hear them before you see them—or you hear something and later decide it must have been an animal, because the builders do not make sounds like that. A high chittering from somewhere above the ceiling, from the interstitial spaces between chambers where no one goes. A scrabbling in the drainage channels. Once, in a larger chamber where three corridors converge, something moves across the floor with a sound like dry fingers on wet stone, and you catch a flash of it in the mineral glow—a body the size of a large rat, but with too many legs, or limbs that work differently than legs should, and a pale eye that catches the faint light and throws it back at you before it disappears into a crack in the wall that should not be wide enough to accommodate it. The builders do not react. It is possible they do not see it. It is possible it is theirs. It is possible it was here before they were.

You do not see the builders' faces. Or you do, and they are unremarkable, and you cannot describe them afterward—or you can, but the description changes each time you try. Broad. Narrow. Flat. The work is what remains consistent: the hands placing stones, the bodies carrying clay, the tools—stone hammers, wooden wedges, copper chisels that are too soft for this work and must be re-sharpened constantly, the rhythmic scraping of the sharpening itself another layer in the sound. The tools are simple. The work they accomplish is not. The construction extends beyond your sight in every direction, chambers and passages and more chambers, a complexity that would require mathematics to plan—but the builders do not use mathematics, or if they do, it lives in their hands, in the memory of their bodies, in a knowledge that does not require notation.

You pass through a section where the walls are newer, the stone lighter, the mortar still damp. The tool marks are fresh and sharp. You touch a block and feel the vibration of the next chamber through it—boom, boom, boom—something being broken, or built, or both. The raw geology is being consumed here, the natural rock of wherever-this-is being hewn away and reassembled into structure. The builders eat the earth and excrete the made environment. They are converting the world, one chamber at a time.

---

The sound is the constant. You cannot escape it. Even in the finished chambers, far from the active work faces, you hear it—transmitted through the stone itself, a low mechanical hum, the building's own heartbeat, the aggregate sound of all the making happening in all the chambers you cannot see. It is loud enough that you must raise your voice to speak to yourself, and after a while you stop trying, and the sound becomes your interior monologue, the rhythm of your thoughts syncing to the rhythm of the work.

The place is strange in the way that functional things become strange when you cannot understand their function. The passages do not lead where you expect them to. You follow what seems like a main corridor and it narrows to a crack, or opens into a chamber with no other exits, or turns back on itself in a way that feels deliberate, as if the layout is not meant for navigation but for something else—ventilation, perhaps, or drainage, or the channeling of forces you cannot name. The builders move through this maze without hesitation. They know where they are going. Or they are not going anywhere—they are simply where they need to be, and when they need to be elsewhere, they are elsewhere.

There are no plants. There is nothing that photosynthesizes. There is no sun. The mineral glow provides no energy for biological processes, only enough light to work by, to see the next stone, the next application of mortar. The ecology here—if it is an ecology—is based on something else. The water that pools in the channels sustains something, perhaps the pale multi-legged things in the walls, perhaps the builders themselves, perhaps processes within the stone that you do not have the framework to recognize. Faint patches of something on the ceilings—not mold, not exactly, but a thin film of organization that seems to follow the moisture, to grow where the condensation is heaviest, and to recede when the chamber dries. It is not alive in any way you are prepared to defend, but it is not not-alive either.

---

You try to find the edge of the construction and you cannot. It extends beyond your reach in every direction, chamber giving way to chamber, passage to passage, the work faces pushing always outward into new territory, consuming the raw world and leaving made world behind. You try to find the center and you cannot do that either. The place has the quality of a mycelium network, or a crystal lattice forming—it grows where the conditions are right, and the conditions are right everywhere, because the construction creates its own conditions.

You are in something that is being built. You are inside the act of building. The landscape is the activity, not the result—the result does not exist yet, may never exist, may not be the point. The making is the point. The sound is the point. The hands in the dark, placing stone on stone, filling the gaps, making the surfaces that other hands will build upon—the point is the continuation.

You could stay here. The sound would become your blood-pressure, your breath. The damp would become your skin's familiar climate. The dark would become the only reasonable amount of light. You would find a place in the construction where you fit, a chamber or a corridor or a gap that matched your dimensions, and you would make yourself useful, or you would simply occupy the space, and that would be enough, because the construction does not judge occupancy, it only requires density, it only requires that every volume be filled, that every surface be touched, that nothing be left raw and unmade for long.

The builders do not look at you. They do not need to. They are working.
