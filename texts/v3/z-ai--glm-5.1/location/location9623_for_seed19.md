# The Meridian Flat

The plateau goes on.

It should not be called a plateau anymore—that word implies something raised, something with edges that drop away into drama. But the edges dropped away so long ago that even the memory of edges has worn smooth. What remains is a surface so vast and so high that the thin air carries no scent, only the faint mineral tang of stone that finished becoming stone eons ago. The Flat stretches in every direction past the limit of sight, and the horizon sits not at the meeting of earth and sky but at the curved limit of the world itself, as if you stand on the crown of something enormous and patient.

Elevation here is not a matter of climbing. The Flat *is* the height. It has always been height. The rock beneath your feet—is it basalt? Is it something older than basalt, some primordial mineral that has no name because no language has lasted long enough to name it?—has been settled into its current form for longer than mountains have existed elsewhere. Whole ranges have risen and weathered to nothing in the time since this surface last changed. Continents have drifted, seas have opened and closed, and the Meridian Flat has simply continued, smoothing itself by imperceptible degrees toward an ultimate flatness it will reach only when time itself wears out.

The coolness is constant. Not cold—nothing so dramatic. The air sits against your skin at a temperature that makes itself known only in the absence of warmth, a neutral cool that never varies because there is nothing left here to vary it. No sun-heated rock, no shaded valley, no water to moderate or exacerbate. The light comes from a sky that seems too high, too pale, and falls without emphasis. It illuminates everything equally. Nothing casts a shadow worth noticing because the surface has been ground so fine, so level, that a shadow would have nowhere to go.

---

There is no water. This needs to be understood not as scarcity but as *absence*. The Flat does not know water. No rain has fallen here—cannot fall here, perhaps—for stretches of time that make the word "drought" laughable. The stone contains no moisture, no frozen crystals hiding in pores, no ancient aquifers trapped in deep fissures. If you pressed your mouth to the rock and sucked, you would taste only dryness so complete it has become a flavor: mineral nullity, the taste of a thing that has given up even the potential for wetness.

And yet—

The Flat *heaves* with life.

---

They come from the lowlands. That is the only explanation, and it is insufficient. The lowlands are hundreds of leagues away in any direction, far beyond the horizon, far below the gradual slope that once constituted an edge. But they come. They come in numbers that make the word "teeming" sound underpopulated.

You feel them before you see them. A vibration in the stone, so subtle it might be imagined—a thrumming at the threshold of perception, as if the Flat itself were a drumhead and something were striking it from below. Then the first ones crest the impossible horizon, and they keep coming, and they do not stop.

They are—what? They are difficult to describe because they seem to be many things at once, or to be shifting between things, or to be something for which the categories have not been invented. Some resemble insects, but at scales that make the comparison grotesque: carapaces the size of shelters, moving on legs that number in the dozens or the hundreds, each limb jointed in ways that suggest geometry rather than biology. Others move like masses of fur and sinew, but the fur is the wrong texture—too regular, too metallic, catching the flat light with a sheen that belongs to mineral deposits. Some flow. Some roll. Some unfold from shapes so compressed they resembled stones and extend into architectures of limb and segment that take long minutes to complete, and when they are fully extended, they are larger than the scattered structures that dot the surface.

The structures. Yes. They are here too—small, primitive, built from the same stone that makes up the Flat, stacked without mortar, shaped by hands that understood only the simplest principles of wall and roof. Some are no more than windbreaks, semicircles of flat rock laid against nothing. Others have doorways, but the doorways face directions that make no sense—into the ground, into each other, into the sky. They are not ruins. Ruins imply collapse, and these structures have never been grand enough to collapse. They persist in their partialness, as if someone began them and then forgot what building was, or as if the act of building itself became unnecessary midway through and was simply abandoned without ceremony.

Who built them? No one is here to ask. Or rather—everyone is here, and no one can be asked. The fauna are too numerous, too present, too *dense* to approach. They fill the Flat the way atmosphere fills a bowl, pressing against each other, layering three or four deep in places, moving in slow tides that have no apparent direction. And they are silent.

---

The silence is the worst part. Or the most beautiful part. It depends on how long you have been here.

So many bodies in motion—millions of them, perhaps, visible at any given moment, and beyond them the promise of millions more emerging from the horizon's edge—and they make no sound. No calls, no chittering, no hoofbeats or scrapes of chitin on stone. Even the vibration in the rock fades once they have fully arrived, as if their presence becomes so total that it cancels itself out. The Flat absorbs their noise, or they have never been noisy, or sound itself has become unnecessary here and has retreated, embarrassed, to places where it is still needed.

You can hear your own breathing. You can hear the small sounds your body makes as it shifts its weight. You cannot hear the creature passing six feet to your left, the one that resembles a quadruped made of folded leather and quiet breathing. It does not look at you. None of them look at you. They move around you the way water moves around a stone—not with avoidance, exactly, but with a disinterest so complete it has the force of physics. You are an obstacle, or you are nothing. They cannot decide, and so they treat you as both.

---

The Flat does not change.

This is not a figure of speech. The Flat *does not change*. The creatures arrive and move in their slow, silent tides, and they leave—eventually, over hours or days or longer, they recede back past the horizon in directions that do not correspond to the directions from which they came—and the surface they leave behind is identical to the surface they crossed. No tracks. No waste. No marks of any kind. The stone is as unmarked as it was before the passage of however many thousands of tons of living matter. As if the Flat refused the evidence of their passage. As if it had reached a state so stable, so final, that even the passage of life could not alter it by a single grain.

The structures do not change either. The wind—when there is wind, which is rare—does not weather them. The creatures do not disturb them. The primitive walls stand as they stood when the last hand placed the last stone, and that hand may have been withdrawn ten thousand years ago or ten million. There is no way to tell. Time has surrendered its tools here. Erosion, that great engine of change, has already completed its work. The Flat is the end-product of a process that began when the world was young, and it has nowhere left to go.

Except—

---

Sometimes the creatures pass through each other.

Not often. Not in a way that seems intentional or even noticeable. But you will see it if you watch long enough: two of the leather-folded quadrupeds occupying the same space for a moment before continuing on their separate ways. An insect-thing and a fur-mass overlapping, their geometries intersecting in ways that produce neither collision nor recognition. They do not react. They simply *are*, and their being is not exclusive.

This is when you begin to understand the weirdness.

The Flat is not entirely real. Or it is real in a way that includes more reals than the one you occupy. The creatures that teem across its surface are not all here in the same sense. They are here in layers, in strata of existence that happen to share a coordinate, and when they pass through each other it is because they are moving through different versions of the same location that have been pressed together by the incomprehensible weight of time. The Flat has existed for so long, in such stability, that it has accumulated realities the way a filter accumulates sediment. Everything that has ever happened here is still happening, faintly, in the same place, at the same time.

The primitive structures make a different kind of sense now. They were built by different hands in different versions of the same place, by people—or things—who understood that building was a way of marking not just space but *layer*. Each wall is a statement: *I was here, in this version, and I shaped this stone*. The structures that face impossible directions face directions that exist in other layers. The doorways that open onto nothing are opening onto rooms that stand in versions of the Flat adjacent to this one, separated by no distance at all, only by the thin membrane of reality that has worn to translucency here.

And the creatures—some of them may be the same creature, seen through multiple layers at once, repeating across the stacked versions of this place like an image reflected between facing mirrors. Some may have lived and died before your species evolved, still walking their ancient paths in a version of the Flat that never ended. Some may be from versions that have not yet occurred, working backward through the layers to the stable ground that underlies them all.

---

The air is cool. The light is even. The silence is complete.

A shape like a ridge-backed serpent the length of a river slides past thirty yards away, and where its body crosses a low stone wall, the wall flickers—becomes taller, shorter, made of a different stone, made of wood, becomes a wall again—and settles. The creature does not notice. The wall does not remember.

Somewhere on the Flat, in one of its layers, someone is building another structure. Their hands are shaping stone. Their technology is simple: leverage, patience, the understanding that a flat stone sits well upon another flat stone. They are building a shelter against nothing, a mark of presence in a place where presence has become uncertain. They will finish, or they will not finish, and the Flat will incorporate their effort into its endless, changeless archive.

The horizon shimmers with the arrival of more. They come. They always come. The Flat receives them, and it does not change, and they pass through it and through each other, and the quiet holds everything—the living and the layered and the nearly-real alike—in a stillness that has outlasted every reason for stillness to end.

You could walk for days and not reach the edge. You could walk for days and not notice you had moved. The Flat is patient. The Flat has been patient since before patience had a name. And in its patient, ancient, impossible fullness, it simply continues.
