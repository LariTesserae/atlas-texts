# The Ascent City of Newstrata

## The First Impression

You come up through the cloud layer and it is already there—waiting, as it has been waiting, as it will continue to wait. The plateau rises not like a mesa but like a thought that has not finished thinking itself. The rock is new. That is the first thing you must understand. The rock is *new*, and it does not yet know what it is.

---

## The Geology That Is Still Becoming

Beneath the city, the earth is still pushing up. You can feel it if you stand still long enough—not a tremor, not a shake, but a slow upward pressure, as though the ground beneath your feet is respiration on a timescale too long for a single breath. The plateau rises by centimeters each year. The surveyors mark it. They have marked it for three hundred years.

The stone itself has the character of something unfinished. It is grey-white and fibrous, shot through with veins of a mineral no one has named because it may not yet be a mineral—it may be on its way to becoming one, currently caught in an intermediate state between the molten and the crystalline. The quarryworkers call it *softstone* or *dreamore* or simply *the pre-,* and they cut it from the exposed faces at the plateau's edge where the wind has done the preliminary work of revealing what the upward pressure has pushed forth.

Walk to the rim and look down. The cliffs do not erode in the normal fashion. They *emerge*. What will become a talus slope is currently a series of protruding forms—future boulders that have not yet separated from the parent mass, their fracture lines just beginning to suggest themselves over decades. The plateau is a body forming itself in public.

---

## The City That Grew While the Rock Grew

Newstrata is built on and in and through this becoming-geology. The first settlers—they would not have called themselves that, they were surveyors and miners who stayed too long—built wooden platforms on the flat upper surface. Those platforms are gone now, absorbed into the lower layers of the city like sediment.

What exists now is a city of layers, each one laid down as the rock rose to meet it. The lowest accessible streets are at the rim-edge, where the original ground level has become a cliff-face, and the buildings there tilt slightly outward because they were built on ground that was not yet done moving. The newest construction is at the center of the plateau, where the upward push is strongest, where the rock is newest, where you can sometimes see it *arriving*—a fresh grey-white surface emerging from the soil like a forehead from water.

The buildings are stone and iron. The stone is local, cut from the pre-, and it behaves like stone in most respects but not all. It is slightly warm to the touch. It holds sound longer than it should. Walls built from it seem to listen. The iron is brought up from below the cloud layer on the cargo lifts that run along the rim, and it is worked in the foundries that cluster at the city's edges where the wind can carry the smoke away—or at least carry it into the clouds, where it becomes someone else's weather.

There are seven major lift-stations. They are the city's circulatory system. Ore goes down. Iron comes up. Food comes up. People go both directions, though more come up than go down, because Newstrata has a way of keeping people.

---

## The Moisture Regime

It is cool here. The kind of cool that is not cold but is always present, a temperature that sits against your skin like a thin fabric you cannot remove. The cloud layer is below you most days, but it reaches up. It reaches up with fingers of mist that find the streets in the early morning, that leave condensation on every surface, that bead on the warm stone and run in tiny rivers toward the drains.

It rains, but not often. When it does, it is a slow rain—a rain that seems to be considering whether it is worth the effort. The drops are large and far apart. They strike the stone with audible clicks, and the stone seems to accept them, to drink them, to incorporate them. After rain, the rock gleams as though it has been oiled.

The water that does not sink into the pre- collects in the channeling system the city engineers have carved over the centuries—a network of grooves and basins that directs runoff to the central reservoirs. The water tastes of minerals that have no names yet. It is safe to drink. It makes you dream more vividly, but that may be the altitude.

---

## The Vegetation That Should Not Thrive But Does

At this elevation, with this temperature, with this thin and intermittent water, nothing should grow as it does. But the vegetation of Newstrata is *lush* in a way that defies the obvious constraints, and the reason is this: the pre- feeds it.

The plants have learned—perhaps that is not the word, the plants have *happened to be*—suited to the chemical composition of the emerging stone. Their roots go down into rock that is still deciding its mineral identity, and they draw from it something that functions as nutrient, as fertilizer, as something more than either. The result is a plant life that is abundant and strange.

The predominant form is a tall, fibrous stalk that the residents call *pillargrass*, though it is not a grass. It grows in clusters three meters high, its stalks thick as a human wrist, topped with broad leaves that catch the mist and funnel it down to their roots. The leaves are dark green, almost black in the dim light, and they move even when there is no wind—they orient toward moisture, toward warmth, toward the pre- where it lies closest to the surface.

Between the pillargrass stands, there is a low ground-cover that forms mats of tiny rounded leaves, pale blue-green, that creep over the stone and seem to be slowly eating it. They are. Over centuries, they break down the rock into something like soil, though it is a soil that shifts beneath your feet, that has not settled into stability.

And there are the growths that appear overnight on the fresh stone faces—pale, shelf-like protrusions that might be fungus or might be lichen or might be something that has not previously needed a category. They appear where the pre- is newest, and they seem to be in conversation with it, or perhaps to be *of* it, an expression of the same process taking a different form.

---

## The Animals That Are Abundant

The fauna is where the reality breakdown becomes difficult to ignore.

There are creatures here that make sense—mountain goats with thick white coats that navigate the rim-cliffs with impossible confidence, foxes with large ears that hunt the smaller animals among the pillargrass stands, hawks that ride the thermals rising from the cloud layer below. These are normal. These are fine.

But there are others.

The *walkers* are the most visible. They are tall, thin, bipedal—though "bipedal" implies a left and a right, and their legs do not seem to be organized on that principle. They move through the outer streets at dawn and dusk, browsing the pillargrass with elongated faces that are all mouth, and they are not alarmed by humans, and humans are not alarmed by them, which is perhaps the strangest thing about them. They have always been here. The city's children grow up seeing them. The visitors from below find them upsetting.

Then there are the burrowers—low, broad, six-limbed things that excavate the pre- with a speed and precision that the quarryworkers resent. They tunnel. Their tunnels become the city's tunnels, eventually, once the engineers have verified the structural integrity and widened them for human use. The burrowers and the city have an unspoken arrangement. They go where the stone is newest. The city follows.

In the reservoirs, there are things that might be fish or might be something that has decided to be fish for now. They are large and slow and bioluminescent, and their light is sometimes the only illumination in the lower streets during the dim hours.

And in the highest, newest construction—at the plateau's center where the rock is still arriving—there are sounds that suggest animals that have not yet fully formed, movements that resolve into shapes only when you look at them from the corner of your eye.

---

## The Dimness and the Light

It is always dim in Newstrata.

The elevation places the city in a perpetual haze of ice crystals and moisture that diffuses what light arrives. The sun, when it can be seen at all, is a pale disc, a bright smear without definite edges. Shadows do not form sharply; they graduate, they suggest. The visual world is one of soft boundaries and implied shapes.

The city compensates with industrial light. The foundries burn bright. The lift-stations are illuminated by electric arcs that crackle and hum. The main streets are lined with gas lamps that the city has never upgraded because the gas is manufactured from the pre- itself—it produces a slow, steady flame of an unusual amber color that seems to hang in the air rather than rise from the mantles.

At night, the darkness is substantial. It is a darkness with texture, with weight. The bioluminescence of the reservoir-creatures pulses in the lower districts. The walkers' eyes catch and hold the lamp-light, reflecting it back as a slow, even glow. And the pre-, where it lies exposed, emits a faint luminescence of its own—a phosphorescence that suggests the stone is processing something, metabolizing something, working through something in its long journey toward becoming final.

---

## The Sound That Is Loud

The city is loud, but it is a particular kind of loud—not the sharp noise of collision and combustion but the sustained, layered sound of continuous process.

The foundries produce a low, rhythmic pounding that you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears. The lifts grind and wheeze as they haul their loads along the rim-cables. The wind, which is constant at this elevation, moves through the pillargrass stands with a sound like breathing, and through the buildings with a sound like muttering.

Underneath all of this is the sound of the pre- itself—a low, subsonic vibration that the newest residents notice as a pressure in their skulls and the oldest residents no longer notice at all. It is the sound of stone becoming stone. It is the sound of geology in progress. It is the sound of the plateau thinking, if a plateau can be said to think, and here it can, because here the rules are different.

The cumulative effect is a soundscape that is not unpleasant but is inescapable. There are no quiet places in Newstrata. Even in the deepest tunnels, the sound of the burrowers and the vibration of the pre- create a kind of acoustic presence that fills every space where silence might otherwise gather.

---

## The Slow Time

Things change slowly here. The plateau rises. The stone emerges. The buildings settle. The vegetation spreads. The animals evolve—visibly, over the span of a human life, the walkers have changed, their legs growing longer, their faces growing narrower, as though they are adapting to a landscape that is itself still adapting.

The city expands at the pace of the pre-'s arrival. New construction happens only when new ground has been given. The engineers mark the growth lines, and the builders follow, and the city's shape is not planned but *permitted* by the upward force that creates it.

Foundries that were built three hundred years ago still operate, their fires never extinguished, their hammers never stilled. Families have worked the same machines for generations. The slow time has made the city patient. There is no rush. The rock will provide when it provides. The lifts will run when they run. The next district will be built when the ground arrives to build it on.

Visitors from below find this maddening. They come on the lifts expecting a city that operates on the logic of commerce and urgency, and they find a city that operates on the logic of geology.

---

## The Reality Breakdown

This is the thing that must be spoken of last because it is the thing that underlies everything else.

The pre- is not fully real yet. It is in the process of becoming real. And this partial reality bleeds outward, affecting everything that comes into contact with it.

The stone buildings *listen*. It is not a metaphor. The walls absorb sound and hold it and sometimes release it hours or days later—a conversation fragment, a child's laugh, the strike of a hammer, emerging from the stone at odd moments as though the building is remembering aloud.

The vegetation is too lush because the boundary between plant and mineral has not been firmly established here. The roots of the pillargrass sometimes become stone. The shelves of fungus on the fresh rock faces sometimes unfurl leaves. The categories are suggestions, not rules.

The animals are abundant because the pre- generates them, or allows them, or dreams them. The walkers were not here before the city was. They emerged from the same process that produces the stone. They are the plateau's fauna in the same way that the plateau's stone is its stone—provisional, in-progress, subject to revision.

And in the newest places—at the very center, where the ground is newest, where the rock is still warm and soft and not yet fully committed to being rock—the rules are thinnest. Gravity is a suggestion. Distance is approximate. The transition between one place and another can happen without crossing the space between. Residents report turning a corner and finding themselves in a district on the opposite side of the city, or stepping through a doorway into a tunnel that should not exist, or looking out a window and seeing the plateau as it was three hundred years ago, before the first surveyor set foot on it.

These events are not considered remarkable. They are part of living in Newstrata. They are the consequence of building a city on ground that has not yet decided what it is.

---

## The People Who Stay

Why do they stay? The visitors always ask.

They stay because the pre- provides. They stay because the work is steady and the city is patient. They stay because the dreams you have when you drink the reservoir water are dreams of a landscape becoming itself, and those dreams are beautiful in a way that fully-formed landscapes cannot be.

They stay because in Newstrata, you are present for the making of a place. You are not living in the finished product. You are living in the process, and the process includes you, and the stone listens to you, and the animals tolerate you, and the vegetation grows around your buildings with an generosity that feels like welcome.

They stay because reality is thinner here, and in the thin places, you can sometimes feel what is on the other side, and what is on the other side is not frightening but *generative*—a reservoir of potential, of things that could be, of the vast unfinished project of existence, pressing upward from below, arriving centimeter by centimeter, taking shape as it rises into the cool dim air.

Newstrata is a city being built by the ground itself, and the people who live there are not its architects but its witnesses, and that is enough.
