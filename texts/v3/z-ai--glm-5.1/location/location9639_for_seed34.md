# The Lattice of Warā

The plateau called Warā begins where the lowlands surrender their ambition. You climb—hours of it, days of it—and the air thins and dampens in equal measure until you are walking through a cold soup that the local breath calls *the veil*. Below the veil, the world is green and loud. Above it, the world becomes Warā.

It is a city. It has always been a city. No one remembers building it, and no one living has ever known it to be unfinished.

---

The stone is tuff—volcanic, pale, shot through with vesicles like the bubbles in bread. It is soft when quarried and hardens in the air over decades, which is why the oldest districts have a wearing-away quality to their edges, a softness that is not decay but the settling of material into its final self. The buildings rise from the tuff and are made of the tuff. Some were carved in situ—rooms and corridors hollowed from living rock, leaving the negative space as streets. Others were cut and stacked, block on block, with such precision that the mortar joints disappear and the walls seem extruded, continuous, as if the stone wished itself upward.

The density is the first thing that strikes a newcomer. Warā does not believe in distance between structures. The buildings shoulder against each other, lean, merge. A second-story bridge connects one block to the next, and that bridge becomes a hall, and the hall becomes someone's front room, and the front room opens to a staircase descending to a courtyard that is also a reservoir, and the reservoir drains through a carved channel to the street below, where the water runs in a shallow groove between the feet of pedestrians and the hooves of the few pack animals that can tolerate the thin air. There is no gap. There is no breathing room. The city is a single organism of stone and water and the slow movement of bodies through its gut.

And yet—sparse. That is the paradox. Sparse in the way a skeleton is sparse once the flesh is gone. There is so much structure, so much built volume, and so little filling it. No forest canopy, no clutter of undergrowth. A few plants grow in the crevices where water pools—small things, hardy, with thick leaves the color of wet slate. Moss in the permanent-shade corridors where the sun never reaches, and in those corridors the moss is nearly luminous, a pale green that seems to generate its own faint light, though it does not. A type of reed grows in the stiller reservoirs, rising from the water in straight thin stalks, and the reed is harvested for weaving but never for food. No tree takes root on Warā. The elevation and the cold and the relentless wet see to that.

---

The water. You must understand the water.

Warā sits above a vast aquifer—no, that word implies something contained, something with boundaries. Warā sits *within* water. The tuff is porous. The plateau is riddled with it, saturated to a depth no one has ever measured, and the water moves. It moves slowly, imperceptibly, the way blood moves through tissue, and it finds every seam and fissure and channel carved into the stone over millennia of occupation and abandonment and re-occupation. The walls weep. This is not metaphor. You can stand in a corridor on any given day and watch the water bead on the tuff surface, gather, trace a path downward along some invisible gradient, and drip from the ceiling into a floor channel that carries it to the next corridor, the next reservoir, the next open-air court where it evaporates in the thin high-altitude light and returns to the air as the veil.

The atmosphere condenses. You feel it on your skin. You breathe it. The wet is never absent. Rain falls on Warā perhaps one day in three—gentle rain, rarely fierce, the sky going from pearl-grey to darker-grey and releasing its water in a steady patient descent that the stone accepts without resistance. But even on dry days, the wet is present. It rises from the ground. It clings. You learn to live slightly damp, and after a month, you no longer notice.

The sound the water makes is the quietest sound in Warā and therefore the loudest, because there is so little else. Dripping. A thousand drips at once, each one inaudible on its own, together forming a texture of sound like a canvas being very slowly splattered with tiny drops of paint. It never stops. You hear it in the deep corridors, in the reservoir halls, in the open streets where the water runs in its carved grooves. It becomes the baseline of all silence, and when you leave Warā, you miss it. You feel the silence of dry places as a kind of absence, a theft.

---

The scale of Warā defeats understanding. You can walk for six days in any direction and not reach its edge. Some say there is no edge—that the city simply thins, the buildings growing farther apart, the streets becoming animal trails, the water no longer channeled but pooling in natural depressions, until you are no longer in Warā but also cannot say when you left. Others claim to have seen the boundary: a last great wall, collapsed in places, beyond which the plateau continues as bare tuff, uncarved, unquarried, empty but for the wind and the wet and the low clouds dragging their bellies across the stone.

The population—there is a population, though small relative to the city's capacity—is concentrated in a handful of districts near the geographic center. These districts are the densest of the dense, six and seven stories of carved and stacked tuff rising on either side of streets so narrow that two people cannot walk abreast, and every room is full, and every roof is a terrace where someone is growing the slate-colored vegetables that constitute most of the local diet, and children move across the roofscape like a second system of paths overlaid on the first, leaping from terrace to terrace with a casualness that would terrify a newcomer.

The outer districts—most of Warā—are occupied only by water and moss and the rare animal. A fox-like creature with tawny fur and oversized paws for padding silently on wet stone has been seen in the deep corridors. The city is too cold, too wet, too sparse in its greenery to support much else. What lives here lives because it adapted to stone and damp and the long quiet.

---

The quiet. You asked about the quiet.

Warā is not silent. But it is *quiet* in the way that only vast old places can be. The density of structure absorbs sound. The wet stone does not echo. The narrow streets and the low ceilings and the labyrinthine corridors swallow footsteps and voices and return only the dripping, always the dripping, and the wind at the edges of the city where the buildings thin and the veil opens and you can see the lowlands below, far away, smudged with forest and cloud.

In the occupied districts, there is human sound: the low murmur of conversation, the clatter of cooking pots, the particular rhythm of stone-cutting tools working new tuff for repairs. But even these sounds are muted, private, contained. The stone keeps each sound close to its source. You do not hear your neighbor's argument two streets over. You hear only your own breathing, your own footsteps, and the water.

At night the temperature drops and the condensation accelerates and the dripping thickens, and you lie in your carved room on your stone shelf with your wool blankets pulled up, and the water runs down the walls in thin rivulets that join the floor channels, and the room is neither indoors nor outdoors but something between—a grotto, a cave that humans made, a wound in the tuff that the water is forever trying to heal, and you sleep to the sound of that attempted healing, and you dream of nothing, and in the morning you wake to the pearl-grey light and the wet on your face and the city still here, still vast, still patient, still offering its strange gift: the experience of being small inside something enormous that does not require you, that does not repel you, that simply contains you, as it contains the water, as it contains the moss-light in the deep corridors, as it contains the fox-padding and the swallows and the long slow weather of centuries.

Warā endures. That is what it does. That is all it does. And it is enough.
