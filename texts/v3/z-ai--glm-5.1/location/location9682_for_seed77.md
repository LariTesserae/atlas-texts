# The Still-Born Continent

The first thing you notice is the regularity.

Not the regularity of architecture—not walls or roads or the grids of city planners—but a deeper, more unsettling order. The ground beneath your feet is flat in the way that only made things are flat. Not worn flat by time and wind, not leveled by water standing patient over millennia, but *finished* flat. Delivered flat. As though someone—or something—poured a floor across the better part of a hemisphere and called it good.

It stretches in every direction. That is the second thing.

---

The surface is a composite—you can see this where cracks have opened, which is not many places. It's gray-beige, dense, faintly granular when you look close, like concrete that was mixed with sand from a river that never existed. There's no aggregate in the usual sense, no stones or shell fragments or the jumbled evidence of natural process. Just a uniform matrix, slightly warm to the touch, extending down who knows how far. You walked for the better part of a day before you found the first edge of a seam.

The seams are the only relief. They run in long, shallow grooves across the plain—not carved, but *left*. Gaps where the surface material simply wasn't placed, or pulled back slightly as it set, leaving channels barely deep enough to trap a standing thread of water. The water comes at irregular intervals. You haven't seen rain yet, but you've felt it—the air thickening, condensation beading on the ground in the cooler hours before dawn, pooling in those seams, drawing faint dark lines across the world's floor. By midday the heat lifts it all back into vapor. The seams go pale again. The cycle continues.

You think the seams might be drainage. Or expansion joints. Or both. You think this because the alternative—that they're decorative, or accidental, or meaningless—feels worse.

---

The heat is a presence. Not the aggressive, punishing heat of a desert at noon, but a steady, comprehensive warmth that rises from below as much as it presses down from above. The surface radiates. The air above it shimmers, but gently—a low haze rather than a mirage, softening the horizon into a band of pale smudge where the gray-beige floor meets the slightly different gray-white of the sky. There are no clouds. There might never have been clouds. The sky has the blank, even quality of a space that hasn't learned to hold weather yet, or has been cleaned of it entirely.

You sweat, but the air takes the moisture quickly. It doesn't feel like evaporation so much as subtraction.

---

There is vegetation. Not much—there are long stretches where the floor is bare and seamless and utterly sterile—but in places, usually near the seams, you find it. Low, matted growth, gray-green and faintly rubbery, spreading in irregular pads like lichen on a headstone. It doesn't root in any soil; there is no soil. It adheres directly to the surface, flattening itself against the composite, drawing something—moisture, minerals, some property of the material itself—through contact alone. When you lift a corner, it peels away with a faint suck, and the surface beneath is slightly discolored, as if the plant has been feeding.

You find taller growth in the wider seams, where more water collects. Sparse stalks, jointed and dry-looking, rising a foot or two before curling over at the tip. They rattle faintly when the air moves—which is rare, and slight. The sound is small and dry and lonely. There is nothing to carry it. No walls, no valleys, no forests to contain or reflect it. It falls out of the stalk and dies.

There are no animals. No insects. No birds. You've checked the undersides of the matted growth, peered into the seams, listened for movement in the hours around dusk and dawn. Nothing. The silence is not the silence of a place where life is hiding—it is the silence of a place where the category hasn't been introduced yet.

---

You keep walking because there is nothing else to do. The terrain does not change in any dramatic way, but it changes in increments. The seams grow slightly wider, slightly deeper, forming a loose grid that you could navigate by if you had a reason to. The matted vegetation appears more frequently, spreading in wider patches, darker where the moisture lingers. You begin to notice a faint odor—not rot, not growth exactly, but the smell of something organic happening slowly. Warmth and damp and patience.

You come to a low area—barely a depression, just a place where the surface dips a few feet over a distance of miles. Here, the seams converge into something almost like a channel, and in the bottom of the channel there is standing water. Not flowing. Not a stream. A long, shallow pool, barely an inch deep, extending ahead of you until it vanishes into haze. The water is warm and absolutely still. No current, no ripples, no life visible in it. It reflects the blank sky and makes the world feel deeper than it is.

You kneel and touch it. It's slick, faintly viscous—not quite water, or not only water. It leaves a residue on your fingers, mineral or biological, you can't tell. The surface tension is high. A drop clings to your fingertip like a small, clear bead before it falls.

This is where the vegetation is thickest—not just the matted gray-green but a new form, darker, softer, rising in low hummocks from the shallow water like moss from a rock. It's the first thing you've seen that looks like it's *thriving* rather than merely persisting. The hummocks are slightly warm when you press your palm against them. There's a very faint pulse of movement—not breathing, not growth you can see, but something slow and rhythmic, as if the vegetation is drawing moisture in time with a cycle you can't detect.

---

You stand in the shallow water and look out across the continent. Because that's what this is—a floor, vast beyond any reasonable walking distance, made of a material you can't identify, laid down in a pattern that suggests intention. It is not natural. You know this the way you know a wall is not natural—by the absence of the irregularities that natural processes produce. No boulders dropped by glaciers, no meanders carved by streams, no varves or strata or the slow, patient signature of deep time. This surface has no history. It has a beginning and a process and a present state, but it doesn't have a past. The erosion that should be here—the weathering, the cracking, the slow dismantling that all surfaces undergo—has barely started. The seams are the first concession. The water pooling in the channels is the second. The vegetation is the third, finding purchase in the gaps, beginning the long work of breaking the surface down into something that could be called ground.

This is a landscape under construction, or just finished, or in the earliest stage of becoming something other than what it was made to be. It's hard to tell which. The processes are running—heat, moisture, growth, the first tentative erosion of the seams—but they're new at this. They haven't had time to make the place their own. The floor is still the dominant fact. The processes are just beginning to write over it.

---

You walk on. The light doesn't change much—there's no sun that you can find, just a diffuse, even illumination that might come from the sky itself or might be a property of the atmosphere. It brightens toward what you think of as midday and dims toward what you think of as evening, but there are no shadows to speak of, no direction to the light. You measure time by your footsteps and the faint rhythm of condensation and evaporation in the seams.

The sound of your walking is the loudest thing in this place. Your footfalls on the composite surface are flat and dead, slightly muffled, as if the material absorbs more energy than it reflects. When you stop, the silence returns immediately—no echo, no reverberation, no sound of your passage lingering in the air. It's as if the place has no memory for sound, any more than it has a past.

You walk for three more days, or what you count as days. The vegetation thickens around the channels, and the channels deepen, and the shallow pools grow wider, and the hummocks rise higher, and the smell of slow organic process grows stronger. But the floor goes on. The flat, made, gray-beige floor goes on. You walk on it because there is nothing else to walk on, and because you have not yet reached whatever it was made for.
