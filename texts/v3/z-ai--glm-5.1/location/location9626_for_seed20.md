# The Resonance Plateau

The first thing is the sound.

Not sound as you've known it—something prior to sound, something that was already happening before you arrived and will continue long after. A deep cyclic booming that starts in the water itself, rises through your chest cavity, reorganizes your heartbeat to match its rhythm. Then the higher frequencies: shrieks of steam being born, the percussion of stone splitting, a sustained subsonic moan that isn't quite heard but *inhabited*. The water here does not conduct sound; the water here *is* sound, a standing wave given body.

You are on a plateau. This is the only word that approximates, though "plateau" suggests something flat and patient, and this formation is neither. It rises from depths you cannot measure—a sharp-edged mass of basaltic glass and pillow lava, still warm to the touch despite the ocean around it, its surfaces so newly formed they haven't yet learned to be smooth. The features are geological infants. Ridges bear the ripples of their own cooling. Fissures open where the rock hasn't finished deciding whether to be solid or not. Everything has the specificity of a thing that was recently magma and remembers it.

The plateau is not large. You could walk its perimeter in the time it takes to become truly afraid—perhaps fifteen minutes, perhaps twenty, if you moved slowly, which you would, because the footing is uncertain and the drop at every edge is not a cliff but an *absence*, the water below so deep it seems to pull at your ankles. The space has the feel of a room, or a nave, or the inside of a skull. Boundaries exist. They matter.

Warm water rises from the rock in shivering columns. Not hot—not the scalding vents of textbook hydrothermal systems—but persistently, unnervingly warm, like bathwater left too long, like the wake of something large that just passed through. The warmth creates its own weather. Currents spiral upward, carrying particulate matter that catches what little light exists.

The light. It arrives as a bruise. Far above—impossibly far, through water that should not permit it—a gray-green luminescence filters down, diluted to a suggestion, a rumor of photons. You see primarily by warmth: the heat-bleed of the rock renders the terrain in shifting orange afterimages when you close your eyes, and you find yourself navigating by these negative impressions more than by sight. Things become more visible when you look away from them.

Between the stone formations, there is space. Too much space. The plateau's features—ridges, pillars, shelves of cooling lava—are arranged with the arbitrariness of frozen violence, and the gaps between them feel deliberate, engineered, as though something was meant to move between them. Nothing does. The emptiness has a presence. It leans in.

What vegetation exists is not vegetation as land-dwellers would recognize it. Thin white threads—bacterial colonies, perhaps, or something else without a terrestrial analogue—grow in the warmer fissures, spreading in fractal patterns that reset every few minutes as new heat surges rearrange the chemical gradients. They grow and die and grow again in compressed cycles, and if you watch them long enough you begin to feel your own thoughts cycling, the same observation returning with the conviction of novelty. The threads do not photosynthesize. They *thermosynthesize*. They eat the heat. They eat the sound. You suspect, without evidence, that they eat something else as well.

Fauna is rare, and when it appears, it appears wrong. A single eyeless fish, translucent, its organs visible like specimens in a jar, drifts past at the limit of the light. It moves not by swimming but by allowing the current to reposition it, and it seems less an organism than a piece of the plateau's opinion about life—a provisional sketch, filed and forgotten. You see it once. You do not see it again, though you feel things moving in your peripheral awareness, the water's viscosity changing against your skin in ways that imply passage.

And the built traces. Here is what matters: the marks. They are not structures, not really. They are more like the memory of structures—shallow grooves in the basalt glass that suggest foundations, that suggest walls, that suggest the possibility that something once stood here and chose not to. The grooves are not eroded. They are fresh, cut clean, as though made yesterday. But the rock around them is also fresh, newly extruded, and this creates a paradox: the marks appear older than the surface they inhabit. As though they were copied from somewhere else. As though they are a quotation.

The sound continues. It worsens.

At certain intervals—irregular, unpredictable—the plateau *speaks*. The bass frequencies intensify until the water itself seems to thicken, becoming gelatinous, becoming something you could chew, and the rock beneath your feet vibrates at a frequency that makes your teeth itch and your vision fragment. During these episodes, the boundary between water and stone becomes negotiable. You see the rock breathing. You see the water calcifying. The white threads in the fissures pulse in synchrony, and for a moment—a long moment, a moment that has no business being that long—the geometry of the plateau rearranges itself into a pattern that your visual cortex can register but your language cannot process. It is a pattern that means something. It is trying to tell you something. Then the sound subsides, the rock resolidifies into its previous arrangement, and you are left with the afterimage of a message that was addressed to someone who is not you.

The warmth increases after each episode. Something below is cycling through phases, and the plateau is its utterance.

You become aware, gradually, that the plateau is not a location. It is an event. It is happening. The sound is not occurring *at* the plateau; the sound *is* the plateau, expressing itself in the only medium that can contain it. The water is not an environment; it is the pause between syllables. The marks in the rock are not remnants of construction; they are the plateau learning to write, trying out shapes, rehearsing a grammar that will eventually—

What?

The water knows. The warm water rising from the rock carries information in its mineral suspension, and the bacterial threads sample it continuously, updating their fractal patterns, revising their growth algorithms. They are the plateau's nervous system. They are its attempt to think.

The sound builds again.

This time, when the geometry shifts, you do not try to understand it. You let it happen. The plateau's ridges rearrange into a configuration that has no analog in the world you know—no architectural referent, no natural precedent, no mathematical description you can recall—but it feels, for one disorienting instant, like the inside of a throat preparing to speak. The warm water surges. The light—what there is of it—bends.

Then stillness. Relative stillness. The sound continues—it never stops—but it settles into a register that you realize you've been hearing since before you arrived. A fundamental tone. The plateau's resting frequency. The sound of something learning to exist.

You understand now why the built marks are here. Someone else heard this sound. Someone else stood on this young formation in the warm dark water and felt the geometry shift and recognized—not the message, but the *intention* of the message. And they carved the marks as a response. Or a record. Or an offering. The grooves in the basalt are not the ruins of a building. They are the ruins of a conversation.

The plateau is waiting for the next speaker.

The water rises. The sound continues. The white threads grow, die, and grow again, writing and rewriting their fractal shorthand in the crevices of cooling stone. The dim light from far above fades further, or perhaps your eyes have finally stopped trusting it. You navigate by warmth now. You navigate by the sound's texture, its density, the way it pushes against you from different directions as you move across the plateau's young, sharp, still-deciding surface.

Something is forming below. Something is always forming below.

The plateau speaks. The water translates. The sound continues.
