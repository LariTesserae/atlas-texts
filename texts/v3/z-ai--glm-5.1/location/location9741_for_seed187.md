# The Green Engine

The floor is not a floor. It is root.

A single continuous mat of root-stock, pale as old bone, spreading across the entire basin in a flattened weave so dense and so old that it has become the ground itself. You walk on wood without knowing it. Your feet press into the give of it—slight, spongy, cool—and wherever the root-surface dips into its shallow valleys, dew gathers and stays. Never pooling. Never flowing. Just *there*, a film of damp that the air holds against the ground like a palm against a cheek.

The basin is small. You could cross it in four minutes at a slow walk. A cathedral-sized bowl, walled by nothing but the vertical rise of the same root-mass curving upward at the edges, climbing into low berms that flatten out into the greater flatness beyond. Inside the bowl: almost nothing. Space. Air. The pale root-floor stretching in every direction, featureless, unmarked.

---

And then the columns.

They stand at distances from each other that feel deliberate—seventy meters, maybe a hundred between each one—and they are the only vertical things in this place. They are not trees. They are not built. They are both.

Each column is a stalk. A trunk. A cylinder of living tissue the color of oxidized copper, rising straight up maybe eighteen meters before splaying into a flat, horizontal cap like a parasol skeleton. The caps overlap slightly, filtering the ordinary daylight into a cool greenish cast. The stalks are ribbed, vertically, with fluting that looks like the cooling fins on an old engine block. And they hum.

That is the sound.

A deep, sustained, mechanical hum coming from inside every stalk. Not the wind-through-reeds whistle of hollow bamboo, but a vibration with harmonics, with overtones, with the faint rhythmic pulse of something turning over slowly inside the core of each column. A rotor. A turbine. Something that has been spinning for longer than the root-floor has been flat.

The hum is loud. Not painful, but inescapable—a low bellowing drone in the 80-hertz range that you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears. When you stand between two columns the sound phases, beats, interferes, and you can feel your ribcage shudder with the difference tone. The whole basin throbs with it. The dew on the root-floor ripples in concentric rings around each column base. The air itself seems to thicken and thin in slow waves.

---

The columns are alive. This is unambiguous. You can see the pulse—if you watch a single spot on the copper-colored bark for thirty seconds, you will notice a subtle swelling and subsiding, a systole, traveling upward from base to cap. The tissue is warm to the touch. Not hot, but warmer than the cool air, warmer than the root-floor. A low metabolic heat.

And the tissue is producing. From the upper sections of each stalk, just below the cap, a fine mist of moisture exhales outward in a steady stream. Condensation breath. It hits the cool air and precipitates into droplets that slide down the ribbing and drip from the fluted edges onto the root-floor below. This is where the damp comes from. Not rain. Not a water table. The columns breathe out wetness. Each one a fountain of humidity that the root-floor drinks and returns and the column drinks again.

There are no mechanisms visible inside the columns. No gears, no shafts, no housings. If you cut one open—and nothing in this place suggests you could; the bark is dense as hardwood, dense as epoxy—you would presumably find living tissue all the way through. Cell upon cell. But something in those cells is doing work. Electromechanical work. The hum proves it. The heat proves it. The moisture proves it.

This is an ecosystem that has *industry*. Not industry it built. Industry it *is*.

---

The fauna are small.

They have to be. There is almost nothing here—no shelter, no understory, no brush or thicket, just the open root-floor and the columns standing in their wide spacing under the cap-canopy. But in the cool damp shadow of each column base, where the drips collect and the hum is loudest, there are creatures.

 Beetles the size of your thumbnail, shellbacks in dark iridescent green, climbing the fluting in spirals, feeding on something in the bark—a sap, a secretion, a waste product of whatever internal process makes the hum. They move slowly, methodically, and they are many: hundreds per column, thousands in the basin, their tiny claw-clicks entirely swallowed by the bass drone.

And the moths. Pale, near-white, with bodies like slender fingers. They roost on the undersides of the caps during the still, cool day, and when the light shifts toward evening they descend in clouds and drink from the dew. They do not flutter. They move with a strange mechanical precision, as if their flight paths were plotted on a loom, and when they settle they align themselves along the fluting in rows so neat they look like they've been placed by a machine that knows where each one belongs.

Perhaps they have been.

---

There is no decay here. Nothing rots. The root-floor does not shed a layer of humus or duff; it is the same pale, compressed mat from edge to edge, with no loose material on its surface. When a moth dies—and they do die, you find them dried and light as paper on the root-floor—they simply desiccate. A column's moisture breath pulls the water from their tiny bodies over the course of a day, leaving chitin and wing-membrane flat and empty. The beetles carry these husks away. Where, you cannot see. Into the root-floor, perhaps. Into the column bases. Into the system.

Because this is the thing: the columns are not *in* the root-floor. They *are* the root-floor, risen upward into verticality at intervals, breathing and humming and drawing and returning. The whole basin is one organism. Or one machine. Or one organism that is also a machine, which has been running for so long—in geological time, in erosion time, in the time it takes to flatten a landscape to its final form—that there is no longer any distinction between its living and its mechanical processes.

The erosion parameter is maximal. This place has reached its end-state. The basin will not deepen. The columns will not fall. The hum will not cease. The beetles will climb, the moths will descend, the dew will gather, the root-floor will drink, and the cool damp air will sit in the bowl under the cap-canopy in its permanent greenish light, and nothing will change, and nothing will change, and nothing will change.

---

You could sit here. The hum would enter you. You would feel your pulse synchronize with the column-pulse, your breathing match the moisture exhale, and slowly—over hours, over days—you would become another small process inside the basin's metabolism. Your warmth joining the heat. Your stillness joining the root-floor. Your damp joining the dew.

This is not a threat. It is not even a danger. It is simply what happens in a place that has finished becoming itself. Everything that enters is incorporated. Everything that stays is *settled*.

The sound never stops.
