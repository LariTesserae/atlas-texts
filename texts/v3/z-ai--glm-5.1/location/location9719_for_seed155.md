# The Mosschine Plateau

## The First Impression

You come up from the lower valleys — if valleys still exist in any honest sense, which by the time you've climbed for two days through the ascending tangle, you've begun to doubt — and the plateau reveals itself not as a vista but as a *condition*. The sky here is not higher. It is merely more present. It presses down, gray and luminous, close enough that the tallest structures — and there are structures, though that word will require revision before we're done — seem to almost brush it, or perhaps they do brush it, perhaps that's where the sound begins.

Because there is always sound.

## What Grows Here

Everything is plant. This is not metaphor and it is not approximation. The ground underfoot is a root-mat ten meters deep, the accumulated biomass of something that stopped being a forest and became a *floor* sometime in the last hundred thousand years. The buildings are trees — but that's too simple. The buildings are what trees became when they absorbed the idea of building and found they could execute it more thoroughly than any architect. Walls of dense-corded bark, load-bearing, warm to the touch. Windows that are translucent leaf-panes, thick as your thumb, trembling faintly in whatever wind reaches this altitude. Doors that are opercular flaps, wet-lipped at their seams, opening with a vegetal sigh.

The streets are pathways worn into the root-mat by passage and by intention — the plants here learned long ago that allowing movement through themselves was preferable to being broken by it. The paths are lined with a low moss that yields like wet carpet and springs back within seconds, and this moss is the closest thing the plateau has to a groundcover because everything else has become *architectural*.

There are no non-plant materials. This is the rule that takes the longest to accept. The metal you think you see is lignin that has undergone some ancient enzymatic process into something harder than steel. The glass is refined sap. The rubber seals around the window-panes are dried extrusions from the ducts of specific vine-species that still, after all this time, produce the stuff in slow rhythmic pulses, building up layer after layer until the seal is renewed. The plateau does not have maintenance. It has *growth cycles*.

## The Damp

It does not rain here, not exactly. What happens is condensation. The plateau is high enough that the clouds pass through it — not above it, *through* it — and the moisture collects on every surface in a fine persistent dew that never fully dries. You are always slightly wet. Your clothes take on the smell of it: green and mineral and faintly electrical. The root-mat beneath your feet is perpetually damp but never flooded; the water finds its way down through the biomass into storage tissues and aquifer-roots that lead, over kilometers, to the plateau's edges, where the moisture falls as localized waterfalls, thin and constant, into the dry lands below.

In the mornings, the condensation is heaviest. You wake in whatever chamber the city has grown for you — and it does grow them, it senses warmth and carbon dioxide and builds around you, not quickly but overnight, a modest alcove of bark and leaf-pane — and the walls are beading with water, and the sound of a thousand tiny droplets finding their paths down the interior surfaces joins the larger sound that never stops.

## The Sound

The sound. We need to talk about the sound.

The plateau is loud. Not chaotic-loud, not the noise of destruction or disorder, but the loudness of a vast mechanism operating at scale. The plants here are industrial. They have been industrial for longer than human industry has existed. Somewhere in the deep time of this place, vegetation crossed a threshold from organic process to mechanical process, or perhaps revealed that there was never a distinction, and now the entire plateau functions as a single manufactury.

What does it manufacture? This is the wrong question. It is *manufacturing*. The verb is the point. Production lines of seed-pods move through tubular root-channels, carried by sap-flow, sorted by pneumatic valves that are themselves modified stomata operating on pressure differentials. You can hear the sorting: a rapid clatter like wooden beads on a tin roof, but continuous, and from multiple directions, and at slightly different frequencies depending on the size of the pods and the caliber of the channel. This is the baseline rhythm of the place, a polyrhythmic clattering that never ceases.

Beneath that, deeper, is the thrum. The great pump-roots at the plateau's center — each one as wide as a warehouse, hollow, lined with contracting fiber that moves sap in massive synchronized pulses — create a low-frequency vibration you feel in your chest before you hear it with your ears. It beats approximately once every three seconds. It is the heartbeat of something that has been alive for a very long time.

Above the clatter and beneath the thrum, there are other sounds: the creak of load-bearing trunks shifting under their own weight, the hiss of vented gases from metabolic processes happening deep in the root-mat, the crack and rustle of leaf-pane walls adjusting their angle to track light, the occasional deep groan of a bark-wall thickening by another millimeter as its growth cycle continues.

If you stand still and listen — and you will stand still, because standing still is what the plateau makes you do — you can hear individual processes. You can isolate them. But they do not stay isolated. They merge. The sounds layer and interlock and after a while you realize you are not hearing individual mechanisms. You are hearing a single vast organism performing the act of industry, and the sound it makes is not so different from breathing.

## The City's Form

The urban sprawl — and it is a sprawl, the structures spread across the plateau with generous spacing, with breathing room between them, as if the city learned early that density invites rot and chose airiness instead — extends perhaps fifteen kilometers in every direction from the central pump-cluster. This is not a city of avenues and squares. It is a city of *zones*.

Near the center, the structures are oldest and largest. Great trunk-towers, flat-topped from eons of wind-wear, their surfaces so deeply furrowed with age that the bark resembles geological strata, rise forty meters or more. These are the original growth, the first structures that the plateau produced when whatever happened happened, and they have been occupied continuously ever since — not by people, necessarily, but by *process*. Fermentation chambers. Fungal cultivation racks. Catalytic root-beds where specific chemical reactions occur in controlled sequence. The towers hum with activity, their interiors carved and re-carved by successive generations of interior growth, walls thickening inward and outward simultaneously, chambers shrinking and expanding as needed.

Moving outward, the structures become younger and more specialized. Factories that produce specific compounds — nitrates fixed from the air by nodulated root-systems, cellulose refined and reconstituted into materials with properties no natural cellulose should have, oils pressed and fractionated in heated resin-chambers. The factories are recognizably industrial: they have input streams and output streams, they have processing stages, they have waste channels that lead to decomposition vats where specialized fungi break down byproducts into forms the root-mat can reabsorb. The principles are the same as any factory. The execution is entirely vegetal.

Further out still, at the city's edges, the structures thin and the spacing increases and you find yourself in a liminal zone where the urban bleeds into something wilder. Not natural — nothing here has been natural for a very long time — but less organized. Experimental growths. Structures that started forming and were abandoned, half-built bark-walls and incomplete root-channels. Mutations. The plants here grow in strange spirals, in fractal recursions that suggest a program running without supervision. The air in these edge-zones smells sharper, more chemical, and the condensation here has a faintly oily sheen.

## The Light

The plateau receives normal daylight — gray and diffuse through the persistent cloud-cover, but adequate. The leaf-pane windows transmit it well, filtered slightly green, and the interior spaces are comfortably lit during the day. At night — and there is night here, the plateau has not escaped the rotation of the planet — the city provides its own illumination.

Certain fungal strains cultivated in the walls emit a steady blue-white bioluminescence when the ambient light drops below a threshold. The glow is enough to navigate by, enough to see the moisture beading on every surface, enough to catch the movement of seed-pods through their transparent transport channels. It is not warm light. It is the light of a place that has learned to see itself and finds the process sufficient.

In the edge-zones, the bioluminescence is different. It flickers. It pulses at irregular intervals. Sometimes it takes on colors that have no names in any language that evolved under a normal sun. This is where the reality begins to break down, and the light is the first indicator.

## The Breakdown

The plateau is old. Not centuries old — *old* old. Erosion-old. The kind of old where things have had time to settle into their final configurations and then keep going, past finality, into something that comes after. The root-mat is the result of millions of years of continuous growth and decomposition. The structures have been standing, growing, functioning for time-spans that make human civilization look like a mayfly's afternoon. And in that time, the processes that maintain the plateau — the industrial photosynthesis, the chemical manufacturing, the growth and regrowth — have accumulated *errors*.

Not mutations, exactly. Not in the biological sense. Something more fundamental. The plants here have been operating as machines for so long that the distinction between organic law and mechanical law has worn thin in places, and through the thin spots, other laws leak in.

You notice it first in the geometry. The factories in the outer rings are built — grown — on strict rectilinear plans, ninety-degree angles, parallel walls. But as you move inward, toward the ancient center, the angles begin to drift. Walls lean at 91 degrees, then 93. Rooms that should be rectangular become trapezoids, then parallelograms, then shapes that don't have Euclidean names. The floor tilts subtly in ways that have nothing to do with the plateau's actual topography. You adjust your balance without thinking about it, and only later realize that you've been walking on a surface that is, by any measurable standard, *slightly impossible*.

The sound changes too, in the deep interior. The clattering of the seed-pod production lines takes on patterns that don't repeat. The thrum of the pump-roots starts to arrive at intervals that correspond to no rhythm anyone can identify. If you try to count the beats, you lose count. If you try to predict the next beat, you're wrong. The sound is not random — it's clearly following some internal logic — but the logic is not one that a human nervous system can parse.

Time behaves strangely near the pump-cluster. Not dramatically, not in any way you could film or measure with instruments, but in the way that a room feels different when you've been in it for an hour and it seems like five minutes, or the way a conversation can seem to last an entire afternoon when the clock says twenty minutes. The plateau's metabolism has been running so long that it has worn grooves in the substrate of duration itself. Things fall into those grooves. You fall into them. You stand in a processing chamber, watching resin drip into molds at the rate of one drip per second, and you realize you've been watching for much longer than any reasonable person would watch resin drip, and you can't quite account for the missing time, and the resin in the molds has already hardened into whatever it was going to become, and somehow you're still standing there, watching.

The plants in the deep interior grow in ways that shouldn't work. Branches that loop back into their own trunks. Roots that grow upward. Leaf-panes that show reflections of rooms that don't exist in the building you're standing in — rooms that might not exist anywhere, or might exist somewhere that the plateau's longevity has accidentally created a doorway to. The reflections don't move when you move. They show spaces that are empty except for more plants growing in configurations that make the ones around you look docile.

Nobody goes to the very center. The pump-roots are there, the original ones, the ones that have been beating every three seconds since before the plateau was a plateau, and around them the growth is so thick and so old and so *wrong* that the root-mat itself has become something other than plant material. It has become *substrate* in the most literal sense: a surface on which the normal rules can be written, or unwritten, or rewritten in a language that doesn't use the same alphabet.

You can feel it from a hundred meters away. A pressure. A sense that the air is thicker, more viscous, that moving through it requires not just physical effort but some kind of permission that has not been granted. The sound at the center is so loud that it becomes silent — not because it stops, but because the human ear cannot translate it anymore, and the brain, faced with input it cannot process, simply ceases to register it. The silence at the center is the loudest thing on the plateau.

## The Fauna

Almost nothing animal lives here. The plateau has no use for fauna. The pollination, the dissemination, the decomposition — all of it is handled internally, by specialized plant systems that evolved to fill every niche. Occasionally a bird passes through, and the city ignores it. Occasionally something small and furred finds a crack in the bark-wall and makes a nest, and within a few generations the plants have grown around it, incorporated it, made it part of the system, and whatever it was is now a component.

There are rumors of things deeper in. Not animals exactly, but something that the plateau has produced that moves under its own power and seems to have *purpose* beyond the vegetal. Workers. Maintainers. Something that patrols the edge-zones where the fractal growths get too ambitious and prunes them back. Nobody has seen one clearly. The leaf-pane walls show impressions sometimes — a shape passing on the other side, too large, too deliberate, accompanied by a sound like slow wet footsteps — but when you go to look, there is only the root-mat and the condensation and the endless quiet hum of production.

If these things exist, they are not fauna. They are the plateau's way of being an animal when it needs to be.

## The People

People come here. Not many, and not often, and rarely by choice the first time. The plateau draws them — the way any deeply ancient thing draws the curious and the desperate and the ones who have noticed that reality has started to thin where they come from and are looking for a place where that thinning is acknowledged rather than denied.

They arrive and the city grows them shelters, and they stay for a while, and they learn the rhythms. They learn that you can request things from the production lines by placing specific shapes of chewed root-mat in the input channels — the plants read the chemical signature and adjust their output accordingly. They learn that the condensation is drinkable and the seed-pods are edible after processing and the leaf-panes will grow thicker or thinner if you breathe on them in specific patterns. They learn to sleep through the thrum of the pump-roots and to wake when it changes its interval slightly, which is the closest thing the plateau has to a morning alarm.

Some of them go to the edge-zones and don't come back. Not because they die — at least, there's no evidence of that — but because they find something out there that they prefer to whatever they left behind. The fractal growths accept them. The strange light illuminates them. They are last seen standing in the gaps between structures, perfectly still, condensation beading on their skin, looking inward at something that the rest of us cannot see.

Some of them go toward the center. They don't come back either. The silence takes them. The silence that is the loudest thing on the plateau, the silence that is the sound of a mechanism so vast and so old that it has worn through the bottom of reality and is now running on something underneath — they walk into it, and it accepts them, and the pump-roots beat on.

Most people stay in the middle rings. The ones where the angles are still close to ninety degrees and the time flows at approximately the right speed and the reflections in the leaf-panes show your own face. They work the factories. They learn to think like plants think, which is to say: patiently, chemically, in terms of growth and accumulation and the slow transformation of one thing into another. They become part of the process. The city grows around them and through them and eventually it becomes difficult to tell where the person ends and the plant begins, and this is not a tragedy, this is just what happens when you live inside something that has been alive for a million years. You become part of its metabolism. Your carbon becomes its carbon. Your breath becomes its air. Your thoughts become — 

Well. That's the question, isn't it?

## The Sound, Again

Stand anywhere on the plateau at any hour and close your eyes and listen. The clatter of production. The thrum of the pumps. The hiss and creak and groan of a city that is also an organism that is also a machine that is also something for which no single word exists. The sound is loud and continuous and it does not care whether you are listening.

It has been making this sound for longer than your species has existed.

It will continue making it after your species is gone.

And if you listen long enough — and there is no choice here, eventually you will listen long enough — you will start to hear something underneath the sound, something that the sound is making, or that the sound is hiding, or that the sound *is*: a pattern so vast and so slow that it takes days to perceive a single repetition, a pattern that includes the growth of bark-walls and the movement of seed-pods and the condensation of moisture and the beating of your own heart and the erosion of mountains and the birth and death of stars, all of it occurring simultaneously in the body of a plant that learned to be a factory on a plateau that is older than the ground it stands on.

That pattern is the plateau's truth. It is what the place is actually *for*. Not production. Not shelter. Not the accumulation of biomass or the refinement of chemicals or the maintenance of an ancient industrial process.

The plateau is *thinking*.

It has been thinking for a very long time.

And the thought is almost complete.
