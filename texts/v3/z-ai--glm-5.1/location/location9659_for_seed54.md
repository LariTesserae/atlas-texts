# The Condensate

The city is still becoming.

You feel it before you understand it—a low, continuous vibration in the stone beneath your feet, less a sound than a state of matter. The plateau groans. Not in the way old things groan, but in the way embryonic things shift, restless, not yet committed to their final shape. The geology here is immature. Basalt flows cool in the dark, their surfaces still tacky, still surrendering heat they haven't decided whether to keep. Faults haven't finished deciding where they'll break. The mountains behind the settlement—are they finished? You can't tell. The ridgeline looks provisional, like a sketch that might be revised.

And the water. The water is everywhere.

It doesn't rain, exactly. The atmosphere condenses. The cold is so deep and the air so saturated that moisture simply... gives up. It forms on every surface—on the flanks of half-formed boulders, on the walls of structures, on the cables that web between buildings like catenary veins. You touch a wall and your hand comes away dripping. The stone weeps. The metal weeps. Even the dark seems to weep, though that's just the condensation forming on your eyelashes, your skin, the fine film of it that coats everything until the whole city shines in the faint ambient glow of its own infrastructure—because there is no sunlight here, not really. Not at this elevation, not in this latitude's long seasonal dark. The sky is a low ceiling of black-gray cloud, and below it, the city breathes.

---

The buildings are dense. They press against each other, shoulder to shoulder, layer stacked on layer in a topography that mimics the raw young mountains behind them. Some are old—really old, stone and mortar old, built when this place was an outpost and the plateau was still mostly ice. But those are the seeds. The newer construction grows from them, around them, through them, using the old foundations as scaffolding for something far stranger.

The technology here doesn't build in the way you'd expect. It deposits. Material accumulates in planned patterns, nanoscale assembly drawing ambient minerals from the condensate itself—each droplet a carrier, each surface a template. You can see it happening if you look closely at the newer walls: a faint crystalline fuzz, like frost growing, but organized. Structural lattices extending themselves centimeter by centimeter, week by week. The city is printing itself, slowly, from the water and the stone and the cold.

The pace is geological. The dynamic is glacial. Things change, but you have to watch for a season to notice.

---

And in the dark, the sound.

It's the first thing newcomers cannot bear and the last thing locals notice: the near-constant thrum of the condensate cycles. Every building harvests water. Every structure channels it through filtration, through processing, through the slow deposition systems that grow the next layer of the city. The water moves through pipes and channels and capillary networks too fine to see, and it all makes sound. A hiss. A gurgle. A deep bass pulse where the main channels run beneath the streets, regular as breathing. The buildings hum with it. The streets sing with it, a wet, complex chord that never resolves because it never stops.

Locals sleep to it. They wake to it. When the filtration cycles shift—twice daily, a change so gradual you feel it in your chest before you hear it—the whole tonal landscape tilts, and something in the middle register drops or rises, and the city's song becomes briefly, subtly different before settling again.

---

The fauna are why the walls are thick.

You don't see them at first. The dark is too complete, and they don't want to be seen. But you hear them. A clicking in the condensation channels—something with too many legs, moving through the water infrastructure like it owns the place. A low chittering from the gaps between buildings, where the deposition scaffolding hasn't closed yet and the raw stone shows through. A sound like fabric tearing, but rhythmic, from somewhere above, where the cable-veins cross the narrow streets.

They came with the water. Or they were always here, beneath the ice, waiting for the warmth of the city's systems to wake them. The biologists—who work in sealed labs three stories underground, and who never go above without sealed suits—call them *pre-adapted*. They mean the fauna evolved for conditions that shouldn't exist: cold, wet, dark, mineral-rich, and humming with frequencies that shouldn't be associated with any known ecosystem. But here those conditions *do* exist, and so the fauna thrive.

They are not large, most of them. But they are numerous beyond counting. The condensation gutters run thick with pale, segmented bodies, each no longer than a fingernail, moving in coordinated waves that almost look like fluid dynamics—like the water itself has learned to crawl. In the deeper channels, where the main pipes carry the harvest, larger shapes move. You catch them in the flash of maintenance lights: something like a flat, eyeless ray, undulating through the flow, filter-feeding on the smaller creatures, its edges translucent and flickering with bioluminescence that dies the instant the light hits it.

Above, in the cable-veins, the chittering things nest. They have—according to the sealed-lab biologists—a metabolism based on piezoelectric effect, harvesting energy from the vibration of the cables themselves. The city's hum is their food source. They eat sound. Or rather, they eat the mechanical energy that produces sound, and the chittering is their waste product: a byproduct of a process that converts vibration into chemical energy. The louder the city, the more they multiply. The more they multiply, the more they dampen certain frequencies. They are, the biologists say with uncomfortable professional admiration, a self-regulating system. The city grows louder; the fauna multiply; the fauna absorb the excess; the city quiets. Equilibrium.

But equilibrium takes time. This place is patient.

---

There are no plants. The concept of a plant doesn't quite apply here. The deposition systems grow the city the way a coral grows a reef—mineral accretion, patient architecture—and the fauna occupy it the way polyps occupy coral, or the way mites occupy skin. It's not an ecosystem that photosynthesis built. It's an ecosystem that engineering built, that vibration built, that the slow mineral logic of condensation built.

The locals—there are perhaps forty thousand of them, in a settlement that sprawls across three shallow valleys of the young plateau—have adapted too. They keep their homes sealed and pressurized. They filter their water twice. They play counter-frequencies in their sleeping chambers to cancel out the chittering, the clicking, the hum. They maintain the deposition systems because the deposition systems maintain the walls, and the walls are all that stand between them and the sheer biomass pressing in from every gap and gutter.

They do not hate the fauna. That would be like hating the rain. The fauna are a feature of the environment, as constant as the condensation, as structural as the cold. You build around them. You build with them, if you're clever. Some of the older families have systems that channel the crawling waves through specific gutters, using their movement to carry particulate out of the filtration network. The ray-things in the deep pipes keep the smaller organisms from clogging the main flow. The chitterers nest most densely near the noisiest infrastructure, damping the frequencies that would otherwise vibrate the deposition systems into structural failure.

It's symbiosis, if you're generous. It's mutual tolerance, if you're honest.

---

Stand at the edge of the upper district, where the buildings thin and the deposition scaffolding gives way to raw, half-formed stone, and look out. The cloud base is low—you're above it, or within it, or below it; the distinctions blur at this elevation. The dark is near-total. But the city glows. Condensate catches the faint operational light of a thousand harvest-systems, a thousand deposition nodes, a thousand sealed windows, and scatters it into a diffuse luminescence that hangs in the wet air like something solid. The buildings rise in their dense clusters, their surfaces slick and shining, their walls slowly, invisibly growing thicker. The mountains behind are invisible, but you feel them—you feel the raw geology pressing at the edge of the settlement, patient, unfinished, waiting to see what the city will become.

And beneath your feet, in the pipes, in the channels, in every wet crevice of this becoming-place, the fauna move in their billions, and the city hums its slow, wet, unfinished song.

It will be a long time before anything here is done.
