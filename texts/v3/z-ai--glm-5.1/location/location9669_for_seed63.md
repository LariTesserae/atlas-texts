#The Throat

It has a name, though not in any language spoken above. Down here, names are pressure-acts, body-signals, something pressed flank-to-flank in the dark. The place itself was named first by the trembling of stone.

---

The chamber sits high—unnaturally high—wedged into the iron spine of a mountain range that has no foothills, only a sudden upward theft of the earth. The peaks here are not sculptural. They are structural. Basalt and hematite and something darker, a mineral that geologists above have never cataloged because it only exists where the heat comes from below and the pressure comes from all directions at once. The chamber is perhaps forty meters long, twenty wide, and the ceiling varies between three and eight meters above the floor in a way that suggests neither cave-in nor architecture but something grown, like the inside of a bone that never had marrow.

The heat arrives before you do. It rises through cracks too thin for fingers, too numerous to count. The whole floor breathes it. Not steam—there is no water here, none, not even as vapor, not even as memory. The air itself is dry in a way that makes your sinuses close and your lips split within the first hour. It is the heat of a kiln left running since before the kiln was invented. The stone underfoot is warm the way a living body is warm, and it does not cool.

No light reaches this place. The entrance—if it can be called that—is a chimney two hundred meters tall, a gap in the rock that narrows to the width of a ribcage before it opens again, and even if you could climb it, the sun above is a dim starvation. Light here is a rumor. Light here is a thing that happened to other places.

So you feel it instead.

---

The floor is not flat. It has a topography of its own: ridges and depressions that are not erosion in any conventional sense. Nothing flows here. The shapes come from pressure and heat and time—the slow extrusion of mineral under stress, the way glass deforms over centuries. In some places the floor has buckled upward into blisters the size of a dining table, smooth and warm, their surfaces touched with a dark iridescence that might be oxidation or might be something else. In other places the floor has cracked in regular, almost geometric lines, and from these cracks the heat comes most insistently, a dry exhalation that tastes of sulfur and iron and a third note, something sweet and chemical that you cannot identify and that coats the back of your tongue.

The walls are the same material—dark, dense, warm—but they are not bare. This is where the built marks are. Not structures. Marks. Grooves carved into the stone with a tool that left no chisel fracture, only a smooth channel, like a finger drawn through wet clay. The patterns are not decorative. They are not language, or if they are, the grammar is exhaustion—they repeat and falter and repeat differently, as if the maker was learning as they went, or forgetting. Some of the grooves are shallow, barely a centimeter deep. Others go to the bone of the mountain. In a few places, the grooves intersect and form shapes that might be maps of something below, or might be the record of something that happened above, or might be the kind of mark a person makes when they have been in the dark for a very long time and need to prove that their hands still work.

No one made these marks recently.

No one has been here recently.

No one except the floor.

---

They are everywhere.

This is the thing that takes the longest to understand, because understanding it requires revising what "everywhere" means. You enter the chamber and you think it is empty because you cannot see, and then your foot comes down on something that yields, and then you understand that the floor is not the floor. The floor is *them*.

They are arthropodal, perhaps, or they were, once, millions of iterations ago. They have exoskeletons, but the exoskeletons are not rigid—they are leathery, flexible, jointed in ways that suggest a creature that evolved to fill space the way water fills a vessel. They are pale. Not white—nothing here is white—but the grey of old ash, the grey of bone left in a fire. Some are the size of a thumbnail. Some are the size of a hand. A few, deep in the tightest crevices where the heat is most intense, are as large as a dog, but you will not see these; you will only feel the displacement of air when they shift.

There are millions.

They coat the floor in layers. They line the walls in the places where the grooves are shallowest. They cluster around the heat-cracks in densities so extreme that the individuals are indistinguishable, becoming a single mass, a fabric of legs and bodies and feeding-appendages that breathes as one organism. When you step, they move—not scattering, not fleeing, but *accommodating*, shifting beneath your weight like grain in a silo, pressing you into the spaces between them. They do not bite. They do not seem to register you as food or threat. You are an obstacle. You are weather.

Their life cycle is this: they eat each other. They eat the shed exoskeletons of each other. They eat the mineral deposits that form in the heat-cracks when the gases cool and precipitate. They eat the faint organic residue left by their own dead, which decompose not through decay—they are too dry for decay—but through a slow enzymatic dissolution that reduces a body to a powder the others consume. This is not cannibalism. This is economy. This is a closed system that has been closed for longer than the mountain has had a name, and the math works because the heat provides the energy, and the heat does not stop.

They do not make sound. This is the part that unnerves. A chamber with millions of living things should roar. It should click and rustle and scrape. Instead there is only the faintest susurration, the sound of leg-joints flexing in sequence, the sound of bodies adjusting against bodies, and even this is so low it is felt more than heard, a vibration in the warm air, a trembling that you might mistake for your own pulse.

Listen long enough and you will hear the rhythm. It is not your pulse. It is theirs. The whole chamber breathes at their pace. The heat rises. The mass shifts. The grooves in the wall fill and empty of bodies as the temperature fluctuates by fractions of degrees, the creatures seeking the warmth they need to digest, to molt, to reproduce in a way that is not quite reproduction—budding, perhaps, or fission, or something for which there is no word because the word would require a category that does not exist above.

---

The marks on the wall were made by hands. This is clear from the spacing of the grooves. Five digits, or four and an opposition. A person—some kind of person—was here. They were here long enough to carve meters of patterns into stone with their bare hands, or with a tool that has since become indistinguishable from the stone. They were here in the dark. They were here in the heat. And at some point they stopped carving, and the chamber filled with the creatures, and the grooves became architecture for something that does not build.

The creatures use the grooves. They press into them for warmth. They molt inside them. The deeper grooves are lined with layer upon layer of shed exoskeleton, compressed by the heat and the pressure of bodies into something like parchment, something like slate, something like a record that no one will read.

---

Standing in the Throat—this is what you will call it later, because the chamber is shaped like one, because the heat rises through it like a breath, because the walls contract slightly when the creatures shift in unison, and for a moment the space feels *alive* around you, a living throat, a living hunger, and you are not the food, you are not even the swallowed thing, you are an afterthought, a crumb in the gullet of something that has been eating itself since before the mountains rose—

Standing in the Throat, you will feel the heat most. Not the dark. The heat. Because the heat is what makes all of this possible. The heat is the sun that never sets. The heat is the water that never flows. The heat is the god and the gravity and the only reason anything lives in this sealed cathedral of consumption, and when you finally leave—through the chimney, through the crack, through whatever gap your body can still fit through—you will carry the heat with you for hours, your skin flushed, your blood thick with it, and the cold air above will feel like a violence, and some part of you, some small and ancient part, will want to go back.

The creatures will not notice you go.

They will close the space where you stood.

The Throat will swallow.
