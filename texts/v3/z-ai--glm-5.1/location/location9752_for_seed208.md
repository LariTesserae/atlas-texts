# The Weeping Plain

The first thing is the sound. Always the sound.

It begins before you see anything — before the dim grey light resolves into shapes, before the mist parts enough to show you where you stand. A roar. Not the roar of a single thing but the accumulated voice of a landscape that has never known silence, that was old when the first root cracked the first stone and is older still now that the roots are gone.

---

The plain stretches flat and featureless in every direction, or near enough to make no difference. The erosion has done its work completely here. What were once mountains have been ground to gentle undulations, what were once valleys have been filled with the fine silts of a billion years of rain. The horizon is a slight curve against a sky the color of wet slate, and the land beneath it is the color of wet slate too, and the pools and channels that cut across it are the color of wet slate deeper down. Everything is the same color. Everything is wet.

The stone — if it is still stone, after so long — weeps. Not from specific springs or seeps, but from every surface. Water emerges from the rock itself, as though the ground is saturated through and through, as though you are walking on a sponge of mineral that can hold no more. Thin films slide down every slight incline. Drops form on the undersides of overhangs and fall with metronomic patience into pools that have been receiving them since before the concept of patience existed. The air is so heavy with moisture that your skin is wet within minutes, your clothes cling, your breath hangs.

And the water makes sound. Of course it does. Every drop hitting every pool. Every film of water sliding over every smooth surface. Every channel — and there are thousands, tens of thousands, a network of interlocking grooves carved over eons into the flat rock — carries its own small stream, and each stream clicks and gurgles and hisses against the stone. Alone, each would be nothing. Together, they are a wall. A tide. A constant, enveloping roar that you feel in your chest more than hear with your ears, that vibrates the thin bones of your skull, that makes conversation impossible beyond arm's length and makes even shouting a gesture of faith.

---

There are no plants. Not a single stem, not a leaf, not a trace of green. The erosion has seen to that — whatever soil once existed here was stripped away ages upon ages ago, and nothing has come back. What remains is bare rock and water and the thin pale films that cling to the wet surfaces where the water flows slowest. The films are not quite alive, or not alive in any way you would recognize — they spread slowly, react to light and moisture, dissolve when the water runs too fast. They might be the ancestors of something that will one day become life, or they might be the last residue of something that was life once, reduced by time to this minimal function. They coat the stone in patches of pale grey and off-white, slightly slick to the touch, and where they are thickest they catch the dim light and give it back as a faint luminescence, a ghost-glow that makes the pools shimmer after the fashion of something breathing.

The animals eat the films. That is the beginning of everything that moves here.

---

And there are animals. So many animals.

They come up from the pools, or they surface from the channels, or they simply emerge from the mist at the edge of your sight and are suddenly present, watching you with eyes that are too large for their heads. They are pale — all of them pale, the grey-white of things that have never needed color, that have lived in dimness and wet since their kind began. Some are small, the size of your hand, with broad flat bodies and too many legs that scramble over the wet rock with a sound like fingernails on slate. Some are larger, the size of dogs or small deer, with low-slung bodies and slick wet hides and broad mouths that graze the films from the stone with a methodical scraping that adds its own rhythm to the roar. A few are larger still — you see them only as shapes in the mist, dark against the pale rock, moving with a slow deliberation that suggests bulk and weight and an indifference to your presence that comes from being the largest thing in a landscape that has had no predators since before the mountains fell.

They are loud. God, they are loud. The small ones click and chirp at frequencies that cut through the water-roar like needles through cloth. The medium-sized ones bellow, their throats swelling with air sacks that amplify their voices past any reasonable volume — territorial calls, mating calls, warning calls, a constant overlay of animal sound on water sound that makes the whole plain feel like the inside of a drum. The large ones, the ones in the mist, make a sound you feel rather than hear: a low, resonant hum that vibrates the water on the rock and makes the pools shiver in rings, that you can feel in your teeth and your spine, that seems to come from the ground itself.

At dawn and dusk, when the dim light dims further and the mist thickens, the chorus reaches a crescendo that is genuinely painful. Thousands upon thousands of voices, clicking and bellowing and humming, layered over the endless rush and drip and flow of water, until the air itself seems to vibrate, until you cannot tell where the sound ends and the wet air begins, until the sound becomes a physical presence, a pressure, a weight that presses against your ears and your chest and your thoughts until there is no room for anything else.

You adapt, or you leave. There is no middle option.

---

The structures are scattered across the plain at intervals that suggest intention — a day's walk apart, maybe, for someone who knows the channels and the pools and the habits of the animals. They are small. Barely large enough for two or three people to sleep in. Built from the stone of the plain itself, fitted together without mortar, the gaps between stones allowing the water to drain through rather than pooling on the floor. The walls are low and thick, the roofs are slabs of the same dark stone, and they are positioned always near the deeper channels where the water flows faster and the films grow thickest and the grazing animals come most often.

Someone built these. Someone with hands and patience and a knowledge of stone that comes from living with it a long time. The tools were simple — you can see the marks on the stone, the crude chisel-work, the places where a harder rock was used to shape a softer one. There are chips of obsidian scattered around some of the shelters, and scraps of bone that might have been needles or awls, and in one shelter you find a length of cord made from something — dried film, maybe, or animal sinew — that still holds a simple hook carved from shell.

The people who built these are gone now, or they have moved on, or they are elsewhere on the plain, following the animals, following the water. But their shelters remain, low and wet and patient, built to the measure of the landscape, eroding so slowly they will outlast anyone who might remember what they were for.

---

The rules of this place, as near as you can tell:

Water moves down. Always down. The plain is not perfectly flat — it tilts, almost imperceptibly, toward a point somewhere in the dim south, and all the channels flow there, and all the animals drift there eventually, and the mist seems to thicken in that direction until it becomes a wall of grey that might be sky or might be something else entirely.

The films grow where the water slows. The animals go where the films grow. The shelters are built where the animals go. Everything follows the water. Everything is downstream of something else.

Sound never stops. You can lie in one of the shelters at the hour that might be night and close your eyes and press your hands over your ears and still the sound is there, vibrating through the stone, through the water, through your own bones. After a while you stop trying to block it. After a longer while you stop hearing it as sound and start hearing it as the plain's own voice, the expression of something ancient and wet and patient that has been speaking since before there were ears to hear and will continue speaking long after the last ear is gone.

The weeping rocks give up their water. The films spread. The animals graze and bellow and die. The water carries everything downstream. The sound continues.

This is the world at its oldest — not dying, not dead, but finished. Arrived at its final form. The erosion has done everything it can do, and what remains is this: flat wet rock, and water, and the thin living films, and the animals that feed on them, and the sound, the sound, the sound that is the closest thing this place has to weather, or to time, or to memory.
