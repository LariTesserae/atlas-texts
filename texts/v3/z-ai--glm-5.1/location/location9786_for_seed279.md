# The Seeping Expanse

The land came up from itself not long ago. That much is obvious in the way the rock still holds its birth-shape—ridges that haven't yet learned to round, grabens that cut with geometrical intent, as if the earth here was fractured by something with a preference for clean angles. There is no soil. There is no softened contour. Everything is edge and freshness, and the water has only just begun to argue with it.

---

Water is everywhere here, but it does not move with the purpose of rivers. It seeps. It exhales from the rock faces in slow tears that find the grooves between strata and follow them downward in branching deltas too fine to track. The stone weeps—literally weeps—from a thousand faces, and where the tears converge in the low places, they form pools that have no bottom you can see. Not because they're deep, necessarily, but because the water carries something—mineral, perhaps, or something less nameable—that turns the light sideways and refuses to let it return.

The ground underfoot is never dry. You can feel it: a tension, a saturation, the skin of the world thin over water. Step hard enough and the surface puckers, squeezes out from beneath your weight like a saturated sponge. Sometimes the water that emerges is clear and cold. Sometimes it is opalescent, carrying a faint luminescence that might be chemical or might be something else remembering what light was.

The air condenses. It has no choice. Cool as it is, and wet as the stone remains, the atmosphere sits at that verge where moisture cannot stay invisible. Breathing feels like swallowing fog. Your skin never dries. Small droplets form on every surface—on the sharp edges of the fresh basalt, on the few structures, on you—and when enough of them gather, they surrender to gravity and add themselves to the slow downward weeping. The whole landscape is in the process of shedding water it has only just absorbed. It breathes in wet and breathes out wet. It has no other mode.

---

There is no plant life. Not a single stem, not a thread of algae, not the faintest stain of lichen on the stone. This absence is more absolute than you expect—it takes a moment to notice how strange it is, because your mind supplies green where there is none, fills the cracks with the assumption of moss. But there is nothing. The rock is bare, and the water moves through it unassisted by any root, and the cycles of growth and decay that you might have called ecology simply do not operate here.

What lives instead is simpler and stranger. In the pools, where the water is deepest and most opaque, something moves—not with the purpose of a fish but with the sluggish rhythm of a process. Long filaments, colorless and nearly transparent, drift in the still water like slow thoughts. They might be mineral accretions. They might be organisms. They are arranged in ways that seem too organized for crystallization and too aimless for life, and when you watch them long enough you start to understand that the distinction may not apply here. They grow, if they grow, at a pace that makes glaciers seem impulsive.

These filaments are the closest thing the Expanse has to inhabitants. There are others—occasionally, in the dim distance, something moves across the stone with a deliberateness that the filaments lack. A shape, low and slow, pressing itself against the wet rock as if listening. It leaves a trail of altered moisture, a slight viscosity in the water film that fades over hours. You never see more than one at a time, and you never see one clearly. They are not frequent. They are not rare. They are simply present, the way weather is present—acknowledged, accommodated, not fully understood.

---

The sound is immense. Not the sound of waves or wind or any single force, but the collected voice of all this water in all this stone. Every seep produces a tiny sound—a thickness, a resistance, a small wet insistence—and together they form a texture of noise that is not quite white, not quite any color at all. It has depth. It layers. Close by, you hear the individual drips and threads; farther away, they merge into a continuous low chord that seems to come from the rock itself. And beneath that, deeper, there is a resonant frequency—felt more than heard—that pulses on a scale of minutes. The Expanse breathes, and this is the sound of its breathing.

It is never quiet here. Even in the most sheltered hollows, even where the seeping slows to its minimum, there is always the wet ambient hum. It does not echo because there is nothing to echo against—no hard walls, no enclosed chambers—just the open dim expanse of wet stone and low sky. The sound simply exists, omnipresent, as fundamental as the moisture.

---

The light is insufficient. The sky—if it is a sky—hangs low and colorless, neither clouded nor clear, simply present in a way that provides illumination without providing brightness. You can see, but only to a certain distance. Beyond perhaps two hundred meters, the air thickens with suspended moisture and the world recedes into a blue-gray indeterminacy. There are no shadows because there is no point source. Everything is lit from everywhere and nowhere, and the wet stone drinks what little light arrives and gives almost nothing back.

Colors exist only in the narrowest range: the gray-black of the fresh basalt, the blue-white of the opalescent water, the faint amber of certain mineral deposits where the seeping has concentrated its invisible cargo. These amber traces are the warmest thing in the landscape, and they appear without pattern—streaking across a cliff face, pooling in a shallow depression, marking a path that the water has decided to take for reasons that belong to the stone and the stone alone.

---

And there are structures. Not many, and not large. In the low places where the seeping water pools before finding its way onward, someone has stacked stone into simple forms—cairns, perhaps, or markers, or something between a shelter and a statement. The stacking is careful. The stones are chosen for their flatness, their fit, and they are placed with a precision that suggests purpose without revealing it. The structures do not keep out water. They do not provide warmth. They simply exist, wet and patient, in the places where the water pauses.

There is no tool mark on any stone. No mortar, no binding, no sign of technique beyond selection and placement. The structures might be very old or very new; in a landscape without weathering, without vegetation, without the usual markers of time's passage, age becomes meaningless. They are here. They were made by something that understood stacking and chose to stack. That is all you can know.

Some of the structures have partially collapsed—not from decay, because nothing decays here, but from settling, from the slow rearrangement that gravity imposes on everything that stands. The collapsed stones lie where they fell, still sharp-edged, still fresh, and the water seeps over them without comment.

---

The Expanse stretches outward. It is not a single feature but a region—a low, broad bowl of freshly broken stone, perhaps the size of a small valley, perhaps larger. The edges are difficult to perceive because the light fails before the land does, but you can sense the gradual rise at the margins, the way the terrain lifts toward something like a rim. From that rim—if you could reach it—the Expanse would probably look like a shallow dish filled with dimness and the memory of water.

But you are inside it, and from here there is no edge. There is only the next seeping face, the next pool, the next structure half-visible in the moisture-haze. The sound continues. The water moves in its patient way, downward always downward, finding every groove and crack that the young stone offers. Something in the pools drifts and rearranges itself. Something on the stone moves with glacial slowness, listening.

This place is not hostile. It is not welcoming. It is simply in process—too young to have developed character, too wet to hold a mark, too dim to see itself clearly. It is becoming something. The water is shaping it, slowly, at a pace that makes human lifetimes feel like flinches. In a thousand years, the edges might soften. In ten thousand, there might be soil. In a hundred thousand, perhaps, the first hardy molecule might learn to cling to the stone and call it home.

But that is not yet. This is now. And now is the slow loud weeping of new rock learning what water means, in a dim and crowded emptiness, under a sky that has not yet decided what it is.
