# The Tepui Atelier

The plateau begins where the clouds begin.

Not above them — *within* them. The sea of white vapor breaks against the eastern escarpment like a slow surf, and the mesa receives it, draws it in, breathes. Condensation forms on every surface within moments of exposure. Skin, stone, leaf — all of it slick within minutes, beaded, running. The air is so thick with moisture that distance turns soft, and the far rim of the plateau — three kilometers away where the land drops again into cloud-forest — seems only a suggestion, a darker smear in the general brightness.

It is hot. Oppressively, uniformly, *geologically* hot. Not the heat of a sun overhead, though the sun is indeed overhead, a white disc visible through vapor-thin clouds. The heat comes from below. From the rock itself. The basalt underfoot holds warmth the way a stove holds warmth hours after the flame is out — not burning, not uncomfortable exactly, but *present*. The soles of your feet register it within seconds. The ground is warm. The ground is always warm.

And the ground is wet.

---

The water emerges everywhere. It does not flow from discrete springs or gather into proper streams at first — it *seeps*. From cracks in the basalt. From the joints between weathered columns. From the soil itself, which is thin and black and organic, perpetually saturated, perpetually warm, smelling of peat and iron and something sweeter underneath, something fermented. Water rises to the surface under pressure from somewhere deep in the mesa's guts, percolates up through fractured rock, finds expression. In the low places it pools into shallows an inch deep, two inches, warm to the touch, clouded with tannins and algae. In the slight depressions between rock ribs it forms chains of linked pools — stepping-stone water, each one a slightly different tint: amber, rust, pale jade, a murky violet where certain bacteria bloom. In the deeper fissures it moves with purpose, becoming streams that slide south and west across the plateau's gentle tilt, falling eventually into gullies that carve narrow notches into the escarpment edge, dropping as warm rain into the forest far below.

The rock weeps. This is the simplest way to say it. Every vertical face, every cliff and cut and exposed column, runs with water. Not dramatically — not waterfalls, not torrents — but steadily. A film. A gloss. The stone is dark where wet, almost black, and where the water runs longest it deposits minerals in thin ridges, building slow accumulations of rust and cream and pale sulfur-yellow that stripe the vertical surfaces like sedimentary ledger-lines. The process has been ongoing for a long time. The deposits are millimeters thick in some places, centimeters in others. The mesa has been bleeding this mineral water for millennia, building its own decorations.

---

The vegetation is impossible. It should not be here — not this *much* of it, not at this altitude, not this relentlessly. But the heat and the water have made a loophole in the rules, and the plants have found it.

The plateau is carpeted. Not covered — *carpeted*, in the way that suggests a fitted thickness, a wall-to-wall density. The ground between bare rock is a mat of low growth: mosses of seven distinct genera, some emerald, some nearly black, some a strange luminous gold where the sulfur concentration is highest; liverworts in ruffled sheets; club mosses standing in miniature forests three centimeters tall; and everywhere, everywhere, the ferns. They rise from every crevice, from every crack where soil has accumulated, from the backs of other ferns. Tiny filmy ferns with translucent fronds. Bracken-type ferns waist-high, arching over the streams. Tree ferns — actual tree ferns, twenty feet tall — clustered in the deeper gullies where the water runs strongest, their trunks thick with epiphytes and their crowns filtering the already-diffuse light into something underwater, something green and gold and perpetually duskish even at noon.

And the orchids. They colonize every vertical surface, every trunk, every mossy rock face. Not the large showy specimens of lowland greenhouses — these are small, precise, numerous beyond counting. White ones, green ones, rust-colored ones that mimic the mineral deposits. Their roots grip nothing but air and condensation, drawing water and dissolved minerals directly from the mist. Some bloom continuously, indifferent to season. Others open only at night, and in the early morning their petals are still out, catching the first thin light, and the plateau seems briefly decorated.

The density is the thing. There is no bare ground except where the basalt itself breaks through, and even then the margins are crowded. Stand in one place and you are touching plants at ankle, knee, hip. The air between the ferns is humid enough to drink. The moss beneath your feet is saturated enough that water rises around your boot-sole with each step. You cannot walk quietly. Each footfall produces a small compression, a seep, a sound like wringing a sponge.

---

The fauna is here but reticent. You hear it more than you see it — and you do not hear much.

Frogs. The plateau runs with tiny frogs, copper-colored and translucent-skinned, their internal workings visible as shadows within their bodies. They sit in the pools by the thousands, by the tens of thousands, each one no larger than a thumbnail, each one producing a single high note on some frequency just at the edge of perception. The combined sound is not a chorus — it is a *tone*, a single sustained almost-inaudible tone that underlies everything, that you feel in your sternum more than hear. When you move, nearby frogs fall silent and the tone drops by a microtone, then rebuilds. They are thermophilic, drawing their body heat from the warm water, and in the hottest pools they gather most densely, stacked on each other, breathing through their skin, metabolizing at a rate that makes them live fast and die young.

Salamanders in the streams — larger, slower, dark as the wet stone. They move against the current with deliberate patience, eating frog eggs, eating algae, eating whatever the water brings. Their eyes are gold and lidless.

Insects in the air, but fewer than the vegetation would suggest. The constant wetness suppresses them. Dragonflies hover over the pools, their wings catching light. Small moths shelter under fern fronds, waiting for the daily rain to pass. Beetles — many beetles, armored and deliberate — navigate the moss forests at ground level, clicking softly as they go.

Above, in the tree ferns, birds. But they are quiet birds. Tanagers and manakins in colors that seem competitive with the orchids — reds, blues, greens so saturated they look wet. They move through the canopy without calling, feeding on insects and nectar, occasionally settling on a perch to sing a song that is brief and plain and nothing like the elaborate performances of their lowland cousins. Sound is dampened here. The humidity absorbs it. Songs don't carry, so the birds don't bother projecting. Their vocalizations are intimate, close-range, almost whispered. You have to be near them to hear.

---

The plateau is large — several square kilometers of relatively flat terrain, tilted slightly, broken by gullies and rock ridges but walkable, open in the sense that you can see the sky, see the far edge, see the shape of the land. You can traverse it in a few hours if you don't mind wet feet. But the density slows you. The ferns catch at your legs. The moss hides uneven ground. The streams cross your path every few dozen meters, requiring careful stepping on water-smoothed stone. There are no trails. Nothing has worn a path here — not animals, not people. The growth is too prolific. Any path would close within days.

And there are no structures. No marks of construction. No cairns, no shelters, no bridges, no signs. This is unusual because the plateau feels *made* — it feels designed, curated, arranged. The regularity of the rock columns suggests masonry. The stepped terraces of mineral deposit suggest intentional shaping. The precision of the thermal vents, each one producing its pulse of steam at regular intervals, suggests engineering. But there is nothing here that was built. The columns are columnar basalt, natural. The terraces are travertine, deposited by the water itself over geological time. The steam vents are fissures in the rock, their rhythms set by the pressure cycles of a hydrothermal system far below, one that has been running since before the word *industry* existed.

And yet.

---

The steam vents are what make the plateau feel like a factory. There are perhaps two dozen of them across the mesa's surface, concentrated along the eastern edge where the basalt is thickest. Each one is a crack in the rock, ten centimeters wide, a meter or two long, emitting a continuous flow of hot vapor — not violent, not geysers, but *steady*, like the exhaust of some buried engine running on geological time. The steam rises into the already-saturated air and condenses immediately, adding to the general wetness, feeding the moss, feeding the ferns, feeding the frogs. The vents pulse. Every forty seconds or so — you time them, standing near one, feeling the warmth on your face — the flow increases, the hiss of vapor rises in pitch, and then subsides. It is as regular as breathing. It has been doing this for longer than any human structure has stood.

The sound of the vents is the loudest thing on the plateau, and it is not very loud. A continuous exhalation, a white-noise hiss, pitched just below the threshold of alarm. Standing near one, you can hear the pressure cycle as a subtle rhythm, and if you close your eyes, you can imagine yourself in a boiler room, in a factory, in the presence of some vast mechanism operating just out of sight. But when you open your eyes there is only the fern, the moss, the wet dark stone, the rising steam, the copper frogs in their warm pool, the orchids gripping the vertical surfaces by the hundred.

The plateau produces. It produces heat, water, mineral deposits, biomass at a rate that seems manufactured. The travertine terraces grow by measurable fractions each year. The vegetation regenerates faster than it decays. The frog populations cycle through generations in months. The hydrothermal system processes unimaginable volumes of water, heating it, mineralizing it, ejecting it into the light where it feeds everything. The place *works*. It is a system in balance, long-established, self-sustaining, productive.

It is an engine. It happens to be made of rock and water and time.

---

In the late afternoon the clouds come up from the east, pouring over the escarpment edge like a slow flood, and the plateau enters its daily rain. Not dramatic — no thunder, no wind, no lightning. Just a steady vertical fall of warm water, continuing for hours, adding to what the ground already holds. The sound of rain on fern fronds is a soft percussion, a whispering. The frogs continue their tone. The vents continue their breathing. The water rises in the pools by millimeters. The streams run faster.

You could stand here for an hour and not notice the change, because the change is the constant. The plateau is always doing this — always wet, always warm, always growing, always depositing, always exhaling steam. The erosion is minimal because the vegetation holds the soil, because the rock is hard, because the system has found its equilibrium. The land does not degrade. It maintains. It is in its own state, as it has been for a very long time.

When the rain passes and the clouds thin and the last light comes through from the west, the whole plateau glows — the wet surfaces reflecting the orange-pink, the steam catching it and going gold, the orchids briefly incandescent. The frogs' tone seems to deepen. The vents breathe. The heat rises from below.

There is no one here. There has never been anyone here, or if there has been, they left no trace, and the moss grew over the place where they stood within a season.

The plateau does not need an audience. It runs anyway.
