# The Murmuring City

The first thing you notice is the light.

It comes from above — not from any sun, not from any fixture — but from the ceiling itself, which is not a ceiling at all but a sky of stone, vaulted and distant, crusted with thick blooms of lithophage lichen that burn with a cold white radiance. They have grown there for millennia. They will grow there for millennia more. Their light is the light of a snowfield under full moon: bright enough to read by, bright enough to see every crack in the pavement, bright enough to cast shadows that stretch long and blue across the ancient floors, but it carries no warmth. The air is cool. It has always been cool here, in the under-realm, where the surface is a rumor and the sun is a myth.

---

The city has no name. Or rather, it has had many, worn smooth like river stones, each inscription eroded into the next until the walls bear only ghost-text, palimpsests of languages no living thing speaks. The streets are wide — impossibly wide for a place built by hand — boulevards of fitted stone that stretch thirty paces across, their surfaces polished by time and the touch of ten billion feet into something like glass. The cobbles are basalt and soapstone, dark and pale alternating, and between them, in the narrowest gaps, condensation collects. This is the water here: not rain, not streams, but the slow breath of the stone itself, exhaling moisture into the cool air, where it beads and runs in thin trickles down the facades of buildings, pools in the gutters, gathers in the carved channels that line every thoroughfare. In the deepest rooms, where the light of the ceiling-lichen does not reach, the walls weep constantly, a slow perspiration that feeds shallow puddles and thin rills, no more than ankle-deep, that cross the floors like veins of mercury before vanishing into cracks too narrow for a finger.

There are no plants. The concept of root, of stem, of leaf, has never taken hold in this place. The green that crawls across some surfaces is not green but pale blue-gray: the ceiling-lichen descended, or its cousin, a mineral-eating fungus that etches slow pathways through the stone, leaving behind it a fine powder like chalk dust and shallow channels like the beds of ancient rivers. The fungus does not grow upward. It does not reach. It spreads, flat and patient, across walls and floors and the undersides of arches, and when it has consumed what it can from one stone, it crosses the mortar to the next. In some quarters of the city, entire facades have been eaten to a depth of an inch, their carvings softened, their edges rounded, their geometry reduced to the gentle curves of things worn by water or wind — but there is no wind here, and the water is too slow to carve. Only the fungus, only time.

---

The buildings are old. Not centuries old. Not millennia old. *Old* old — the kind of old that makes geology seem hasty. They were built with hand tools, with stones too heavy for one person to carry, fitted together without mortar in the older sections and with a crude lime compound in the newer ones (though "newer" here still means older than any surface empire's founding myth). The walls are thick. The doorways are tall. The windows are narrow and set high, designed for light to enter but not wind, because there is no wind, because the air moves only when something displaces it. Some structures rise four stories, their upper floors accessible by interior ramps rather than stairs — the builders had legs that bent differently, or perhaps had more of them. The rooms are spare, their purposes unclear. Many contain nothing but shallow basins set into the floor, long dry, or stone benches worn smooth by generations of sitting, or alcoves that might hold offerings or might hold nothing at all.

The city is sparse. For all its size — and it is vast, a network of districts and plazas and connecting roads that would take a full day to cross on foot — the space between buildings is enormous. Wide streets. Empty plazas. Courtyards paved in stone and given over to nothing but the slow advance of the fungus and the quiet pooling of condensation. There are gaps in the city where buildings simply do not exist, as if the builders left room for something that never arrived, or as if something was here once and has since been removed, stone by stone, leaving only the foundation marks like scars in the bedrock. The density is that of a city remembered rather than a city lived in — the architecture of a place that was designed for far more than it ever contained.

---

And yet it is not empty.

The fauna own this place now. They have for ages beyond counting.

The ceiling-lichen sheds a fine spore-dust that drifts downward like snow in slow motion, and where it lands on wet stone, things emerge. Not plants. Not exactly animals either — or rather, animals of a kind so simple and so successful that they make complexity seem like a distraction. The floor of every wide street is covered in a slow, constant motion: pale arthropods the size of a thumbnail, segmented and legless, like living grains of rice, sliding across the damp stone on pads of mucus, feeding on the fungus, feeding on each other, feeding on the lichen-dust. They are white, nearly translucent, and in the cold light from above they seem to glow, or perhaps they are simply so thin that the light passes through them. There are millions of them. Tens of millions. In the wider plazas, the ground seems to *move*, a slow and deliberate shifting, like the surface of a pool disturbed by a thousand tiny currents. You cannot take a step without treading on them, and when you lift your foot, they fill the space behind it in seconds, flowing back into the depression like water.

Larger things move through the streets. Predators, though the word seems too dramatic for what they are: flat, low creatures like pale stingrays without tails, gliding on pads of the same sticky mucus, their mouths underneath, their senses distributed across the broad surface of their bodies. They are the size of dinner plates. They drift through the rice-worms and the rice-worms part around them, re-forming in their wake. Some have learned to climb the walls, suctioning themselves to the damp stone and working their way up to the higher windows, where they rest in the shallow pools and feed on the moisture. From inside a building, looking out through a narrow window, you might see one pressed against the opening like a face, eyeless and motionless, waiting for nothing in particular.

And larger still: the *walkers*. They are the inheritors of this city, the closest thing it has to citizens. Bipedal in the loosest sense — they stand on two thick columns of muscle and cartilage, but these bend in three places, giving them a rolling, tilting gait like a ship in rough water. Their bodies are ovoid, featureless except for the cluster of pale tendrils at their apex, which they use to test the air and the stone and the moisture. They have no eyes. They have no mouths that anyone has found. They feed through the pads of their feet, drawing nutrients from the damp stone, from the rice-worms they crush underfoot without noticing, from the lichen-dust that settles on their skin and is absorbed. They are the size of a human. They move through the wide streets with a slow, purposeful meandering, entering buildings through doorways designed for their shape, resting on benches designed for their weight, standing in the shallow basins as if in ritual.

No one knows if they built the city. The question has been asked. The architecture suits them — the ramps, the wide streets, the tall doorways, the shallow basins — but the stone is so old, so deeply worn, that any tool marks that might have answered the question were erased eons ago. Perhaps they built it and forgot. Perhaps they found it and moved in. Perhaps they and the builders are the same species, separated by time into forms that would not recognize each other. The walkers do not seem to care. They do not maintain the city. They do not repair it. They do not alter it. They simply *use* it, moving through its spaces with the casual occupancy of a creature in its natural habitat, which perhaps, now, it is.

---

The turbulence is everywhere, but it is a quiet turbulence.

The rice-worms are in constant motion, flowing, parting, re-forming, their populations surging and crashing in cycles that play out over hours or days, a boom and bust ecology compressed into the space of a single street corner. In the damp corners where condensation pools, the worms gather so thickly that they form a living paste, and then the flat predators come, gliding in to feed, and the paste dissolves in a thousand directions, and then the predators drift on, and the worms return, and the paste reforms, and the cycle begins again. It is not violent. It is not loud. But it is ceaseless, and it is complex beyond any simple model — the kind of complexity that emerges from simple rules iterated a billion times, patterns within patterns, waves within waves, the logic of sand dunes and weather systems applied to living things on a floor of ancient stone.

The walkers add their own motion. They do not sleep, or if they do, they sleep standing, in the basins, for hours at a time, before resuming their slow navigation of the city. Sometimes a walker will stop in an intersection and stand for days, its tendrils extended, testing the air, before turning down a street it has never taken, or so it seems, moving with sudden purpose toward some distant destination that only it can sense. Sometimes two walkers will meet in a plaza and stand close together, tendrils interlacing, for hours or days, before separating and moving on. Sometimes a walker will enter a building and not emerge for weeks. The city is full of these small dramas, these patterns of movement that seem meaningful but resist interpretation, a society — if it is a society — conducted in silence, through touch and taste and the slow exchange of chemicals on damp stone.

The greatest turbulence is invisible. The moisture. The condensation rises and falls in cycles that have nothing to do with weather — there is no weather here — but everything to do with the breathing of the stone, the slow thermal pulse of the deep earth, the exhalations of the ceiling-lichen. The walls weep, then dry, then weep again. The pools fill, then empty, then fill. The thin rills in the channels swell to streams, then shrink to threads, then swell again. Every surface in the city is in a constant state of wetting and drying, and every creature that lives here has organized its life around this rhythm, this slow tide of moisture that moves through the stone like blood through a body.

---

The sound of the city is the sound of water moving very slowly over stone.

It is the sound of condensation gathering into droplets heavy enough to fall from the ceiling of a room, landing in shallow pools with a sound like a finger tapped against a glass. It is the sound of the rice-worms, which make no sound at all individually but collectively produce a faint, continuous *rustle*, like the stirring of dry leaves in a wind that does not exist. It is the sound of the flat predators, which are silent, and the walkers, which are silent, and the buildings, which are silent, settling imperceptibly into foundations that were laid in an age before memory. It is the sound of your own breathing, which seems loud here, too loud, a rough and irregular intrusion into a quiet that has been building for longer than your species has existed.

Sometimes, in the deepest rooms, you can hear the stone itself: a low, subsonic hum, felt more than heard, the vibration of the earth's heat moving through the rock, the pulse of the deep, the sound that was here before there were ears to hear it.

---

Stand in the central plaza at the city's heart — a space so wide that the far buildings are lost in the soft glare of the ceiling-lichen, a space paved in black basalt worn smooth as ice, a space where the rice-worms move in rivers and the flat predators drift like clouds and the walkers cross in their slow, tilting way, paying you no attention, because you are not relevant to their economy of moisture and stone and spore-dust — and you will feel what this place is. Not a ruin. Not a relic. Not a dead thing preserved in amber. A living system, ancient beyond reckoning, stable beyond change, operating at a pace that makes human civilization seem like a nervous twitch. A city that has outlasted its builders, if it ever had builders, and will outlast everything that now lives within it, including the walkers, including the worms, including the lichen, because the stone is patient, and the moisture is patient, and time, here, is so deep that even erosion seems to move with dignity.

The light falls from above, bright and cold. The water beads on the walls. The creatures move in their patterns. The city breathes.

It has been breathing for a very long time.
