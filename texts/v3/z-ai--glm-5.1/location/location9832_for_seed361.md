# The Garden in the Throat

You enter through a crack that shouldn't be there.

The plateau stretches in every direction — if plateau is even the word for something this old, this reduced. Once it was a mountain range. Once it was teeth. A hundred million years of rain and wind and patient chemical hunger have ground it down to a gentle swell above the lowlands, a wrinkle in the earth's skin so subtle you might walk across it without noticing the incline. The stone is smooth like a river stone is smooth, but at continental scale. Everything sharp has been forgiven by time.

And then: the crack. A meter wide, trending diagonal, hidden behind a stand of tower-straight cycads whose trunks are beaded with condensation. You smell it before you see it — warm, wet, alive. The exhalation of a closed mouth opening.

---

Inside, the throat opens into a chamber roughly the size of a small cathedral. The ceiling is low enough to touch if you stand on your toes. The walls are the same ancient stone as the plateau above, but here they're colonized.

Not by moss. Not by lichen. By something that decided to be both.

The vegetation is lush in the way of things that have had infinite patience. Mats of deep-green growth cover every surface that isn't actively dripping, and each mat is punctuated by upright structures — not quite fronds, not quite stems — that reach toward the chamber's single source of light: a long, lateral fissure in the eastern wall, about chest height, through which daylight enters in a flat band that crawls across the floor as the sun moves. The light is ordinary. Sunlight. Warm. But the chamber transforms it. Everything it touches seems to hold it a beat too long, as if the surfaces here have learned to be greedy with photons.

The plants are spaced with intention. A fern-analogue here, its curled tips still furled with morning dew. A cluster of thick-leaved rosettes there, catching drip from the ceiling. Between them: bare stone, dark and damp. The emptiness is not absence. It is architecture. Each plant has its territory, its watershed, its angle on the light-fissure. Nothing crowds. Nothing needs to. The chamber has been stable for longer than most species persist, and the plants here have solved every problem that adjacency creates by simply... not creating it.

There is moisture everywhere but no standing water. Condensation beads on the ceiling and runs down the walls in slow, erratic rivulets that join and separate like conversation. The air is warm and close, held at a temperature that feels biological — as though the chamber itself is metabolizing, generating heat through some slow digestion of stone and light and time. You are damp within minutes. Your skin tastes of minerals.

---

And then there is the sound.

It begins as you descend into the chamber, a low vibration that you feel in your sternum before your ears register it. As your feet find the stone floor, it resolves into layers. The lowest layer is the drip and run of condensation — countless small impacts on stone and leaf, each one tiny, but together forming a textured white noise that seems to come from everywhere. Above that: a rhythmic clicking, metronomic and precise, like bone on chitin. Above that: a sustained, almost tonal hum, the kind of sound a thousand throats might make if they were all resonating at the same frequency. And threading through all of it, a high, irregular chittering that rises and falls in bursts of frantic activity and then, without warning, stops. Silence for three heartbeats. Then it begins again.

The source of all this sound is the fauna.

---

They are everywhere.

The chamber hosts a density of animal life that seems to violate the modest proportions of the space, and the violation is the first true strangeness — the sense that this ecosystem is too full, too loud, too *much* for this hidden pocket in an aged plateau. But it has been here, they have been here, for longer than the plateau remembers being tall, and the math has long since stopped caring about your sense of proportion.

The dominant presence is an arthropod-like creature about the length of your hand, with a segmented body the color of wet amber and too many legs — you count fourteen on one, nineteen on another, and you begin to understand that leg-count here is not fixed the way you expect it to be. They move across every surface — walls, ceiling, the stems of the plants, the bare stone floor — in a constant, purposeful flow. Some carry droplets of condensation in their mandibles, moving with evident direction toward cracks in the wall too small for you to identify. Water-bearers. Others carry fragments of the plant-matter, or the pale, husked bodies of smaller creatures you cannot identify. The clicking sound is their feet on stone. Each one alone is nearly silent. There are thousands.

Above them, in the air itself, are the hummers. Not insects — you discard that word almost immediately. They are more like seeds that learned to fly: small, ovoid bodies with no visible wings, no visible means of propulsion, hovering and drifting in the warm air as though buoyancy is simply their natural state. They are a deep, iridescent blue-black, and when the light-fissure's beam crosses their path, they flash with a purple sheen that hurts to look at directly. The hum comes from them. Not from their bodies — from the air around them, vibrating at some frequency you feel more than hear. They drift in loose, slow formations, and when two pass close, they adjust — repelling or attracting, you cannot tell which — with a fluidity that suggests communication.

The chittering comes from deeper in the chamber, from crevices in the far wall where the light does not reach. Something lives back there. Many things. You hear them moving — a dry, scratching sound beneath the chittering — but you do not see them. They see you. The chittering stops when you move toward them and resumes when you step back. A boundary. Understood.

There are other creatures. A thing like a flat, pale ribbon that flows across the ceiling, consuming the condensation-mats as it goes and leaving a trail of bare, glistening stone that is slowly, visibly recolonized. Creatures like the arthropods but smaller, faster, with bodies that seem to phase between solidity and translucence as they move through patches of shadow. Something in the plant-rosettes that has the same general shape as the leaves but detaches and reattaches and was probably watching you from the moment you entered.

The fauna is not hostile. It is not friendly. It is *present* — overwhelmingly, loudly, continuously present — and its presence has nothing to do with you. You are a disruption in a pattern that was established before your species existed, and the pattern is already accommodating you, flowing around you the way water flows around a new stone. The arthropods adjust their paths by centimeters. The hummers drift slightly wider. The things in the dark crevice track you with sound instead of sight. You are noted. You are not important.

---

The built structures are scattered throughout the chamber, and they are the second strangeness.

There are three. Perhaps four, if you count something in the far wall that might be structure or might be erosion — the line is not clear here.

The first is a pillar, roughly waist-height, emerging from the floor near the center of the chamber. It is not stone. It is not metal. It is something that has the texture of polished bone but the color of the deep amber arthropods, and its surface is covered in markings so fine they might be texture rather than script. When you lean close — you do lean close, you cannot help it — the markings seem to shift, not changing their content but changing their orientation, always facing you, always readable, even though you cannot read them. The pillar is warm to the touch. Warmer than the air. It hums at a frequency just below the hummers' range, and you realize this is not a remnant. This is active. This is doing something.

The second structure is a shelf or ledge built into the western wall, formed from the same amber-bone material. Objects rest on it. One is a sphere, perfectly smooth, the size of your closed fist, that appears to contain a liquid that does not respond to gravity — it stays fixed in the center of the sphere regardless of how you tilt it, a small, trembling droplet of something silver and alive. Another is a flat plate etched with a pattern that your eyes cannot quite hold — it looks like a map, or a circuit, or a root system, and each interpretation lasts only a moment before slipping into the next. A third object is simply a void: a space on the shelf where light does not go, a rectangular absence about the size of a book, held in shape by a faint frame of the amber-bone material. You do not touch the void. You understand, without knowing how, that it is full.

The third structure is the most unsettling. It is a line — a single, taut thread of something too thin to see directly, running from near the ceiling to a point on the floor close to the far wall. You can only see it by the way the air distorts around it, a faint shimmer like heat-haze, and by the way the arthropods walk around it rather than over it. It vibrates. Not from the ambient sound — at a frequency independent of the chamber's noise, something regular and deliberate, a pulse. When you place your hand near it — near, not touching — you feel a cold that is not temperature. A subtraction. The line is removing something from the air. Processing it. You step back.

---

The technology here is not ruins. It is not leftover. It is infrastructure.

Something built this pocket, or found it and modified it, and the modifications are still running. The chamber's stable temperature, its persistent humidity despite no visible water source, the impossible density of life in a space this small — these are not natural phenomena, not exactly. They are the output of systems that operate below the threshold of your perception and far beyond the reach of your understanding. The pillar, the shelf, the line — these are the visible components. The rest — the condensation cycles, the atmospheric regulation, whatever mechanism sustains the hummers' flight, whatever process the arthropods serve when they carry water to the wall-cracks — is embedded in the stone itself, in the air, in the biology of the creatures who have co-evolved with it for epochs beyond counting.

You are standing inside a machine that learned to be an ecosystem. Or an ecosystem that learned to be a machine. The distinction, here, has not been meaningful for a very long time.

---

The light from the fissure is moving. Afternoon. You have been here longer than you intended.

As the beam of sunlight slides across the floor, it crosses the central pillar, and for a moment — just a moment — the markings on its surface resolve into something you can almost read. Not language. Not image. Something between, a kind of notation that your brain is almost equipped to process, and in that almost, you feel the shape of a message that has been broadcasting since before the plateau was flattened, before the mountain range was ground down, before the stone was stone:

*We are still here. The garden is maintained. The count continues.*

And then the light passes, and the markings are just markings again, and the arthropods flow around your feet like a river of amber, and the hummers drift in their uncountable orbits, and the things in the dark crevice resume their chittering, and the condensation beads and rolls and feeds everything it touches, and the chamber breathes its warm, mineral breath, and you understand that you are a guest in a room that has been waiting — not for you, not for anyone, just waiting — with the patience of stone that used to be a mountain, holding its small, teeming, impossible world together in its throat.
