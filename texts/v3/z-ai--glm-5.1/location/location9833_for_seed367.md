# The Throat

It is called the Throat, though no one remembers who named it or why. The name sticks because it fits—this place feels like something swallowed and kept.

---

You enter through a crack that could be mistaken for a shadow on the plateau wall. The plateau itself is almost a billion years old, worn down from what was once a mountain range into a flat, heat-struck expanse of ferric stone and compressed ash. Everything sharp was taken by wind long ago. Everything that could fall has fallen. What remains is the bottom of things—the mineral memory of what used to stand.

But the crack leads inward, and inward is different.

The passage is barely wide enough for shoulders. It descends—not far, maybe thirty meters—but the light falls away quickly. The stone here is oxidized to deep maroon and black, veined with quartz that catches whatever dim light reaches this deep and holds it for a moment, like something remembering sunshine. The air warms as you descend. Not the dry heat of the surface, but something closer—thicker—air that has been breathed before.

Then the passage opens.

---

The Throat is a cavity, roughly ovoid, perhaps forty meters across at its widest and half that in height. It is a pocket torn from the plateau's interior long ago—a gas bubble in cooling volcanic rock, or a chamber dissolved by ancient waters that no longer exist. The ceiling is low and smooth, worn by ages of friction and pressure into something like the inside of a skull. The walls curve inward gently, then flatten into a floor of fine, pale sediment—powdered mineral that has rained down from above over countless millennia and compacted into something between dust and stone.

No water flows here. No springs, no seeps, no condensation on the walls. The rock is dry to its core. The air carries a mineral sharpness—silicon, iron, something sulfurous—but no moisture. Your lips crack within the first hour. Your skin begins to scale.

And yet the Throat is full of life.

---

The vegetation grows in layers so dense they obscure the walls entirely. It is not green. It is amber, deep ochre, rust, the orange-brown of old blood—and it does not drink water.

The Throat's flora runs on a different chemistry. The sediment floor is rich in perchlorates and other reactive salts, and the root-masses that thread through it draw these compounds up through lattice-like vascular structures, using the energy of slow exothermic reactions to build their tissues. The plants are slow-growing—some of the oldest specimens may be thousands of years old—but they are thick, interlocked, layered shelf upon shelf and tendril upon tendril, forming a living architecture that climbs the walls and drapes from the ceiling in curtains of filament and frond. When you brush against them, they feel warm. Faintly, persistently warm, like a sleeping animal.

The air is thick with their output—fine spores and chemical exhalations that catch the dim light in haze. Everything in the Throat smells like burnt sugar and iron. The light itself seems to come from no single source: the quartz veins in the ceiling fluoresce faintly under the chemical mist, and the older growth-bodies of the vegetation carry bioluminescent patches, cold amber and pale sulfur-yellow, blinking on and off in rhythms that have nothing to do with any cycle you'd recognize.

It is never bright here. It is never fully dark. The Throat exists in a perpetual dimness that makes distances uncertain and edges soft.

---

Against the far wall—half-consumed by the vegetation, built into and around and through it—is the settlement.

It is not large. Perhaps two dozen structures, if you can call them structures. They are built from the same mineral sediment as the floor, packed and shaped by hand into walls and partitions, then allowed to be colonized by the vegetation, which reinforces them, binds them, makes them part of its own body. The buildings breathe. You can watch their walls swell and contract, slowly, over the course of hours. Doors are gaps left in the growth; windows are translucent membranes of plant-tissue that let the dim light filter through in warm, honey-colored shafts.

The people who built this place—and their descendants who still live here—work with stone and wood and bone. Their tools are hand-knapped, hand-ground, hand-bound. They have never smelted metal. They have never needed to. The vegetation provides most of what they require: structural material, chemical feedstock, slow heat, and—through processes they have refined over generations—fermented compounds that serve as fuel and medicine and the thin, acrid fluid they drink in place of water.

They number perhaps a hundred. They do not leave the Throat. There is nothing on the plateau for them, and the passage is narrow enough that they have learned to think of the world as this place, this cavity, this warmth.

---

But the people are not what you notice first.

The fauna is what you notice. The fauna is all you notice, at first, until you learn to see past it.

The Throat teems.

The air is never still with wings—chitinous, rustling, too many to count. The insects here are large and numerous, from thumb-sized crawlers that work through the vegetation in columns of thousands, to fist-winged fliers that click and drone through the amber haze, feeding on the chemical nectar the growth-bodies weep from their surfaces. The walls move if you watch them long enough—shifting, rippling, as crawl of various kinds makes its way through the filament forests, harvesting, being harvested.

There are vertebrates too—or the local equivalent. Eyeless, smooth-skinned things the size of a forearm that slide through the vegetation in pursuit of the insects. Pale, legless hunters that drop from the ceiling on threads of their own making and coil around their prey. Small, many-legged foragers with sensing whiskers that fan out from their heads like fronds, picking through the sediment for chemical traces. They are quick and numerous; you see dozens in the first minute, hundreds in the first hour.

And there are the larger ones. Not large by surface standards, but large here—bound to this pocket, adapted to it. A species of climbing reptilian that haunts the upper walls, iridescent and quiet, feeding on the fliers and the occasional small forager. Something that might be a amphibian but has no need of pools, its life cycle completed entirely in the moisture-free chemical soup of the Throat, its eggs laid in the warm tissue of the growth-bodies themselves. The people have names for all of them. The people have categories, taxonomies, knowledge that has no writing, preserved in memory and chant.

The sound is constant. Click, drone, rustle, chirp, hiss—the layered noise of an ecosystem in full motion, reverberating off the cavity walls, amplified by the enclosed space until it becomes a single textured roar. Not deafening, but inescapable. You feel it in your sternum. You feel it in your teeth.

---

The Throat has been like this for a very long time.

This is what the maximum erosion means—not that the place is eroding now, but that it has already eroded. It has already been reduced to its most stable form. The plateau above lost its mountains, its valleys, its rivers, its weather, millions of years ago. What remains is what cannot be worn away further—the mineral substrate, the chemistry, the warmth from below. And in this pocket, sealed and self-sustaining, the ecosystem has had time—deep, geological time—to settle into equilibrium. Every species here fills a niche that has been filled the same way for longer than the surface has had its current coastlines. The predator-prey relationships are tight, cyclical, ancient. The vegetation's chemistry has been optimized over epochs. Nothing here is new. Nothing here is experimenting. The Throat is a completed system—a final form.

And yet it is not static. It throbs. The ecosystem cycles through micro-seasons driven by the slow pulse of chemical concentrations in the sediment, the faint fluctuations in geothermal warmth, the reproductive rhythms of species that have been dancing with each other since before the plateau had a name. The sound rises and falls. The bioluminescence brightens and dims. The air thickens and clears. The people move through their settlement, tending their fermentation vats, harvesting the growth-bodies, culling the insects that grow too numerous, feeding the midden-beasts that clean their waste.

It is loud. It is warm. It is close.

---

Stand in the center of the Throat at its quietest hour—there is no night here, only the dim and the dimmer—and turn slowly. The walls are invisible behind the vegetation, which is invisible behind the haze, which is invisible behind the motion of wings and crawlers and the amber glow of slow chemistry. The ceiling is a dark suggestion above you. The floor is soft sediment and root-mass. The air tastes like iron and sugar and old stone. Sound comes from everywhere and nowhere—the composite voice of a pocket-world that has been speaking to itself, in its own tongue, for longer than anything on the surface has been alive.

This is the Throat: a cavity in ancient rock, high on a plateau that has forgotten it was ever mountains, where life learned to run on salt and warmth instead of water, where a hundred humans live inside a living wall of ochre growth, where the fauna owns every cubic centimeter of air and surface, where the dim light glows like a memory of fire, where everything has already become what it will be.

It is not waiting for anything. It is not going anywhere. It is here, complete, ancient, loud with its own becoming—finished and still alive, final and still in motion, the last form and the only one this place will ever need.
