# The Mesa Apalala

The first thing you notice is the sound.

Not wind—there is wind, yes, but it moves like something half-asleep, pushing warm and slow across the mesa-top and murmuring through the clustered stems with a sound like breathing heard through cloth. No: the first sound is the chorus. Insect-song, thrumming from every surface. The clicking chant of shell-backed things in the thatch. The hollow booming of the drum-beetles in the clay walls, each one no larger than a thumbnail, each one hammering its abdomen against the mud-brick in a rhythm that syncs and desyncs across the settlement in patterns too slow to perceive, patterns that shift over hours, that have been shifting here since before anyone can remember. The sound is constant. It rises and falls like a tide that never reaches shore.

---

The mesa stands alone.

Not alone the way a mountain stands alone—something that rises and can be climbed, something that belongs to the land beneath it. Apalala has been separated. Eons of erosion have eaten the softer stone around it until what remains is a flat-topped column of ancient hardpan, two hundred meters high and perhaps forty across at its widest, a platform of compacted mineral and calciumite that holds its shape the way bone holds its shape: because everything softer has already been taken. The sides are near-vertical in places, scalloped and fluted by ten million years of particulate wind, by the slow sandblasting of epochs. Nothing climbs those sides. Nothing could.

The top is nearly flat. The erosion that ground the surrounding landscape to nothing ground the mesa-top too, levelled it, polished it like a tooth worn smooth by age. What remains is a surface of pale, hardened earth—cracked into a polygonal tessellation that nothing grows in, nothing grows on, a pavement of mineral silence ringing the edges where the settlement clusters toward the center.

That is the paradox of the place: life presses inward, not outward.

---

There is no water here.

Not in the way other places have water. No streams, no wells, no rainfall that accumulates. What happens is this: each long dusk, as the warm air rises from the lowlands far below and meets the cooler layer at the mesa's height, a fog forms. Not a dense fog—a gauze, a veil, a breath of moisture that passes through the vegetation and is gone within an hour. The plants have learned to take what they need in that hour.

And they are lush. Impossibly lush for a place with no water, and yet they are here, pressing against each other in a density that seems to contradict the barren mineral beneath. The broad-leafed succulents with their waxy skins, pale green and grey and sometimes flushed with a mineral red at the tips, where the calciumite they draw up tints their new growth. The rope-vines that drape from every structure, binding roof to wall to neighboring wall in web-thick curtains, their fibers strong enough that the settlers have used them for rope and twine and rough-woven cloth for generations. The moss-mats—not true mosses, but something analogous, something that spreads in velvety pads across any surface that holds still long enough—coating the lower walls, the shaded ground, the crooks where beam meets beam, softening every angle, consuming the geometry of the architecture one slow season at a time.

The roots of everything tangle together beneath the surface, threading through the cracks in the hardpan, reaching deeper than anyone has ever dug. Some say they reach all the way down through the column of the mesa, drinking from something at the base. Others say they have simply learned to hold what the fog gives and waste nothing. Either way: the vegetation is a single interlocked system, each plant touching its neighbors, sharing, competing in slow motion, a dense and breathing mass that fills every gap the settlers leave open.

---

The settlement is called nothing, or has been called something for so long that the word has worn smooth and lost its meaning.

Sixty-three structures, last count, though count is difficult because the structures grow into each other. Built from the mesa's own material—pale blocks cut from the edges where the hardpan was softer, once, generations ago, before the cutting stopped because there was no more soft edge to take. The walls are thick, foot-thick, made without mortar: the blocks are shaped to fit, each one hand-chiseled with stone tools, each one unique, fitted to its neighbors the way the plants fit root to root. The roofs are woven rope-vine plastered with the same pale dust mixed with fibrous pulp, and they sag in the middle as they age, and the moss-mats colonize the sag, and after a generation a roof becomes a garden, and after two generations it becomes a hill, and someone builds a new structure on top of it.

This is why the settlement appears to be climbing itself.

The oldest structures are at the bottom—single-story, half-consumed by vegetation, their doorways green tunnels, their interiors taken over by the useful fungi that the settlers cultivate in the dark. Above them, the second generation: taller, two stories, the lower floors given over to the drum-beetles and the curing of fiber, the upper floors for sleeping and the small rituals of a people who have been here so long they have forgotten the word for *elsewhere*. And above those, the newest: platforms, half-rooms, open-air workshops where the evening fog collects on angled leaves and drips into clay catchments, where the children spread the pale fiber-cloth to absorb what moisture they can, where the old women sit and chip at stones and talk in voices that merge with the beetle-drone and the wind.

Forty meters across, and perhaps two hundred people, and their animals, and their insects, and their plants, and the structures that contain them all in a density that would feel suffocating if it were not all anyone has ever known.

---

The animals are everywhere.

The scarab-goats—small, sure-footed, their hides the same pale grey-green as the succulents, their hooves cracked and wide for gripping the worn stone—they wander the paths between structures, they climb the roofs, they stand on the mesa's edge and stare outward at the emptiness with an expression that might be patience or might be vacancy. They eat the moss-mats and the younger vine-growth, and their dung is mixed with dust for building, and their hair is spun into a coarse thread, and when they die their shells are kept—beautiful things, iridescent in the dim light, hung from doorways as markers or ground into powder for the paints the children use on the interior walls.

The moths come at dusk. Clouds of them, following the fog upward, their wings soft and pale and enormous, each one the width of a hand. They are drawn to warmth, and they cluster on the walls and roofs and on the skin of the settlers, and they are harmless, and their bodies are gathered in the morning by children who grind them into a protein paste, and this is one of the staples, has always been one of the staples, the taste of dusk made into food.

The lizards are underfoot—quick, pale things with translucent skin that shows the dark pulse of their organs beneath. They eat the insects. The insects eat everything else: the fiber, the moss, the dead skin, the waste, the old wood of the looms, the bindings of the tools. Nothing is wasted because the insects ensure waste does not exist. They process. They transform. They return.

And in the cracks of the hardpan, deep in the polygonal seams at the mesa's edge, there are things the settlers do not name. Not large. Not dangerous. But old, and patient, and belonging to the stone in a way the settlers—despite their centuries of occupation—do not. Occasionally one is seen: a pale limb withdrawing, a flicker of movement in a crack too narrow for anything to live. The settlers leave offerings of fiber-paste at the cracks. Not out of fear. Out of courtesy. This was their mesa before it was anyone's, and they were here when the rock was soft.

---

The light is always dim.

Not dark—never fully dark, not even at the depth of night, when the stars are dense and white above and the mesa stands in a ring of absolute emptiness, a platform suspended in nothing, and the wind comes up from the lowlands carrying warmth and the faint mineral smell of the world below. But dim, always dim, because the mesa is high enough that the fog-layers live below it, and the fog-layers catch the morning sun and hold it, and what reaches the settlement is a diffuse glow, a brightening of the grey, a warmth without sharp edges. The afternoons are brighter—sometimes the fog burns away far below, and the sun comes through thin and clear, and everything on the mesa casts a shadow so sharp it looks carved from stone. But this lasts only two or three hours, and then the fog returns, and the dim settles back like a hand on a shoulder, familiar and weighty and patient.

The settlers have adapted. Their eyes are large, their pupils wide. They do not work by sight alone; they navigate by sound, by touch, by the particular smell of each structure and each path. They know where they are the way roots know where water is: not through reasoning, but through orientation, through the slow accumulation of being-in-place.

---

Time moves slowly on the mesa.

Not because the settlers are slow—though they are, by the standards of places that change, they move with the deliberate care of people who have learned that haste breaks things that take generations to build. But because the place itself is slow. The erosion has nearly stopped; the hardpan is too old and too hard to erode further, or it erodes at a rate that means nothing to human timescales, a millimeter per century, a rounding of an edge, a deepening of a crack that will not split the stone for another ten thousand years. The vegetation grows and dies, but slowly, a single rope-vine taking a generation to reach from ground to roof, a moss-mat spreading a hand's-width per year. The structures settle. The roofs sag. The moss comes. A new structure is built on top. The process takes longer than a life.

The children are born into the same settlement their grandparents were born into, and it has changed, but subtly—a roof lower here, a wall higher there, a path that used to be open now closed by a new structure, a door that used to lead outside now leading into a room that wasn't there before. The mesa accumulates. It does not replace. Nothing is torn down; things are built upon, grown into, absorbed. The settlement is a living thing in the way a coral reef is a living thing: many small presences, each one adding its small mass, each one dying and becoming substrate for the next.

The drum-beetles keep the rhythm. The fog comes and goes. The moths arrive in their clouds. The scarab-goats stand at the edge and stare outward at the emptiness that surrounds this place, this single flat anvil of ancient stone rising above a world that has worn itself to nothing, this last high place where the dense and breathing mass of life clings to itself in the warm dim air, growing slowly, changing slowly, settling into a form that may be its final form, or may simply be the form it will hold for the next ten thousand years, until even the hardpan begins to yield, and the roots that reach downward finally find whatever they have been reaching for all this time.

---

The sound never stops.

Even at night, even in the deepest dim, the beetles drum, and the wind pushes through the rope-vine curtains with its patient breath, and the moths beat their soft wings against the walls, and somewhere in the settlement a child is crying, or an old woman is chipping stone, or a goat is shifting its weight on a sagging roof, and the sound of it all merges into a single sustained tone that is not harmony and not noise but the sound of a place that is still, after everything, still alive—still here—still holding on to its small high edge of the world.
