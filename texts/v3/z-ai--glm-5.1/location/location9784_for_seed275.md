# The Rootworks

Beneath the surface of what was once a plain, there is a city growing.

Not built. Not assembled. *Growing.*

---

You descend through a gap in the earth — a fissure that wasn't here ten years ago, won't be here in another ten, already knitting closed at its edges with pale fibrous tissue — and the light does not fail. It changes. The sun's grey daylight filters through thirty meters of translucent root-wall, refracted into something like the light of an overcast morning that never progresses toward noon. You can see. You can always see down here. The rootworks produce their own faint luminance at depth, a cool phosphorescence from the cambium, so that the further you descend, the more the light becomes the place's own rather than borrowed from above.

The first thing you notice is the scale. The cavity stretches beyond sight in every direction — not a cavern but a *hollow*, a region-sized absence in the rock, as though something was removed rather than carved. The ceiling is a mat of roots so dense it reads as architecture: buttresses, arches, load-bearing columns, all of it living wood, all of it still growing. The floor is not stone but a compressed humus of older growth, dead tissue returned to substrate, firm enough to walk on, warm underfoot with slow decomposition.

The second thing you notice is the quiet.

---

There should be more sound. There *are* sounds — the creak of wood under tension, the subsonic thrum of sap in vessels wider than your thigh, the tick and settle of structural fibers adjusting to weight — but they compose something closer to the sound of a cathedral at night than a city at work. And work is happening. You can see it across the open floor: the industrial apparatus of the Rootworks, spread generous and sparse across the hollow's breadth.

Pumping stations stand at intervals of two hundred meters, their housings grown from woody stems trained around iron frames. You can see the frames — salvaged, welded, rusting — and you can see the wood engulfing them, bark folding over bolts, roots threading through grating, the plant claiming the machine without quite replacing it. The pumps themselves are mechanical. Diesel, some of them, though the diesel here is pressed from seed-oils and smells vegetal when it burns. Others are electric, fed by the turbine halls you can barely make out in the middle distance where the draft flows strongest.

What the pumps move is not water.

---

The Rootworks run on *sap* — but that word is a convenience, a translation of something that doesn't have a precise equivalent. It is the circulatory fluid of the mass-organism that makes up the city, and it is not water-based. It carries dissolved minerals, fixed nitrogen, carbohydrate compounds, and a family of complex organics that serve functions the surface world has no names for. It is viscous. It is faintly iridescent under the phosphorescent light. It moves through the root-city in vessels the size of sewer pipes, and the pumps keep it moving when the organism's own peristalsis cannot keep pace with industrial demand.

You stand near a main line and watch the sap pulse past a transparent section of trunk — a viewing port, deliberately grown for inspection — and the rhythm is not unlike a heartbeat, though slower, though variable, though responsive to pressures you cannot sense. The fluid is gold-amber and carries a faint warmth. The whole place is cool, surface-cool, cave-cool, but the sap-lines are warm to the touch, and near them the air is tolerable, even comfortable, even after the chill of the descent.

There is no water here. None. The chemistry does not require it. What moisture the air holds comes from transpiration and respiration, and it stays trapped in the hollow, cycling between organism and atmosphere, never leaving, never arriving. You could drink the sap, technically. It would sustain you for a while. It tastes like liquid straw and green copper and something your tongue cannot categorize, a receptor-stimulation that maps to no known flavor.

---

The vegetation is not decorative. It is not present. It is *constitutive*. The streets are root-flattened pathways where the surface growth has been trained and pruned and trampled into something walkable. The buildings are dense thickets, hollowed out, their interiors shaped by selective growth and careful excavation — you can see windows, pale membranes stretched across gaps in the wood like drum-skins, filtering the light further into something interior and gentle. Doors are valves, thick petal-lips that fold aside when pushed and slowly close behind you. Stairs are buttress-roots, grown in spirals, their surfaces scored by generations of feet.

The density is low. This is the thing that surprises visitors from above: the Rootworks is a city, but it is not a *crowded* city. Structures stand with generous space between them — fifty meters, a hundred, two hundred — and the space is not empty but *planted*, low ground-cover spreading between buildings, maintenance paths threading through, work-yards where the root-trainers operate. The hollow is vast and the Rootworks uses only a fraction of it, spreading outward each year by another few hundred meters, pushing the cavity walls further, dissolving rock with root-acids and filling the void with living mass.

You can stand in the middle of a thoroughfare and see the city's edge in the distance where the structured growth gives way to raw substrate, the humus thinning to bare rock, the first pioneer-roots reaching into stone, the *frontier* of the organism's expansion. It looks like a tide line. It looks like a glacier's leading edge, if glaciers were alive and patient and vegetable.

---

The fauna are not yours. They belong to the Rootworks.

Insects first — or the things that occupy the insect niche. They are jointed, chitinous, multilegged, recognizable in function if not in taxonomy. They crawl the bark in lines, tending aphid-analogues that feed on sap and excrete the concentrated sugars the root-city requires. They are the organism's immune system, swarming any breach, any infection, any foreign growth, with a ferocity that is quiet and absolute. You have been warned not to bleed in the Rootworks. The cleaners cannot distinguish between your tissue and an invader's.

Larger things move in the spaces between buildings. You see them at the edge of vision — graceful, many-limbed, moving on the ground and the walls and the ceiling with equal ease. Arboreal hunters, bred or evolved or simply *emerged* from the ecosystem, they keep the grazer populations in check and are in turn harvested by the root-trainers when their numbers grow too large. Their calls are the closest thing to birdsong in the hollow: low, resonant, coming from vibrating membranes on their thoraxes, a sound like someone bowing a single note on a cello, sustained and then fading.

They are not hostile. They are not friendly. They are *present*, the way weather is present, the way gravity is present. They are part of the system.

---

The industry of the Rootworks is the industry of maintenance and expansion. The pumps keep sap moving. The pruning machines — great mechanized arms on rails, cutting heads that spin and clamp and cauterize — keep growth directed along planned vectors. The press-works extract fuel-oil from seed-beds the size of football pitches. The foundries work the salvaged metal that the organism cannot yet replace, forging the iron bones that the wood grows around. The workshops build the machines that tend the machines that tend the plant that is the city.

It is an ecosystem and a factory and a settlement, and the boundaries between these categories are not drawn. The workers live in the buildings they maintain; their waste feeds the substrate; their breath contributes to the atmosphere; their labor is not different in kind from the labor of the insects or the sap-pressure or the slow metabolism of the mass. The Rootworks does not distinguish.

You meet a woman at a pumping station who has lived here for eleven years. Her hands are stained amber to the wrist. She explains the maintenance schedule with the affection of a farmer talking about difficult land. She says *we* when she means the workers. She says *it* when she means the plant. She says *the works* when she means everything together, the whole system, the city-organism-industry, and she does not seem to feel the need to separate these terms.

She is pruning a feeder-root when you leave. The cut is clean. The sap beads and seals. Behind her, the station hums at a frequency you can feel in your sternum.

---

The Rootworks is not finished. This is the overwhelming impression: that you are walking through something in the process of becoming. The hollow expands. The growth presses outward. New buildings are not constructed but *germinated*, seeded in the substrate, fed concentrated sap until they reach habitable size in two or three years, then hollowed and shaped and occupied. The infrastructure extends in tendrils ahead of the residential density, pumping stations standing alone in raw rock, power lines threaded through bare branches, service before settlement, the body's circulation reaching before the tissue fills in.

At the deep edge, where the hollow opens into chambers still being excavated, you can watch the process in real time. The pioneer-roots — thick as your arm, pale as bone — push into the stone with a slow pressure that produces a sound like grinding teeth. The rock spalls. The roots thicken. Secondary roots fill the cracks. Tertiary roots follow, fine as hair, secreting acid that eats the stone to sand. The sand mixes with organic matter. The substrate forms. Ground-cover spreads. And behind it all, the sap-lines extend, the pumps thicken, the infrastructure grows, the city follows its own vanguard outward into the dark.

It will take a century to fill this hollow. By then, another will be opening. The Rootworks has been growing for three hundred years. There is no plan to stop.

---

Standing at the center of the thoroughfare, equidistant from the residential thickets and the frontier growth, you can feel the place breathe. Not metaphor. The hollow's atmosphere cycles with the organism's respiration — slow, tidal, taking hours, the air pressure rising and falling by fractions, the draft moving one direction and then the other through the turbine halls. You breathe with it. Everyone does, unconsciously, after the first day.

The light is the same as it was an hour ago. The same as it will be in an hour. The cool phosphorescence of the cambium, the filtered grey from above, the amber glow of sap-lines. It does not change. There are no seasons here, only the slow expansion, the slow growth, the slow industry of a city that is also a plant that is also a machine that is also a home.

It is quiet. It is working. It is alive.
