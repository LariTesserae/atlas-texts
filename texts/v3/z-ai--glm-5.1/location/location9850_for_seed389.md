# The City of Slow Extrusion

It begins with heat. Not the heat of a sun—though there is sun, ordinary and mid-range, casting shadows that behave themselves—but heat from below. From within. The ground here is warm to the touch, and in the deep crevices between structures, it approaches uncomfortable. You learn not to rest your palm flat on any surface for too long.

---

The city stands at a height that makes the horizon feel incorrect. Too far away. Too much sky between you and the edge of the world. The air thins as you ascend through the district layers, and by the upper construction tiers, breathing takes deliberate effort—not dangerous, just noticeable, each inhalation a small contract you sign with your body. The elevation is extreme enough that the city exists in a state of permanent mild vertigo. You adjust. You don't stop adjusting.

Below and around: a ridge system that has not yet decided what it wants to be. The geology is raw. Immature. Basalt flows still dark and unweathered, their surfaces glassy and sharp-edged, catching light in ways that read as wet even when they aren't. Fumarole channels cut through the rock in branching patterns, some still issuing vapor, others dormant. The land has not been smoothed by time. It looks carved—recently, impatiently—as if something dragged a massive tool across the crust and left the shavings where they fell. In the lower valleys between ridges, you can see where erosion has begun its work: a rounding of edges here, a settled talus slope there. But the work is young. Decades, perhaps. Not centuries. The mountains are still becoming mountains.

---

And into this emerging rock, the city has been set.

Not built on. *Set into.* The distinction matters.

The construction is dense—oppressively so in the lower tiers, where structures lean against each other like conversation partners who have forgotten their personal space. Walls share walls. Foundations interlock. The city grows upward more than outward, and from a distance, the central mass reads as a single improbable shape, a mineral growth that happens to contain rooms. Only when you enter do you understand the multiplicity of it: the hundreds of thousands of individual chambers, corridors, workspaces, machine-halls, all pressed together and connected by stairwells and lifts and passages that were clearly added after the fact, cut through existing walls with a pragmatism that suggests nobody owned the walls in the first place. Or everyone did.

The building material is local. Of course it is. The volcanic glass, the fresh basalt, the heat-altered stone—it's all pulled from the ridge system and shaped into blocks and beams and plates. But the shaping is rough. Industrial without being finished. You see tool marks on load-bearing columns. You see gaps between fitted stones that have been filled with a dark, rubbery compound that flexes slightly when you press it. The compound is warm. It is always warm.

---

The city produces. That is its function. That may be its only function.

The factories are integrated into the residential layers—there is no separate industrial district, no zoning principle that separates the furnace-rooms from the sleeping-quarters. You walk down a corridor and the wall on your left is the outer hull of a dehydration plant, thrumming at a frequency just below hearing, and the wall on your right is the shared partition of a habitation unit, and from behind it you hear nothing because the occupants are elsewhere or quiet or have learned to live inside the thrum.

The machinery runs on geothermal steam. The city sits above a vast reservoir of heated water, pressurized and ancient, and the wells tap into it at intervals throughout the structure. Steam rises through pipe-networks in the walls. It turns turbines. It drives presses and looms and extrusion-molds. It condenses in the upper passages and runs down channels cut into the corridor floors, and this is where the damp comes from—not rain, not not exactly, though rain does happen, brief and warm and mineral-tasting, falling from clouds that seem embarrassed by their own lack of commitment. The real moisture is internal. The city sweats.

Condensation beads on the ceilings of the lower corridors. It gathers in the joints between stone blocks. It runs in thin, persistent streams down the inside walls of the deep machine-halls, where the steam pipes are thickest and the heat meets the cooler stone and the water surrenders its warmth. The air tastes wet and metallic. You are always aware of water here—not as a presence, not as a body you can see, but as a process. Water moving through phases. Water working.

---

There are no plants. This is the detail that visitors find most unsettling, though they cannot always name it at first. The absence is total. No vines exploiting the moisture on the corridor walls. No moss in the cracks. No stubborn grasses in the courtyards where the rain collects. The stone is bare. The rubbery compound in the joints is bare. The damp surfaces grow nothing but a faint mineral bloom—white, crystalline, probably calcium or something adjacent—that appears overnight and is scrubbed away by the maintenance crews and appears again. This is not an ecosystem. This is a machine that happens to be made of stone and steam.

Or almost. There is the *other* growth.

In the deep passages, where the heat is strongest and the condensation runs heaviest, a substance accumulates on the walls. It is not fungal—tested, confirmed, categorically not fungal—but it behaves in a way that suggests metabolism. It is orange-brown. It is warm to the touch, warmer than the surrounding stone. It grows in branching patterns that follow the steam pipes, and when the pipes are moved, it dies back and re-establishes along the new routes within days. The workers call it *routing* or *the routing* and they do not disturb it. It does not appear to harm the stone. It does not appear to do anything at all except grow and die and grow again according to the positions of the hot pipes. When you ask what it is, you receive shrugs. When you ask if it is alive, you receive a longer silence than the question seems to warrant, and then another shrug.

---

The fauna are rare and strange and not discussed often. Occasionally, in the upper tiers near the open air, you will see something moving at the edge of your vision—something small and dark and quick, legged, possibly jointed, possibly not. It will be gone before you focus. The workers leave small dishes of mineral-water in the corners of the upper corridors. When you ask about this, they say it keeps *them* content. When you ask who, they look at you as if the answer is obvious and also unimportant.

In the machine-halls, there are larger presences. Not animals—mechanisms. But mechanisms that have been running for so long, maintained by so many hands following procedures that were written by people who are now dead, that their original purpose has become unclear. There is a press in Sublevel 7 that stamps out metal plates of a specific and unusual shape. Nobody knows what the plates are for. They are stored in a warehouse that is now three-quarters full. The press continues to operate. It is supervised. The supervisor's manual describes the maintenance schedule but not the function. When asked, the supervisor—a patient woman with heat-reddened cheeks—says: "It makes the plates. That is what it does."

This is not an unreasonable answer, here.

---

The city is quiet. This is the most difficult thing to convey, because the city is also full of machines and full of people and full of steam moving through pipes at pressure. But the sound is—submerged. Muffled. The stone absorbs noise. The density of the construction traps it. The machines operate at low frequencies that you feel more than hear, vibrations in the floor and the walls and the breastbone. When people speak, they speak softly, not out of reverence but out of habit; the sound carries unpredictably, and nobody wants to be the voice that intrudes. The dominant acoustic is a low, continuous, almost sub-audible hum, and beneath it, the drip and rush of condensed water in the channels, and beneath that, silence—or something so close to silence that the distinction is theological.

At night (and there is night here, the ordinary sun descending behind the ridge-system, the ordinary darkness rising), the quiet deepens. The machines do not stop. The steam does not stop. But the human sounds withdraw, and what remains is the sound of the city breathing—inhaling through its pipe-networks, exhaling through its vents—and the mineral tick of cooling stone, and the faint, almost imaginary sound of the routing growing, cell by cell, millimeter by millimeter, following the heat.

---

The city changes slowly. This is perhaps the strangest thing about it, stranger than the routing or the unclear machines or the things in the upper corridors. The city changes at the speed of geology. New corridors are being cut—not built, *cut*, carved out of the existing stone mass—and the work proceeds at a pace that suggests no urgency whatsoever. A corridor might take two years to complete. A new machine-hall might take five. The construction crews work with patience that borders on ritual, measuring and cutting and fitting, and when you ask about the schedule, they mention dates that seem impossibly far away, dates that assume everyone involved will still be alive and still be here, and they say this without complaint, as if time were a resource that can be spent slowly precisely because there is so much of it.

The ridge-system below is still rising. Still becoming. The city is rising with it, or being risen—lifted by the same forces that push the basalt up through the crust. The foundations shift, by millimeters per year, and the flexible compound in the joints accommodates the movement, and the workers monitor the stress-points and make adjustments, and the city remains standing, and will remain standing, probably, for a duration that exceeds any useful definition of "probably."

Nothing here is temporary. Nothing here is in a hurry. The heat rises. The steam turns the turbines. The water condenses and runs through the channels and falls back down to the reservoir to be heated again. The routing grows. The plates accumulate. The corridors extend, slowly, into stone that has not yet finished being stone.

And above it all, the sky—ordinary, mid-lit, unremarkable—presides over a place that does not require its attention, and receives none.
