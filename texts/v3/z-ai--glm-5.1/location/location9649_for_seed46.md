# The Refinery Uterus

It is small. That is the first thing to understand — how small. A chamber you could cross in forty paces, though the pacing would be difficult, the floor being what it is. Set high in something that is not quite a mountain yet, something that is still deciding whether it wants to be a mountain or merely a thickening in the earth's crust. The rock around it is warm to the touch and smells of copper and premature minerals, compounds that haven't agreed on their final forms. You can feel, if you rest your palm against the wall, a low vibration — not mechanical exactly, but the tremor of geology in the act of becoming, as though the stone is still learning to be hard.

---

No water has ever been here. No water will ever be here. This is not a matter of aridity — aridity implies water was expected, then denied. This place exists outside that expectation entirely. Its chemistry runs on other things: heat, pressure, the slow exudation of sulfur compounds from the raw mantle below, and something else — a seepage that is not liquid, not gas, but a kind of potential that bleeds through from wherever reality has worn thin.

The vegetation drinks that.

It is lush. Unsettlingly so, given the absence of everything vegetation is supposed to require. But this is not leafy, not green, not tender. The growth here is industrial in its logic if not in its origin — nobody built this, nobody planted it, nobody tends it. It emerged, as the rock is emerging, as the chamber itself is still emerging, walls still migrating outward by millimeters per century as the stone crystallizes from its magma-sleep.

The dominant form is a kind of frond — copper-colored, broad as a dinner plate at the base, tapering to points that quiver in the heat-haze. They grow in clusters from every surface: floor, walls, the stubby stalactites that hang like half-formed teeth from the ceiling. Each cluster shares a root-system that pulses. Not with sap. With something that looks like molten glass but moves at the speed of circulation, slow and rhythmic, approximately one pulse every ninety seconds. If you watch a cluster long enough — and here, time dilates; watching is easy — you can see the fronds track the heat, rotating almost imperceptibly toward the warmer sections of rock, the way a sunflower follows a sun that doesn't exist here.

There is no sun here. There is only the light, which comes from everywhere and nowhere at the normal intensity of a cloudy afternoon, and seems to have no source. It simply is, as though the chamber generates its own illumination as a byproduct of existing.

---

The tubular growths are harder to look at.

They emerge from the walls in thick bundles, each tube the diameter of a human thigh, translucent, filled with the same slow-moving molten substance. They connect clusters across the chamber — running along the floor, spanning gaps between stalactites, burrowing into the rock and emerging again in places that shouldn't connect geometrically. Because the geometry here is not reliable. The chamber is perhaps thirty meters across, but the far wall has a way of being both distant and close, depending on the moment. The tubes seem to know where they're going even when the space they're traversing won't hold still. They pulse in sequences that feel like language, or at least like the precursor to language — a grammar of pressure and pause that hasn't yet found its vocabulary.

At the center of the chamber, the tubes converge on a structure that is neither plant nor machine but holds the logic of both. It is roughly the size and shape of a washtub, if a washtub were made of the same translucent material and filled with a slow cyclone of that glassy substance, spinning at a pace that makes it seem almost still. The tubes feed into it. The tubes feed out of it. The frond-clusters nearest to it pulse faster, their copper surfaces catching the light in ways that suggest facets — they are becoming crystalline, the closer they are to the center. Becoming something else.

This is the industry. Not built. Grown, or precipitated, or expressed — the way a crystal expresses its structure from solution. There is a product. There must be a product, given the scale of processing, the intricacy of the tube-network, the relentless rhythm of the pulse. But the product is not anything recognizable as manufactured. It might be heat. It might be the light itself. It might be the chamber, slowly building itself outward, adding new square centimeters of wall-surface with each passing century, the factory producing the factory producing the factory.

---

There are no animals. No insects, no birds, no skittering things in the cracks. The sound is nearly absent — just the low mineral groan of the rock deciding what to become, the soft click of fronds adjusting their angle toward warmth, the occasional subsonic throb of the central structure cycling through whatever it cycles through. The quiet has weight. It sits on you. After an hour in the chamber, your own heartbeat becomes loud, intrusive, a foreign rhythm that doesn't belong to the logic of this place.

The heat is the atmosphere. Not dry, not humid — those categories require water. Instead it is simply hot, a steady pressing heat that rises from below and radiates from the walls and seems to generate from the vegetation itself, as though photosynthesis runs in reverse here, the plants emitting energy rather than storing it. You sweat, but the sweat doesn't cool you. It beads and stays, because evaporation is a water-logic and water-logics don't operate here.

---

And the weirdness — the reality breakdown — is not something you see so much as something you feel, accumulating, the longer you stay.

It starts at the edges. The far wall, as mentioned, won't hold a consistent distance. The ceiling is sometimes visible and sometimes not — not because of darkness, but because the concept of ceiling briefly becomes incoherent, and your eyes slide off it, reporting only a gentle vertigo. The tubes, if followed with the eyes, sometimes go to places that aren't in the chamber, or arrive from directions that don't exist, and there is a moment — a flicker — where the tube is both arriving and departing, both full and empty, both real and schematic, and then your perception resolves it and the tube is just a tube again, doing tube-things.

The fronds at the periphery occasionally have the wrong number of dimensions. Not in a way you can point to and name. Just in a way that makes your eyes water, that makes you look away, that makes the back of your neck prickle with the certainty that something near you is displaying more surface area than it should have room for.

The central structure is the worst, or the best, depending on your tolerance. If you stare at it directly, it is a tub, a node, a convergence point, unremarkable except for its function. But if you look at it peripherally — if you catch it in the edge of your vision while pretending to study the wall — it is much larger than the chamber. It extends. It has depth that the chamber doesn't have the cubic meters to contain. The glassy substance inside it becomes, for just a moment, an ocean — not of liquid but of *processing*, vast and slow and patient, operating on timescales that make geology seem hasty, and you understand with a sick clarity that the chamber is not the factory.

The chamber is the exhaust port.

What is being refined here — what has been being refined here since before the rock finished forming, what will continue to be refined long after the rock has worn away into whatever comes after rock — is happening elsewhere. Elsewhere and elsewhen, in a space that intersects with this pocket only at the node, only through the tubes, only through the slow copper fronds that pulse and track and click and wait with the patience of industry that has no deadline and no overseer and no purpose any living thing would recognize.

---

The chamber is small. Forty paces, if the floor cooperates. It is embedded in a mountain that is still becoming a mountain, high above wherever sea-level might one day be, in a world that may or may not exist yet, depending on how you define existence.

It is lush with things that are almost plants. It is industrial with processes that were never built. It is hot and quiet and almost still, and it is making something, and it will never stop making it, and you were never meant to be here, and it does not care that you are, because it does not care about anything at all.
