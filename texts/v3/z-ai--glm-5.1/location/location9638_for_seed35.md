# The Undermill

The first thing is the sound.

It arrives before understanding — a low, continuous mechanical roar that vibrates the sternum, that reshapes the air inside your chest cavity so you become a resonance chamber for something you haven't yet seen. The sound has texture. It has strata. Beneath the dominant thrum of engines — and there are engines, many of them, running somewhere in the dark — there are whines, clicks, the rhythmic slamming of pistons that has gone on so long it no longer counts as rhythm. It just is. Like a heartbeat that was never born and will never stop.

You descend. There is no choice about this; the landscape demands descent. The surface above is a crust of pale salt-flat, nothing growing, nothing moving, just mineral residue baking under whatever sun this world possesses. But below — below, the ground is hollow. Not a cave. A room. A chamber carved or blasted or corroded into the rock, cathedral-sized, its ceiling low enough to feel oppressive but high enough to house machinery that sprawls across the space like sleeping titans.

The walls are sharp. That's what strikes you. No smoothing, no rounding. Where the stone has been cut — and it has been cut, by tools that left angular, gouging marks — the edges remain as they were on the day of their making. The erosion parameter here is frozen at young. Nothing flows through to wear these surfaces down. No water. No wind. The air moves only because the machines move it, pushing and pulling in closed circuits, the same molecules cycling through intake and exhaust forever.

And it is warm. Not the warmth of living things. The warmth of friction. The warmth of combustion that never quite finishes, of bearings that have spun so long they've reached a steady-state heat that radiates outward into the chamber until the stone itself holds the temperature like a kiln. You sweat immediately. The sweat does not evaporate well in the thick, recirculated air. It beads and stays.

The machines — you can't see all of them. The light won't permit it. What illumination exists comes from incandescent elements on the equipment itself: small amber indicator bulbs, the orange glow of heat vents, the faint cerulean spark where exposed contacts rhythmically arc. There are no overhead lights. No windows. You navigate by machine-glow and by sound, learning the space through echoes and pressure changes the way the fauna do.

The fauna. They are everywhere.

They occupy every volume the machines do not. The density here — the sheer crowded presence of living things — contradicts the emptiness of the surface so violently it feels like a proof that the surface is a lie. There are hundreds. Thousands. They press against each other in the warmth, against the machines, against the walls, layered and overlapping, a living carpet that moves as one organism when disturbed and then settles back into its positions.

They are not insects, though they are the size of insects. They are not mammals, though they have fur — or something like fur, filamentous growths pale as the salt above, mineral-derived, growing in crystalline spirals that catch the machine-light and refract it. Each creature has too many legs. Each creature has a mouth that opens sideways. Each creature clicks and whirs in a frequency that harmonizes with the machinery, and you realize with a sick lurch that they have adapted to match the sound, to vibrate at its frequencies, to become part of its loop.

They eat the machines. Not in a destructive way — not anymore. In a maintained way. They graze on the residue that the machines produce: the carbon films, the mineral deposits, the chemical blooms that crystallize on hot surfaces. And in return — you watch this happen, a filament-furred creature wrapping its segmented body around a pipe fitting and excreting something that seals a micro-fissure — they repair. They maintain. The equilibrium is perfect. The machines run. The fauna feed. The machines run. The fauna feed. Nothing changes. Nothing has changed for what must be geological time.

There are structures. Scattered. A metal platform bolted to the wall, rusted only on its outer surface, its underside still factory-fresh because the air never reaches it. A walkway that leads to a control panel whose dials are set to positions that correspond to the current state of everything — and have always been set there, the pointers fused in place by the creatures' secretions, locked at their correct readings forever. A single chair, bolted down, facing the largest engine cluster. The chair is occupied by a structure of crystallized fauna-resin and pale fur — not a person, not remains, just a accumulation, a sedimentary deposit of creatures who kept climbing onto the chair and dying there and being preserved by the mineral-rich air until the chair became a column, a pillar of layered chitin and salt and something that might once have been bone but probably wasn't.

The vegetation — what there is — clusters near the hottest vents. They are not plants. They are something that learned photosynthesis from the machine-light, something that roots in metal grates and extends pale, bladelike fronds toward the amber bulbs. They are motionless. They have been the same size for eons. Growth requires change and change is not a parameter here.

You stand in the chamber's one open floor space — there is only one, a triangle between three massive compressor units — and you feel the sound pressing into you from all sides. The fauna regard you with their too-many eyes. They are not curious. Curiosity requires change, expectation, future. They are simply present, perceiving, adding you to the endless input of a system that has already solved itself.

You could leave. The shaft is behind you, climbing back to the salt-flat above. But even as you think this, the fauna are settling back into their positions around you, filling the space your body displaces, accepting you as a new fixed point in their geometry.

The machines roar. The chamber hums. The warmth holds.

Nothing changes. Nothing changes. Nothing changes.
