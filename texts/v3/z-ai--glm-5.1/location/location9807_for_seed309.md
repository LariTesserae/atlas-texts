# The Rootworks

The first thing is the sound. It arrives before understanding does—a low, continuous mechanical thrum that rises and falls like breath, layered with the white noise of ten thousand moist bodies shifting against ten thousand surfaces. From somewhere deeper in, a rhythmic clanking repeats at intervals too regular to be natural, too arrhythmic to be machined. Beneath it all, the creak of living wood under slow, constant strain.

You descend. The stairs are root. Not root covered in something else, not root transformed—the steps are the buttressed root of something enormous, their surfaces worn smooth by generations of foot traffic and the patient polishing of moisture. The rail is a vine that has learned to be rigid, its bark compressed into something like ironwood, its curves following the ergonomics of grip. It pulses faintly under your palm, and you realize it is still growing, still adjusting its angle by millimeters per year to better accommodate the hands that hold it.

The Rootworks opens below you like the inside of a throat.

---

The space is cathedral-scale but not cathedral-shaped. It is more like the negative space left when something massive was removed from the earth—or more accurately, like the positive space created when something filled it. The walls are root-mass. The ceiling is root-mass. The floor, where visible, is a composite of compressed organic matter and the ancient remains of industrial substrate—concrete that has long since been colonized, broken, and eaten from within, its aggregate now serving as mineral supplement to the root systems that replaced its structural function.

The scale is difficult to parse because the space is not singular. It is a chamber, roughly, perhaps forty meters at its longest axis, but the walls are deep. What appears to be a surface is merely the outer skin of the root-mass, and it extends back meters, decades, perhaps centuries of growth, layering inward. Alcoves and passages branch off, some large enough to walk into, some too small for anything but the creatures that use them. The space is sparse in the way that a forest is sparse—the emptiness is structural. The central chamber has room to move, room to breathe, but you are never more than arm's reach from something alive.

The light is industrial and insufficient. Electrified lines run through the root-mass in channels that may have been carved or may have been grown—the distinction stopped mattering long ago. Bulbs encased in resin nodules (sap, you realize, hardened sap) cast a warm amber glow that reaches perhaps fifteen meters before being swallowed by the humidity. The air is thick. Not fog, not mist—density. The warmth holds the moisture, and the moisture holds the light, and the light holds your attention because it is all that stands between you and the total dark.

---

The damp is everywhere but not constant. It accumulates. Condensation forms on every surface—the root-walls weep, the machinery sweats, the air itself feels heavy with water that has not yet decided whether to fall or remain suspended. When it does fall, it is irregular: a droplet landing on your shoulder, then silence, then three quick taps against metal somewhere to your left, then nothing for minutes. The sound of dripping becomes part of the mechanical rhythm, indistinguishable from it, perhaps intentional.

There are pools. Not rivers—rivers imply flow, imply destination. These are collections, low places where water has gathered and been held by root-dams and the stubborn geometry of settled concrete. They are dark and still until something enters or exits them, which happens frequently. The surface tension breaks, reforms, breaks again. You learn to listen for the particular splash of something small leaving the water, because it often precedes the sound of something slightly larger arriving.

The water tastes like minerals and tannin. It stains the concrete orange-brown where it pools. It stains everything, eventually.

---

The vegetation is not present. The vegetation *is* the place. This is the central fact that takes the longest to fully absorb.

The root-mass that forms the walls is part of a single organism—a fig, you are told, though the word means very little down here where no one has seen sky or fruit in living memory. The parent tree, if it still exists, is somewhere above, somewhere outside, perhaps a kilometer up through soil and stone and the forgotten layers of the surface. What exists here is the deep root system, the seeking tendrils that found a cavity and filled it over centuries, growing inward when outward was impossible, thickening and hardening and learning to bear loads that roots were never meant to bear.

The root learned to be architecture. It learned to be load-bearing. It grew buttresses where stress patterns demanded them. It thickened floors where traffic was heaviest. It opened passages where air flowed and sealed them where it didn't. It is still doing this. The Rootworks is not a building; it is a process.

And within the root, embedded like fossils in amber, are the machines.

---

The industry here is old but not dead. The engines—there are three that still function—run on a fuel that is produced within the root-mass itself. Sap, fermented and compressed in chambers that the root grew specifically for this purpose, fed through lines that are part vine, part pipe, part something in between. The engines generate electricity. The electricity runs the lights. The lights allow the workers to maintain the engines. The cycle is ancient and closed.

The factory function is less clear now. There are lathes and presses arranged along the eastern wall, their operational surfaces colonized by moss and lichen but not irreparably so. There are channels in the floor, carved by water or by intent, that once carried away some product or byproduct—coolant, lubricant, waste—to the pools. There are assembly stations where the root has grown through and around the frames, incorporating the metal into its structure, making the furniture part of its body. The machines could be made functional again. Perhaps they still are, occasionally, when the need arises. But the need for what they once produced has been subsumed by the need to maintain what they have become.

The industrial rhythm is the heartbeat of the Rootworks. Even when the machines are idle, they tick as they contract and expand with the temperature. Even when the electricity is diverted, the current hums through the root-walls at a frequency you feel more than hear, a vibration in your teeth and the base of your skull.

---

The fauna is the overwhelming fact. You were warned, but the warning was inadequate.

They come in layers.

The first layer is the insects—the crawling, the scuttling, the multitudinous. Beetles with carapaces the color of the root-walls, moving in lines that suggest purpose and organization. Millipedes as long as your hand, their legs producing a sound like dry rustling that is never absent. Flies that cluster around the light sources, their bodies forming a constant halo of tiny shadows. Gnats that breathe the condensation, that live and die in the moisture cycle, that form clouds dense enough to catch the amber light and transform it into something like fog.

The second layer is the arachnids and their kin. Spiders have colonized every junction of root and machine, their webs spanning gaps that the light doesn't reach, their presence indicated by the tiny reflections of multiple eyes when you pass too close. Centipedes hunt along the root-surfaces, their movements too fast to track, their venom potent enough that you have been told to check your boots every morning.

The third layer is the vertebrates, and they are the reason for the noise.

Bats—no, not bats, something that occupies the bat's niche but is not quite right. They roost in the high root-ceiling by the hundreds, their bodies producing heat that contributes to the ambient warmth, their droppings feeding the substrate that feeds the root that feeds the cycle. When they move, they move together, a single organism of leather wings and high shrieks that you cannot hear but feel as pressure against your eardrums. They navigate by sound, and the sound they make is the sound of the Rootworks' voice.

There are rodents, or rodent-analogues. They chew. They chew constantly—root-mass, machine insulation, the organic coating on electrical lines, each other. The chewing is a background texture, a grinding that sits beneath the mechanical hum and the dripping and the chittering and the engine-throb. You stop noticing it after an hour, and that is dangerous, because the things that chew the insulation sometimes chew things that should not be chewed, and the result is sparks, and the result of sparks in an enclosed space made of living wood is—well. That is why the fire watch exists.

There are larger things. You hear them more than see them—fluid movements in the deeper passages, the displacement of air when something substantial passes through a corridor adjacent to yours. The root has openings, and the openings go somewhere, and the things that live in the somewheres come here to drink from the pools and to eat the things that eat the things that eat the detritus. The food chain is long and complex and completely invisible, operating in the spaces between your attention.

The fauna is not hostile, you are told. It is simply present. It fills every niche, occupies every cubic centimeter that can support life, and many that seem like they shouldn't. You are walking through a living room in the most literal sense—every surface is occupied, every volume contains something that breathes, and the air you share with them is processed, filtered, and returned by their bodies and the root's respiration in a cycle so tight that you are, in a very real sense, breathing the exhalations of a thousand small lungs.

---

The strangeness accumulates.

You notice that the root-surfaces near the machines have developed nodes—growth patterns that mirror the shapes of gears, of pistons, of the components they abut. It could be coincidence. It could be the root simply growing into available space. But when the number-three engine cycles into its compression stroke, the nearby root-node compresses as well, and when it releases, the node opens. The timing is too precise. The fit is too exact.

You notice that the insect lines follow paths that correspond to the hidden channels in the root-mass—channels you were told carry the fermented sap to the engines. The insects are too small to carry meaningful quantities, but they travel in formation, and they travel toward the engines, and their bodies are coated in something that glistens like fuel.

You notice that the bat-things avoid certain passages, and that the things in the deeper corridors never enter those same passages, and that the workers—when you can find them, when they emerge from wherever they go between shifts—use only those passages and no others. There is a geography here that is not written down, that is not marked, that is communicated through something other than language.

You notice that the lights do more than illuminate. They pulse—slowly, almost imperceptibly, at a frequency that matches the root's growth cycle. The light is feeding something. The light is signaling something. The light is a word in a conversation between the electricity and the organism that has been going on for longer than anyone has been counting.

---

The Rootworks is a room that is also a machine that is also a creature. It is industrial in the way that a stomach is industrial—processing, converting, excreting, maintaining itself through constant operation. It is urban in the way that a body is urban—organized into districts of function, traversed by circulatory routes, governed by systems that respond to feedback. It is alive in ways that make the boundary between "ecology" and "economy" meaningless.

You stand in the central chamber. The engine throbs. The water drips. The insects move. The root grows. The fauna breathes. The lights pulse. The sound is immense and constant and particular—specific to this place, this depth, this intersection of living and making.

You were told the Rootworks was built. You were told it was discovered. You were told it was grown. All three stories are true. None of them are sufficient.

Somewhere in the root-mass, a node opens. A passage closes. A line of beetles arrives at an engine bearing the fuel the engine needs to produce the light the beetles need to navigate to the fuel. The cycle continues. The sound continues. The place continues, doing what it has done for longer than anyone remembers: being itself, in every direction, at once.
