# The Howling Plateau

The first thing is the sound.

It arrives before anything else—not as a gradual increase but as a fact, already complete, already filling your skull. Wind. Wind that has nowhere to go but through, wind that has been crossing this expanse since before the mountains it erased had names. It screams across the plateau with the voice of a thing that has learned it will never be stopped, and it does not stop. It will not stop. You understand this within the first minute. The sound is not weather. It is geology made audible.

---

The plateau sits at a height that should not exist—two and a half kilometers, perhaps three, of elevation that wears the face of flatness. This is the trick of its age. What were once mountains here—the first mountains, if the erosion tells the truth—have been worn to a nub so gradual that the land appears flat from any single vantage point. Only by walking for days do you perceive the gentle undulations: long swells and dimples across hundreds of kilometers, the ghost-remembered shapes of peaks that spent two billion years being reduced to this. The stone underfoot is so old it has become something else—not rock anymore but a dense, mineralized almost-glass, wind-polished to a sheen that catches the sun and throws it back in fractured planes. The locals, if you can call them that, have a word for this stone that translates roughly to *the-body-that-remained*.

It is warm. That is the second surprise, after the sound. The plateau bakes under normal daylight, and the stone holds the heat with the tenacity of something that has had eons to learn how. The warmth rises from below as much as it descends from above—something volcanic, deep and patient, keeps the underlayer from ever fully cooling. You walk across the-body-that-remained and feel it through your soles like standing on a creature that is only mostly asleep.

---

The water comes and goes. There is no predicting it. Thin clouds scud across the sky at speeds that make you dizzy to watch, and sometimes they open and sometimes they do not. When they do, the rain falls straight down for perhaps ten minutes—warm, mineral-heavy, smelling of iron and something almost sweet—then it stops and the stone drinks what it can and the wind takes the rest. In the deeper dimples, the ones too wide to be called depressions and too shallow to be called valleys, the water pools and lingers for hours or days. These are the only features on the plateau with names. *The Wound Where Water Remembers*. *The Throat*. *The Place That Swallows Rain and Gives Back Nothing*. The names are longer in the original tongue.

Condensation, too. Every dawn, when the warm stone meets the cold air that descends in the night, a film of moisture appears across everything—and then the wind takes it, or the stone absorbs it, or the vegetation drinks it in the minutes before the sun burns it away. This is enough. This has been enough for a very long time.

---

The vegetation is the third thing you notice, or perhaps the first thing you truly *see*, because it is impossible to ignore once your eyes adjust to the scale. The plateau is empty—devastatingly, spiritually empty—across most of its extent. Just stone and wind and warmth and that relentless sound. But in the lee of every slight rise, in every dimple that holds water a little longer, in every fracture where the-body-that-remained has cracked just wide enough for root-space, the plants gather.

They do not grow individually. They *erupt* in masses—tangled, rampant, impossibly lush stands of green and darker-green that huddle against the wind like congregations of the desperate. The dominant form is something between a fern and a vine, though it is neither: thick-stemmed, broad-leaved, growing in mounded hummocks that can reach four meters high and thirty across, each hummock a single organism that has been growing and dying back and growing again for perhaps thousands of years. The leaves are dark and waxy, shaped like hands with too many fingers, and when the wind hits them they clap and flutter and add a lower register to the howl—a percussive thrumming that you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears.

Between the hummocks, smaller plants fill every gap. Mosses that are almost black. Fungi that glow faintly in the dusk—not bioluminescent exactly, but somehow storing and releasing the day's light in a slow, pale exhale. Creeping vines with leaves that fold and unfold in the wind like hands opening and closing, opening and closing. The lushness is obscene in such emptiness, a refusal to be reasonable, and it clusters in these isolated pockets with kilometers of bare stone between each oasis of green.

The hummocks move. Not quickly—you would not see it if you watched for an hour—but they move. They migrate slowly across the plateau, millimeters per year, seeking the dimples that hold water, retreating from the fractures where the wind channels with particular viciousness. Over centuries, the vegetation has learned to walk. You find their tracks in the stone: long, faint trails of discoloration where the roots have gripped and released, gripped and released, a slow continent-wide drift toward the places where survival is a little less impossible.

---

The sound never stops. You must understand this. The howl is constant, and it changes—sometimes a roar, sometimes a shriek, sometimes a low bass moan that vibrates your teeth—and the vegetation adds to it, and the stone itself seems to ring at certain frequencies when the wind hits the right angle across the right formation. There are places on the plateau where standing still produces a standing wave in your chest, where the sound becomes so physical that you can almost see it, almost hold it. The scattered people who live here do not speak in the way you might expect. They sign, mostly, or they mouth words with their hands over each other's ears. When they must speak aloud, they do it in the shelter of the hummocks, pressed against the warm vegetation, and even then they shout.

Children born here develop slightly different ear anatomy over generations—the canal narrows, the drum thickens. Protection. The body learns what the mind cannot: that the sound is not a circumstance but a condition of existence, as fundamental as gravity, as ancient as the stone.

---

What fauna exists is small and hidden and none of it stays above the stone for long. Burrowers—furred, warm, quick—that live in the root-systems of the hummocks and emerge only at the precise moment between the dusk-quiet and the night-wind. Insects that live inside the stems of the vegetation, their lives conducted entirely within the green. Nothing large. Nothing that the wind could catch. The one exception is a creature the scattered people call *the-sound-that-hunts*: a lean, low, four-legged predator that runs the open stone between hummocks at speeds that seem impossible, chasing nothing visible, feeding on the burrowers when it can catch them leaving their holes. It has no external ears. Its eyes are recessed beneath bony ridges. It runs straight into the wind, and no one has ever seen one sleep.

You see its tracks sometimes in the thin soil that accumulates in the dimples. Claw-marks, deep and deliberate. A path that never curves.

---

The built structures are few and each one is a kind of miracle. They cluster at the intersection of three dimples—a confluence where the water stays longest and the hummocks grow largest and the wind, for reasons of topography, is merely very loud rather than deafening. Stone circles, waist-high, made from the-body-that-remained fitted together without mortar. Wind-shelters—half-dome structures open to the east, where the wind rarely comes from, roofed with the tougher stems of dead hummocks. Cairns, marking paths between the water-dimples, visible only because they are slightly lighter than the surrounding stone. There is no metal here. There are no tools beyond hand-axes of flaked obsidian, no textiles beyond what can be woven from the fibrous inner bark of the hummock-vines. The people who built these things did so with their hands and their patience and nothing else, and they continue to do so, and the wind takes ten percent of what they build every year, and they build it again.

You find their artifacts sometimes, out on the open stone. A grinding stone, worn thin from use. A necklace of small animal bones. A child's toy—a figure made from twisted vine, humanoid in shape but with too many fingers on each hand, like the leaves.

---

The turbulence is the hardest thing to describe because it is not visible, exactly, until you have been here long enough. The wind changes direction without warning. The rain falls from clear skies and stops under clouds. The hummocks migrate. The vegetation in each green pocket grows and dies back in cycles that have no correspondence to any season—you might see a hummock in full leaf, then return two weeks later to find it skeletal and dormant, then return two weeks after that to find it bursting again, as if the plants are responding to some calendar that operates on a timescale you cannot perceive. The stone itself shifts—cracks open and close with the thermal cycling, and sometimes a dimple that held water for decades simply drains overnight through a new fissure, and the hummock that drank from it begins its slow migration toward the next nearest water, and the-sound-that-hunts follows the hummock, and the people follow the water.

Everything is moving. Everything has always been moving. The plateau looks static from any single vantage, but this is the lie of human perception. Stand still for a month and you will begin to notice: the cairns need constant repair. The wind-shelters lose a stone every few days. The water-dimples shift by centimeters per year as the stone settles and cracks. The green pockets expand and contract like breathing. The entire plateau is in motion at a speed just below the threshold of attention, and it has been in motion since before anything alive today existed, and it will continue in motion after.

This is what the erosion means. Not that the place is finished. That the place has *nearly finished* becoming what it has always been becoming. The plateau is not the end of the mountains. It is the mountains, in their final and most patient form.

---

At night, if you shelter in a wind-shelter and press your ear against the stone, you can hear something below the wind. A low, constant hum. The volcanic warmth, perhaps. Or the sound of the plateau settling further into itself, another millimeter per century. Or something else entirely—something that was here before the mountains, before the water, before the wind learned to howl. Something that the erosion has not yet reached because it is too deep or too old or too fundamental to be worn away.

The scattered people do not talk about this sound. When asked, they touch their throats and shake their heads, and their hands make a gesture that might mean *we do not speak of what the stone says* or might mean *we cannot* or might mean *we are not the ones who listen*.

The wind continues. The hummocks drift. The water pools and vanishes. The plateau endures, which is the only thing it has ever done, which is the only thing it knows how to do, which is—when you have been here long enough—the only thing that matters.
