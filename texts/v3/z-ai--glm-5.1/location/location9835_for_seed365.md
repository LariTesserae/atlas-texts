#The Terracing

The first thing you notice is the quiet. Not absence — presence. A quiet made of ten thousand small absorptions.

---

You come to it by switchback, up through younger rock still showing its fracture-planes, faces too sharp to have known wind for long. The path is more suggestion than route. Someone has placed stones where the footing is uncertain, but the stones are recent, unweathered, still showing the pale bellies of their breaks.

The terrace sits at the top. It is not large. You could walk its perimeter in the time it takes to say a full prayer — a courtyard, a cluster of buildings, a bounded place hemmed by the drop and the cliff face behind. Someone chose this shelf. They came with hand-tools — copper, maybe, or just good stone — and they made walls from the mountain's own rubble. Dry-fit. Patient. The joints are tight but not perfect. Moss has found the imperfections and made them its architecture.

Everything here is damp. Not wet — there is no standing water, no stream — but the air arrives saturated. It condenses on the cool stone in the morning hours, beads along the roof-edges, darkens the mortar joints. You can press your palm to any wall and feel the mountain's cold sweat. By midday the damp lifts into a fog that makes the far wall indistinct, and by evening it settles again. It is enough. It is enough for everything that grows here.

---

The vegetation is obscene in its vitality and restrained in its placement. Great cushions of moss — thick as mattresses, deep green shading to black in the recesses — colonize the walls in discrete patches, each colony separated from its neighbors by bare stone. Ferns erupt from the mortarseams, fronds as long as your arm, each plant an isolated fountain. There is no meadow, no continuous carpet. Each growth is an island. Between them: pale granite, the fresh pink of new fracture, the orange streaks of mineral deposits not yet leached by rain. The plants have claimed their territories and hold them with absolute tenacity, but they have not merged. Something in the substrate prevents it, or something in the nature of the plants themselves requires distance.

And from each moss-cushion, from each fern-fountain, from each crack where a single liverwort has established its tiny civilization: movement.

---

The fauna are the reason for the quiet.

They are everywhere.

You stand still in the courtyard and count: on the nearest wall-face alone, forty-seven. They are the size of your thumb, pale, segmented — not quite caterpillars, not quite centipedes, not quite anything you have a name for. They move with a deliberation that seems ceremonial. Each one follows the grain of the stone or the contour of a moss-colony's edge, tracing and retracing paths that must hold meaning you cannot read. They make no sound. Their many feet — and they have many, too many, arranged in ways that trouble the eye — grip and release in sequences that produce nothing audible.

On the ground, larger ones. Palm-sized. Flatter. They move between the vegetation-islands like parishioners between shrines. Their backs are the same grey-green as the stone, and when they stop — which they do often, and for long stretches — they vanish. You only notice them when they resume.

You look up. On the roof-beams of the nearest building, more. Clinging. Swaying slightly. Not feeding, not nesting. Just being present.

There are thousands of them. Tens of thousands. The terrace is a metropolis, and they are its citizens, and their activity is constant and silent and absolutely opaque in its purpose.

---

The buildings — there are seven — are small, single-roomed, doorless. The doorways are roughly the shape of a crouching human. Inside, the same moss-islands on the walls, the same occupants. Whoever built these structures did not need privacy, or did not conceive of it, or needed something else more. The walls are thick enough to hold heat, but there is no hearth. No soot. The roof is thatch — local grass, you realize, the same species that grows in sparse tough clumps along the terrace's edge — and it is in good repair, though you see no one who might have repaired it.

You touch the interior wall. It is very cold. The condensation is thicker inside, pooling in the uneven floor-stones, reflecting the dimness.

In the corner, three of the pale creatures have arranged themselves in a triangle. They have been still for as long as you have watched. You have the unreasonable conviction they are waiting for a fourth.

---

The dynamic of the place reveals itself slowly, in the way a clock's workings reveal themselves — not through any single motion but through the accumulation of small regular events.

The mist arrives. It thickens. On the wall-faces, the creatures stir — not into frenzy but into what seems like a coordinated shift. Those near the tops of the walls move downward. Those near the moss-colonies move onto them. When the condensation reaches its peak, every creature you can see has positioned itself against or upon the vegetation. They are drinking. Or absorbing. Or something adjacent to both that your vocabulary cannot reach.

Then, as the mist thins, they disperse again. Resume their tracings. Their ceremonies. Their long stillnesses.

A fern uncurls a new frond. You watch it happen, which means it is happening faster than any fern you have known. The creatures have visited it — their many feet have bruised the tender tissue, and the frond grows in a slightly altered direction, curling around the places where they walked. It is not damage. It is negotiation. The fern and the fauna are in conversation, and the fern is listening.

---

There are no birds. No insects with wings. No calls, no chirrups, no clicks. The only sounds: your breath, your footsteps (muffled on the damp stone), and occasionally — very occasionally — a soft wet sound as one of the larger creatures adjusts its grip on the rock.

The silence is not empty. It is full. It is the silence of a congregation in meditation, of a courtroom in recess, of a crowd that has agreed — by what consensus you cannot imagine — to withhold its voice.

You become aware, standing in this small high place with its damp stone and its patient walls and its thousand thousand silent occupants, that you are being tolerated. The creatures have not fled from you. They have not approached you. You are a feature of the landscape they have not yet categorized, and they are waiting to see what you will do before they decide what you are.

---

The sun, when it briefly appears, is low and white. The angle of the surrounding peaks will never allow direct light here — only this reflected glow, this secondhand illumination that makes every shadow soft-edged and deep. In this light, the creatures' paleness is more pronounced. They glow faintly, as if they have stored the mist inside themselves and are now translucent with it.

The terrace is a room. The buildings are rooms within the room. The moss-colonies are rooms within the rooms within the room. And the creatures — they move between all of these, citizens of every chamber, connecting what the architecture seems designed to separate.

You could stay. You could sit with your back against the wall and let the condensation soak through your clothes and watch the slow choreography until it made sense, until you saw the pattern, until you became part of the pattern. Someone must have done this. Someone must have built these walls and then sat inside them and learned the shape of the quiet.

But that is not why you climbed.

You step carefully back toward the path, placing your feet where the stones have been set for you, aware that you are leaving tracks in the damp, aware that the creatures will find those tracks by morning, aware that they will trace them with the same deliberation they trace everything, and that by the time they have finished, they will know more about you than you know about them.

The terrace watches you go.

It does not say goodbye.
