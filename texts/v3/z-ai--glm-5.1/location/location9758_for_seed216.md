# The Vegiform

---

It is high. That is the first thing. The air thins into something that barely holds you, and what it does hold is wet. Not rain — the sky is too far away to matter here. The water comes from below, from within, from the walls and floors and the thick breathing mass that is every surface. Condensation beads on condensation. The air drinks itself.

You are somewhere that was built. There is no natural substrate left — no stone, no soil uncovered, no bare mineral face. Everything you touch is made. And everything that is made is plant.

---

The architecture is old. Not old in the way of ruins — it has never been allowed to ruin. It has been maintained across spans that make the word *maintenance* absurd, spans where the maintainers became maintained, where the boundary between gardener and garden dissolved like sugar in the constant drip. The walls are living wood, grown from cuttings taken from cuttings taken from the first shaping, and those first shapings are so far back that even the wood doesn't remember them. Its grain tells stories in spirals that don't resolve. You follow a line with your finger and it turns back on itself, becomes another line, becomes the edge of your fingerprint.

The floors are root-mat, dense as any flagstone, warm from the processes beneath. Walk and you feel the slight give, the resonance of liquid moving through vascular tissue at scales too large to sense directly but which you feel as a hum in your shins. Beneath the floor, roots braid with roots braid with roots, and in the spaces between, water moves. Always water. It percolates. It weeps from joints where one living wall meets another, forming constant threads of downflow that have carved channels in the wood over centuries — no, the wood has *grown* channels, adapted to the water's preference. The building learned where it wanted to leak and agreed.

---

The scale is difficult. You stand in a corridor — if corridor is the word, and it isn't — and the ceiling is lost above you, not because it is so far but because the light fails before it reaches that height. The walls recede on either side into a dimness that might be distance or might be the wood simply darkening with age. You walk for an hour and the passage does not change. You walk for a day and it does not change. The same living walls. The same seeping joints. The same faint luminescence from the lichen that coats everything in patches, a gray-green glow like the memory of moonlight.

The Vegiform is continental. It was always continental, or it grew to become so — the distinction lost to the same temporal dissolution that made the maintainers into the maintained. From above, if above existed in any meaningful way, it would look like a forest canopy stretched across a plateau the size of a small sea. But there is no forest. There is only the one thing. Interlocking. Self-referential. A single organism in the legal sense, if organisms had lawyers, and here they might.

---

The plateau lifts the Vegiform above the cloud floor, which is where the dimness comes from. Below — far below, kilometers below — clouds mass permanently, a white ocean that traps and scatters what light the sun offers. What reaches the Vegiform is attenuated, blue-gray, diffuse. It is always approximately twilight. The circadian rhythm of the place has divorced itself from astronomy; the plants follow water-cycles instead, opening and closing with the slow pulse of sap that rises and falls on schedules set by temperature gradients and the mysterious tides of the deep root-mass. It is always approximately twilight, and it is always approximately warm, and it is always approximately wet. The approximations are the closest thing to weather.

You learn to read the walls. When the lichen brightens, water is coming — the condensation will thicken, the seeping will quicken, the air will swell until it is almost drinking. When the lichen dims, a dry pulse, relatively speaking; the walls will feel almost firm, the floor almost solid, the air merely humid instead of saturated. These cycles take days. Weeks. There is no hurry here. Hurry requires a deadline and deadlines require a future distinct from the present and in the Vegiform the present extends in all directions like root-hairs.

---

Fauna moves through the corridors. That is not the right word. The passages. The interstices. The gaps where the Vegiform did not grow solid, or where it grew and then retreated, leaving spaces that it maintains as if by contract.

The creatures are numerous but dispersed across the vastness, so that you may walk for hours before you see one, and then three in a moment, as if they also travel in the thin spaces between the Vegiform's attention. They are built from the same logic as the architecture. They are not separate from it. The beetles — if beetles they are, and the word is a kindness — have shells that mimic the bark-grain so perfectly that you only see them when they move. The larger grazers, quiet as steam, have legs that branch and rebranch like rootlets, finding purchase in the living floor, and their mouths are adapted to crop the lichen without damaging the substrate beneath. They excrete and the excretion is absorbed. There is no waste. There has not been waste for longer than the Vegiform has been keeping track, and the Vegiform does not keep track.

There are things that move through the deeper passages that you hear only as pressure-changes, displacements of the damp air, and you do not follow them. You do not follow them because following them would mean going further down, and further down the architecture becomes less coherent. Not damaged — nothing here is damaged — but older. The grain of the walls spirals tighter. The luminescence fades. The water moves with more purpose, and you begin to feel that you are in a throat.

---

Sound does not travel far. The living walls absorb it. Your footsteps are eaten. Your voice, if you speak, reaches perhaps ten meters before the wood takes it, and what returns is not echo but a soft creaking, a settling, as if the Vegiform is adjusting its posture in response to your vibration. The loudest sounds are the quiet ones: the endless drip, the creak of slow growth, the subsonic pulse of sap in the major vessels that you feel in your chest more than hear. When the wind moves across the upper canopy — the Vegiform's roof, its skin, its interface with the sky — it produces a sound like breathing, which of course it is.

The fauna are similarly quiet. The grazers move in near-silence, their branching feet distributing weight across the root-mat floor. The beetles communicate through the substrate, tapping the wood with modified mandibles in patterns too rapid and too specific for you to parse. Sometimes you feel the tapping through your boots, up your shins, and for a moment the floor seems to be thinking.

---

Here is where the reality bends.

You walk a corridor you have walked before. You are certain — the lichen patch on the left like a continent, the weeping joint ahead, the place where the floor-root splits into three ridges. You walk it again the next day and it is the same. The next day, the same. But on the fourth day the lichen patch is larger, or you are closer to it, or the wall has grown toward you. The weeping joint weeps from a different angle. The ridges in the floor are four.

The Vegiform is not changing. You are changing within it, or your perception is, or the Vegiform is *slightly* different each time in ways that only become visible through repetition, like a face aging in a time-lapse. You cannot trust memory here because memory requires a stable substrate and the substrate is alive and growing and it grows differently in the presence of the observer. The observers — you, the grazers, the beetles — are variables the Vegiform incorporates. It shapes around you. You leave grooves.

In the deep places, where the walls narrow and the grain spirals, this effect intensifies. You think of turning left and the passage turns left. You think of the way you came and there it is behind you, except it wasn't before. The architecture responds to intention, or what it interprets as intention, and its interpretations are old and slow and not quite aligned with human thought. It means well. You think it means well. It has kept you warm and wet and alive, which is what it was built to do, ages ago, by hands that used stone tools and patience and a relationship with growing things that your language does not have words for.

---

Who built it? The question persists like the damp. The Vegiform does not tell. The grain does not record in any way you can read. The tools that shaped the first cuttings — you have found them, embedded in the walls, grown around and incorporated: stone blades, wooden wedges, fiber ropes petrified into the root-mass like fossils. Primitive. Patient. The builders had no metal, no engine, no power but time and the careful insistence of living things trained to grow in particular ways. Generation after generation of pruners and grafters, people who understood that the fastest way to build a wall is to ask a plant to become one and then wait.

And they waited. And the plants became. And the Vegiform grew, and the builders grew into it, and the distinction between the shapers and the shaped became the thinnest of membranes, and then it became nothing, and now there is only the growing, which is also the built, which is also the living, which is also the keeping.

---

You find a chamber — or it finds you, which is the same thing here — where the walls open into a space that feels vast. The lichen on the ceiling is bright enough to see by, truly see, and what you see is: water. A pool, or a lens, or an eye. The floor dips into a bowl of living wood, and the bowl holds water that has collected from the constant weeping, and the water is still and reflects the lichen-light and in it you see the Vegiform from above, from below, from within. You see its corridors as veins. You see its chambers as organs. You see its canopy as skin. You see the grazers as blood cells. You see the beetles as nerve impulses. You see yourself as a thought the Vegiform is having, a fleeting pattern in a vast and patient mind that does not think in the way you understand thinking but which nevertheless maintains itself, grows itself, adapts itself, and has been doing so for a span that makes your presence a blink.

You sit by the water. The air is wet. The walls breathe. Something taps in the floor, rhythmic, questioning.

You are being asked to stay.

You are being asked to stay the way a room asks you to stay — by being warm, by being dry enough, by being there. The Vegiform does not compel. It merely persists, and in persisting, makes persistence seem like the only reasonable response. You could leave. You could retrace your steps, or try — the passages shift, but they shift toward you, not away, and if you insisted, you would find the way out. The Vegiform was built to shelter. It would let you go.

But the lichen is bright, and the water is cool, and the walls are warm, and the silence is not silence but the sound of something alive being alive at a pace too slow to hear, and you have been walking for so long.

You stay.

The floor adjusts beneath you, slightly, a root-ridge moving to support your weight more comfortably. The wall behind you thickens, insulating. The dripping ahead quickens, filling the pool, raising the water level by millimeters, making the reflection clearer.

The Vegiform has been doing this for a very long time.

It is good at it.
