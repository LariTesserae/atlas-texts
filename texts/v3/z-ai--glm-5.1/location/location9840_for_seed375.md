# The Churden

It is not a ruin. Ruins imply something that was once whole. This place — this system, for it moves too much to call a structure — has never been finished. It is still becoming.

You enter through a gap that was not there yesterday. The churden grows its apertures overnight: soft-edged openings in walls that harden by morning into permanent thresholds. No one made this. No hand shaped the chambers or laid the corridors. The walls grew — mineral extrusions from a substrate you cannot find, rising in faceted columns that branch and reconnect overhead, forming a low canopy of interlocking planes. The effect is architectural. Unintentionally so. You walk beneath vaulted intersections that recall nave ceilings, and you think: *someone built this*, but no. The churden only builds itself.

The scale is intimate. The tallest columns reach perhaps four meters before forking. The corridors are wide enough for two people to walk abreast, though no two people are here. The whole system — as far as anyone has mapped — spans the area of a modest hamlet, bounded on all sides by a low ridge of the same mineral, as if the growth exhausted its perimeter and hardened there, containing itself.

---

There is almost nothing inside.

This is the first thing you notice after the architecture: the emptiness. The corridors connect chamber to chamber, and each chamber holds perhaps one thing — a single formation, a growth, a presence — and then meters of bare floor before the next. The density of construction mocks the density of contents. You can walk for minutes past the same pale flooring, the same facet-walls, seeing nothing.

The flooring itself: a composite, layered like shales but warmer, almost lukewarm to bare feet. It is not stone. It is not organic matter. It splits in places along lines that look intentional, like tiling, and in other places it curves smoothly, like cooled wax. There is moisture trapped deep in the composite — you can smell it, a mineral dampness, but it never reaches the surface. The churden is a sealed system. Nothing evaporates from it. Nothing pools.

---

The light comes from the walls.

Not from all of them, and not consistently. Certain facets — you learn to recognize them by their angle, a tilt between twenty and thirty degrees from vertical — catch something and hold it. There is no visible light source in the churden. Above the canopy, if the canopy could be penetrated, there would be sky — you assume — but the canopy cannot be penetrated. The facets seem to absorb ambient energy from outside and re-emit it here, inside, at a wavelength that keeps the corridors at a perpetual dusk. You can see. Barely. Colors are suggestions. The walls are the color of sand mixed with ash. The floor is slightly darker. The rare growths are slightly lighter. That is the full palette.

You do not need a light source. You cannot make the place brighter. You learn to read the dimness.

---

The vegetation is the architecture.

This is the understanding that takes longest. The columns, the walls, the forking canopy — they are not mineral formations that *support* plant life. They *are* the plant life. Or rather, they occupy a category that has no clear boundary between vegetable and mineral. The churden photosynthesizes. You are certain of this, though you cannot find chlorophyll or chloroplasts or any analog you recognize. The facets that hold light — they are the leaves. Spread across every surface, flattened into crystalline efficiency, they catch what exists of illumination and convert it into the slow labor of growing more churden.

The sparse growths you find in chambers — the upright things you first mistook for separate organisms — are not separate. They are buds. Shoots. Nodes where the churden is trying a new form, a new direction. Some stand as columns that will eventually join the ceiling. Some spread as shelves that will become walls. Some are failures: small, hard, dark nubs that stopped growing and will not grow again, sitting alone in their chambers like artifacts in empty rooms.

You do not touch them. Not because you fear contamination. Because they look like they are thinking.

---

The fauna: there is one kind.

You will not see it for some time. You will hear it first — no, you will *feel* it, a faint vibration in the floor, rhythmic, like a heartbeat transmitted through stone. Then, in a corridor ahead or behind, a shape passes. It moves low. It has legs — you count six, then eight, then stop counting — and a body that conforms to the corridor's width, pressing against both walls as it moves. It does not navigate the churden. It *inhabits* it, the way blood inhabits a vessel.

It is not large. Perhaps the size of a dog, long and flat and segmented. Its carapace is the same color as the floor. You realize, after the third or fourth sighting, that it is eating the churden. Not the living growth — the failures. The dark nubs. It moves from chamber to chamber, consuming what the churden has abandoned, digesting it into something you never see the output of. A cleaner. A recycling system. Perhaps the only animal here, doing the only job here that needs doing.

It does not approach you. It does not avoid you. You are irrelevant to it, and it is almost irrelevant to the churden — except that without it, the failure-nubs would accumulate, and the chambers would fill with the dead attempts of a system that cannot stop trying.

---

The silence is not total. But it is close.

The churden creaks. The composite flooring shifts slightly as temperature differentials pass through it — cool air from somewhere, warmer air from somewhere else — and the facets adjust their angles by fractions of millimeters to track whatever light they harvest. You hear this: a low, irregular series of clicks and settles, like a house that is barely settling, like wood that is barely expanding. It is the sound of the place continuing to be built.

The fauna-thing makes no sound you can hear. Your own footsteps are muffled by the flooring's slight give. Your breath sounds enormous, and you learn to breathe quietly, not out of fear but out of courtesy. The churden is working. You are walking through its workshop.

---

The processes are visible over time.

You notice first in a chamber you passed through on the first day: a shelf-growth that was flush with the wall is now projecting outward by a centimeter. A new bud has appeared in an alcove that was empty. A corridor has narrowed slightly — you check your notes, confirm the measurement, yes, the walls have grown inward by four centimeters on each side. The churden is expanding inward, filling its own spaces, growing denser.

You realize: this is why the corridors are wide. They were wider once. The empty chambers were fuller once, or will be fuller eventually. The churden oscillates between growing outward and filling in, and you have arrived during a filling-in phase. The architecture you walk through is the architecture of this moment. In a year, it will be different. In a decade, it may be impassable, or it may have opened new chambers, new routes.

You are in a room that is slowly becoming a smaller room.

---

And the strangeness: the strangeness is the recognition.

Not déjà vu exactly. Something more specific. The churden's accidental architecture — its nave-vaults, its corridor-grid, its chamber-and-hall arrangement, its threshold-entrances — you have seen this before. Not in a place. In a *practice*. In the way humans build when they build on purpose: the logic of circulation, the separation of passage from dwelling, the sizing of spaces to fit human bodies.

The churden does not know you exist. It grew before you arrived. It will grow after you leave. And yet it built spaces that fit you. The corridors are your width. The ceilings are your height. The chambers hold exactly what a person could use — one bed, one table, one shrine's worth of space.

You are the only thing here that the architecture serves. And the architecture was not made for you.

This is the thought that stays.

---

You leave through the same gap. By the time you return to the ridge, the gap has begun to harden at its edges. In a week, it will be too small. In a month, it will be a wall again.

The churden did not open the gap for you. But it opened a gap, and you were the right size to use it, and now you are the right size to leave, and the timing is such that you cannot help but feel — against all evidence, against all reason — that a place with no mind, no tool, no intention, no technology, no audience, built itself into the shape of something that could receive you.

The churden does not know what it is.

Neither, perhaps, does anything that grows.
