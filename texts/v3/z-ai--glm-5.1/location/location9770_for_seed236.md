# The Grown Ward

The mesa lifted three seasons ago. Maybe four. The rock still remembers the pressure of what lay above it—its surface is grooved, fluted, almost tooled, as though something vast and patient dragged itself across the stone and left furrows deep enough to hide in. The edges of the plateau haven't softened yet. They're severe. Broken. You can cut your hand on the rim where the older strata sheared away and left the inner geology exposed: dark bands of something like basalt, paler veins of a mineral no one has named because no one here names things that way.

---

There is no water here. This is the first thing to understand, and the hardest, because the place insists otherwise.

The growth is *loud* with green. Not the pale, conservative green of desert survivors—the deep, saturated green of things that have never wanted, that have never been taught caution. The colony sprawls across the mesa's crown in a dense tumble, maybe forty meters across at its widest, and every surface is occupied. Layered. Stacked. Fronds overlap shelves overlap creeping stems overlap the papery husks of earlier growth, and beneath all of that, the older structures—call them walls, call them chambers, call them the bodies of what came before—form a dense substrate, a living foundation that the current residents simply grew into and through.

The plants drink something else. Not from the sky. Not from any pool or vein you can find. The roots—pale, thick, almost warm to the touch—descend through cracks in the basalt and don't stop at the stone. They go *into* it. Follow them far enough and you'd find them threading through mineral seams, wrapped around crystalline structures, dissolving and absorbing something that has nothing to do with rain or rivers. The vegetation here runs on geology. On heat. On whatever the young rock still holds from its formation, still cooking slowly in the warmth that rises from below.

The mesa is warm. Not hot, not uncomfortable—warm the way a body is warm. The stone holds temperature the way a living thing would, and if you press your ear to it, you can almost hear something like circulation. Almost. The sound is too low, too slow. More of a presence than a sound. A hum at the threshold of noticing.

---

The ward—let's call it that, though no one built it and no one maintains it—stands at the mesa's center. Dense. Urban. A settlement-scale structure made entirely of growth.

It began, presumably, with a single colonist. A spore, a seed, a fragment. It found the warmth, found the mineral wealth, and it *proliferated*. The first chambers were simple: bulbous, the size of a head, the size of a torso. Shelter. Then the next generation grew from the walls of the first, adding corridors, adding depth, adding second stories that sag under their own weight but never collapse because they're still alive, still adding fiber, still thickening. The architecture is accidental in the way that coral is accidental—each addition responds to what came before, builds on it, cannibalizes it where necessary. Older chambers are subsumed. New ones bud from every surface.

The result is dense. Dense the way a medieval quarter is dense, the way a forest floor is dense—everything touching everything else, no gaps, no resting places, just the continuous slow negotiation of bodies in proximity. You could walk through the ward, if you moved carefully, if you didn't mind the walls yielding slightly against your shoulders, if you didn't mind the warmth and the closeness and the sense that the space around you was *responding* to your passage.

But nothing here walks quickly.

---

The fauna arrived on their own. They always do.

They came up the mesa's flanks in the weeks after the lifting—small things, armored things, things with too many legs and things with none. They found the colony and they moved in. Not as parasites. Something closer to tenants. Something closer to *organs*.

The pale arthropods—translucent, the size of a thumb, moving in lines of twelve or fifteen—tend the inner passages. They eat what the walls discard. The walls seem to produce waste specifically for them, papery flakes of old growth that the creatures harvest and carry into chambers no one has mapped. A circulation system. Digestion. Something being moved and processed.

The larger things—the warm, dark, low-bodied creatures that look like someone's guess at a reptile crossed with someone else's guess at a mollusk—they move through the outer corridors with a kind of patient certainty. They know where they're going. The walls know they're coming. Openings dilate. Passages widen. The ward accommodates.

Everything is slow here. The warmth encourages it. The density requires it. You cannot rush through the colony without breaking things, and the things you would break are alive, and they would grow back, but the pace of regrowth is glacial. Days. Weeks. The fauna have learned to move at the speed of the architecture. They rest in the chambers. They wait for the passages to open. They sleep in the warm dark while the walls breathe around them, and when a corridor finally yields, they move forward a few meters and sleep again.

There is no urgency. The mesa is not going anywhere. The warmth is constant. The mineral seep is steady. There is nothing to chase and nothing to flee.

---

Sound does not travel well here. The growth absorbs it. The density of wall and creature and frond and fiber creates a medium that swallows noise, that dampens vibration, that leaves even the loudest events muffled and private. The colony operates at the volume of a body at rest. You hear your own breathing, your own heartbeat, and beyond that—nothing. Or almost nothing. The deep slow pulse of the rock. The creak of a wall thickening by a millimeter. The faint, faint click of arthropod mandibles processing waste in a chamber two walls away.

If you stand in the ward's central chamber—a space maybe four meters across, maybe five, its ceiling a lattice of interlocking growth that filters the warm normal light into a green-gray glow—you can feel the place around you. Not metaphorically. Physically. The warmth. The slight moisture of living air. The pressure of so many surfaces in such a small volume, all of them alive, all of them engaged in their slow processes, indifferent to you but not unaware of you.

You are not food. You are not a threat. You are a temperature change. A slight disturbance in the air's composition. The colony adjusts around you the way it adjusts around everything—it grows to accommodate, it seals what isn't needed, it opens what is.

---

This is a young place. That matters.

In a century, the mesa's edges will soften. The sharp geometry of the uplift will round. The colony will have consumed every available surface and begun to cannibalize itself more aggressively, building up rather than out, growing stranger and denser and more complex. The fauna will have speciated. The chambers will have deepened into something like a city, something like an organism, something like a tomb for the original colonist whose body now forms the lowest foundations.

But that hasn't happened yet. Right now, the ward is still becoming. Still figuring out what it is. The walls are thin. The passages are uneven. The architecture hasn't settled. It's a town under construction, if the construction were biological and the builders were patient and the blueprint were being written by the weather and the stone and the slow warm chemistry of a world that doesn't need water to be alive.

Stand here. Be still. The colony doesn't care about you.

But it will grow around you if you stay long enough.
