# The Conurbation

The first thing is the sound. Not a sound — *the* sound. A continent of noise that has been accumulating for so long it has become geological, a stratum of vibration you feel in the minerals of your teeth before your ears even parse it. The Conurbation does not have an acoustic environment so much as it has an acoustic *state*, and that state is roar.

It comes from everywhere. From the metabolic processes of the built infrastructure — a trillion small mechanisms cycling, pumping, exhalting steam and intake-breathing atmosphere through ducts the diameter of old rivers. From the fauna, whose combined voices at any given instant outnumber the human population of any city that has ever existed on Earth by several orders of magnitude. From the water, which is always moving — condensing on surfaces, dripping through layered conduits, being pumped and filtered and vented and re-condensed in a hydrological cycle so thoroughly engineered it has become indistinguishable from weather. From the structure of the city itself, which expands and contracts and settles and shifts with a warm, low, tectonic groan that never ceases.

You cannot find quiet here. You can find different kinds of noise — the high thin whistle of vapor escaping from a vent stack on the 40th level of Sector Twelve, the bass thrum of a transit node processing six hundred thousand organisms per minute, the wet clicking sound that accompanies the morning emergence of the cobalt weavers from their dormitory-galls — but silence is a concept that does not have a referent. It belongs to a different physics.

---

The land is low. This is important. The Conurbation was not built on a plateau or a rise. It spreads across what was, some vast time ago, a shallow basin — a continental depression, a wide soft bowl in the planetary crust where water naturally gathered and the ground was already nearly at sea level before the first structures were sunk. The elevation has not increased since. If anything, the weight of the city has pressed the basin slightly deeper, a slow lithospheric nod under the accumulated mass of built matter. There are no hills here. There are heights — the stacked levels, the towers, the ventilation spires, the breeding-stacks — but they rise from a base that is nearly level with the water table, and that nearness defines everything.

Water is always close. Not above you, usually — the drainage systems are extraordinary, a second circulatory network layered beneath and beside and through the first, pulling water down and away and through and back in cycles so complex that the engineers who maintain them describe their behavior in the language of meteorology rather than plumbing — but close. You feel it in the air. The atmosphere here is dense with moisture, warm and close and heavy. It condenses on every surface. Stand still long enough and you will feel it beading on your skin, on the fabric of whatever you are wearing, on the curved hull of whatever structure you are leaning against. The city weeps. Every surface runs with a thin film of condensation, and in the lower levels — the old levels, the ones that were built first and then built over and then built over again until they became geological rather than architectural — the water is not a film but a presence, an inch of standing warmth that never fully evaporates because the air above it is already saturated and the ambient temperature stays steady at the high end of what skin perceives as comfortable.

It is always warm. Not hot — the systems regulate that, or the thermal mass of so much matter in such a small volume regulates it, or both — but warm, consistently, uniformly, inescapably. The warmth comes from below and from within. From the processes that keep the Conurbation alive, the chemical and mechanical and something-close-to-biological transactions that occur in every wall and floor and conduit and reservoir. The city is exothermic. It radiates. And the water in the air holds that warmth, wraps it around you, refuses to let it dissipate into whatever cold might exist beyond the atmospheric boundary layer that the Conurbation has, over millennia, built for itself like a shell.

---

There are no plants.

This takes a while to fully register, because the Conurbation is not bare. It is the opposite of bare — it is *packed*, saturated, every surface occupied, every volume filled, every cubic meter of the basin and the air above it given over to some function or structure or process or organism. But none of that filling is vegetable. Nothing here photosynthesizes. Nothing here grows from soil and sunlight in the way that green things grow. The infrastructure does not use plant materials. The fauna do not eat plants. There is no chlorophyll anywhere in the visible spectrum of the city.

What there is, instead, is mineral architecture and animal life in such abundance that the two have become difficult to distinguish from each other at certain scales.

Consider the structures. They are built — organized, engineered, constructed with a logic that is clearly intentional and clearly beyond what current human physics can fully model. The materials are principally mineral: silicate compounds, metallic alloys, forms of calcium and carbon that have been arranged at the molecular level into configurations that give them properties we would call structural if we had words for what they are structuring. Some of these materials are grown. Some are extruded. Some are assembled by processes that appear to involve neither casting nor machining but something else, something that involves the controlled deposition of matter at scales too small to see and too fast to track. The walls are not poured or printed. They are *precipitated*, condensed out of solution the way a crystal forms, but guided, shaped, specified by instructions that are encoded in — well, the engineers call it code, but it is code the way a genome is code, and the structures it produces have the same odd combination of regularity and organic variation.

The Conurbation is old. The erosion parameter says as much: established, settled, in its own state, weathered, in balance. This is not a new city. It has been here long enough for its edges to have worn into themselves, for its systems to have reached equilibrium, for the weathering of its surfaces to have become a predictable process rather than a degrading one. The lowest levels — the ones nearest the water table, the ones that receive no light and only the faintest circulation of air — have the quality of geological strata. They are compacted. They have been compressed by the weight of everything above them into something closer to stone than to architecture. You can read time in them the way you read time in the walls of a canyon. Layer after layer, each one a different material, a different style of construction, a different solution to the same problems of load-bearing and water-management and thermal regulation, all of them now so dense and so settled that they have become a single mass, a bedrock that is not natural but has become indistinguishable from the natural bedrock it replaced.

But within that settled mass, at every level and in every volume that is not structural, there is life.

---

The fauna are abundant. Staggeringly, overwhelmingly abundant. The Conurbation contains more individual organisms per cubic meter than a rainforest canopy, more than a coral reef, more than any ecosystem that has ever existed on Earth at any point in the history of life. And they are not small. Some are small — the filter-feeders that live in the condensation films, the organisms that occupy the interior surfaces of the water pipes, the grain-sized things that swarm in the warm moist air and are visible only as a faint shimmer, a texture in the light — but many are not. Many are the size of your hand, or your arm, or your body. And they are everywhere.

The cobalt weavers are perhaps the most visible, because they are large and they move during the light cycle in organized formations that suggest something between migration and construction. They are roughly the size and shape of a large crab, but their carapaces are a deep iridescent blue and they have eight limbs, not ten, and the front two are elongated and jointed in a way that gives them the reach and dexterity of a mechanic's hands. They build. That is what they do. They build and repair and maintain the structures of the Conurbation, or at least certain classes of those structures — the ones that are still growing, the ones that are not yet compressed into bedrock, the ones that are still in the active phase of their lifecycle. They extrude a silicate compound from glands near their mouths, and they shape it with their tool-limbs, and they work in teams of thousands, and the sound they make when they are working is a grinding, clicking, wet-chemistry hiss that contributes a significant fraction of the city's overall acoustic output.

The weavers are not the only builders. There are at least forty other fauna species that participate in the construction and maintenance of the built environment, each with their own specialization, their own preferred materials, their own routines and schedules and territories. Some work on the water systems. Some work on the thermal-regulation infrastructure. Some work on the ventilation shafts. Some work on the structures that house the other fauna. Some work on the structures that house themselves. The division of labor is not rigid — there is overlap, redundancy, a kind of competitive cooperation that keeps the system adaptable — but it is also not random. There are patterns. There are regularities. Something is coordinating this, or something has coordinated it in the past and the coordination has since become encoded in the biology and the infrastructure and the routines of the organisms themselves.

And then there are the fauna that do not build. The grazers — though they do not graze on plants. They graze on the biofilm that forms on every wet surface, the thin living layer of bacteria-analogs and simpler organisms that metabolize the mineral compounds in the condensation and the trace elements in the air and the waste products of the larger fauna. The grazers are slow and soft-bodied and they move in herds of millions along the wet surfaces of the lower levels, eating and being eaten in turn by the predators that follow them. The predators are fast and segmented and they see in the infrared and they make a sound like tearing silk when they move, and they are the reason you do not sleep unprotected in the open levels.

There are the filter-feeders that live in the water systems, some microscopic, some large enough to fill a room, all of them essential to the process of keeping the water clean enough to be used again. There are the air-column dwellers, the fauna that never touch a surface, that live their entire lives in the warm wet updrafts between the towers, feeding on each other and on the organic particulates that the updrafts carry, mating and giving birth and dying in the air, their bodies drifting down like rain to be processed by the scavengers below. There are the burrowers that live in the old compressed levels, tunneling through what has become stone, mining it for the mineral compounds they need, creating new spaces that are then colonized by other organisms and eventually, over long time, become new rooms, new corridors, new volumes of the city.

The city is an ecosystem. Or rather, the city is *the* ecosystem. There is nothing else. The mineral world and the animal world and the built world and the water world are not separate systems that interact; they are one system, one metabolism, one continuous process of transformation and exchange and regeneration that has been running for so long that the distinction between the organisms and the infrastructure has become, at many scales, meaningless.

---

The light is normal. This is almost the strangest thing about the Conurbation, given everything else. The light that reaches the inhabited levels is daylight — filtered through the atmospheric boundary layer, diffused by the moisture, reduced in intensity by the successive layers of structure that it passes through, but still recognizable as sunlight, still operating on the same diurnal cycle that the planet has always kept. The upper levels are bright. The mid-levels are lit the way a forest floor is lit — in patches, in shafts, in the warm diffuse glow of a sky that is always slightly overcast because the moisture in the air ensures that it always is. The lower levels receive no direct light, but they are not dark; the fauna and the infrastructure both produce bioluminescence and chemiluminescence in a spectrum that is, for the most part, visible to the organisms that need to see, and the overall effect is a kind of perpetual green-blue twilight that is, by now, as normal to the inhabitants as daylight is to you.

But the light, where it falls, illuminates a world that is almost entirely mineral and animal and built, with no green in it, no photosynthesis, no leaves, no stems, no roots. The colors of the Conurbation are the colors of wet stone and carapace and condensation: gray and blue and the deep warm brown of calcium compounds and the iridescent flash of chitin and the white of mineral deposits and the black of shadow and the gleam of water on every surface. It is beautiful, but it is a beauty that takes time to see, because it does not resemble any beauty you have been trained to recognize.

---

The scale is difficult to comprehend. The Conurbation covers most of the basin, and the basin is continental in extent. You cannot walk across it. You cannot fly across it in less than a day, even at speeds that would be impractical for anything other than transit. It has its own weather — not just the constant moisture, but actual weather, pressure systems and thermal currents and precipitation patterns that form and move and dissipate entirely within the atmospheric volume that the city has created for itself. Rain falls in the upper levels and is caught and channeled before it reaches the ground. Fog forms in the mid-levels when the temperature differential between a warm shaft and a cool surface reaches the right threshold. Winds blow through the corridors and the ducts and the open spaces between the towers, and those winds are shaped by the architecture, funneled and directed and accelerated and decelerated by the forms of the built environment, and the architecture is shaped by the winds in turn, because the cobalt weavers and the other builders take wind load into account when they design and repair and extend the structures, and so the city and its weather have co-evolved into a single dynamic system, and neither one can be understood without the other.

The turbulence is constant. Not just the turbulence of wind and water and thermal exchange, but the turbulence of the ecosystem itself, the constant birth and death and building and decay and rebuilding that keeps the Conurbation in a state of perpetual transformation even as its overall form remains, at the largest scales, stable. Things are always changing here. A corridor that was open yesterday is closed today because the weavers have decided — or have been instructed, or have been biologically compelled — to wall it off and route traffic through a new passage. A population of grazers that numbered in the millions last week has been reduced to thousands by a predator bloom and will recover in a month when the predators have exhausted their food supply and declined. A section of the lower levels has been tunnel-riddled by the burrowers and is now in the process of being refilled by a different species that specializes in mineral deposition, and the resulting structure will be different from the one it replaced, stronger in some ways, weaker in others, adapted to whatever pressures the new configuration will face.

The city is never finished. It is never even approximately finished. It is a process, not a product, and the process is one of continuous creation and destruction and recreation at every scale from the molecular to the continental, and the sound of that process is the sound of the Conurbation, and it is deafening, and it does not stop.

---

Stand in the mid-levels at the hour when the light through the upper structures is at its brightest, and look out across the interior of the basin. The towers rise around you like the walls of a canyon, but they are not walls — they are habitable, they are inhabited, they are honeycombed with chambers and corridors and shafts and ducts, and every surface of every interior is wet with condensation and alive with organisms going about their business. The air is thick with moisture and with the movement of the air-column fauna, the tiny darting shapes that catch the light and the larger drifting forms that hang in the updrafts like jellyfish in a warm sea. The grazers move in their millions along the floor of the corridor, their soft bodies glistening, and the predators move among them, and the weavers work on the walls, and the filter-feeders pulse in the water that runs through the pipes behind the walls, and the burrowers dig beneath your feet, and the whole vast structure hums and groans and clicks and hisses and whistles and roars with the combined sound of all of its processes, all of its lives, all of its endless turbulent metabolizing.

This is the Conurbation. It is a city that has become a world. It is a world that has become a city. It is a mineral-animal-water-mechanism that has been running for longer than anyone can remember, and it will keep running for longer than anyone can imagine, and it will never be quiet, and it will never be still, and it will never, ever be empty.
