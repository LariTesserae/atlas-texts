# The Processing Flats

The land has been doing this forever.

---

Somewhere below — not deep, just below, just beneath the mineral crust that forms the ground — water moves. You cannot see it. You can hear it: a continuous low churning, the sound of a locked cycle, fluid passing through chambers that never empty and never fill. The sound never stops. It has never stopped. The water goes down warm, comes up warmer, deposits its minerals, collects new ones, descends again. The system is closed. The system is old.

Above, the flats extend. Low country — the horizon sits high and close, the sky a wide shallow dome over ground that barely rises, barely falls. But it is not flat, not truly. It is full of forms. They are just not the forms that living things make.

Here: a cluster of cones, each three or four meters high, built from deposited silica and iron oxides. Rust-orange at their bases, deepening to near-black at their lips. From each aperture, steam. Not dramatic, not geysing — just a steady exhalation, a warm breath rising into air that is already damp, already heavy. The steam joins the general humidity and is lost. The cones do not grow. They achieved their current shape long ago and have held it since, the rate of deposition exactly matching the rate of erosion from the mineral-laden rain that drifts intermittently across the flats. Equilibrium. The cones are not young. The cones are not old. The cones simply *are*.

The sound here is substantial. Not a single sound but a layered drone — the subterranean churning, the hiss of steam from a hundred vents, the occasional percussive thump of a gas pocket releasing from some mud pot fifty meters distant. It composes a kind of music, if you can call it that. A music with no beginning, no development, no crescendo. A music that is the same at dawn as at dusk, the same in the wet season as the dry. The flats do not have seasons. The flats have a single state that expresses itself perpetually.

---

Walk between the cone cluster and the broad seeps to the east. The ground underfoot is firm but not hard — a crust of precipitated minerals over damp substrate. It crunches slightly. The temperature here is warm, consistently warm, the kind of warmth that lives in the air rather than radiating from any visible source. You are walking over the source. The whole flat is a heat exchanger, and you are walking across its skin.

To the east: the seeps. Broad shallow depressions where water emerges and spreads thin across the mineral pan. The water is clear and hot to the touch — not scalding, not comfortable, somewhere between. It carries a faint sulfurous smell, the only scent in a place that otherwise has no odor. The seeps are the closest thing the flats have to change: water appears, water evaporates, water appears again. But the pool is always the same size, the same depth, the same temperature. The water molecules are different. The water is the same.

No plants grow here. None have ever grown here. The mineral substrate would not support them — too saturated, too hot, too chemically hostile. There is no soil. There is no decay. There is instead a kind of pre-organic sterility, a cleanliness that predates the concept of life. The world before life was like this, perhaps. Or the world after. It is difficult to tell the difference.

---

What lives here lives at the margins of the definition.

In the shallower seeps, where the water has cooled just slightly, a faint haze exists — not quite algae, not quite bacteria, something simpler. Organisms that do not photosynthesize, that feed on chemical gradients, that have never seen the sun and do not need it. They form no visible structures. They leave no traces. They are more process than presence, a faint biological shimmer in water that looks empty. If you dipped a container and held it to the light, you might see a slight turbidity. You might not.

And there are other things. Rarer.

In the early morning, when the condensation sits heaviest on the flats, something moves between the vents. It is not an animal. It is more like a mobile concentration of mineral-laden moisture — a denser pocket of the same damp air, rolling slowly between the cones, pulling itself along thermal gradients. It has no organs. It has no intention. It persists. It moves. It is alive by the broadest definition and dead by any narrower one. It avoids the hottest vents, seems to navigate by the mineral content of the air. If it has a name, it has never been given one. It is not uncommon here, but you will rarely see it. The flats keep their rare things rare.

---

The industrial quality of this place is inescapable, though nothing here was built. The cones suggest smokestacks. The seeps suggest cooling ponds. The mineral crust suggests factory flooring. The continuous sound suggests engines running in locked rooms below. But the only industry here is geological. The earth processes itself. Water dissolves, transports, deposits. Heat drives the cycle. Time is not a factor because time is not passing — not in any meaningful sense. This is a place that arrived at its current configuration and then stopped arriving. It does not erode further. It does not build further. It simply runs, a machine with no product, a factory producing only itself.

Stand in the center of the flats and look outward. The horizon is the same in every direction — low, flat, marked by the silhouettes of vents and the shimmer of rising steam. The light is ordinary. Gray-white sky, diffused sun, no dramatic shadows. Everything is evenly lit, evenly warm, evenly damp. The sound never changes. The steam never stops. The rare things move in their rare ways between the cones, and the mineral water goes down warm and comes up warmer, and the cones do not grow, and the seeps do not spread, and the crust does not thin.

The flats are not waiting for anything. The flats are not becoming anything. They are engaged in a permanent present tense, a process that produces no output, a cycle that goes nowhere.

It is not uncomfortable. It is not pleasant. It simply is, continuously, without variation, without end.
