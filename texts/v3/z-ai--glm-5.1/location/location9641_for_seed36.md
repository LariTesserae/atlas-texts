# The Green Expanse Beneath

The first thing you notice is that the light comes from everywhere and nowhere.

It seeps. It has no source — or rather, it has ten million sources, each one small as a fingernail, and all of them vegetable. The vault overhead is not stone but a living ceiling of interlocked fronds, each broad as a road, overlapping like shingles on a roof that has never known a carpenter. Between the fronds, luminous glands pulse with a steady, amber-white radiance — not flickering, not wavering, just *on*, as they have been, as they will be. The light is warm on the skin. It is not the sun. It has never been the sun.

You are below. Not below in the sense of a cave — caves have walls of rock, floors of stone. Here the walls are root-mass. The floor is root-mass. The pillars that descend from the ceiling and rise from the floor, meeting sometimes in mid-air to form arches that hold nothing up — these too are root, living root, thick as old temples, their surfaces furred with fine pale tendrils that drink the condensation from the air. The entire chamber, which stretches further than you can walk in a day, is inside something alive.

---

The heat is not the heat of a summer afternoon. There is no sun to beat down. This heat rises — from below, from the root-floors, from somewhere deeper still where the earth remembers being molten. It is a slow, even heat, like standing near bread that has finished baking and will never cool. The air is thick with it. Not oppressive — the plants would not allow that — but *present*, the way a hand on your shoulder is present. You feel it in your lungs. Every breath is a breath taken inside a living body.

The dampness is not rain. Rain would require a sky. What happens here is simpler: the heat meets the coolness of the root-walls and water appears. Not running, not pooling in any directed way — just *forming*, bead by bead, on every surface. You see it on the broad fronds overhead, gathering into lines that follow the veins of the leaves, tracing slow calligraphies before releasing and dropping. The drops fall with soft, individual sounds — *tunk, tunk, tunk* — into the root-floor, which absorbs them immediately, like a sponge that has been waiting. There are no puddles. There are no streams. The water appears, travels a short distance, and is gone. The air tastes of it — mineral and green.

---

The vegetation is not *a* vegetation. It is vegetation as a condition of existence.

There is no substrate beneath the plants. The plants *are* the substrate. What looks like ground is a compressed mat of older growth — stems that grew horizontally, leaves that folded and thickened, roots that fused — layered decades deep, perhaps centuries deep, warm and yielding underfoot like walking on the palm of a hand. Nothing rots here. Or rather, things rot, but the rot is immediately taken up by the next layer of growth, so that the floor is always rising, imperceptibly, a millimeter per decade perhaps, the whole space very slowly filling with its own living matter like a throat that will never quite close.

The plants do not have names. Or they do, but only the plants know them. There are the frond-makers, the ceiling-builders, whose leaves overlap and lock and hold the luminous glands. There are the pillar-growers, whose roots descend and ascend with such patience that they have become architecture. There are the mat-formers, the ground-weavers, the ones that fill every gap and seal every seam. There are the thin pale ones — threads, really — that cover every surface like hair, that drink the condensation, that seem to be the circulatory system of the whole. They may all be one plant. They may all be one organism, expressing itself in different forms the way a single body expresses bone and muscle and nerve. There is no way to tell where one root ends and another begins.

And there is no gap. Stand in any direction and look: plant. Turn: plant. Look up: plant. Look down: plant. The only spaces are the spaces between stems and fronds, and even these are filled with the thin threads, filled with light, filled with warm damp air. It is not suffocating — the space is large, the ceiling is high, the pillars create avenues and clearings — but it is *complete*. Nothing is bare. Nothing is waiting to be filled. The space is already full of its own life.

---

The few animals that exist here are barely animals at all.

There are mites — or something like mites — that live in the root-floor, visible only as suggestions of movement, as a slight disturbance in the surface fuzz. They tend the rot. They are the teeth of the floor. Without them, the dead layers would accumulate and choke the system; with them, everything is processed, returned, made available. They are almost too small to see and almost too numerous to count.

There are also, very rarely, pale shapes that move through the upper fronds — something like caterpillars, something like worms, legless and soft, thumb-thick and hand-long, glistening with the same condensation that covers everything. They eat the light-glands. You see one every few hours if you are watching, and it moves with the slowness of a creature that has never needed to hurry, that has no predator and no purpose beyond its own slow metabolism, munching its way across the luminous ceiling, leaving a dark trail of consumed glands behind it. The trail does not last. New glands form. The light is never diminished for long.

That is the fauna. Mites and the slow eaters of light. Nothing else. No birds, no insects, no frogs, no fish. The silence is not absolute — the *tunk* of condensation, the faint creak of root-pillars adjusting to their own growth, the soft sound of the pale caterpillars chewing — but it is close to absolute. The silence of a place where nothing calls and nothing answers.

---

The built structures are a question without an answer.

There are seven — or perhaps nine, depending on what you count — objects in the Expanse that are not plant. They are not stone either, because stone does not exist here, has never been uncovered here. They are made of something that might once have been wood, or might once have been bone, or might be something else entirely that has no name on the surface. They are small. The largest is the height of a child's knee.

One is in the shape of a bowl, or was — it has cracked and the roots have grown through the crack, splitting it further, so that it resembles a flower that has opened past its prime. It sits in a depression in the root-floor, and condensation collects in it, and the pale threads drink from it, so that it has become a kind of local watering hole for the circulation of the space. It may have been placed. It may have fallen. It is impossible to know.

Another is a stick — no, a rod, too straight to be natural — that someone drove into the root-floor long ago. The floor has grown up around it, swallowing its base, and now it stands like a small flagpole with no flag, the top of it just at chest height. It is smooth and gray and warm to the touch, warmer than the surrounding plant-matter, as though something inside it still holds heat. The luminous fronds grow around it but not on it. They leave it a small clearance of darkness, the only shadow in the Expanse.

The others are fragments. Shards. A curve that might have been part of a circle. A flat piece with grooves that might be decoration or might be wear. They are distributed without obvious pattern across the Expanse, some in the open avenues, some half-consumed by the root-floor, some tangled in the threads at the base of pillars. They are old. They are older than the plants can remember, and the plants do not remember anything, they only grow.

Nobody built them. Or somebody did, once, in a time before the plants, in a time before the light-glands evolved, when this space was dark and perhaps empty. But that time is gone. The plants have no history. They have only the present, which is the same as the present before it, which is the same as the present to come.

---

The equilibrium is the strangest thing.

Stand in one place for an hour and nothing changes. Stand for a day and nothing changes. The light does not dim or brighten. The heat does not rise or fall. The condensation forms and falls at the same rate. The pillars do not grow visibly, though they are growing. The fronds do not wilt, do not flourish, do not turn or reach. The pale caterpillars chew at the same speed. The mites process at the same speed. The roots drink at the same speed. Everything is happening, but nothing is changing.

This is not stillness. Stillness is the absence of motion. This is motion so perfectly balanced that it produces no net change — a clock that ticks but never advances, a breath that goes in and out but never tires. The Expanse is alive, vigorously alive, but its life is circular. Growth and decay are the same process. Consumption and production are the same process. The water that falls from the fronds is the same water that the roots draw up, is the same water that condenses on the walls, is the same water that was here before you arrived and will be here after you leave.

Time passes here, but it passes the way water passes through a sieve — leaving nothing behind. There are no seasons. There are no cycles except the great cycle of uptake and release, which has no beginning and no end. The Expanse was not made. It grew. And it is still growing, but it is growing toward nothing, reaching toward nothing, becoming nothing other than what it already is: more of itself, fuller of itself, itself all the way down.

You could live here. The air is breathable. The water is drinkable. The heat is tolerable. There is nothing here that will kill you, and nothing here that will help you, and nothing here that will remember you after you leave. The plants will grow over your footprints in the root-floor. The condensation will wash your scent from the threads. The light-glands will shine as they shone before. The Expanse does not know you are here. The Expanse does not know it is here. It simply is — hot, bright, damp, green, quiet, complete.

A world inside a world, breathing in and breathing out, forever.
