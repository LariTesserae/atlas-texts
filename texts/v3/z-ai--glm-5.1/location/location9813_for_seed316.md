# The Weeping Expanse

It begins with sound.

Not a sound — *sound itself*. A roar so total it has texture, weight, grain. It presses against your chest like a hand. It rises from everywhere and nowhere: from the sodden ground, from the bruised sky, from inside your own skull where the vibrations settle and nest. After enough time here, you stop hearing it the way you stop hearing your own pulse. It becomes the acoustic equivalent of air — something you move through, something that moves through you.

---

The land goes flat. Not the flat of plains or prairies — those still have *gesture*, the memory of uplift. This is the flat of *giving up*. Something here finished its becoming so long ago that even the concept of horizon has worn smooth. The eye stretches across a plane so extensive it curves with the world's own bending, and there is nothing — no rise, no ridge, no cleft — to give the distance any purchase. Scale collapses. You cannot tell if the pale smudge out there is a stone or a hill or a mountain reduced to its final statement.

This is the geometry of deep time. Erosion has done its work and then done it again and then kept working long past the point where there was anything left to erode. The land is not ground so much as the *memory* of ground, a residue that persists because something must. What remains is a surface so ancient it has lapped all the way through geology and arrived at something before it — a pre-language of mineral, a place where the distinction between "land" and "what land used to be" has become meaningless.

And yet: the place is *full*. Packed. There is no emptiness here despite the flatness, despite the spareness of living things. The atmosphere itself takes up the slack. The air is so dense with moisture it almost refuses the name — it is a medium, a suspension, something you swim through more than breathe. At the warmest hours, when the temperature rises to something like the inside of a closed mouth, the air becomes nearly solid with humidity. Water does not fall here so much as *accumulate*, materializing on every surface in beads that swell and merge and run in threads that themselves join and divide in patterns too fine to track. The ground does not absorb it. The ground *is* it — a saturated mat of mineral and moisture so complete that stepping feels like pressing into a sponge that was already pressed before you arrived.

Condensation is constant. You watch it form on your own forearms. You watch it bead along the faint ridges that cross the plain like old scars, thin lines of slightly-harder substrate that the erosion almost-but-not-quite consumed. These ridges weep. They weep the way a wound weeps — slowly, continuously, without urgency or relief. Water emerges from their surfaces as if the stone itself were generating it, as if the mineral had learned to cry and never learned to stop. The water gathers into films that spread and join and eventually find their way to the low places — and everywhere is a low place, so the water simply *spreads*, a sheet of wet so thin it gleams rather than reflects, a skin of moisture that covers everything and never dries.

It is always dim. Not dark — there is a sourceless, directionless luminescence that seems to come from the air itself, from the water suspended in it, a kind of glow that illuminates without casting shadows. You can see, but the seeing is soft-edged, as though light here has grown tired and diffused itself into a gentler, less committed form. Colors exist but they are all the colors of wet things: the grey of wet clay, the umber of wet stone, the pale green of wet air where something almost-alive drifts at the edge of visibility. Nothing is bright. Brightness would require a boundary between lit and unlit, and here, light has been so thoroughly absorbed by the saturated atmosphere that it exists only as a general condition, like warmth, like damp.

---

Vegetation is scarce, and what exists is *wrong*.

Not wrong in the way of poison or thorns — wrong in the way of something that has misunderstood its own instructions. There are growths that might once have been grass but have become something closer to hair, fine and pale and spreading in mats across the wetter ground, slowly pulsing with a rhythm that does not match any wind. There are stalks that rise from the saturated soil in clusters of two or three, featureless and faintly luminescent, that lean toward whatever moves and then remain leaning after it passes, as though they have learned a posture of attention and forgotten how to release it. In the lowest places, where the water gathers into something almost substantial, there are mats of material that hover between plant and mineral — thin, papery sheets of I-don't-know-what that laminate themselves to the ground in overlapping scales, each one slightly warmer than the surface it covers, each one slowly, slowly expanding at a rate you can only perceive by looking away and looking back hours later.

Roots do not hold here. The ground is too saturated, too unsettled, too *indifferent* to the architecture of anchoring. Anything that tries to establish itself deeply finds only more wetness, more of the same yielding medium, and so the vegetation that survives does so by being *shallow* — by spreading rather than reaching, by skimming the surface of existence the way the water skims the surface of the land. Nothing grows tall. The dim air and the constant moisture have negotiated some agreement about limitation, and the plants abide by it, keeping low, keeping thin, keeping almost-invisible.

There are occasional thicker patches — clusters of something stalk-like and dark green that stand in sparse groves where the ridges provide marginally more structure. But even these are sparse, isolated, separated by vast stretches of bare wet ground. They do not communicate. They do not form ecosystems. They are remnants, or they are experiments — it is impossible to say which.

---

And the sound. I need to return to the sound because it is not background here. It is *governance*.

The roar has layers. Beneath the dominant frequency — a deep, ongoing thrum that vibrates in your molars and in the long bones of your legs — there are textures that come and go. A grinding, as of immense surfaces shifting against each other at a rate too slow to see but fast enough to hear. A hissing, like the exhalation of something continental. A clicking, rapid and irregular, that rises from the ground in waves and recedes in waves, as though the earth beneath your feet were speaking in a language of percussion. Sometimes there is what might be singing — a sustained, modulated tone that rises from the wet ground in specific locations and hangs in the dense air for long minutes before fading. It is not birdsong. It is not insect song. It might be the sound of water moving through channels too small and too numerous to map, or it might be something else entirely, something that produces sound the way fire produces heat — as a byproduct of being.

The volume is such that you feel it before you hear it and hear it long after it has passed. Standing still, you become aware that your body is *resonating* — that the sound has found the frequencies of your chest cavity, your sinus passages, the fluid in your inner ear, and is playing them like instruments. After a day, you begin to produce the sound yourself, or believe you do — a humming that might be your own voice or might be the place speaking through you, using your architecture as yet another surface to vibrate against.

---

Fauna is rare, and when it appears, it arrives as an event.

You might walk for days and see nothing that moves under its own power. Then, in the dim warmth of a long flat morning, a shape will cross your path — low, many-legged, moving with a deliberation that suggests it knows exactly where it is going even though there is nothing to go toward. It will be pale, as everything here is pale, and wet, as everything here is wet, and it will not acknowledge you. It will simply continue, a small body of purpose crossing an immeasurable surface, its trajectory perfect and meaningless.

There are other things. In the air — or in the thick medium that has replaced air — there are drifters, organisms so thin and so translucent they are more gap than substance, nets of biological material that float in the humidity and feed on I-cannot-imagine-what. They cast no shadows. They make no sound. You see them only when the light catches them at certain angles, and then you see that the air is *full* of them, that you have been walking through a slow rain of living tissue so fine it barely exists, that the atmosphere is not merely damp but *inhabited*.

Something larger moves in the far distance sometimes — a shape at the very edge of visibility that might be an animal or might be the land itself shifting, settling, adjusting its position by increments too small to measure but large enough to perceive as motion. You cannot approach it. The flatness defeats pursuit; the shape recedes as you walk toward it, or dissolves, or was never there, or was always there and will always be there, a permanent fixture of the horizon's unending statement.

---

The built things are almost not worth mentioning, except that they are the only breaks in the surface, and so they become significant by default.

There are stones that have been shaped. Not many — a cluster here, a single standing element there, always in the lowest places where the water is deepest and the ground most saturated. They are small. The largest comes to your hip; the smallest would fit in your palm. They have been worked — you can see the evidence of intention in their angles, in the deliberate flatness of certain faces, in the repetition of a notch or groove that appears across multiple stones and must therefore mean something. But the meaning has worn past recovery. The shapes are so eroded, so softened by the immense time they have spent in this wet and trembling place, that their original forms can only be guessed at. They might have been tools. They might have been markers. They might have been the cornerstones of structures that the land has since absorbed so completely that only these dense, hard fragments remain, refusing to dissolve, persisting as the last syllables of a sentence whose other words have been washed to nothing.

There is one place where the stones form a rough circle, perhaps three meters across, the elements barely distinguishable from the natural ridges except for their regularity. Water pools inside the circle to a slightly greater depth than outside, as though the arrangement has created a depression that the saturated ground respects. The water in this pool is warmer than the surrounding wetness. Not by much. But enough to feel if you place your hand in it — a subtle increase in temperature that suggests something underneath, some slow metabolism of stone and moisture that the circle has captured or catalyzed or commemorated.

No one built here. Not in any way that implies settlement or construction. But something happened here, repeatedly, long ago — something that required these stones to be selected and shaped and placed in these configurations in these locations, and the happening has left a residue so faint it can barely be called a memory. The place knows it was used. That is all.

---

And now the difficulty: the turbulence.

Everything I have described — the flatness, the wetness, the sound, the sparse and odd life, the dim glow, the ancient stones — all of this is true, and none of it is stable. The Expanse does not *hold*. It generates and dissolves in a cycle so rapid and so total that the landscape you walk through at noon is not the landscape you walked through at dawn, is not the landscape you will walk through at dusk. The erosion I described is not a past tense. It is ongoing. It is *accelerated*. The land is being created and destroyed at a pace that makes the concept of "terrain" into a convenience, a way of describing a momentary arrangement of elements that will be rearranged before you can finish the description.

The ridges shift. Not dramatically — you do not see the ground buckle and reform — but you return to a ridge you walked beside an hour ago and find that it has migrated, or softened, or branched, or extended itself in a new direction. The weeping changes: the water emerges from different places at different times, finding new paths through the stone, abandoning old ones, creating and erasing its own channels in a slow, wet choreography that the sound accompanies and amplifies until the whole place seems to be *performing* its own becoming.

The sparse vegetation appears and disappears. A mat of the pale hair-grass that was here yesterday is gone today, and in its place is a patch of the papery scale-stuff, or bare wet ground, or something that was not there before — a growth that does not match any of the categories I have tried to establish, a new form emerging from the saturated medium as though the place is trying out solutions, running variations, searching for a configuration that holds. Nothing holds. Everything is provisional. The Expanse is a laboratory in which the experiment is *existence itself*, and the hypothesis being tested is whether form can persist in a medium this wet, this loud, this ancient, this indifferent.

The sound changes too, and this is the most disorienting part. The dominant thrum modulates, and when it modulates, the *character* of the place modulates with it. A higher frequency produces a sharpening — the dim light becomes marginally less dim, the wetness marginally less wet, the vegetation marginally more distinct, as though the place is trying to become a *different place*, a place that makes sense, a place where things grow and light falls and sound carries meaning. Then the frequency drops and the place collapses back into its saturated, dim, roaring default, and the moment of near-coherence is revealed as a tease, a flutter of almost-possibility that the turbulence generates and destroys as easily as it generates and destroys the shape of the weeping ridges.

Standing in the middle of this — and there is only a middle, there is no edge, no boundary, no point at which the Expanse yields to a different kind of place — you are inside a process. Not a landscape. A *process*. The erosion has eroded the distinction between the land and the forces that act upon it. The water has dissolved the boundary between the wet and the dry. The sound has merged the listener and the listened-to. The density is the density of *becoming* itself — the packed, saturated, unoccupiable fullness of a place that is too many things at once and cannot stop being any of them.

---

At night — and there is a night, a dimming of the already-dim, a cooling of the already-warm, a shift in the sound's register toward something lower and more sustained — the place dreams. I use the word carefully. I do not believe the Expanse is conscious. But in the darkness, the turbulence produces forms that have no daytime equivalent. Shapes rise from the saturated ground — not plants, not animals, not minerals, but *shapes*, temporary volumes that persist for minutes or hours before collapsing back into the wet. They are warm to the touch. They make sounds that are not part of the dominant roar — smaller sounds, specific sounds, sounds that resemble language the way the vegetation resembles grass: following an instruction set that has been imperfectly received.

You can walk among them. They do not impede. They are not solid; they are *dense*, regions of heightened presence that you move through like moving through a warmer pocket of water in a lake. Inside them, the sound changes — it becomes internal, private, as though you are hearing the place's version of your own heartbeat. Outside them, the roar reasserts itself and the shapes recede into the general dimness and you are alone again in the flatness, in the wetness, in the endless ongoing erosion of everything that tries to be.

The shapes are the Expanse's attempt at structure. They are what the place does when it has exhausted its other options. They are the stones, the vegetation, the animal-forms, the architecture — all of them compressed into a single gesture of almost-creation that rises in the dark and falls in the dark and leaves no residue, no trace, no memory except the faint warmth in the ground where they stood, which the water absorbs within minutes and carries away into the general saturation.

---

This is the oldest place. Not the first — there is no first, there is only older and older and older until the concept of age becomes another thing that erosion has worn past meaning. But the most *settled* into its age. The most comfortable with being the end-product of every process that has ever acted upon it. It does not change because it has arrived; it changes because change is what it *is*, now. The turbulence is not disturbance. The turbulence is *homeostasis* — the particular stability of a system that has discovered that the only way to persist is to refuse to be any single thing for longer than a moment.

The Expanse weeps and roars and generates and dissolves and has been doing so for longer than anything has been doing anything, and it will continue to do so for longer than anything will continue to do anything, and the sound it makes — the total, deafening, unceasing sound — is the sound of that continuation, the noise of a place that has become its own process and will never, never, never be quiet.
