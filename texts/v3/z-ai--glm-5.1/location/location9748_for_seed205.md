# The Mat

The ground is not ground. It never was.

Beneath your feet: a living floor, meters thick, dense as muscle, warm as a body. The Mat—they call it that, those few who live here, though it has older names in older mouths—the Mat extends to every horizon and beyond, continental in its reach, a single contiguous organism or a conspiracy of organisms so intergrown that the distinction collapsed eons ago. You walk on root that became rhizome that became substrate. You walk on something that breathes.

---

The plateau sits high—two, perhaps three kilometers above whatever lies beneath, though no one has seen beneath in any living memory. The Mat covers it entirely. Whatever geology raised this landform, the plant ate it, incorporated it, became the new surface. Now there is only the Mat, rolling in long slow swells where it follows the buried topography, dipping into broad basins that catch the morning's condensation, rising toward ridgelines where the air thins and the growth changes texture.

It is hot. The heat has weight. At this elevation the sun arrives unfiltered and the Mat absorbs it, drinks it, holds it through the night like a stone holds the day's warmth. By midmorning the air above the surface shimmers. You feel the heat rising from below as much as descending from above—a double radiance, the Mat exhaling what it swallowed. Sweat does not cool you here; the air is too damp, too close. It sits on your skin like another membrane.

---

The damp arrives in cycles, though not as you'd expect rain. Morning: condensation. The night's cooling pulls moisture from the Mat's upper layers, and by dawn every surface wears a film of warm water. You wake wet. Your belongings wake wet. The air tastes of it—mineral and vegetable, a tea brewed from things you cannot name. This moisture beads, gathers, runs in slow rivulets down the Mat's slight gradations, filling the shallow basins that pocket the surface. By midday most of these pools have returned to the air as vapor. Some persist in the deeper folds, dark and warm and alive with things that swim.

And sometimes—rarely, unpredictably—rain. Real rain. It comes from clouds that aggregate above the plateau's interior, clouds the Mat itself seems to call into being, so much moisture does it exhale. These storms are brief and violent and the Mat drinks them with what can only be called relief, its surface perceptibly swelling, perceptibly softening underfoot, like a sponge left in a basin.

---

Against this living floor, the Columns.

They rise at intervals that seem regular until you try to measure them—roughly a kilometer apart, plus or minus the margin of a long walk, the margin of exhaustion and uncertainty. Each Column is a vertical extension of the Mat, a trunk or stem or pillar of the same dense tissue, but differentiated, hardened, rising forty, sixty, a hundred meters before branching into a crown. If the Mat is the body, the Columns are the limbs, reaching for the sky with a patience measured in centuries.

They are not trees. They approximate trees the way a cathedral approxulates a cave—same general shape, entirely different principle. The Columns are continuous with the Mat below, no seam, no root-collar transition, just a thickening and a rising. Their bark—if that word applies—is layered like shale, dark and warm to the touch, and from every cleft and fissure springs secondary growth: fern-like fronds in colors that shouldn't exist this far from any coast, moss-analogs that glow faintly in the long dusk, trailing filaments that the wind sets swaying.

The space between Columns is vast. You can walk for an hour and reach only the next one, or walk for three hours and find nothing but the unbroken Mat stretching ahead, its surface gently varied, sometimes smooth and tight, sometimes buckled into low ridges where subsurface rhizomes have shifted. The sparseness is the thing: so much space, so much emptiness, and yet none of it dead, none of it bare. The Mat is the presence that fills the space simply by being there, continuous, inescapable.

---

Now the sound.

The sound is not one sound. It is ten thousand sounds collapsed into a single sustained roar that you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears. The fauna—there is no adequate word. The fauna are not a feature of this landscape. They *are* this landscape, as much as the Mat, as much as the Columns.

Start with the insects, because you cannot start anywhere else. They coat every surface in layers, in drifts. The Mat's outer skin crawls with them—grazers, you realize, feeding on the living tissue, kept in check by the Mat's own defenses, its secretions, its periodic shedding of whole epidermal layers that roll up like old skin and reveal the fresh wet growth beneath. The hum of their wings fills the frequency range from a low grinding buzz to a high whine just past the edge of hearing, and in the heat of the day this hum becomes a physical pressure, a vibration that blurs your vision if you stand too long in one place.

Above the insects, the fliers. Not birds—nothing here is birds—but vertebrate-analogs, warm and quick, that hunt the insect clouds in formations so dense they darken the sky momentarily as they pass. Their calls are harsh and rhythmic, signaling, always signaling, and when a column of them descends to feed the sound multiplies into something like artillery, a crackling roar that echoes off the Columns and returns from different directions at different delays until the air itself seems to be shouting.

At ground level, the crawlers. Armored and slow or soft and fast, they move across the Mat in lines that follow chemical trails only they can read. Some are the size of your thumb, some the size of your torso, and they pay you no attention unless you step on them, in which case the attention is immediate and memorable. Their sounds are low, percussive, bodily—clicks and grunts and the dry scrape of limb against limb.

And the larger things. You hear them before you see them—if you see them at all. They move between the Columns at a pace that suggests they own the distances, that the kilometers between mean nothing to them. Their calls are the bass notes beneath the roar: long, low moans that travel through the Mat itself, vibrational, felt in the feet. You cannot tell if these are herbivores or predators or something for which the distinction is irrelevant. You know only that they are large and they are there, always there, just beyond the next swell of ground.

The sound never stops. It quiets, slightly, in the deepest hour of the night, and then it surges again at first condensation, the whole ecosystem shifting phase, and you lie in your shelter and feel the world screaming its appetite around you.

---

The shelters. Yes—there are shelters.

They are difficult to see until you are nearly upon them, because they are made of the Mat. Or rather, they are made from the Mat, cut and shaped and woven, and this makes them nearly invisible against the surface. A lean-to. A half-dome. A platform lashed between two low ridges of rhizome. The people who build them—and there are people, you have seen them, or seen evidence of them—work with hand tools: stone blades, mostly, and bone, and the rare piece of worked metal that must have come from somewhere far away, traded through distances you cannot imagine across this single living floor.

Their structures are small. One person, perhaps two. A hearth-ring scored into the Mat's surface, the tissue carefully killed and hardened to prevent it from growing over the fire. A rack for drying whatever they harvest. Cordage made from the trailing filaments, wound and braided with a skill that must be generational. No walls, really—walls are pointless when the predators can follow scent and the rain comes from above and the Mat itself would simply grow over any barrier in a matter of days. Just a roof, a windbreak, a marking of place that says: *I was here. I am here. I am still here.*

You find them scattered at irregular intervals—near the standing pools, usually, or at the base of a Column where the secondary growth provides cover. Never in clusters. Never villages. The people of the Mat live alone or in pairs, and this is not loneliness but survival, because the fauna would find a concentration, would overwhelm it, would consume it down to the bone-tools and the cold hearth and the memory of heat.

---

The Mat has a rhythm. You learn it.

Dawn: the condensation rises as mist, and with it the first chorus, the grazers and the fliers and the things that feed on the moisture itself. The Mat's surface darkens with wetness and the air thickens until you feel you could drink it.

Midmorning: the sun burns through and the mist lifts and the heat begins in earnest. The surface steams. The insects rise in columns so dense they seem solid, and the fliers come for them, and the larger things come for the fliers, and the pyramid of consumption builds throughout the day in a roaring cascade of metabolism.

Afternoon: the heat peaks and something in the Mat responds, some chemical tide, and the surface blooms—flowers? not quite, but analogous structures, eruptions of color and scent and sweetness that attract pollinators and in turn attract everything that eats pollinators. The columns run yellow, red, impossible violet. The smell is overpowering, sweet and rotten at once, the smell of life processing itself.

Dusk: a last surge of activity as the light fails, then a rapid withdrawal, everything that is vulnerable retreating into burrows and hollows and the gaps between the Mat's layers where the night-things cannot reach. The sound drops to a simmer. The larger things begin to move, their moans traveling through the ground, and you feel them like distant earthquakes.

Night: the Mat breathes differently, pulling moisture down, closing its pores, hardening its surface slightly. It is not sleep—the Mat does not sleep—but a contraction, a turning-inward. And in the darkness the night-ecology runs: pale, eyeless, silent except for the sounds of capture, the muffled struggle, the wet sounds of feeding.

You do not sleep well here. The ground is warm and alive beneath you and it moves, slightly, like a sleeping animal, and the sounds never fully cease, and in the morning the condensation finds you again and you wake damp and hungry into the same roar.

---

The weirdness: you feel it in the ratio of things. Too many animals for the available water. Too much growth for the available light. The ecosystem should not sustain itself at this volume, this density of biomass, this deafening throughput of consumption and decay. Something else is feeding it. The Mat itself, perhaps—pulling heat from below, pulling mineral from somewhere deeper than any root should reach, generating energy by some process that is not photosynthesis as you understand it. The Columns are too tall for their spacing; they should shade each other, compete, but they don't, they seem to share, to coordinate, as if the decision to grow is made communally, distributed across the kilometers of connected tissue.

And the people fit into this wrongness, or fail to fit, or fit in a way you cannot parse. They harvest from the Mat with tools that are almost apologies—stone and bone, the gentlest possible violence—and they live at the margins of the ecosystem, not at its center, surviving on what the greater machinery does not need. They are not lords of this place. They are not even tenants. They are more like a particular kind of grazer that learned to build fires, and the Mat tolerates them the way it tolerates everything: as process, as input, as another current in the endless circulation of energy and matter that is the only law this place obeys.

You stand on the surface and feel it breathing and you hear the roar of ten million lives processing themselves into each other and you understand: this is not a landscape. This is an organism the size of a continent, and you are standing on its skin, and it does not know you are there, and it does not need to know, and this is the most terrifying thing—not hostility, not indifference even, but an existence at a scale where you are simply below notice, a mite on the hide of something that has been breathing since before your species learned the word for *breath.*

The heat presses down. The sound continues. The Mat goes on.
