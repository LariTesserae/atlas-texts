# The Shallow

A hollow in the world, barely deep enough to be called a place at all.

You reach it by descending — not far, perhaps the height of a person, down a gradual slope of compacted mineral that has been settling into itself for longer than anything remembers. The land here is low to begin with, barely rising above the surrounding flats, and this depression is simply where the surface gave up some small ambition and folded inward. Not carved by water. Not split by fault. Just a gradual, patient yielding. The ground decided to be lower here, and so it became so.

The floor is hard-packed, almost polished — a conglomerate of fine dust and mineral that has compressed under its own patience into something like stone but not quite. It holds warmth the way a body holds heat after the sun is gone. And the sun has been gone from this place for some time.

---

Light arrives here only as a rumor.

The hollow opens northward, a shallow throat perhaps twenty paces across, but the angle is wrong for direct sun. What enters is reflected, diffused, secondhand — the memory of light bouncing off the pale flats above and finding its way down in greatly reduced circumstance. At midday you can see well enough to move. The walls reveal themselves as layers: pale ochre giving way to a deeper tan, then a band of something almost white, chalky and soft, then the return to tan. Strata laid down in some ancient fluvial episode, then exposed and left to the slow work of air.

By late afternoon the hollow dims. By dusk it is a dark room. The darkness is not absolute — the open mouth prevents that — but it is substantial. It is the darkness of a place that does not generate its own light and has no reason to.

The warmth persists through the night. The mineral walls radiate what they absorbed, slowly, grudgingly, like a creature that does not want to share. Even in the coldest hours before dawn, the hollow is warmer than the flats above. This is its single generosity.

---

There are plants here, if you can call them that.

They grow in the crannies where the walls meet the floor, in the narrow seams where the strata shift and create a ledge of softer material. Not many. Perhaps a dozen individuals in the entire hollow, and all of them the same kind: low, gray-green rosettes of thick, fleshy leaves that press themselves flat against the rock. They are lithophytes — stone-dwellers — drawing what they need from the mineral itself and from the thin films of dew that sometimes condense in the pre-dawn cool. They do not flower. They do not grow quickly. A rosette the width of a hand may be thirty years old, and it will be the same rosette thirty years from now, barely larger, barely different.

Their roots are more like holdfasts. They grip. They do not seek.

There is a larger cluster near the back wall where a thin vein of something chalkier provides marginally more purchase. Seven rosettes there, overlapping, their leaf-edges touching. This is as close as this place comes to abundance.

The air tastes of calcium and old warmth. It is very dry. Your lips crack within an hour.

---

The silence is not complete, but it is close.

If you sit long enough — long enough for your own sounds to settle, for your breathing to slow and your small adjustments to cease — you may hear something. A faint, irregular ticking from the walls as thermal expansion and contraction work their slow arithmetic. A soft settling, somewhere beneath you, as fine particles shift in some microscopic readjustment. Once, perhaps, a dry leaf from one of the rosettes detaches and falls, scraping lightly against the stone on its way down.

These sounds do not constitute a soundscape. They are exceptions. The baseline is absence.

No wind reaches here. The hollow is too shallow for significant air movement, too low, too sheltered. What breeze exists above does not descend. The air inside sits, warms, cools slightly, sits again. It has been doing this for a very long time.

---

No animals live here. None visit.

The hollow offers nothing to a living creature that moves. No water, no food, no shelter from predators that would not themselves be deterred by the walls. Occasionally a wind-borne insect might find itself deposited at the lip, but it does not stay. There is no reason to stay. The place is not hostile; it is simply irrelevant to the concerns of anything that breathes and seeks and flees.

This is part of its character: the hollow is a place that has been left out of the conversation between living things. It exists in a different register. The mineral exchanges that define it — the slow oxidation, the microscopic expansion and contraction, the imperceptible settling — would proceed identically whether anything observed them or not. They have been proceeding identically for millennia.

---

Stand in the center and turn slowly. You will see the walls rise gently on three sides, perhaps twice your height at the deepest point, sloping back up to the flats. The open mouth gives onto the pale expanse beyond, featureless under whatever light is available. The floor is essentially flat. The only vertical elements are the walls themselves, and they are not dramatic — more like the edges of a bowl than cliffs.

The total area would fit inside a modest building. You could walk its perimeter in two minutes. You could know it completely in an afternoon, every stratum, every rosette, every subtle variation in the floor's compacted surface.

And yet it would not be fully known. Because the hollow's defining quality is its stasis, and stasis is not the same thing as simplicity. The balance here — between the warmth absorbed and radiated, between the microscopic erosion and the compaction that resists it, between the rosettes' imperceptible growth and the equally imperceptible decay of their oldest leaves — this balance is not static but dynamic in the way a spinning top is dynamic: always in motion, always settling, always correcting, but appearing from outside to be perfectly still.

The hollow is in its own state. It has been finding this state for a very long time, and it will continue to find it for longer still. It is not waiting for anything. It is not becoming anything else. It is what it is, doing what it does, holding what it holds: warmth, quiet, darkness, a few gray-green rosettes pressed against the stone, and the long, slow patience of mineral at rest.
