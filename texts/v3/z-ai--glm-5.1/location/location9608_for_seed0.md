# The Nursery

The land is young. Not newborn — it has been here long enough to be found — but young enough that it still startles at its own existence. You can feel this in the edges. The ravines cut sharp as if carved with a blade still being sharpened. Ridges haven't learned to be gentle. Stone faces expose themselves in flat, clean planes, unweathered, almost embarrassed by their own nakedness. The erosion hasn't come yet to teach this terrain patience. Everything still has its original shape, and the original shapes are severe.

And into this severity, lushness has exploded.

It shouldn't work this fast. That's the first wrong thing you notice — though it takes time to notice it, because the sound makes thought difficult. The vegetation has colonized the sharp geography with a desperation that feels almost like panic. Ferns thick as bedrolls cascade over cliff-faces that still show the drag-marks of whatever made them. Trees — but "trees" is too organized a word for what these are — massive stalks, ribbed and jointed like the legs of insects, rise from the low ground in stands so dense their canopies lock together into a single roof. They have had perhaps a generation to grow. They have grown as if they had ten.

The canopy is the sky here. You forget there is anything above it. What light exists comes through in shafts that don't so much illuminate as exist — pale columns standing in the dark air like architectural features, like pillars in a cathedral built by accident. The darkness beneath is substantial. It has a quality of material. You move through it the way you'd move through water, displacing it, feeling its resistance. Your eyes never adjust because there is nothing to adjust to. The floor of this place operates on a different sense-realm entirely.

You navigate by sound. Everyone does. Everything does.

---

The sound is a wall. It is a climate. It is the dominant feature of the landscape more than the ferns or the ravines or the dark. From canopy to soil, the sound does not stop. It has no quiet intervals. Insects first — a droning baseline so constant it becomes the silence against which other sounds register, a sub-harmonic buzz that lives in your teeth and in the joints of your jaw. Over that, the calls: birds with voices like dropped glass, frogs that drum their own bodies, things that have no name in any language you know that bark and click and shriek in frequencies you feel more than hear. The territorial declarations. The mating signals. The warnings.

And there are so many warnings.

The fauna is not merely present. It is the condition of the place. You are never not in the presence of animals. You are never not being watched, sniffed, assessed. The density of living bodies per cubic meter of air is obscene — in the canopy, the understory, the root-tangle, the soil itself. Things move in every direction at once. You put your hand against a trunk and realize the bark is moving — beetles, ants, a species of small flat lizard that camouflages against the wood grain, all flowing in parallel streams with different destinations. Something drops onto your shoulder. Something else lifts off from the ground near your foot. The air is a medium through which life moves as much as it moves through the branches.

At night — and the distinction between day and night beneath the canopy is mostly theoretical, a shift from absolute dark to slightly-less-dark — the larger animals emerge. You hear them. A crashing in the undergrowth that could be something the size of a horse, moving with the confidence of an apex creature. Pairs of eyes at knee-height, at waist-height, at the height of your own eyes, reflecting nothing because there is no light to reflect, but catching the faint bioluminescence that drifts up from the forest floor like cold steam. The fungi glow. Not all of them, but enough — clusters of small pale caps on fallen logs, shelf-fungi on standing trunks that pulse with a slow rhythm like breathing, a lichen-like growth that traces the ground in lines, drawing paths you cannot tell are paths or just patterns. In this faint glow, you catch glimpses. A hide. A wing. A mouth.

---

The moisture is strange. It doesn't rain, exactly. Or it does, but infrequently, and when it does, the canopy absorbs most of it before it reaches the ground. What reaches you is a secondary precipitation — drip from leaves that have already held the water for hours, releasing it in large cold drops that fall from unpredictable heights. But the dominant dampness is not rain at all. It is condensation. The warmth of the place — and it is warm, a deep wet warmth that lives in the air and in the soil and in the bark of the stalk-trees — meets surfaces, meets the cool stone of the ravine walls, meets the underside of broad leaves, and becomes water. You can watch it form. You can stand still and feel it forming on your own skin. Every surface is wet. Every surface has always been wet. The stone gleams. The trunks run with it. Pools collect in the sharp young hollows of the terrain, and these pools are not stagnant — they are alive with larvae, with algae, with small darting fish that have no business being this far from any river but are here anyway.

There is a river. You hear it more than you see it — a low grinding beneath the wall of biological sound, a resonance that changes pitch as the water shifts its course. And it shifts often. The young landscape hasn't settled into its drainage patterns yet. The river is still deciding where it belongs. It undercuts banks in days, creates new channels overnight. You can find the old courses — dried runs through the undergrowth, the mud still holding the texture of water, the roots of the stalk-trees already reaching down into the abandoned streambeds as if they knew, as if someone told them.

---

Someone might have told them. This is the second wrong thing, the one that takes longer to notice because the first wrong thing — the speed of the growth — is so loud and so green that it occupies all of your attention at first. But there is a pattern beneath the chaos. Not a design. Not a plan. A tendency.

You find it in the tool-stones.

They are not built structures — there are no built structures here, nothing that registers as construction or artifact. But in the hollows where the pools form, on the ledges above the young ravines, at the bases of certain stalk-trees that are larger than their neighbors, you find stones that have been struck. Flaked. Some have edges that are too precise, too functional, to be the result of erosion or accident. Hand-axes. Scrapers. A hammer-stone worn smooth on one face from repetitive impact. The work is primitive — pre-metal, pre-refinement, the simplest possible engagement between a hand and the world — but it is work. Someone made these.

You find other traces. Sticks stripped of bark and worn at one end, left leaning against trunks as if their user intended to return. Piles of shells — seed-pods, actually, from the canopy above, cracked open with consistent force in a consistent pattern. A place where the soil has been scraped away to expose the stone beneath, the scraping-marks still fresh, still sharp. Not agriculture. Not industry. Just... use. The earliest kind of use. The moment before use becomes habit becomes custom becomes culture.

But whose use?

---

You see them, eventually. Or you see something.

The darkness makes certainty impossible. But there is a shape that recurs — low to the ground, moving with a deliberation that is different from the frantic energy of everything else alive here. Not running. Not fleeing. Moving with intention. You catch it at the edge of the bioluminescent glow, where the lichen-lines trace the ground. It seems to follow the lines. Or maybe it made them.

It is not human. Or it was human once, a long time ago, before the canopy closed and the sound became the world. It has adapted in ways that are difficult to see clearly but that you can feel the shape of: a body built for the dark, for the density, for the damp. Eyes that don't need light. Ears that can parse the wall of sound into its constituent warnings. A relationship with the terrain that is older than the terrain itself, which is impossible, which is the third wrong thing, the one you don't want to think about.

There are more than one. The sound changes when they are near — not louder or quieter, but differently organized. As if they are modulating the noise, adding their own calls to the cacophony in a way that means something to others of their kind. Language buried in the roar. Signal inside the noise.

They use the tool-stones. They follow the lichen-lines. They know when the river will shift and where the pools will form. They live here the way the beetles live here — as a native presence, so thoroughly adapted that they have become part of the landscape's metabolism.

---

The turbulence is not just the river. It is everything. This place is in a state of continuous re-creation. Trees fall — you hear the crack and the long groan and the crash and then the explosive scatter of creatures evacuating the falling structure, and then the silence, a silence that lasts perhaps three seconds before the sound-wall reconstitutes itself, before the territory-claims are reasserted, before the mating-calls resume. The fallen tree becomes a new architecture. Within a day, colonizers have claimed it. Within a week, it is part of the floor's topography, a ridge of softening wood and sprouting fungi. Within a month, it is gone. Eaten. Grown through. Absorbed back into the standing biomass.

The speed is the fever. The lushness is the symptom. Something is driving this place at a rate that exceeds the normal permissions of biology and geology. The young landscape shouldn't be this fertile. The darkness shouldn't support this much photosynthesis. The sound shouldn't be this loud — there shouldn't be this many individual voices to make it. Something is feeding the system. Something at the root of it, in the soil or in the stone or in the water that collects in the sharp young hollows, something that is not quite nutrient and not quite energy and not quite information but some fourth thing for which there is no word.

The creatures who use the stone tools know this thing. They are adapted to it the way they are adapted to the dark. They are not disturbed by the wrongness. To them, it is not wrong. It is simply the way the world works: fecund, loud, dark, warm, and constantly becoming.

---

You will not stay long. The sound alone makes that decision for you — after a day, it lives in your skull independent of your ears; after two days, you cannot remember what silence felt like; after three, you begin to parse it, to separate the strands, and that is when you know you have to leave, because understanding the sound means joining it, means becoming part of the communication, and once you are part of the communication you are part of the system, and the system consumes everything that enters it with the same indiscriminate hunger.

But before you leave — standing on a ridge of stone so young it hasn't accumulated a full layer of moss yet, looking down into the dark tangle of the lowland, feeling the warmth rise from it like breath, hearing the million simultaneous declarations of life — you understand what this place is.

It is a nursery. A place where the rules are suspended and generation happens at a pace that would be impossible anywhere else. A place that is still becoming, and in its becoming, makes everything else become too. The sharp geology, the rushing river, the dense canopy, the overwhelming animal presence, the tool-users moving through the dark with their crude stones and their purposeful silence — they are all the same process at different speeds. The landscape is an engine. The sound is the engine running. The darkness is the engine's fuel — or its exhaust, or its product, or its shape — and the warmth is the heat it gives off.

You turn away. The sound follows you for a long time. Even after the canopy thins and the light returns and the stone beneath your feet has been weathered smooth by ages of patient erosion, even then, in the quiet, you hear it: a low hum at the threshold of hearing, a resonance that got into your bones and never quite left, the sound of a place where creation is still happening, where the world is still being made, where the making will never stop.
