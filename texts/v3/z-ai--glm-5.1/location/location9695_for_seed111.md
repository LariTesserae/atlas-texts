# The Condensation Cathedral

The world broke open here. Not long ago—not in the way mountains measure time. The rock still has the audacity of new things: edges that haven't learned to yield, faces that catch light like broken glass, spires that rise as if the earth meant to make a point and then forgot to soften it.

You are high. So high that the clouds don't pass above you but through you, and this is the first principle of the place: *everything breathes wet*. The stone is cold enough to pull water from whatever air moves past it, and air always moves past it, because at this elevation the winds have nowhere to go but through the gaps and over the ridges and down into the narrow cathedral of space the uplifted rock has made.

---

The space itself is small. You could walk its length in the time it takes to become genuinely afraid—three hundred paces, maybe four hundred, if you're being careful about where you step. It's a cleft, really. A wound in the new rock where something pulled apart during the rising, leaving walls that lean toward each other at angles that feel intentional but aren't. The ceiling, if you can call it that, is the overhang of two massive slabs that nearly meet overhead. They block most of the sky. What light makes it through is the thin, exhausted light of extreme altitude filtered through cloud and stone—grey upon grey, a darkness that isn't absolute but might as well be for how little it reveals.

You navigate by sound and by the feel of condensation on your skin.

---

The damp has no source and no end. It doesn't rain here, not exactly. Instead, the moisture simply *appears*—condensing out of the fog that pushes through the cleft in slow, patient waves, beading on the sharp rock faces, gathering into veins that trace down the walls like the stone is sweating. Pools form in the irregularities of the floor, in the gouges and fractures that the uplift left behind. They're never large. Some are no bigger than your palm. The largest might fill a basin. But there are hundreds of them, thousands maybe, and they reflect nothing because there's almost nothing to reflect, just the faint mineral gleam of wet stone in whatever grey light the clouds permit.

The rock itself drinks and sweats and drinks again. The cycle takes hours. The pools fill and seep and fill. Nothing rushes.

---

Vegetation is almost an afterthought here, almost a rumor. In the places where condensation runs most consistently—where two wet walls meet and the water traces the same path long enough to make a small channel—there are growths. Not plants, exactly. Thin pale tendrils that might be lichen or might be something that learned to be lichen from a textbook it never read. They're colorless, almost. A green so faint it reads as grey until your eyes adjust, and then you realize the lower walls are *covered* in the stuff, a fine matte of threadlike structures so dense they feel like velvet when you touch them. They don't flower. They don't fruit. They just spread, slowly, in the places where water moves most consistently, and they seem to feed on something other than light because there isn't enough light here to feed anything.

Whatever they are, they are not why the place is so full.

---

The fauna is why the place is so full.

You hear it before you understand it. The sound is continuous and layered and loud—the kind of loud that stops being about volume and starts being about *saturation*. A sound that fills the cleft like water fills a container, pressing against the walls, pressing against your ears, pressing against the inside of your skull. It's not one sound. It's ten thousand sounds overlapping and interfering until your brain stops trying to separate them and just receives the whole mass of it as a single presence: *the sound the place makes*.

Clicking. That's the substrate. A rapid, irregular clicking like a thousand people tapping small stones against each other in patterns that never repeat. Underneath the clicking, a low hum that you feel in your sternum more than hear with your ears. Above it, occasional chittering—short bursts of complex noise that might be communication or might be something else entirely, something that serves a function your biology didn't evolve to comprehend.

They are everywhere.

---

The creatures—I hesitate to call them insects because the word carries assumptions that fail here, but I don't have a better one—the creatures are small. Most of them are no longer than your thumb. Some are no larger than a grain of the sharp grit that litters the cleft floor. But there are *so many of them*, packed into this small space with a density that makes the air feel thicker, makes the darkness feel *grainy*, textured, like you're breathing in a living medium rather than atmosphere.

They coat the walls in patches that shift and rearrange, moving over each other in slow waves that the pale lichen-thing doesn't seem to mind or even notice. They cluster around the condensation pools in densities that turn the water's surface into a seething membrane. They hang from the ceiling of the cleft in strands that look, in the faint light, like organic chandeliers—thousands of them linked by some secretion, some web, some substance they produce that hardens into thin cables between the overhanging stones.

When the fog pushes through particularly thick, they spread out to catch the moisture on their bodies. You can watch a wave of them extend outward from the walls, reaching into the open air of the cleft, holding position for minutes, and then retracting when the fog thins. They're drinking. Or breathing. Or both. Or something that serves both functions in a biology that doesn't distinguish between them.

Their carapaces—if that's what those are—are the color of wet stone, grey-brown and faintly iridescent. When the sparse light catches them at certain angles, the walls shimmer as if embedded with chips of abalone. This is beautiful in the way that things are beautiful when they are overwhelming and alien and you have no framework for judging them otherwise.

---

The strangeness accumulates the longer you stay.

You notice that the clicking changes pitch when you move. Not because you're startling them—they don't startle, not in any way you can detect—but because they're *tracking* you, adjusting some quality of their sound in response to your position in the cleft. When you stand still, the sound settles into a pattern. When you shift your weight, the pattern fractures and re-forms around your new location. It's not alarm. It might be mapping.

You notice that the pools don't just reflect nothing—they seem to *absorb* light. When you hold your hand over one, you can't see your reflection in the water. The surface shows only a faint impression of depth, of darkness extending down into a fracture in the rock that the pool sits in. The creatures cluster around these pools most densely, and you realize they're not just drinking—they're *entering*. Crawling down into the cracks beneath the water, disappearing into the rock, and other individuals are emerging, climbing up from the same cracks, wet and glistening. The pools are entrances. Or exits. Or both. The cleft is just one room in a much larger architecture that extends down into the mountain along the fracture network the uplift created.

You notice that the wind doesn't move the fog the way it should. The fog has a willfulness about it, a tendency to linger and thicken in the places where the creatures are most dense, as if the moisture is being produced or attracted by their presence. The air near the walls is noticeably wetter than the air in the center of the cleft, and the creatures are clustered exactly where the wettest air is. You can't tell if they're following the moisture or making it.

You notice that some of the strands hanging from the ceiling are vibrating at frequencies you can barely hear—a deep pulse below the hum, below the clicking, at the threshold of perception. The strands near the vibration are empty of creatures, but the strands next to them are thick with clusters that seem to be *listening*, their bodies oriented toward the vibration, their clicking paused. When the vibration stops, they resume. When it starts again, they go still. Something is being communicated. Something is being received.

---

The place has rules, though no one wrote them down.

*Move slowly.* The creatures don't fear you, but they respond to speed. Quick movement makes the clicking intensify, the hum deepen, the air feel heavier with their presence. Slow movement—patient, deliberate, almost geological in its pacing—lets you pass through them as if you're just another feature of the rock.

*Don't touch the pools.* Not because anything will happen to you. Because you'll disturb whatever process is occurring beneath the surface, and the creatures will notice, and the sound will change, and the cleft will become a different place for as long as it takes them to re-establish whatever pattern you've interrupted. Hours. Maybe days. The dynamics here are slow. Patience is the operating principle of everything.

*Don't look for the bottom of anything.* The fractures go down. The pools go down. The sounds come from down. The creatures move down and come back up changed—or maybe they're different individuals entirely, and you can't tell because they all look the same to eyes that weren't made to distinguish them. There is more below than above. The cleft is an *opening*, not a destination.

---

And the place is young. This is the thing that stays with you. The rock is sharp because it hasn't been here long enough to soften. The lichen-thing is thin because it hasn't had time to thicken. The creatures are dense because the niche is new and they're still in the phase where the space can't yet limit their numbers. The sounds are loud because the acoustic properties of the cleft haven't been modified by erosion, by wearing smooth, by the patience of water and wind over geological time. Everything here is still arriving, still filling the space, still establishing what it will become.

Give it a million years and the edges will soften. The pools will carve proper channels. The lichen-thing will be a carpet. The creatures will reach equilibrium with whatever the mountain becomes. The sound will quiet as the walls learn to absorb it.

But that hasn't happened yet. Right now, the place is still becoming. It is raw and new and full of a life that hasn't learned its limits, and it is dark and wet and loud with the sound of ten thousand small bodies clicking and humming in a space no larger than a cathedral that was never built by human hands, and the fog pushes through and the condensation forms and the pools fill and seep and fill again, and below everything, in the fractures you cannot see the bottom of, something vibrates at a frequency you can barely hear, and the creatures go still and listen.
