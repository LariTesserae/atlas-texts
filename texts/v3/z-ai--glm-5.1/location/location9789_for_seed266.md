# The Grey Ascendant

## The Rise

It begins with a slope you don't notice at first.

You walk on what feels like level ground, and only after hours—perhaps days—do you realize you have been climbing. The land rises so gradually, across so many hundreds of kilometers, that the concept of "elevation" becomes meaningless. There is no peak. There is no valley. There is only the ongoing, patient tilt upward toward something that might be a plateau or might be the simple exhaustion of the crust itself, thinning under a warm and indifferent sky.

This is the Grey Ascendant. A continental rampart, ancient beyond stratigraphy, where the stone has been rising so long that erosion gave up trying to reduce it and instead entered a kind of truce. The surface is weathered, yes—rounded, smoothed, its edges softened across geological ages—but it has not been *diminished*. The land simply *is*, in the way that a boulder too large to move becomes a part of the landscape's intention.

---

## The Dryness

There has never been water here. Not in the way you mean it.

Oh, there are minerals that suggest hydration occurred once, in some deep epoch before the rise began—but the chemistry is wrong, the crystal lattices too perfectly ordered, too *deliberate*. If water existed here, it did not stay long enough to carve. It left no riverbeds. No alluvial fans. No fossil shorelines. Instead, the stone wears its dryness like a skin: a fine, warm powder that coats everything, that rises in slow columns when the wind touches it, that tastes of salt and iron and something else—something that makes the back of your throat close.

They call it *selifrith*. The dust. It moves in breathing patterns, settling and lifting, settling and lifting, as though the land itself respires. It gets into machinery. Into lungs. Into the gaps between thoughts. After enough years, the people who work the high ridges stop noticing it, and that is when it enters them properly, and they become—according to the old miners—*selisk*. Dust-touched. Changed in ways that show up first in the eyes, which develop a fine grey film that is not cataract but something more like a lens, an adaptation to a light that was never meant for water-based vision.

You do not drink water here. There is none to drink. What sustains comes up from below, pumped through crystalline pipes that hum at frequencies just below hearing—thick mineral brines, heated by the depth, rich in compounds that the body can learn to parse. It takes three weeks to adapt. The first week, you vomit. The second, you stop feeling hungry. The third, you forget what thirst was.

---

## The Warmth

The temperature holds steady around 38 degrees. Warm but not punishing—the kind of warmth that suggests a body, something vast and metabolically active beneath the surface. The stone is warm to the touch. Not at the surface, where selifrith cools in the air, but a hand's width down, where the rock becomes almost uncomfortably hot. The miners say the Ascendant is *cooking*. Slowly. Over millions of years. A geological simmer that will never reach boil.

This warmth means the air moves, but reluctantly. Convection currents form, weaken, reform elsewhere. Weather exists—vast, slow weather, systems that take weeks to cross the continental breadth—but it is weather without water. Dust storms. Pressure fronts. Electrostatic sheens that crackle along the horizon at dusk, turning the sky the color of hammered copper. No rain. No clouds in the moisture sense. Only the haze of fine particulates, suspended in the warm column of air, catching the light.

---

## The Fauna

This is what unsettles visitors: the place is *populated*.

Not by anything you would recognize at first. The creatures of the Ascendant are not built on the logic of water and carbon, though they occupy space in ways that suggest bodies. They are—according to the xenobiologists who have spent lifetimes failing to classify them—*mineral-catalytic autotrophs*. They eat stone. They breathe the sulfur compounds that vent from fissures in the rise. They reproduce by *budding*, splitting off fragments of themselves that contain fully formed crystalline matrices, which then grow by accreting selifrith from the air.

The most common are the *sketh*. Low, wide, slow. Six-sided, like flat rocks that have decided to be elsewhere. They move at the speed of glaciers, grinding across the surface on abrasive undersides, consuming the upper layer of stone and excreting a fine powder behind them—the selifrith itself, which is, apparently, their waste product. The entire dust ecology of the Ascendant is sketh excrement. This does not bother the miners. It bothered the first visitors tremendously. Now it is simply a fact, like gravity.

Larger things move in the deep valleys between ridges. The *vorel*—translucent, warm to the touch, standing three meters at the shoulder on legs like basalt columns. They graze on exposed ore seams, their mouths grinding and re-grinding, breaking down mineral bonds with enzymatic processes that operate at temperatures that would cook organic tissue. They are placid. They ignore humans entirely. They have been known to walk through buildings, slowly, destructively, without apparent malice—simply traversing a route they have followed for ten thousand years, before the building was there, and that they will continue to follow for ten thousand years after it falls.

And in the hot zones, near the vents, the *illi*—small, quick, crystalline things that flash in the light like shattered glass. They swarm. They communicate through piezoelectric clicks that resonate in the stone itself. They are the only creatures on the Ascendant that seem to *pay attention* to human activity, clustering around machinery, watching from ridgelines with formations that might be eyes. The miners tolerate them. The illi, for reasons not understood, are drawn to electrical systems. They will sit on power lines for days, clicking, and when they leave, the current runs 3% stronger. No one knows why.

## The City

It is called Verath, and it has no right to be here.

A city of two hundred thousand, built on a slope so gradual that every street tilts—imperceptibly, constantly—toward the lower horizons. The buildings are made of the stone beneath them, cut and fitted and mortared with mineral pastes that harden into something stronger than the bedrock. They are *low*. Nothing rises above four stories. The wind at this elevation—the constant, slow, warm wind—would tear apart anything taller, given time. And the Ascendant has nothing but time.

Verath exists because of the *seams*.

Beneath the surface, in veins that run for kilometers, are concentrations of *corthunite*—a mineral that does not exist anywhere else on the planet, that may not exist anywhere else in the accessible universe. Corthunite stores electrical potential. Not like a battery. Like a *promise*. When struck, it discharges. When heated under pressure, it discharges. When *asked*, in ways that the engineers have learned to formulate through processes they describe as more negotiation than extraction, it discharges—cleanly, massively, in currents that can power the machinery of a city for a year from a vein the size of a man's forearm.

The seams are mined through shafts that descend at angles, following the corthunite deeper into the rise. The work is dangerous—not because of the mineral, which is inert until activated, but because of the things that live in the deep stone. The *benthic* sketh, vastly larger than their surface cousins, moving through solid rock at rates measurable in centimeters per year, their bodies generating heat and pressure that can collapse a tunnel without warning. The miners listen for them. The stone speaks, when you know how to hear it.

---

## The Industry

The city's industry is *refinement*.

Corthunite comes out of the ground raw, veined with impurities, shot through with fissures that make it unpredictable. The factories—long, low structures along the city's eastern edge, their roofs thick with accumulated selifrith—process it. Crush it. Sort it by resonance frequency. Cut it into usable cores. Pack it into shipping containers that will be loaded onto the *crawlers*, the massive multi-wheeled vehicles that descend the rise over weeks, carrying corthunite to the lowlands where water still falls from the sky and people still drink it.

The machinery is *loud* by the standards of the Ascendant, which means it produces a low, constant thrum that can be felt in the teeth. The locals find it soothing. Visitors find it maddening—the way it never stops, the way it vibrates in the chest cavity, the way it seems to come from the stone itself rather than from any visible source. The engines run on corthunite, of course. Everything here runs on corthunite. The city is a closed loop of extraction and consumption, a thing that eats its own foundation and powers itself with the digestion.

Electricity flows through Verath in patterns that the illi follow. Streetlights flicker at frequencies that correspond to their clicking. The miners say the illi are *praying* to the current. The engineers say they are *communing* with the corthunite. The scientists say nothing, because they have been measuring the phenomenon for thirty years and the data refuses to resolve into anything that resembles a hypothesis.

## The Quiet

And yet—quiet.

The vast, enveloping quiet of a place where sound dies in the dry air before it can travel far. The sketh grind, but too slowly to hear. The vorel walk, but each step takes seconds, and the impact is absorbed by the stone. The illi click, but at frequencies that register as sensation rather than sound. The factories thrum, but at the edge of perception. The wind moves, but without moisture to carry, it makes only the faintest whisper across the selifrith.

What you hear, in Verath, is breathing. Your own. The person next to you. The slow respiration of a city that exists at the pace of stone.

Conversations happen in low voices. Not because of secrecy, but because sound feels *intrusive* here. The Ascendant does not shout. It does not demand. It simply *is*, and the people who live on it have learned to match its register. Arguments in Verath are conducted in murmurs. Declarations of love are barely louder than breath. Children learn early that a whisper carries further than a shout in dry air—that the selifrith transmits vibration in ways that reward restraint.

At night, when the factories slow and the crawlers rest and even the illi fall silent, you can hear the land itself. A low, almost subsonic groan. The rise, still climbing, still pushing upward at rates that will be measurable only in geological time. The stone, settling under its own impossible weight. The warmth, radiating upward from depths where the mineral veins glow with corthunite's stored lightning.

It sounds like a sleeping animal. It sounds like a held breath. It sounds like patience.

## The Rules

There are rules, here on the Ascendant. Not laws—those exist too, mundane and bureaucratic—but *rules*. Things that are true because the land makes them true.

*You do not dig straight down.* The benthic sketh travel vertically. A shaft that descends without angle is a shaft that will, eventually, be occupied.

*You do not touch the vorel.* Not because they are dangerous—they are not, or no more than any creature that weighs several tonnes and does not notice you—but because contact with their skin leaves a residue that causes the selifrith to adhere, to build up, to calcify. Miners who have touched vorel develop stone-bonded plaques on their hands within hours. It does not kill. But it does not stop, either.

*You do not follow the illi.* They lead, sometimes. Away from the city, out along the ridges, toward the deep interior of the rise. Those who follow them return—most of them—but they return *different*. Their eyes are clearer. Their hearing is sharper. They can taste the corthunite in the stone beneath their feet, and they know, without being told, which seams are rich and which are exhausted. They also stop sleeping. Not immediately. Over the course of years. And after enough years without sleep, they begin to *hum* at the frequency of the stone, and the illi gather around them in reverent formations, and eventually—quietly, without fuss—they walk into the deep shafts and do not come back.

*You do not ask the corthunite for more than you need.* This is the rule the engineers enforce most strictly, and the one most difficult to explain to outsiders. Corthunite *responds* to intention. When you extract it with greed—with the desire for more, for excess, for accumulation beyond requirement—it gives freely, lavishly, dangerously. Veins that should yield a year's power will empty themselves in hours, flooding the extraction systems with current that melts conduits and kills operators. And then they go *quiet*. The mineral, the stone, the entire geological formation withdraws into something that is not silence but *refusal*. The corthunite will not discharge again—not here, not for decades, perhaps not for centuries. The seams must be abandoned. The shafts sealed. The city moves to new veins, always moving, always following the mineral's willingness to be found.

The miners have a word for this: *vethar*. It means *offended stone*. It also means *the consequence of taking without asking*. It also, in older dialects, means *the face of something that has decided you are not welcome*.

## The Horizon

From the highest ridge above Verath, where the surveyors maintain their instruments and the illi gather to watch the sunset turn the selifrith to gold, you can see the edge of comprehension.

Not the horizon—that is simply the curve of the planet, the limit of sight. What you can see is the *scale*. The rise continues. Beyond Verath, beyond the mining shafts, beyond the crawling paths of the benthic sketh, the land keeps tilting upward into regions where even the mineral economy thins and the stone grows hot enough to deform. No one has been there. The crawlers cannot make the grade. The air grows thick with sulfur and thin with everything else. The illi will not go.

But the vorel do. In ones and twos, across the long months, the great transparent creatures turn from their ancient grazing routes and begin the slow climb toward the interior. They do not return. Whatever they find there—whatever has always been there, at the impossible summit of an endless rise—remains unknown.

The surveyors have measured it. The ascent continues for at least another two thousand kilometers before their instruments fail, before the heat and the pressure and the strange magnetic fields generated by corthunite deposits of unimaginable size render measurement meaningless. And beyond that? More rise. More stone. More warmth, rising from below, patient and ancient and indifferent.

The Ascendant does not end. It simply becomes too vast for anyone to remain who could report back.

---

## What Remains

What remains, in the end, is the dust.

Selifrith, settling and lifting, settling and lifting. The respiration of a continent. The waste product of creatures that eat stone and breathe sulfur, going about their mineral lives in warm, dry silence. The medium through which light diffuses and sound carries and the illi pray to currents they may have invented.

The dust gets into everything. Into the machines, despite the filters. Into the food, despite the processing. Into the blood, despite the body's defenses. After enough years in Verath, you become part of the place—not metaphorically, but chemically, mineralogically, the selifrith embedding itself in your tissue, your bone, the electrical patterns of your thoughts.

The old miners say this is not a bad thing. They say the dust *remembers*. That it carries imprints of every creature that has ever moved through it, every step of every vorel crossing, every sketh grinding, every illi-clicking communication, every human footfall on the long slow rise. That when it enters you, you enter the Ascendant's memory, and it enters yours, and the boundary between person and place becomes—like everything else here—gradual, patient, and too slow to perceive.

They may be right. They may be telling stories to make the silence feel less like emptiness and more like *listening*.

Either way, the dust continues to breathe. The stone continues to rise. The warm air moves reluctantly across a continent that has never known rain, and the city of Verath continues its patient work of extraction, and the corthunite continues to glow in its deep veins, and the illi continue their observances along the power lines, and somewhere, far above and beyond the reach of measurement, the vorel walk toward something that has no name, their translucent bodies catching the light of a sun that shines on a world that does not need water to be alive.

The quiet holds.
