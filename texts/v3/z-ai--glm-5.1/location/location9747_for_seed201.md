# The Cirque of New Teeth

The first thing is the light.

It comes from everywhere and nowhere — no filtered green, no dappled shade, no gentle diffusion. Just raw white morning pouring through a gap in the peaks above, striking the cirque floor with the clarity of something newly made. At this elevation the atmosphere barely exists to soften it. Shadows fall in hard lines. Every edge announces itself.

The cirque itself is small — you could walk its perimeter in the time it takes a campfire to catch. A bowl bitten from the mountainside, perhaps three hundred feet across, walled on three sides by stone that still carries the claw-marks of the glacier that carved it. That ice is gone now, retreated beyond the lip, and what it left behind is so freshly exposed it almost hums with the shock of air. The rock is granite, light grey and feld-flecked, its faces unweathered, its corners still sharp enough to cut a hand. No lichen scale. No rounding. No patience of age. The stone looks like it was broken yesterday, and in geological terms, it was.

---

Despite the cold — and the cold is serious, the kind that enters through the knuckles and stiffens the jaw — the cirque is *green*.

Not the pale frost-resignation of alpine scrub. Not the gray-green of lichen making do. This is a genuine lushness, almost tropical in its enthusiasm, and utterly wrong for the altitude. Cushion plants cluster in dense mounds, their leaves waxy and dark, pressing against the rock surfaces as if trying to warm them. Mosses carpet the lower walls in thick mats, emerald and black-green, holding moisture like sponges. Small flowers — saxifrages, the size of a thumbnail, white-petaled with centers of furious yellow — emerge from crevices in absurd abundance. The effect is a garden that has no business being here.

The water arrives invisibly. Each night, condensation freezes across the stone in thin films. Each morning, the bright light releases it, and the moisture runs down the granite faces in a slow weeping — not a stream, not a waterfall, just a persistent damp that keeps the moss swollen and the cushion plants turgid. In the lowest point of the cirque, where the floor dips, a shallow pool collects, no larger than a bath, its surface rippled by cold wind. By afternoon, most of the moisture has evaporated or been drunk. By nightfall, the condensation begins again. A place that waters itself by breathing.

---

The animals are everywhere, and they are silent.

Pikas dart between the cushion plants, their small bodies low and fast, but they do not call. In lower meadows, pikas shriek and chatter — here, they move like they're holding a secret. Marmots occupy the talus at the cirque's edge, their honey-brown bodies visible on the warm rocks, but they do not whistle. Even the wind comes thin and hushed, stripped of force by the surrounding walls, making only a faint pressure-sound against the stone.

Snow hares move through the moss in the early hours, their white feet leaving no impression. Above, soaring on the thermal that rises from the sun-heated rock, an eagle turns in tight circles — but the cirque is too small for extended flight, and after a few revolutions it peels away over the lip, seeking wider sky. A weasel, white in winter coat, observes the pool from beneath a shelf of granite, its eyes two black points in a face made expressionless by cold adaptation. There are insects, too — small dark flies that seem to live their entire lives in the sun-warmed zone above the moss line, born and dying in the same patch of light.

The ecosystem is running, cycling, alive. Birth and death and pursuit compressed into a space the size of a modest church. But the compression does not create density. Each creature occupies its own radius. The distance between the marmot and the weasel is perhaps forty feet — a trivial space in lowland terms — but here, at this altitude, in this silence, it feels like country. The emptiness is not an absence but a kind of spacing. A decision the place has made about how much presence it can tolerate per cubic foot.

---

On the eastern wall, where the morning light strikes first, someone has built a cairn.

It is not large — knee-high, maybe, constructed from the same sharp granite that litters the cirque floor. The stacking is careful but not skilled. The stones balance through patience rather than shaping. It could be a waypoint, a marker, an offering, or simply the work of hands that needed to do something in a place that demands response.

Below the cairn, a wind-break: three walls of stacked stone, chest-high, open to the west. No roof, no mortar, no smoke-stain. It has been here long enough for moss to colonize its base but not long enough for the mortar-free joints to settle. Inside, the stone floor is slightly warmer than the open cirque — a trick of the construction, or perhaps the body-heat memory of whoever sheltered here.

No other structures. No tools. No debris. Just the cairn, the wind-break, and in a crevice near the pool, a piece of worked bone — a fish-hook, absurdly, or something like one, carved from the rib of something large, with a hole bored through its wider end. No fish within miles. No water deep enough to fish in. The bone has been there long enough to yellow and crack, but not long enough to decompose. It fits in the palm. Its purpose is not recoverable.

---

The cirque runs on cycles that don't quite synchronize.

The condensation-freeze-thaw cycle happens daily. The pika and marmot cycles of forage and retreat happen on their own schedules. The weasel's hunting follows the pika, but with a lag — it arrives where the pika was, not where it is. The eagle's thermal depends on the sun heating the rock, which depends on the light angling correctly, which shifts as the earth turns. The moss grows steadily, a millimeter a year perhaps, but the stone degrades faster, its sharp edges softening imperceptibly as the water finds its way into micro-fractures and expands in the nightly freeze.

The place is young and it is busy. It will not be this way for long — geologically speaking, a few thousand years before the edges round, the vegetation stabilizes, the animals reach equilibrium. Right now, everything is still arriving, still establishing, still surprised to be here. The rock is still getting used to being touched. The flowers are still getting used to blooming. The light — the light is the only thing that has been here all along, and it has no patience for any of it. It just falls, hard and white and present, and waits for the cirque to catch up.

---

If you stand in the center, where the pool gathers and dissipates and gathers again, you can feel the place working. Not mystically — physically. The cold pushes down through your feet. The light presses on your closed eyelids, red-gold. The silence is so complete you can hear your own blood moving, and beneath it, the faint click and settling of stone adjusting to its new exposure. The cirque is a room that has just been unlocked, and everything inside it is still deciding where to stand.
