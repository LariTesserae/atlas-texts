#The Plateau of Congregations

---

The first thing you notice is the sound. Not a single sound but a composite—one vast fabric of noise woven from ten thousand threads, so constant it becomes a kind of silence of its own. The deep bass thrum of engines somewhere below. The drip and rush of water finding its thousand paths down vertical surfaces. And over it all, layered like a blanket, the chittering. The clicking. The soft, methodical sound of millions of mandibles working, moving, being alive.

You are high. The air tells you this even before your eyes do—not thin in the way mountain air is thin, but dense and wet and warm, pressing against you like a hand. This is the paradox of the place: you stand at elevation where clouds should pass below you, yet the atmosphere here has decided to stay. It condenses on every surface. It beads on the railings, runs in slow rivulets down the walls, collects in the grooves between paving stones until every horizontal surface shines with a skin of water that never quite evaporates. The warmth comes from below, from the infrastructure itself—the boilers and furnaces and exhaust systems that breathe heat into the plateau like a body maintaining its temperature. The clouds do pass below, sometimes. When they do, you can look out from the upper galleries and see them drifting like white rivers through the lower districts, and for a moment you understand where you are: above the world's weather, inside weather of your own making.

---

## The City That Grew

They did not build this city all at once. That is clear from the way it stands. No planner arranged these streets, no architect drew these lines. The structures climb and lean against one another like a crowd that has stood too long in one place—each building settling its weight into its neighbor, finding stability through proximity. Brick and iron and poured concrete, materials that repeat with small variations across the entire plateau, as though the place was constructed from a vocabulary of only a dozen words and those words were combined into sentences that ran on and on until they became a language no one remembers inventing.

The oldest sections are at the center, where the erosion is most visible. Not decay—erosion. The difference matters. Decay implies neglect, but these walls have not been neglected. They have been *worn*, the way a stone in a river is worn, by the passage of time and water and contact. The edges of doorframes have softened. The steps in stairwows have dipped in the middle where generations of feet have pressed them down. The iron railings are smooth from handling, their original paint long since become a memory, then a stain, then nothing at all. The buildings here have arrived at their shape the way a landscape arrives at its shape—through the patient work of forces too slow to witness.

Outward from the center, the city gets younger, and the edges get sharper. Newer construction, less weathered, though "new" is relative. Even the outermost districts have stood for longer than living memory. The whole plateau has the quality of something that has finished becoming what itself. It is not growing. It is not declining. It simply *is*, the way a mountain is.

---

## The Water and the Ways It Moves

Water is everywhere here, but it does not behave as water behaves elsewhere. In other cities, rain falls and drains away. Here, the water rises. The plateau sits above an aquifer so pressurized that water seeps upward through the stone itself, emerging in weeps and seeps across every vertical surface. The walls weep. The pillars weep. The undersides of bridges drip with a steady, rhythmic patience that becomes a kind of timekeeping if you listen long enough.

In the lower galleries, where the infrastructure runs, the water collects into shallow channels cut into the floors—some carved deliberately, some worn by the water's own insistence over decades. These channels join into streams that join into rivers that flow through tunnels beneath the city, and the sound of this subterranean drainage is a constant low murmur, like a conversation happening in the next room, just below the threshold of comprehension.

After the rains—and it rains frequently, warm sudden downpours that appear without warning and leave without ceremony—the whole city shines. Every surface becomes a mirror. The light, which is ordinary daylight, gray and white and sometimes gold when the sun finds a gap, gets multiplied and scattered until the air itself seems to glow with a diffuse, sourceless illumination. On those days, walking through the streets is like walking inside a cloud that has learned geometry.

Condensation is the city's most patient sculptor. Where water evaporates and re-condenses daily, it leaves behind mineral deposits—calcium, iron, sulfur—that build up in fractal patterns on walls and columns. These growths are white, orange, rust-colored, and they give the older buildings an organic quality, as though the city is slowly growing a shell. Or shedding one. It is hard to tell the difference.

## The Congregations

And then there is the fauna.

They are not wildlife in any sense that word implies. They are not visitors to this place. They are *residents*—more resident, perhaps, than the people, who came later and built around them. The creatures have been here since before the plateau was a city, and they have remained through every phase of construction, adapting to each new architecture as it rose around them, incorporating the infrastructure into their lives with a pragmatism that borders on the architectural.

There are many species. That is important to say, because the word "fauna" might suggest a single dominant creature, and that would be inaccurate. There are dozens—perhaps hundreds—of distinct kinds, and they exist in such numbers that the word "overwhelming" is not hyperbole but description. You cannot walk a street without stepping near them. You cannot stand still without them climbing past you. You cannot sleep without hearing them.

The largest are the carapace laborers—creatures the size of large dogs, segmented and many-legged, with shells that range from black to deep amber. They move slowly, deliberately, carrying debris or building material or food through the streets in processions that follow routes so old the stone is worn into grooves beneath their feet. They do not acknowledge humans. They do not avoid them. They simply continue, with a patience that matches the city's own. When a carapace laborer encounters an obstacle—a person, a cart, a fallen beam—it waits. It does not go around. It waits for the obstacle to move, and eventually the obstacle moves, because the laborer will wait longer than anything else will stay.

The smallest are almost too small to see—grain-sized organisms that live in the wet surfaces of the walls, in the mineral deposits, in the condensation itself. They give the city's damp walls a faint shimmer when the light hits them correctly, a living iridescence that shifts and pulses with a rhythm too slow to track in a single glance. If you press your hand to a wall and pull it away, your palm is left with a residue of warmth and motion, as though you have touched something that is breathing.

Between these extremes are the congregations that give the plateau its name: vast assemblies of mid-sized creatures that gather in the open spaces—the courtyards, the wide streets, the factory floors that have been abandoned long enough for the fauna to claim them. These gatherings serve purposes that no external observer has definitively identified. The creatures arrange themselves in patterns—concentric circles, spirals, long parallel rows—and remain in these formations for hours or days, clicking softly to one another in frequencies that overlap with the industrial hum of the machinery below. Sometimes a congregation will begin to move in unison, rotating slowly like a living mechanism, and the sound of their movement is like the sound of a vast clock ticking. Then they stop. Then they remain. Then, eventually, they disperse, and the space they occupied is marked with a residue—chemical, organic, mineral—that stains the floor in the exact pattern of their arrangement. These stains accumulate over time, layer upon layer, and in the oldest courtyards the ground is a palimpsest of congregations, each one visible as a ghost beneath the next.

The people of the plateau have learned to live alongside these creatures with a matter-of-factness that would seem like indifference if it were not, in its own way, a form of respect. You do not disturb a congregation. You do not harm the laborers. You do not try to eradicate the wall-shimmerers, because they are part of the building now, and removing them would be like removing the mortar from between bricks. The fauna are infrastructure. They are architecture. They are the plateau, as much as the stone and the iron and the water.

## The Machines

Below the streets, in the deep galleries where the water flows and the heat rises, the engines run. They have always run. The people who currently operate them inherited them from people who inherited them from people who inherited them, and no one alive knows who built them first or what purpose they were originally meant to serve. They run because they run. They produce heat and vibration and a low electrical current that is distributed through the city's wiring—wiring that has been spliced and extended and repaired so many times that its original pattern exists only as a rumor in the oldest infrastructure maps, themselves so faded they are nearly illegible.

The engines are not efficient. They consume fuel—a dense, dark petroleum that seeps up from wells beneath the plateau—and they produce waste, and the waste is processed through systems that are themselves ancient, and the byproducts of that processing are vented through stacks that reach above the rooftop level, where they dissipate into the perpetual dampness and come down eventually as a fine, warm rain that smells of sulfur and iron and something else, something organic, something that might be the dissolved excretions of the congregations above.

The factories that use the engines' power produce goods—hardware, fittings, pipe sections, bricks, wire—that are used to maintain and slowly extend the city. There is no export. There is no external trade. The plateau is self-contained, a closed system that has been running long enough to reach equilibrium. The things that are made are used to repair the things that are wearing out, and the things that are wearing out are worn out at roughly the same rate as the things that are being made, and so the city stays the same size, the same shape, the same population, year after year after year. The slowest kind of dynamic. A pace of change that can only be measured across generations.

---

## The Light and the Time

Daylight here is ordinary but filtered. The atmosphere's density—the water, the warmth, the haze of vented steam—softens the sun's edge. Shadows do not have hard boundaries. Instead, they graduate, blurring into the illuminated spaces beside them, so that every corner of the city exists in a state of partial shadow, partial light. Nothing is fully bright. Nothing is fully dark. This gives the plateau a quality of existing in perpetual late afternoon, even at midday, and the people who live here have adapted their sense of time accordingly. They do not measure the day by the sun's position. They measure it by the schedule of the engines, which run in cycles as regular and indifferent as the turning of the earth.

Night falls, and the city does not darken so much as deepen. The electrical current powers lights—old filaments in glass housings, mounted on walls and suspended on wires across the wider streets—and these give off a warm, amber glow that catches the wet surfaces and multiplies. The water reflects the light; the creatures reflect the light; the mineral deposits on the walls catch and hold the light and release it slowly, so that even after the filaments are switched off, the city retains a faint luminescence, like an afterimage, like a memory of brightness that lingers in the stone.

At night the congregations grow louder. The clicking intensifies. The carapace laborers continue their processions under the amber glow, their shells clicking against the wet stone with a sound like a hundred small clocks being wound at once. And somewhere in the lower galleries, the engines maintain their rhythm, and the water maintains its flow, and the heat rises through the floors, and the whole plateau sits in its own atmosphere, above the clouds, inside its weather, patient and vast and alive in ways that have nothing to do with the people who live in it and everything to do with the fact that it has been here long enough to become a thing in itself—not a city, not a habitat, not a machine, but a single entity composed of stone and iron and water and warmth and creatures and time, all of it working together in the slowest possible way, maintaining itself, balancing itself, continuing.

---

You stand at the edge of a high gallery, where the railing is worn smooth by a century of hands, and you look out across the rooftops toward the horizon, and the plateau stretches in every direction, vast and dense and layered, and the mist rises from the streets below, and the congregations click in their courtyards, and the water runs down the walls beside you, and the sound is everywhere and constant and after a while you stop hearing it, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat, because it is not a sound in the landscape. It *is* the landscape. It is the sound the plateau makes by existing.
