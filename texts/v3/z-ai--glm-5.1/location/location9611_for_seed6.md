# The Cradle Tank

It was built last Tuesday. Or last century. Time moves strangely when everything is already submerged.

---

Somewhere — below, above, behind — there is a floor. You have never seen it. What you know is this: a cathedral-sized volume of warm water, enclosed entirely by manufacture. The walls curve upward and inward, ribbed with structural pilasters of extruded ceramic, each one seeded along its length with irrigation channels that weep a constant mineral suspension into the surrounding sea. The water is never still but moves only slowly — convection cells rotating over hours, carrying warmth from the heating elements buried in the architecture, drawing cooler currents down along the walls where the vegetation grows most thickly.

And it grows. God, it grows.

Every surface that is not actively maintained as a working channel is claimed. The pilasters wear collars of broad-leaved *something* — not quite angiosperm, not quite fern, each leaf the size of a dinner plate and layered so densely the ceramic beneath is only rumor. Root systems have colonized the irrigation channels entirely, drinking directly from the nutrient flow, and where roots meet they fuse into dense mats that become substrate for more growth. Cascades of vine-like growth drape between the pilasters like the webbing of some insane spider, each strand thick as a wrist, surface textured with tiny suction-cup leaves that grip the moisture from the passing water. At the junctions where structural members meet, growth has accumulated into masses the size of automobiles — composting internally, generating their own warmth, sprouting fungi and liverworts and things that are neither from crevices wet and dark.

It is all construction. There is no natural rock here, no sediment, no substrate that was not placed by intention. The ceramic walls are printed in layers still visible if you press close enough — fine striations running in parallel lines, the signature of the fabricators. The pilasters are bolted to cross-members forged and milled. The irrigation system runs on pumps — four of them, redundant, mounted in housings at the cardinal points, each one a drum of brushed steel and copper winding, clicking softly through their rotations. Click. Click. Click. The loudest sound in the space. You can hear them from anywhere, a mechanical heartbeat so regular it becomes landscape.

The light comes from the walls themselves — or rather, from panels set into the walls between the pilasters, behind the growth. They glow with the steady warmth of midmorning sun, frequency-tuned to photosynthetic wavelengths, and the leaves have oriented themselves toward each one with a precision that looks deliberate. Shadows exist only where growth is so dense that light cannot penetrate — interior volumes of the larger plant-masses, dark and warm and rotting slowly.

It is high. This is the strangeness you must adjust to. The space sits atop something — a tower, a pillar, a platform rising from depths you cannot see. The water surrounding the structure is open and vast, oceanic, and if you found a window (there are none) you would see only blue fading to black. But inside the Cradle Tank, the water is warm and close and held. The walls are the world. The depth is only a story the pressure tells at the seams.

No fish visit. No crustaceans colonize the root-mats. No worms work the compost. The ecosystem is plant and machine only, and the machine is patient. The pumps circulate. The lights glow. The nutrient channels weep. The growth consumes and grows and dies and becomes substrate for more growth, and the cycle is so slow a human observer would need weeks to notice change.

But change accumulates. The pilasters bear more weight each month. The root-mats thicken. A pump will fail eventually — not this year, not next, but in the measure of the place's time — and when it does, the growth in its quadrant will yellow and thin and die back, and the dying will open light to seeds that had been waiting in the dark, and something new will rise.

The fabricators that built this place are not present. Were they ever? The construction has the feel of a print job left running — the machine gone on to other tasks, the product slowly filling its designated volume, overgrowing the specifications. The vegetation was intended. The density was not. Or perhaps it was. Perhaps this is exactly what a cradle is for: to fill completely, to become so packed with life that the walls strain, and then to crack, and then to spill its contents into the open water outside.

But that will take years. Decades. The pumps click. The light holds steady. The leaves turn in their slow heliotropes, and the roots drink, and the mass of growing things gets incrementally heavier, and the warm water circulates around it all like a long breath that has not yet been released.

It is quiet. It is full. It is waiting.
