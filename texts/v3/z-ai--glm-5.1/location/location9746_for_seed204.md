# The Pocket City of Condensation

You enter through a fissure most maps would never mark — a tall, narrow split in granite at somewhere above four thousand meters, where the rock face gives way not to more rock but to a cavity the size of a small district. The city sits inside the mountain like a geode's crystal cavity: unexpected, organized, and wet.

---

The first thing is the condensation. Every surface weeps. Not dramatically — no torrents or waterfalls — but persistently, as though the stone itself has a slow, constant tearsheet. The air inside the pocket holds temperature differently than the world outside; the mountain's cold seeps through the walls while something deep beneath maintains a slight differential, and that difference manifests as water. It beads on the faceted ceilings. It traces the angled supports of the walkways. It runs in micro-rills down the glass-smooth panels of the habitation blocks, collecting in channels cut precisely for this purpose — though whether the channels were cut for the water, or the water came because the channels were cut, is the kind of question the residents stopped asking long ago.

The water goes somewhere. That is the city's primary process. You can hear it if you stand still: a low, distributed trickling, like rain on a roof that never stops, layered with the deeper hum of whatever infrastructure moves the water from collection channels to storage to use to release. The sound is constant but not loud — it lives at the threshold of notice, something your ears accept as background within minutes and then forget until you focus again.

---

There are no plants. This is the second thing a visitor registers after the wetness — the absence of green, of photosynthetic life, of anything that grows by sunlight and cellular division in the familiar way. The pocket receives light through the fissure and through embedded luminous panels in the ceiling that simulate a daylight cycle, and the light is adequate, normal in quality, but nothing uses it the way plants would. The surfaces that aren't structural or habitational are bare mineral: the granite of the cavity wall, dark and freshly faced, the edges still sharp where the space was opened. This is young stone. The fracture patterns suggest the pocket was excavated rather than naturally formed — or if naturally formed, then finished, squared, made architectural by intent.

What lives here instead of plants are processes. The channels carry water. The panels emit light. The habitation blocks — narrow, tall, spaced apart with deliberate emptiness between them — maintain their interiors at temperatures just above uncomfortable. The infrastructure is the ecosystem. Nothing blooms but everything runs.

The architecture is sparse by design or necessity. There are perhaps forty structures in the pocket, each one a slender tower of dark composite material anchored to the cavity floor and braced against the ceiling or walls where the geometry demands it. They stand with significant distance between them — you could walk between any two and have room to forget the adjacent buildings existed. The emptiness is not vacancy but the negative space of an organized system: air flows through those gaps, water vapor circulates, the condensation has room to form and fall and collect without being intercepted by cluttered construction.

The towers are not uniform but they share a language. Flat planes angled to catch and direct water. External conduits and channels that make the fluid routing visible from outside. Windows that fog and clear with the micro-weather of the pocket. The materials are beyond what you would expect for a settlement at this altitude in this cold — something in the composites resists ice formation, maintains flexibility at temperatures that should make them brittle, conducts something (heat? data? both?) through veins that pulse faintly under the surface when you watch long enough.

---

The cold is serious but not hostile. It sits at the edge of survivability for an unprepared human — the kind of cold that would kill you slowly if you stood still, that makes your breath visible, that puts frost on any surface where the condensation lingers too long. But the city manages it. The structures are heated. The walkways between them are sheltered by retractable windbreaks that deploy from housings along the railings, creating covered passages when the draft through the fissure turns sharp. The residents move quickly and purposefully between buildings, dressed in layers that look designed rather than improvised, fabrics that might be thermally active at the fiber level.

There are not many residents. You might see a dozen during a full cycle of the light panels. They do not linger outside. The pocket is a working place, and the work is maintenance — adjusting flow rates in the channels, replacing panels that have fogged permanently, checking the anchor points where the towers meet the stone. They move with the economy of people who inhabit infrastructure rather than community. Or perhaps the community is the infrastructure itself, and what looks like solitude is actually a form of distributed presence, each person a node in a system too large for any single awareness to hold.

---

At the pocket's deepest point, where the cavity narrows and the ceiling angles down toward a junction of structural supports, there is a sound beneath the other sounds: a low vibration, felt more than heard, that suggests something very large and very deep is operating far below the floor. This is where the water goes. This is possibly where the heat differential originates. The residents do not go there, or if they do, they do not talk about it in the quiet, functional way they talk about channel maintenance and panel replacement.

The pocket city does not explain itself. It functions. Water condenses, is collected, is used, is released or stored or processed in ways the architecture makes visible but the technology does not fully disclose. The residents maintain the systems. The mountain holds the cold. The fissure breathes air from the outside world, which arrives already chilled and leaves slightly warmer, slightly wetter, carrying the faint mineral smell of the place out into the atmosphere above the altitude where anything grows.

At night — when the light panels dim to a deep amber that makes the condensation glow on every surface like a city of constant, quiet candles — you can stand on one of the walkways and look down the long axis of the pocket and see the whole system at work: the water moving, the towers standing in their patient spacing, the wet dark stone catching light from angles that make it look like something frozen in the moment before it became something else. It is beautiful in the way a machine can be beautiful when it has no audience and runs perfectly anyway. It is lonely in the way a room is lonely when it was built for a purpose you can sense but not name.

The city goes on collecting water from the air. The mountain goes on being cold. The residents go on maintaining what they maintain. And somewhere below, the deep machinery hums its single long note, and the condensation forms again, and falls, and is gathered, and the cycle continues, each pass through the system slightly different from the last, the way a clock is slightly different each tick even though it measures the same interval — the same and not the same, the same and not the same, the wet stone weeping and the cold air holding and the pocket city doing whatever it is that pocket cities do when they are built into mountains at the edge of the livable world and left to run.
