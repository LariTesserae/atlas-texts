# The Birthing Chamber

It begins with the sound.

A rhythmic wet percussion—somewhere between heartbeat and hammerfall—that you feel in your spine before your ears register it. The sound has weight. It presses against your chest like a hand.

You are inside a pocket. A room. A space that should not exist at this altitude—somewhere above the world, in the thin and freezing places where nothing should grow, where air itself struggles to stay—yet here it is: a chamber no larger than a small shrine, carved or perhaps *grown* into the flank of a peak that has no other name because no one has survived the climbing to name it.

Everything here is made.

There is no natural stone surface, no raw geology. Every centimeter bears the mark of hands—tool marks, yes, the scoring of something like bone or sharpened slate—but also something older and less intentional. The walls have the texture of cartilage. They have been scraped, shaped, hollowed out by someone or something working with the patience of centuries and the desperation of a body trying to excavate itself from the inside. The ceiling is low. You must crouch. The floor is uneven in ways that suggest it was laid down in sections, by a maker who kept changing their mind about what the room was for.

And it is still being made.

You can hear it. That wet percussion—that is construction. Somewhere just beyond the walls, or perhaps *within* them, something is still building. The chamber flinches with each impact. Fine particulate—stone dust, or something like it—drifts from the ceiling with every blow. The room is embryonic. Half-finished. It has the quality of a place that has not yet decided its final shape, and may never. There are alcoves that lead nowhere, shelves carved for objects that do not exist yet, niches that almost fit a hand but not quite—the architecture of a dream that keeps shifting upon waking.

It is hot. Punishingly so. The kind of heat that makes the air feel solid, that turns breath into an act of labor. Heat that has no business at this altitude, where the world outside is ice and wind and nothing. But there is no outside here, really. The chamber is its own climate, its own sealed system, and the heat rises from below—from somewhere under the floor, where the percussion is loudest, where the building happens. The stone—or whatever this material is—radiates warmth like a living thing. Like an incubator.

And the water. Oh, the water.

It is everywhere. Not flowing, not pooled in any sensible way, but *weeping*. The walls exude moisture. The ceiling drips with the regularity of a slow, methodical rain. Condensation forms on every surface the moment you wipe it clear. The air is so saturated that you can taste it—mineral and faintly organic, like the inside of a greenhouse after a storm. Thin rivulets trace the tool marks on the walls, following the grooves left by whatever primitive instrument shaped this place, so that the chamber seems to be crying along the scars of its own making.

In this heat, in this wet, life has erupted.

Not intelligently. Not with the quiet deliberation of an established ecosystem. Vegetation here is *frantic*. Mosses and liverworts carpet the lower walls in thick, dark green mats that pulse visibly with absorbed moisture. Fungal threads—pale, almost luminous—spread across the ceiling in fractal patterns that seem too precise, too *calculated* for natural growth. Small ferns erupt from every crack and crevice, their fronds uncurling so quickly you can almost watch them reach toward the dim light. And the light is dim—a grey-green half-glow that seems to come from the walls themselves, or from the vegetation, or from nowhere at all. You cannot find the source. You stop looking.

There are no animals. Not really. Once, you think you see movement in the corner of your eye—a rapid skittering, something small and jointed retreating into a fissure—but when you look, there is only moss, only wet stone, only the deep green insistence of growing things. The chamber is too young, perhaps, for fauna. Or too wrong. The few living things that might survive here have not found it yet, or have found it and fled.

Because the chamber does not behave.

The geometry is wrong in ways that accumulate. You turn and the room turns with you, or fails to, or turns a moment too late. Distances shift. The wall that was an arm's length away becomes two, then half, then simply *uncertain*—you cannot tell if you are touching it or not. The tool marks on the stone sometimes spell something in a language that was never spoken. When you close your eyes, the percussion sound is louder, as though the construction is happening behind your eyelids instead of beyond the walls.

Time does not move correctly here. You have been in the chamber for minutes, perhaps, but the moss has crept three inches up the wall since you arrived. The ferns have doubled in size. Something has grown in the time you have been reading this.

The walls are warmer now.

The percussion continues. Wet. Rhythmic. Patient. Something is still building this place, extending it, hollowing out new chambers that may or may not exist yet, that may already exist behind walls that have not been built, that will be built, that are being built now, have always been built—

You notice a new alcove that was not there before.

It is exactly the size and shape of your body.

The moss is already growing inside it.
