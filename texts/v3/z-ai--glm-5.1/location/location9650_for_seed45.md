# The Ascendant

The first thing you notice is the warmth coming from below.

Not from above—there is no above here that offers anything. The sky, if it can be called that, is a low ceiling of mineral haze, a dark amber bruise pressed down close enough to touch on the higher peaks. No sun resolves behind it. No stars. Just a diffuse, directionless glow, like heat seen through closed eyelids. The light that reaches the ground is the light the ground itself refuses to keep—bounced off wet-black stone and lost.

It is warm the way a kiln is warm. Radiant. Patient.

---

The land has no business being this high.

You are standing on something that is still deciding whether it wants to be a mountain range or a continent. The elevation is obscene—several kilometers above whatever nominal sea level this world once recognized, though the concept feels like a joke here. There is no sea. There may never have been one. The horizons don't behave correctly; they curve too soon, or not soon enough, and the air thins into something that isn't quite air at all. Breathable, but only just. Only provisionally. As if the atmosphere itself was pushed upward by the rising rock and hasn't yet figured out how to leave.

The plateau—and it is a plateau, though the word feels too settled, too finished—stretches in every direction with a scope that makes your eyes ache. Open. Immense. The kind of distance that doesn't resolve into features so much as it accumulates into weight. You can see far enough to watch the stone change its mind.

Because it does change its mind.

---

This is a place that is still being made.

The erosion parameter reads near zero, and you understand why immediately: there hasn't been time. There hasn't been water. There hasn't been wind in the slow, patient, sculpting sense—only gusts that arrive hot and sudden from the geothermal vents, carrying sulfurous breath and nothing else. The rock is raw. Born yesterday, geologically speaking. Some of it is being born right now.

You can hear it, if you stop moving and let the silence settle: a low, constant groan from somewhere deep in the crust. Not the sharp complaint of an earthquake. Something slower. A hectare-scale uplifting. The land mass is still thrusting itself upward, driven by mantle pressures that have no intention of stopping, and the fresh stone emerges through cracks and fissures with the dull authority of something that has nowhere else to go.

The surface is shattered into enormous polygons—basalt columns the size of buildings, still attached at their bases, tilting at angles that shouldn't persist but do. Between them, the new rock wells up dark and vitreous, like cooled glass that hasn't quite decided to be solid. You can see the flow patterns. You can see where the stone was liquid minutes ago, or hours, or days—the timescale is impossible to judge because the heat keeps everything pliable, everything provisional. Edges that should be knife-sharp are instead vaguely softened, not by erosion but by their own residual warmth, still settling into their final shapes.

Nothing here has finished becoming what it will be.

---

Water is absent so completely that the absence has its own texture. Your lips crack within the first hour. Your skin tightens. The air pulls moisture from you with a steady, impersonal greed—not dry like a desert, which at least has the decency to be cold at night, but dry like an oven, like the inside of a furnace that has been running for a thousand years. There is no dew. No frost. No hint that water was ever here or will ever return. The minerals in the stone—iron, sulfur, something dark and dense you can't name—have never known dissolution. They sit in their crystalline lattices, oxidized by heat alone, and they will sit there until the planet's core cools.

Which, given the turbulence, won't be soon.

---

The dynamic is what unsettles you.

Not violence, exactly. Not the theatrical chaos of a volcano or a storm. Something more fundamental. The land reconfigures itself on a timescale you can almost perceive. Walk away from a formation and turn back an hour later, and it will have shifted—not dramatically, not obviously, but the angles will be wrong. A ridge that blocked the eastern view has subsided. A crack you stepped over has widened into a fissure. The surface you're standing on has risen by centimeters, and you only notice because your boot prints from the outbound leg now sit in shallow depressions.

The plateau breathes.

Not metaphorically. The geothermal pulse—the same one pushing the whole mass upward—operates in cycles. You feel them as changes in the warmth beneath your feet, as subtle shifts in the way the amber haze presses down, as long, low vibrations that travel through the stone and into your sternum. During the exhales, the vents open wider. Gas escapes—colorless, mostly odorless, but thick enough that the light dims further and the far features dissolve. During the inhales, the ground tightens. Cracks seal. The fresh stone at the fissures goes glossy and then opaque as it cools and locks.

It is during the inhales that you notice the structures.

---

They are not ruins. Ruins imply that something finished being built before it started falling apart.

These are partial. Incomplete. As if someone—or something—began to construct and was interrupted, or is still in the process of constructing, or builds and dismantles on a schedule too slow for you to perceive.

The first one you find is a low wall, knee-height, made from the same basalt as the surrounding polygon columns but stacked with a deliberation that nature doesn't bother with. Dry-fit. No mortar. The stones are fitted so precisely that you suspect they were shaped by something with a concept of flatness, of right angles. But the wall doesn't enclose anything. It starts at a fissure and ends at a fissure, as if the fissures are the architecture and the wall is merely a line connecting them.

The second structure is more ambitious. A tower—or the base of one, or the idea of one. Two meters tall, perhaps three meters in diameter, hollow inside, with a single low opening facing the direction the heat comes from. The stonework is crude but intentional. You can see where the builders (builders?) used the natural columnar fractures as starting points, augmenting them with placed stones, extending the geometry outward.

There is no tech here. No tools. No marks of metal on stone. Whoever made these either used their hands or used the stone itself—levering, fitting, balancing—in a way that predates the concept of implements. It is the architecture of a species that hasn't invented the hammer but has invented the wall.

Or it is the architecture of something that doesn't need hammers.

---

You find more of them as you walk. Sparse. Scattered. Much space between presences, as the parameters note. A cairn here, aligned with a vent. A shallow basin carved—not quarried, carved—into the glossy new stone near a fissure, its interior polished smooth. A line of standing stones, none taller than your waist, arranged in a curve that might be an arc or might be a spiral; it's hard to tell because three of the stones are still half-embedded in stone that hasn't finished cooling, as if the land is producing them, not eroding around them.

The structures share a logic you can't access. They reference each other—the cairn is visible from the tower, the basin is positioned at the convergence of two fissure-lines—but they don't form a settlement. There's no shelter here. No hearth. No storage. No water source to protect or territorial boundary to mark. The warmth makes shelter unnecessary, and there's nothing to store and no one to defend against.

So what are they?

The weirdness parameter is low—0.6, barely above baseline—and you feel that accuracy. This isn't a place that breaks rules. It's a place that follows rules you haven't learned. The structures aren't alien. They're just... unexplained. The work of something that operates under different pressures, different needs, different assumptions about what a structure is for.

Maybe they're markers. Maybe they're instruments—measuring something about the geothermal pulse that you can't detect. Maybe they're the mineral equivalent of coral, built by an organism you wouldn't recognize as an organism. Maybe they're messages, and the medium is the only part that survived.

Maybe they're still being written.

---

Vegetation is nearly absent, but not entirely. In the warmest spots—directly adjacent to the vents, where the stone reaches temperatures that would blister skin on contact—there is something. Not a plant. Not a fungus, exactly. A film. A coating. A mineral-biological hybrid that exists at the boundary between crystalline growth and organic process.

It's amber-colored, darker than the haze but the same hue, and it spreads across the hot stone in fractal branching patterns that follow the stress lines in the basalt. When you lean close (not too close; the heat is fierce), you can see that the film has texture—fine, regular ridges, like the gills of a mushroom crossed with the lattice of a crystal. It pulses. Slowly. In time with the geothermal exhale.

It isn't alive in any way you'd recognize. It has no cells, no membranes, no DNA. It's a self-organizing mineral process that happens to metabolize heat and sulfur and excrete a silicate residue that looks, under certain conditions, like growth. Whether it qualifies as life depends on where you draw the line, and the line has never been drawn here.

The film is the only thing that moves on its own. Everything else moves by being moved—the stone by the uplift, the gas by the pressure, the haze by the convection. But the film spreads. Retracts. Reconfigures. It's the closest thing this place has to an ecology, and it's an ecology of one.

You notice, after a while, that the structures are always positioned at the edges of the film's territory. Never inside it. Never too far from it.

As if whatever made the structures was aware of the film. Was tracking it. Was building at the boundary.

---

Fauna is absent. No animals. No insects. No scuttling things in the cracks. The silence is not total—the groan of the stone, the hiss of the vents, the subsonic pulse of the mantle below—but it is close to total, and the sounds that exist are all the sounds of geology, not biology. No calls. No footsteps. No breath but your own, and your own sounds wrong here, too wet, too organic, too temporary.

You are the only thing that expires in this place. Everything else is either already mineral or on its way there.

---

The darkness is the final weight.

Not the darkness of night—night implies a day to follow. This is the darkness of a world that doesn't prioritize light. The amber haze filters what little glow reaches the surface from whatever source powers it, and the result is a perpetual crepuscular dimness, a twilight without morning. You can see, but only just. Only enough to navigate. The far features—other ridges, other formations, the faint suggestion of structures on the next plateau over—exist as silhouettes, as darker shapes against the dark.

Your eyes never adjust fully because there's nothing to adjust to. The light level is constant. Unchanging. The warmth is constant. The silence, punctuated by the geological pulse, is constant. The only variable is the land itself, still rising, still cracking, still producing new stone and new fissures and new surfaces for the amber film to colonize.

You realize, eventually, that you've been standing in the same spot for longer than you intended. Not because you're tired. Not because you're lost. Because the place doesn't encourage departure. It doesn't encourage anything. It simply persists, and persistence is contagious, and standing still is easier than moving when the ground might shift beneath you.

The plateau exhales. The vents open. The haze thickens.

In the distance, barely visible, a new structure is emerging from the fresh stone at a fissure. Or an old structure is being revealed as the stone around it subsides. It's impossible to tell the difference here, in a place where everything is still arriving, where the architecture of the land and the architecture of the builders are made of the same material, following the same pressures, obeying the same rules you haven't learned.

You could stay. The warmth would sustain you—hypothermia is impossible here. The air, though thin, would keep you conscious. There is nothing to attack you, nothing to compete with you, nothing to notice you at all.

But there is also nothing to sustain you in the other sense. No water. No food. No softness. No shade. No end to the becoming.

This is a place that is still being born, and it will not wait for you, and it will not hurry for you, and it will not explain itself to you. It is warm, and dark, and high, and raw, and it will go on being those things long after you've stopped being anything at all.

The land inhales.

The fissure closes.

The structure—whatever it was, whatever it is becoming—disappears back into the stone.

And the plateau keeps rising.
