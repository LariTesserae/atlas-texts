# The Rootworks

The sound finds you before the place does.

It comes up through the soles of your boots—a low, continuous vibration that resonates in your molars, your sternum, the fluid behind your eyes. Then the passage opens, and the sound becomes everything. A roaring chord of machinery and pressure and mechanical breath that never ceases, that has not ceased for what must be years. Decades. The sound is so total it stops being heard and becomes simply the condition of the air, a physical fact like temperature or gravity.

---

You are underground. Not in a cave—in a chamber. A chamber so wide the far wall has retreated into suggestion, into a darkness that might be distance or might simply be where the light gives up. The ceiling lifts away too, lost somewhere above, and the space between floor and ceiling is filled with columns.

Roots.

They descend from above like pillars, like buttresses, like the legs of something vast standing on the world's underside. Pale—nearly white—and thick as roadways, they rise from the floor and climb toward the ceiling in parallel, in spirals, in gentle curves that follow nothing visible. The surface of each root is cool and dense, faintly ridged, damp with condensation that beads and runs in slow tracks downward. When you touch one, the cold seeps through your fingertips immediately. Not the sharp cold of frost, but the steady, patient cold of deep stone, of growth that has never known warmth.

The roots are alive. You can feel it—a faint pulse beneath the surface, impossibly slow, the heartbeat of something operating on an annual rhythm rather than a human one. They continue growing. They will continue growing.

Between the root-columns, space opens. Vast, unoccupied space. You can see perhaps sixty meters before the next column rises, and then sixty more, and sixty more—a grid of living architecture that extends past sight, past the reach of the few industrial lamps mounted on pylons driven into the wood. The floor is root too, or rather—a matting of root layered on root, older growth compressed beneath newer, forming a surface that yields slightly underfoot and springs back. The ceiling is the same, the walls where they exist, the overhangs and shelves and ridges. Plant-as-substrate. Plant-as-ground. The only stone is what shows through where the roots haven't yet colonized—sharp-edged, fresh-faced rock that tells you this place is young, newly opened, still being claimed.

---

The water comes from above. Not rain—there is no sky here—but condensation. The cold air meets colder stone, and moisture collects on every surface in a thin film. It gathers in the crotches where roots divide, pooling in shallow basins of pale wood, still and clear and frigid. It drips. Each drop that falls from the ceiling lands with a sound that should be distinct, that should be recognizable as a single event—but the roar swallows it. The machinery swallows everything. You see the drops impact the pools by the ripples they make, by the momentary distortion of the surface, but you cannot hear them. Your ears belong to the sound now.

Some of the pools are larger—cisterns formed by root-growth, natural collection basins that hold water chest-deep and cold enough to numbing. The water tastes of minerals and wood. It tastes of slow things.

---

The industrial infrastructure threads through the roots like parasites in a host body. Pipes run along the columns, bolted into the living wood with heavy hardware that has scarred the surface; around each wound, the root has callused, grown thicker, gripped the pipe like a fist around a wrist. Cable trays follow the same paths, bundled wires descending to junction boxes mounted at floor level—half-consumed by new growth, their metal faces fogged with condensation. The cables carry electricity to places deeper in, further down, to the source of the sound.

There are structures. Scattered, alone, each one an island in the root-grid. A pump station on a platform of treated timber and rusted steel, its engine visible through an open housing, turning over with a mechanical rhythm lost in the greater roar. A shelter with bunks—frost on the blankets, the door hanging open, the wood of the frame already soft with moisture. A tool rack with saws and axes, handles warped. A desk with paper gone translucent and cold. Signs that people were here once. Signs that they are not here now. The root-mat has begun to grow over the shelter's roof, pale tendrils exploring the frost-heaved shingles.

Each structure stands alone. You can walk for twenty minutes between them, through root-columns, through empty damp air, through the unchanging roar, and arrive at another with the relief of a traveler reaching an oasis—only to find it as abandoned as the last. The space between is vast and sparsely inhabited. The place feels depopulated in the way a city feels depopulated in the hours before dawn: not empty, but emptied.

---

The sound comes from below. Or from within. Or from everywhere at once—it's impossible to locate. There are turbines somewhere in the system, driven by water pressure or diesel or the slow force of the roots' own growth pressing against mechanical harvesters. There are processing stations where the root-matter is cut and pulped and compressed, where living tissue becomes material becomes product. The pipes carry this away—upward, presumably, toward a surface that might as well be legend. The scale of the operation is regional. You could walk for hours in any direction and not reach the perimeter of the root-system, not find a wall, not find a boundary between this chamber and the next. They connect through natural passages where the roots forced cracks in the stone, through tunnels blasted or drilled, through vertical shafts where the columns thin and the darkness above becomes absolute.

The noise is worst in the deep places. The chambers closer to the processing stations, where the roots are actively harvested—the cut surfaces are raw and pale, weeping sap that moves so slowly it seems frozen, collecting in drops that might take hours to fall. The machinery there is larger. Crushers. Presses. Conveyor belts that move root-chips toward something hidden behind a wall of living wood and welded steel. The roar becomes a physical pressure, a resistance in the air, and you find yourself pressing forward against it like pushing through water.

---

The ecosystem is nearly closed. The roots pull what they need from the stone, from the percolating water, from the air itself—minerals dissolved and transported upward through miles of living tissue to something vast and photosynthetic on the surface, far above, far away. Here in the dark, the roots simply grow. They grow downward, outward, colonizing. And there are other things.

Small, pale organisms in the spaces between root-columns. Segmented, slow, adapted to the cold. They move across the root-surfaces with deliberate patience, feeding on dead tissue, on the fungal growths that bloom in the damper recesses, on each other. There are few of them—this place does not support abundance—but they are present. A rustling in the dark that might be wind or might be movement. A wet clicking from somewhere near the ceiling that resolves, if you wait long enough, into a pale shape clinging to the root, feeding, existing.

The roots are the substrate. The roots are the architecture. The roots are the ground and the walls and the framework on which everything else exists—the machinery, the scattered structures, the occasional sign of human passage. The roots were here before any of it. They will be here after.

---

Standing in the chamber, the sound filling you, the cold settling into your bones, you begin to understand: this place is not a ruin. It is not a remnant. It is a process. The roots grow. The machines run. The water collects and is pumped away. The cold persists. The sound continues. Each element feeds the others—the roots providing material, the machines providing... what? Direction? Processing? The purpose is unclear. The scale suggests industry, suggests production, suggests a product being extracted and refined and shipped somewhere—but to what end, and for whom, and whether anyone still receives it at the surface, none of that is visible from here.

What is visible: the pale columns extending into darkness. The cold beading on every surface. The pipes and cables running like veins through living wood. The few structures standing alone in the vastness. The sharp-edged stone where the roots haven't reached yet, freshly exposed, waiting. The slow pulse of the roots beneath your hand.

The sound continues. It has always continued. It will continue. The place is patient.
