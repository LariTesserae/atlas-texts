# The Rhizome Continent

There is no ground here.

There is no stone, no soil, no mineral substrate beneath your feet. There is only plant, and plant, and plant again, extending in every direction across a high plateau so vast it generates its own weather patterns, its own atmospheric cells, its own barometric moods. The continent is alive in the most literal sense—it is a single continuous organism, or perhaps a commune of organisms so thoroughly enmeshed that the distinction has long since ceased to matter.

You stand on the surface of it. The word "surface" does inadequate work. You stand on the uppermost layer of a rhizomatic mass that descends—how far? No bore has reached its bottom. The deepest cores return samples still cellular, still metabolizing, still warm with the slow combustion of whatever strange chemistry sustains life without water.

For there is no water. This is the fact that reshapes everything you think you know about biology. No rain. No rivers. No dew. The air carries a humidity so low it strips the moisture from your lips in minutes, yet the plant-throngs around you show no thirst, no wilt, no yearning. They run on something else. Your instruments detect it: a mineral solvent, silicon-based, circulating through their vessels in place of sap. It tastes of copper and ozone when it weeps from broken stems. The old explorers called it lithamnor, the stone-blood. The plants drink geology itself.

---

The landscape unrolls before you in vast shelves and terraces, all formed from the accumulated body of the rhizome. Elevation here is significant—you stand somewhere near two kilometers above what would be sea level, if this world had seas. It doesn't. The plateau rises from nothing, from the same endless plant-mass that continues below the theoretical datum, compressed by its own weight into something approaching bedrock density but never quite ceasing to live. The horizon sits far away, stretched by the height into a pallid ellipse, and everything between here and there is the same material in different configurations.

Sparse, though. That is what surprises visitors. They expect density—jungle, thicket, the choking impenetrability of a rainforest floor. Instead there is *space*. The major structures stand separated by considerable distances, like monuments in a plaza, or pillars in a cathedral whose ceiling has been removed. You can see for kilometers between them. The air is clear, empty, almost painfully vacant of visual clutter. The plant-forms rise from the rhizome mat liketermite mounds or cooling towers, but organic, curved, ribbed, translucent where the light catches them.

And the light. The light is ferocious. A white-blue radiance pours from a sun that hangs lower in the sky than you expect, larger, as though the atmosphere here is thinner—or as though the rhizome's own respiration has altered the spectral composition of what reaches the surface. Everything is illuminated. There are no gentle shadows, only the sharp geometry of light and dark. The plant-surfaces glow where they're thin enough to transmit it; the thicker masses drink the light and return nothing, standing as silhouettes against the brilliant middle distance.

It is cool. Not cold, but cool enough to raise flesh on your arms. The elevation and the planet's orbital position conspire to keep temperatures in a range that feels like late autumn, perpetually. The plant-mass seems to generate its own heat—you feel it rising from the ground through your boots, a temperate warmth that suggests enormous slow processes occurring beneath you—but the air above holds its chill.

---

The sound is the thing that stays with you.

It begins before dawn, or what passes for dawn here: a low vibration that you feel in your chest before you hear it with your ears. By the time the light has strengthened to full intensity, the continent is roaring. The rhizome is *working*. Every major structure is a processing node, and the processing never stops. There are sounds like grinding, like the slow crushing of stone. There are rhythmic thuds that could be pistons, could be heartbeat, could be the footfalls of something very large walking very far away. There are shrieks of tension as cellulose cables contract and release. There are exhalations—great sighing bursts of processed atmosphere venting from sphincters at the tops of the pillar-forms, releasing clouds of something that isn't steam, that dissipates without condensing.

It is deafening. Not in the way a factory is deafening, though the analogy holds: in the way a heartbeat would be deafening if you stood inside the ventricle. The sound is organic, rhythmic, polyrhythmic, cyclical. It has patterns that almost resolve into meaning before dissolving back into noise. After three days you begin to distinguish voices in it—the grinding is separate from the pumping, the pumping is separate from the exhalation, and beneath all of it is a constant low-frequency hum that seems to come from the rhizome itself, from its lowest depths, as though the entire continent is resonating like the body of a cello.

You have to shout to be heard. You have to touch the person you're speaking to, to confirm they're real against the wall of sound. After a week, you stop trying to speak at all. You develop a system of gestures. You begin to understand why the fauna here communicates by color-change and vibration rather than by vocalization.

---

And the fauna is abundant, once you learn to see it.

The animals here are not what you'd expect. Nothing furred, nothing feathered. They are chitinous, translucent, quick. They move in the spaces between the major structures, in the sparse corridors where the light falls unobstructed. Some are small enough to fit in your palm, and these travel in herds of millions, their footsteps contributing their own thin sound to the continent's symphony. Others are large—truly large, the size of vehicles—and these move slowly, almost reverently, pressing their feeding appendages against the rhizome surface and drinking the lithamnor as it rises.

They have no need of water either. They have evolved in concert with the plant-mass, their biochemistry mirror to its own. When they die, they dissolve into the rhizome within hours, consumed so thoroughly that no bone, no shell, no trace remains. The plant-mass takes everything. It is not predatory—not in any active sense—but it is utterly, absolutely efficient. Nothing is wasted. The continent has been running its cycles for geological ages, and it has learned to leave no residue.

The largest fauna—the ones you notice first—are the aerostats. They fill the sky between the pillar-forms, cruising on the thermals that the rhizome generates. They are not birds. They are not anything you have a word for. They are sacs of gas, translucent, veined with darker lines that might be circulatory, trailing feeding tendrils that drag along the rhizome surface like anchors. They pulse with the continent's rhythm. When the sound peaks, they contract; when it ebbs, they expand. They are the most visible evidence that the whole continent breathes.

---

There are no structures here. No buildings, no roads, no marks of habitation. Nothing has been *built*. And yet the continent is industrial, profoundly, comprehensively industrial. The rhizome itself is the industry. The pillar-forms are refineries. The cables that span between them are transport infrastructure. The exhalation vents are exhaust systems. The grinding sounds are extraction—the plant-mass is mining its own depths, pulling lithamnor from lower strata, processing it, distributing it to the growing tips.

This is what unsettles the engineers who visit: the recognition that industry does not require intention. The rhizome has no mind, no plan, no blueprint. It has only response—billions of years of chemical and mechanical response, each growth-form optimizing for throughput, for efficiency, for the continuation of its own processes. The result is industrial. The result is *functioning industry* at a scale that dwarfs anything human hands have constructed. But there is no factory, only the fact of production. There is no worker, only the work.

You can see it most clearly in the turbulent zones, where the rhizome is actively reshaping itself. New pillar-forms erupt from the mat over the course of hours—not slowly, not gradually, but explosively, thrusting upward with enough force to shake the ground for kilometers. Old structures collapse inward, their structural cellulose reabsorbed, their processing capacity redistributed to the new growth. It is chaotic. It is *anthill energy*, the frantic purposeful motion of agents with no central command. Creation and destruction occurring simultaneously, continuously, across the visible horizon. The continent is never the same shape twice. Every hour, every minute, it reconfigures.

The turbulence is most intense at the edges of the plateau, where the rhizome is actively *expanding*—pushing into territory it has not yet colonized, growing over itself, building new ground from nothing. But even here, in the established center, in the weathered ancient zones where the forms have stood for millennia, there is constant renewal. The balance is not stasis. The balance is a dynamic equilibrium, a standing wave, a flame that keeps its shape while its substance changes utterly.

---

This place is old. The erosion parameter—the geologists prefer "establishment"—is extreme. The rhizome has been doing this for a very long time. The forms here are settled, weathered, self-consistent. They have found the shapes that work and they repeat them with variation, like music with a theme. The pillar-forms are not identical—they cannot be, in a system without central planning—but they are *recognizable*, *categorized*, as though the rhizome has discovered an optimal architecture and refines it endlessly rather than seeking novelty for its own sake.

But the turbulence contradicts this, or complicates it. The continent is never finished. It is always testing new configurations, always running experiments at its edges, always ready to replace the established with something that processes more efficiently. The weathered center and the chaotic margin coexist, sometimes within sight of each other. You can stand with one foot on rhizome that has held its shape for ten thousand years and the other on ground that will be unrecognizable by tomorrow.

This is the unusual thing. Not the absence of water, not the plant-as-architecture, not the scale or the sound—these are comprehensible, these are parameters you can hold in your mind. The unusual thing is the *combination*: a world that is simultaneously ancient and newborn, stable and chaotic, silent (in the way of long-established ecosystems) and deafening (in the way of heavy industry running at full capacity). It holds these contradictions not by resolving them but by *being* both at once, in different places, in the same continuous body.

---

Night, when it comes, does not bring silence. The rhizome does not sleep. The grinding continues, the pumping, the exhalation. The sound changes register—deeper, perhaps, slower, as though the continent is running at a different cycle—but it does not cease. The pillar-forms glow faintly in the darkness, bioluminescent patches that pulse in complex patterns. The aerostats are still visible, dark silhouettes against the faint glow, still cruising, still feeding.

You lie on the surface of the rhizome and feel it working beneath you. The vibrations pass through your body. Your ribs resonate. Your teeth buzz. You are, for this moment, a small soft thing on the surface of an industrial organ that does not know you are here, that would not care if it did, that will continue its processes long after you have left, long after your species has left, long after anything resembling your biochemistry exists anywhere in the universe.

The continent does not need you. The continent does not need anything. It *is* need, is processing, is the conversion of mineral to structure to waste to mineral again. It is a closed system that has closed itself so thoroughly that it has become its own physics, its own chemistry, its own reason.

You sleep, eventually. The sound becomes a kind of silence—not quiet, but a continuous input that your brain stops registering, like the sound of your own blood. You dream of pistons. You dream of roots. You dream of a ground that looks up at you through a thousand unlidded eyes and finds you interesting, possibly, as a source of novel chemistry, but not necessary.

You wake. The light returns. The continent is still running.

It will still be running when you go.
