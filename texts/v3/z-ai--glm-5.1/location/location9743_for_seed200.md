# The Seething Flat

The land has not decided what it is yet.

This is the first thing you must understand. The Seething Flat—no one calls it that, because there is no one to call it anything—has not finished becoming. It exists in a state of perpetual inception, a geography half-spoken, as if the earth were still clearing its throat before the first real word of continent.

---

The heat comes from below.

Not from the sun, which is a pale smear behind perpetual steam, a wet brightness without direction or hour. The sun is almost decorative here—a rumor of light, a dim silver wash that makes no shadows because shadows require contrast, and here everything glows with the same wet, infrared certainty. The real heat rises through the stone like breath through a body. The ground is warm when you touch it. Warm the way skin is warm. Warm the way something alive is warm, from the inside, for reasons that have nothing to do with the sky.

This is a low place. Almost the lowest place. The world slopes down to here from every direction—not dramatically, not with cliffs or canyons, but with a long, gradual subsidence, as if the earth itself were slumping toward something soft. And soft it is. The elevation barely exists. Sea level is not a boundary here; it is a negotiation. Water and land have not agreed on terms. The flat extends for miles—tens of miles—a region large enough to have weather, to have horizons, to have distance that humbles walking. But the horizons are strange. They are not clean lines where earth meets sky. They are smears, condensation zones where the steam rising from the hot ground meets the cooler air and surrenders itself to fog. The world ends in white, in opacity, in the refusal to be seen from any distance.

The rock here is new.

That word—new—feels wrong for geology, but it is the only word that fits. The substrate has not been worn. Has not been ground by glaciers or carved by rivers or fractured by frost. It is raw stone, still cooling from some deep extrusion, still settling into its mineral identity. In places it is smooth, almost vitreous—a black glassy surface like frozen skin, warm to the touch. In other places it has begun to crack, but the cracks are not the work of erosion. They are the work of cooling, of contraction, of stone remembering that it wants to shrink. The cracks are clean-edged, sharp, unweathered. No water has rounded them. No root has widened them. They are architectural, almost geometric, hexagonal and pentagonal patterns spreading across the flat like the cell-structure of some vast organism.

But the rock is not bare. It is never bare. That is the second thing you must understand.

---

The water is everywhere and nowhere in particular.

It does not flow in the way water flows in mature landscapes—no river channels, no stream banks, no delta distributaries. The hydrology here has not yet organized itself. Water seeps. It wells. It condenses. The flat is a place where the water table is so close to the surface that the distinction between wet ground and shallow pool becomes philosophical. You walk on ground that is firm—mostly—but every depression fills, every hollow becomes a seep, and the black glassy rock is streaked with mineral tracks where hot water has wept from the stone for what must be years, must be decades, must be as long as this place has existed. The weeping rocks leave orange and white trails—iron, calcium, sulfur—all the dissolved chemistry of the deep earth laid out in dried tears on the flat's dark face.

And from those seeps, the steam rises.

Not violently. Not like a geyser. There are no geysers here—no focused vents, no dramatic eruptions. The heat escapes diffusely, evenly, as if the entire flat were a single shallow breath. The steam is warm and wet and smells of sulfur and iron and something else, something organic and thick, something that is not a geological smell at all. The air is saturated. At the near-surface—the meter or two where anything breathes—the humidity is so total that condensation is a constant event. Moisture appears on every surface, on every leaf and stone and carapace, not because it rained but because the air can hold no more and must let go.

It does rain, of course. It rains often. The perpetual steam feeds clouds that hover low and gray and spill their water back down in warm, directionless drizzles that barely qualify as rain—more like a continuous settling, a continuous return. The rain does not erode. It is not strong enough. It is the land's own moisture coming home.

---

The vegetation is impossible.

Not impossible in the sense that it should not exist. Impossible in the sense that it should not be this abundant, this dense, this *overwhelmingly present* in a landscape this young. The erosion parameter is nearly zero. The ground has not developed soil in any meaningful way—no deep loam, no weathered horizon, no slow accumulation of organic matter mixed with mineral dust. And yet.

The flat is *green*. Not the bright green of a meadow or the dark green of a forest—it is a green so dense it borders on black, a green that absorbs light rather than reflecting it, a green that seems to generate its own faint bioluminescent glow in the perpetual dimness. The plants here have solved a problem that plants elsewhere have not had to solve: how to live on rock that is still deciding what it is.

They are pioneers, but not the sparse, struggling pioneers of volcanic islands or burnt landscapes. They are pioneers in the way an army is pioneers—they have arrived in force, in *numbers*, and they have brought everything they need with them.

The foundation is microbial. Everywhere the rock is wet—and everywhere the rock *is* wet—there are biofilms, slimy mats of thermophilic bacteria and archaea that have colonized the warm stone. They are green, some of them, and purple, and orange, and a deep arterial red. They stain the black rock with living color. They are the first soil, the first organic matter, and they are millimeters thick but they are *everywhere*, a continuous living skin on the newborn geology.

On top of the microbial mats, the macrophytes grow. Ferns—enormous ferns. Not tree ferns, not the elegant specimens of cloud forests, but low sprawling ferns that spread by rhizome, that carpet the flat in fronds as wide as a human arm is long. They root in the biofilm, in the thin organic soup that the bacteria have made, and they extend horizontally, runner over runner, layer over layer, until the flat is a mattress of ferns three feet deep in places. You cannot see the rock for the ferns. You cannot see the ground. Walking would be walking on a green sea, each step compressing fronds and releasing warm water from the soaked biofilm below.

And among the ferns, the reeds. Not grasses—the grasses have not arrived yet, or have not found this place—but reedy plants, horsetails and clubmosses, ancient lineages that remember when the world was young and wet and hot and dim. They stand in the shallow seeps and pools, in the places where the water rises above the fern carpet, and they grow tall—six feet, eight feet—slender vertical lines in the horizontal green, their jointed stems clicking softly when the nonexistent wind brushes them.

And then the moss. The moss is *oppressive*. It covers everything that holds still long enough—the rocks at the seep-line, the fallen horsetail stems, the bases of the ferns, and in the wettest places it rises into hummocks and mounds, sphagnum-like, saturated with hot water, each step releasing a warm mineral belch from the organic matrix below. The moss is the slow architect of this place. It is building the first real soil. It is building the ground that some future landscape will stand on.

The canopy—if it can be called that—is not closed. There are no trees. The flat is too young for trees, too low, too wet, too hot. But the ferns and the horsetails and the moss build a ceiling nonetheless, a layered density of vegetation that creates its own microclimate, its own darkness, its own wetness. Below the topmost fronds, the light drops to almost nothing. Below the second layer, it drops to nothing. The ground beneath the ferns exists in perpetual twilight, perpetual damp, perpetual warmth.

---

The fauna is the revelation.

Not because it is strange—strange would imply deviation from expectation, and this place has no expectations to deviate from. The fauna is the revelation because of its *quantity*, its *insistence*, its absolute refusal to be absent from any cubic centimeter of this landscape.

The sounds begin before dawn, if dawn is a concept that applies here—if the dim silver light can be said to intensify and fade in any meaningful cycle. The insects start it. Not crickets—nothing so discrete and musical. The sound is a thrum, a continuous low-frequency vibration that you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears, a sound generated by millions of wings and thoracic processes and the simple physical reality of that many small bodies existing in that much humidity. The insects are everywhere. They breed in the standing water, in the seeps, in the saturated moss, in the warm organic soup that passes for soil here. Midges, gnats, flies whose names have not been given because the taxonomists have not been here and never will be. They rise in clouds from the ferns when anything moves through them. They settle again within seconds. They are the air as much as the steam is the air.

On the ferns, the amphibians.

Frogs. Frogs in the way the flat has ferns—in *numbers* that shift the category from individual to phenomenon. They are small frogs, most of them, thumbnail-sized, green-black and brown-black and the exact red-brown of the moss, and they sit on every frond, every stem, every rock that protrudes above the waterline. They are thermophilic frogs, adapted to water temperatures that would kill a temperate amphibian. They breed in the hot pools. Their tadpoles develop in water that is nearly body-temperature. They have no predators here—or rather, they have predators, but they have more offspring than predators can consume, and so they exist in a state of overwhelming numerical supremacy, and their chorus is the second sound of the flat.

The chorus. It is not beautiful. It is not the singing of a few frogs in a night pond. It is a wall of sound, a continuous croaking and clicking and bubbling that rises and falls in waves depending on temperature and humidity and some internal rhythm that no human has ever decoded. It is loud enough to mask the sound of footsteps. Loud enough to make conversation difficult. Loud enough that after an hour, you stop hearing it—it becomes the silence of this place, the baseline against which all other sounds are measured.

And there are reptiles. Small lizards, skink-like, that dart across the rocks at the edge of the hot pools, snatching insects from the steam. They are fast, metabolic furnaces in the already-furnace air, and they are everywhere you look once you learn to look for them—a flash of movement, a rustle in the moss, the subtle displacement of a frond that indicates something with legs has just passed.

And in the water—in the shallow, hot, mineral-saturated water that stands in every low place—there are other things. Not fish. The water is too shallow, too variable, too mineral for fish. But crustaceans. Small pale shrimp-like creatures that have emerged from some deep aquifer, some subterranean ecosystem that connects to the surface only through these weeping rocks. They are blind, some of them, or nearly blind, adapted to the darkness below, and they have risen into the surface pools and found warmth and nutrients and they thrive here in the mineral broth, their pale bodies visible against the dark rock like grains of rice in soup.

And above it all, where the steam thins and the dim silver light becomes almost strong enough to see by, the birds. Not many birds—not in the oppressive humidity of the lower flat—but a few, wading birds with long legs and longer beaks, picking through the shallow water, eating the shrimp and the tadpoles and the frogs that are too slow or too young to escape. They are silent birds. They do not call. They move like herons move—slow, deliberate, each step placed with care on the uncertain ground—and they are the only large animals in this landscape, the only things that break the pattern of small and numerous life.

---

The flat does not change.

This is the strangest thing. The flat is new—it is *emerging*, it is embryonic, it is under construction—but it does not change. It exists in a frozen equilibrium, a steady state that should not be possible in a landscape this young, this raw, this close to the heat that made it.

The steam rises at the same rate. The water seeps from the same rocks. The ferns grow and die and grow again in the same places. The frogs breed and are eaten and breed again in numbers that never increase and never decrease. The insects rise in clouds and settle in clouds and the density of their clouds never varies. The weeping rocks leave their orange and white mineral trails and the trails do not extend, do not grow, do not erode. The biofilms stay the same thickness. The moss stays the same height. The horsetails stand at the same joints and click in the same windless air.

It is as if someone started a world and then paused it—except that it is not paused, because things are alive, things are breathing and eating and breeding and dying. But the *system* is paused. The landscape has found a configuration that works, a balance of heat and water and life that can sustain itself indefinitely, and it has settled into that balance with the certainty of a chemical reaction reaching equilibrium.

There is no succession here. No ecological transition. In other young landscapes, the moss comes first, then the grasses, then the shrubs, then the trees. The biome matures. The soil deepens. The canopy closes. The species replace each other in a predictable sequence. But here, the flat has skipped nothing and begun everything at once, and it holds all of it in a single suspended moment—moss and fern and microbe and insect and frog and lizard and bird and shrimp and steam and water and rock, all coexisting in a density that should be impossible, a saturation that should collapse under its own weight, and it does not collapse. It holds.

The flat holds.

Perhaps it will change someday. Perhaps the heat from below will fade, and the steam will stop, and the water will cool, and the tropical dimness will give way to something more temperate, and the succession will resume, and the trees will come, and the landscape will mature into something that a human would recognize as a wetland or a forest or a coast. Perhaps this is just the beginning, and the equilibrium is temporary, and the flat will become something else.

But perhaps not. Perhaps this is the stable state. Perhaps this is what the earth makes when it makes a place from heat and water and raw stone and the first things that arrive to live there—this dense, wet, loud, dim, teeming, *stuck* landscape that has found its form and will hold it for as long as the heat holds.

The flat does not wait. The flat does not progress. The flat *is*.

---

If you were here—and you are not, because no one is here, because this place has never been found or named or mapped—you would feel the heat first. In your lungs, in your skin, in the moisture that would condense on your eyelashes and drip down your temples like sweat that is not yours. You would hear the frogs before you saw them, the wall of sound that is this place's silence. You would see the green—always the green, the overwhelming saturated living green—and you would not see the rock beneath it, because the rock has been answered, the rock has been colonized, the rock has been *answered* by a life that did not need soil or time or erosion to arrive.

You would feel the ground firm beneath your feet, and warm, and wet, and you would know that you were standing on something that was still becoming, still deciding, still half-formed—and you would know that it did not matter, because the life had already won, had already covered every surface and filled every pool and sung every song and eaten and bred and died and done it all again without waiting for the geology to finish.

The flat is not patient. The flat is not in a hurry. The flat is in equilibrium, and the equilibrium is green and wet and loud and hot and dim and *full*, so full that there is no room for anything else, no room for change, no room for the future.

The flat is now. The flat is only now. The flat has always been now.
