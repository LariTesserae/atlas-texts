# The Condensation Chamber

It is small. That is the first thing you must understand — small the way a throat is small, the way a cavity behind a wall is small. You could span it with your arms extended, fingertips touching weeping metal on one side, fingertips touching weeping metal on the other. A pocket. A blister in the body of something vastly built.

And it is all built. There is no floor that was ever ground. No wall that was ever stone. The chamber is entirely manufactured — pressed steel plates overlapping like the scales of something mechanical and unborn, riveted with a regularity that suggests intention but not yet purpose. The rivets weep. Every surface weeps. Water emerges from nowhere visible, beads along seams, traces the geometry of construction with its running. The chamber is high — you feel it in the pressure, or the lack of it, that thin-air emptiness behind your eardrums — but you cannot see outside. There is no outside. There are only the plates, the seams, the rivets, and the water that insists on existing here as if the chamber were a lesson in condensation.

It is cold enough that your breath would hang, if you were breathing here, which is difficult because of the sound.

---

The sound.

It comes from everywhere and nowhere. A deep mechanical thrumming that is not quite an engine — not yet. Something that wants to be an engine, something in the pre-engine state, the embryonic throb of industrial process that has not resolved into purpose. It shakes the water droplets on the walls. You can watch them vibrate in place, trembling on the verge of falling but never quite falling, held in that trembling by the frequency of the sound. The sound is deafening but not painful. It fills you. It becomes the pressure you feel, replacing atmosphere, replacing breath. It is the sound a machine makes before it knows what it will produce.

Beneath the thrum: ticking. Hundreds of small hard sounds. A mechanical rain that is not rain.

---

The creatures are the ticking.

They cover every surface. They are small — the size of your thumbnail, some smaller, a few as large as your knuckle — and they are dense. Dense the way a surface can be when something covers it completely, layered, overlapping, near-touching. You cannot see the metal beneath them except where the water runs too fast for their grip. They are pale, almost the color of the condensation, and you understand slowly that they *are* the color of the condensation because they drink it. Their carapaces — if they are carapaces — glisten wet. They move, but barely. A shifting. A resettling. The way a crowd shifts in a room too small for it, each body adjusting against its neighbors, never finding more space because there is no more space.

They have no eyes. They have mouths, or mouth-like apertures, pressed flat to the wet metal. Feeding. Drinking. Whatever distinction exists between those acts here.

They are not insects. They are not crustaceans. They have too many legs — or not legs, something like legs, segmented and jointed and ending in grips that hold the vertical surfaces with a tenacity that seems structural, as if they were designed for this specific metal, this specific wet, this specific angle. And perhaps they were. Perhaps they *are* the construction, or part of it, a feature of the chamber the way a gill is a feature of a fish.

They make sound when they shift. The ticking. Hundreds of mouth-parts releasing and reattaching. It is deafening in aggregate.

---

There is almost nothing growing here. Almost. In the crevices where two plates overlap, where the water pools in a seam too thin for the creatures to reach, something pale and threadlike extends — not roots, not stems, something between wire and secretion. It is difficult to call it vegetation. It is difficult to call it alive. But it is present, pushing into the condensation with a slowness that makes geological time look hasty, growing at a rate that might be measured in molecular additions. It is the only still thing here. The creatures move around it, or over it, indifferent. Perhaps it is not food. Perhaps it is not anything they recognize.

---

The light is dim and it does not change. It comes from nowhere you can identify — a phosphorescence in the water itself, perhaps, or a property of the metal when it is this cold and this wet, or a byproduct of the thrumming, sound transduced into something visible at the edge of perception. The chamber glows like something almost luminous but not quite, like a memory of light, like the light that exists behind closed eyelids. You can see, but only just. The creatures are shapes. The water is movement. The vegetation is a guess.

---

The chamber does not change.

This is the strangest thing, and you feel it slowly, the way you feel cold — not as information but as a fact of your body. The water beads in the same places. The creatures shift but do not progress. The sound maintains its frequency exactly. The pale threads in the seams extend at their impossible slowness but they never seem longer than they were, never seem shorter, maintain a length that might be the length they have always had. There is no decay. No drying. No accumulation. The water that runs is replaced by water that condenses. The creatures that shift return to where they were. The thrumming finds the same note each time, and each time is the same time.

It is not frozen. That would imply a cessation, a stopping. This is different. This is equilibrium so precise that it has become a kind of permanence, a held breath that is also breathing, a loop that does not iterate because it has never not been iterating. The chamber is not trapped in time. The chamber *is* a time — a single moment, dense and saturated and deafening, extended like the pale threads in the seams, into whatever this place is.

---

If you were here — and you are not, because there is no room, because the creatures fill every surface, because the chamber is the size of a cavity and you are too large for it — but if you were, you would feel the cold in your bones, the wet on your skin, the sound in your chest like a second heartbeat. You would watch the creatures drink the weeping walls and you would understand that they are maintaining the chamber, or the chamber is maintaining them, or the distinction is meaningless here, where everything is made and nothing is natural and the only law is the persistence of what already is.

You would notice, eventually, that the rivets are not quite rivets. That they have tiny apertures. That the water comes from inside them. That the creatures sometimes press their mouth-parts to those apertures and drink more deeply, and when they do, the thrumming seems to — no. The thrumming does not change. The thrumming never changes.

But for a moment, in the dim phosphorescent wet, you might have thought it almost began.
