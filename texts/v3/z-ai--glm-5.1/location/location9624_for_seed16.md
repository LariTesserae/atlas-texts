# The Amber Ward

Somewhere — inside something — there is a city grown from thirst.

---

You enter through a crack that was not meant to be a door. The outside is irrelevant; the outside has never understood this place. The crack is just wide enough for a body, and the body that made it wore away the salt-cristite edges over centuries of patient scraping, hollowing the passage a millimeter at a time with a stone tool that still sits in a niche beside the threshold, smooth as a river stone from ten thousand grips.

Inside: heat. Not the dry heat of a desert noon but the close, vulcan heat of a body, of a room where something enormous breathes slowly just beneath the floor. The air is thick and tastes of mineral and something sweeter — the trace vapor of amber sap, which is not water, which has never been water, which does what water does in other places but does it differently here, slowly, with a different viscosity and a different hunger.

The chamber is not large. A city could be large, but this city is not. This city fits inside a salt dome's heart, a bubble in the ancient mineral that swelled and hardened and was then colonized — first by the growth, then by the hands that learned to live inside the growth. You could walk its longest axis in four hundred paces. You could whisper at one end and be heard at the other, if the growth were quiet, which it never is.

---

## The Growth

It is green only in the way that copper is green. The vegetation here — and it is vegetation, it photosynthesizes, it roots, it fruits, it dies and is replaced — carries its chlorophyll in cells tinted by the amber sap that serves as its circulatory fluid. The result is a palette of deep oxide, rust-verdigris, petrol sheen on wet leaf-surfaces. In the dim light, the growth looks almost black. When the bioluminescent nodules pulse — and they pulse constantly, slowly, like a heart that has never known urgency — the leaves reveal themselves in flashes of dull garnet and bronze.

It is lush. Terrifyingly so, for a place without a drop of water.

The growth drinks something else from the salt-cristite substrate. The old miners — there were miners once, before this was a city, when it was only a hollow being scraped hollow for the mineral wealth of its walls — the old miners called it *ligor*, though whether that was their word or a word the growth taught them is unclear. Ligor seeps from the mineral walls in beads that never evaporate, that collect in channels the roots have carved over millennia, that tastes of iron and sulfur and something sweet and something that makes the tongue go numb for hours after.

The plants have learned to do everything with ligor that other plants do with water. Their cells are inflated with it. Their fruits are swollen with it — heavy, drooping things the size of fists, with skins like taut bronze and interiors like amber jelly. Nothing eats the fruits. They fall. They split. The ligor pools. New roots find it. This is the whole cycle, and it has been turning here for longer than the city has been here to watch it.

But lush does not mean uniform. The growth clusters. There are chambers where the vegetation is so dense you must push through curtains of hanging root-fiber and skirt around trunk-columns thick as torsos, where every surface is furred with the small flat leaves that grow in overlapping shingles, where the bioluminescent nodules are so numerous that the air seems to pulse with a slow bronzen heartbeat. And then there are the emptinesses — vast stretches of bare salt-cristite floor, pale and crystalline, where nothing grows because the ligor doesn't reach, where the silence is different, where you can hear your own blood moving and the distant sound of the growth breathing on the other side of the chamber.

---

## The City

The city is built from the growth and built into the growth and built despite the growth, and these three relationships have never been resolved and probably never will be.

The first thing to understand: the builders had no metal. What they had was stone and ligor-tempered wood and the fiber of the growth's outer bark, which can be separated into layers as thin as paper and as strong as rope. They had obsidian that they knapped into edge-tools — there are knapping floors in the eastern quarter, still littered with the flakes of a thousand years of blade-making. They had the salt-cristite itself, which can be split into blocks when you find a vein that runs true, blocks that are translucent and faintly warm and that hold the dim light like held breath.

The second thing to understand: they built *with* the growth. Not around it. Not despite it. The growth was already here when the first refugees or miners or exiles found this place, and the growth was here in density and complexity that would take centuries to clear. So they didn't clear it. They pruned it. They trained it. They wove its living fibers into lattice walls and grew its trunk-columns where they needed support and hollowed its massive root-masses into chambers that the growth then lined with new tissue, smooth and warm and faintly glowing.

The city is the growth. The growth is the city. The distinction is a visitor's fiction.

There are structures that are clearly buildings — rectilinear, intentional, with doorways and levels and the marks of edge-tools on every surface. A meeting hall in the central chamber, its roof the interlocked canopy of a single enormous plant that was trained over three centuries to grow flat and wide. Storage niches carved into the salt-cristite walls and sealed with doors of woven bark-fiber. A workshop where obsidian blades are still made, though the hands that make them have been still for a very long time. Dwellings stacked three levels high in the southern quarter, connected by ladders of notched ligor-wood, each dwelling a pod hollowed from a root-mass with a single window cut through the living wall.

And there are structures that are clearly the growth asserting itself — buttress roots that have shouldered through a wall, a canopy that has swallowed a rooftop, a fruit-laden branch that has pushed through a doorway and now hangs in what was once a corridor, heavy and bronze and slowly leaking ligor onto the floor. The city is always being built and always being un-built. The growth does not know it is architecture. It grows. The hands that shaped it have shaped it for so long that the shaping has become a kind of weather, a pressure the growth responds to and grows around and sometimes through.

---

## The Sound

It is never quiet.

The growth makes noise the way a city makes noise — not with intention, but with the accumulated sound of a thousand processes happening at once. The vent-leaves — broad, cup-shaped structures that grow at intervals along the major trunks — open and close as the temperature shifts, and they hiss. The hissing is constant, a background like steam venting, like a kettledrum played very softly with a very large mallet. When many vent-leaves open at once — during the hottest part of the cycle, when the heat from below pulses strongest — the hissing becomes a roar that fills the chamber and makes conversation impossible.

The trunks creak. They are always growing, always shifting their weight, and the sound of their settling is like the sound of a wooden ship in heavy seas: groans, cracks, the occasional sharp report as a fiber snaps somewhere inside. The root-masses make a lower sound, a rumble that you feel more than hear, as they slowly expand through the salt-cristite substrate, cracking the mineral a millimeter at a time.

And the nodules pulse. Each bioluminescent nodule — and there are thousands, tens of thousands — brightens and dims on its own cycle, and each cycle is accompanied by a faint electrical hum, a sound like a throat clearing at the edge of hearing. Together, the nodules make a chorale of hums, overlapping and interfering, producing beats and interference patterns that wax and wane across the chamber in slow rhythmic waves.

At the height of the heat-cycle, when the vent-leaves roar and the trunks crack and the nodules all pulse at once, the sound is almost unbearable. It is the sound of a living thing the size of a district. It is the sound of a process that does not know you are listening.

---

## The Absence

No animals. Not now, not ever, as far as anyone can tell. No insects to pollinate the growth — the growth pollinates itself, or does not pollinate at all, reproducing through root-runners and fruit-fall and the slow division of its nodular clusters. No vermin in the store-niches. No birds in the canopy. No sounds of scuttling or breathing or chewing except the sounds the growth itself makes.

The city has no scavengers. When a fruit falls and splits, the ligor pools until the roots find it. When a structure collapses, the growth grows over it. When the hands that made the tools stopped using the tools, the tools stayed where they were set down, undisturbed, collecting a fine mineral dust that is also — somehow — alive, or near-enough to alive that it grows slowly, imperceptibly, coating every still surface in a faint bronze bloom.

There are no bodies. This is the detail that visitors find most difficult. The city shows every sign of long habitation — the worn thresholds, the knapping floors, the dwellings with their personal niches still holding personal objects — but there are no remains. No graves. No bones. No indication of where the builders went or whether they went at all, whether they became something else, whether they were absorbed by the growth they lived inside, whether they simply walked out through the crack that was not meant to be a door and into the irrelevant outside and never returned.

The tools are still sharp. The dwellings are still warm. The meeting hall still has places set, as though the hands that shaped this place stepped out for a moment and are expected back.

The growth does not seem to notice the absence. It grows as it always has — lush, dense, clustered, breathing, hissing, creaking, pulsing its slow garnet light. It was here before the hands. It may have been here before the chamber. Some of the root-masses are so old, so deeply embedded in the salt-cristite, that they may predate the bubble itself, may have made the bubble by their own slow pressure, may have cracked the mineral apart over eons and then grown into the crack and widened it and shaped it into this warm dim chamber where a city could be grown.

---

## The Rules

There is no water here. Bring water and it will find you. The growth will reach for it — slowly, imperceptibly at first, and then with a determination that is either vegetal tropism or something more deliberate. A water-skin left on the floor will be ringed by root-tips by morning. A drop of sweat that falls on bare salt-cristite will be absorbed into the mineral and the mineral will sweat it out again and the roots will find it and the growth will remember it.

This is the only rule that matters: the growth does not know water, and it wants to.

Everything else follows. The tools are made without water. The dwellings are cleaned without water. The bodies that lived here — if they lived here — learned to live without water, or learned to keep it away from the growth, or learned something else that is not recorded in any script because there is no script, only the marks of edge-tools and the slow patient training of living wood.

The heat is constant. The dimness is constant. The sound is constant. The growth is constant.

The city is patient. It has always been patient. It was patient before the hands came and it is patient now that the hands are gone, and if new hands come through the crack that was not meant to be a door, the city will be patient with them too, will offer them dwellings already grown, will teach them the sound of the vent-leaves and the taste of the fruit and the weight of the heat, will watch them learn to build with the growth or be built around by the growth, and will not mind either way.

The growth does not mind. The growth grows.

That is what it does in this hidden, hot, low place inside the mineral heart of something larger. It grows. And the city grows with it, around it, through it. And the sound grows. And the light pulses. And the ligor seeps and the roots reach and the fruits fall and the vent-leaves hiss and the nodules hum their slow electric chorale, and the chamber breathes, and the heat rises, and the hands — the hands that made the tools, that trained the wood, that carved the meeting hall from a living root-mass — the hands are somewhere else now, doing something else, or being something else, or being nothing at all, and the city does not wait for them because the city does not know how to wait, the city only knows how to be the growth and be grown and grow, and the sound of it fills the chamber like a pulse, like a breath, like a sentence that never quite ends.
