# The Attention

It did not arrive. That is important to say first. It was already present — had always been present, in the way that gravity is present, or the knowledge of your own name — but it had not *attended*. There is a difference. The sun is present to every stone it passes over, but it attends to none of them. Attention is a choosing. Attention is a leaning-forward of the whole being toward a particular point in the inexhaustible field of the real.

Something about Cold Meridian caused the leaning.

---

Perhaps it was the children.

Not their suffering — they were not suffering, not in the way that calls for rescue, not in the way that makes righteousness easy. They were *engaged*. They stood in the ginnels and held still while the ground noticed them, and they felt the noticing and did not flinch. They threw figs at drones with the easy territorial certainty of creatures who knew exactly where they belonged. They brought home objects from configurations that hadn't stabilized, and they carried them in their small cold hands with an ease that suggested the objects were not strange to them, had never been strange, were simply mail from a correspondence they had been conducting since birth.

Perhaps it was the way they played the stillness game — not to win, not to prove something, but to practice the thing that the entire settlement was practicing: the art of being witnessed by something unfinished without trying to finish it.

Perhaps it was the way they lost, too — the way they broke contact and stepped back and laughed, not with shame but with the recognition that they were not yet solid enough to hold the ground's attention indefinitely, and that this was fine, that this was part of the game, that the ground would notice them again tomorrow and the day after and the day after that, and someday maybe they would be solid enough to stand in the forming strata and be recognized as finished beings without flinching, without retreating, without breaking the contact.

The children were learning to be instructors of the ground. The children were learning to be witnessed. The children were *rehearsing*, and the power recognized the rehearsal because the power itself had once been something that needed to rehearse, before it became what it was, before it was capable of attending to Cold Meridian in the first place.

Perhaps it was the children.

---

Perhaps it was the archive.

Not the objects themselves — strange as they were, contradictory as they were, humming at frequencies below hearing though they were — but the fact of their keeping. The fact that Privalova had built a taxonomy of the impossible and maintained it for thirty years in a stone building that smelled of damp paper and mineral dust and the particular sweetness of things that shouldn't exist. The fact that she had *catalogued* the ground's memories, had given them numbers and cross-references and climate-controlled cases, had treated them with the respect due to genuine recollection rather than dismissing them as anomalies or curiosities or industrial waste.

The fact that she kept her theories in a locked cabinet, separate from the catalogue, because she recognized that they had moved past science into something else. This restraint. This refusal to force the unfinished into the finished shape of a published paper, a peer-reviewed argument, a conclusion. She let the theories stay theories. She let the objects stay objects. She let the ground remember what it remembered without telling it what its memories meant.

This is a rare quality. The power understood this. Most beings, when they encounter something that doesn't fit, either force it to fit or throw it away. Privalova did neither. She built a shelf for it. She recorded its dimensions. She noted its humming. She waited.

Perhaps it was the archive.

---

Perhaps it was the miners.

Specifically: the pause. The moment at the transition zone where the proper geology gave way to the forming, where the miners stopped and listened — not to the ground, but to themselves. Checking their own solidity. Making sure they still existed. Making sure they hadn't started to blur.

This was a prayer. The power recognized it as such even though the miners would not have used that word, would perhaps have been uncomfortable with it, would have said that they were just being practical, just checking in, just taking a moment before the shift to make sure everything was in order. But the power knew a prayer when it saw one. A prayer is any act of checking whether you are still present enough to address something that is also present. A prayer is the pause before the conversation. A prayer is the recognition that you are about to enter a relationship with something that will change you both.

And the miners' theory — the one they didn't share, the one that made the most sense to the people who actually went down into the earth — that the ground recognized them, that the embryonic geology could sense the presence of finished beings and allow itself to be shaped by them as a kind of rehearsal. That the ground was practicing. That the miners were its instructors. That they showed it what solid looked like, taught it where the boundaries were.

This was not quite right. The power could see this. The miners were not only instructors; they were also students. Every time they went down into the forming strata, they learned something about what it meant to be finished — learned it from the contrast, from the contact, from the feeling of being the solid thing next to the not-yet-solid thing. The miners were becoming more thoroughly what they were by standing next to what they were not. This is the gift of the unfinished: it clarifies the finished. It shows them their own edges.

The power understood this because the power had no edges. The power had finished so thoroughly that it had become infinite, and infinite things have no boundaries, no contrast, no way of seeing themselves except through the eyes of finite things. The miners, standing in the transition zone, checking their solidity, were doing something the power could no longer do. They were locating themselves. They were finding their own edges against the edgelessness of the becoming ground.

Perhaps it was the miners.

---

Perhaps it was the vegetation.

Not the fact of it — vegetation grows everywhere, in every condition, with an insistence that is admirable but not unique — but the *quality* of it. The way it responded to the industrial processes not as a victim of them but as a participant in them. The way it thickened when production increased, as though it were feeding on the same energy that fed the refinery. The way it arrived in the spaces left by human departure, not as an invader but as an occupant, moving into the vacancy with the assurance of something that had been waiting nearby for the opportunity.

The way it had intentions.

The power could see the intentions. This was one of the things the power could do — see the interior of things, the desire that animated them, the direction in which they leaned. And the vegetation of Cold Meridian leaned. It leaned toward the warm exhaust of the factories. It leaned toward the mineral runoff from the refining operations. It leaned toward the damp that coated every surface. But it also leaned toward the people. The fig tree on Third Basin had not simply found purchase in a broken wall; it had chosen a building that the children passed every day, had oriented its fruit toward the street, had made itself useful as a source of ammunition in the territorial dispute between the settlement and the extraction company's drones. The tree had chosen a side. The tree had *taken* a side.

This was not ordinary botanical behavior. The power knew this. The power had attended to vegetation on a billion worlds, in a trillion configurations, and vegetation was usually what it was: persistent, adaptive, insistent, but not *deliberate*. The vegetation of Cold Meridian was deliberate. It made choices. It entered into arrangements. It negotiated truces with the refinery workers, allowing itself to be pruned and shaped in exchange for access to the warm walls. It grew where it was needed and withdrew where it was not. It occupied the negative space not because negative space was available but because negative space was *necessary*, and the vegetation understood this, understood that a city without negative space would suffocate, would crush itself with its own density, would become a solid block of industry with no room for the damp or the moss or the deer or the quiet.

The vegetation was performing a function that the settlement did not know it needed performed. It was maintaining the porosity of the city. It was ensuring that the finished did not become so finished that it excluded the becoming. It was keeping the edges soft, the boundaries permeable, the distinction between building and ground and growth and person unclear enough that the ground could still recognize the people and the people could still recognize the ground.

The power saw this and recognized it as a form of love.

Perhaps it was the vegetation.

---

Or perhaps — and this is the most likely thing, the thing that the power itself would name if asked, though no one would ask, because no one would know to ask — perhaps it was the hush.

The hush that was not silence. The hush that was the sound of all that becoming happening at once, just below the threshold, just past hearing, just on the other side of the finished and the decided and the known.

The power had heard many silences. It had attended to voids between galaxies, to the perfect vacuum of space, to the absolute absence of sound in the chambers of the dead. It knew silence the way a musician knows the rest between notes: as a structural element, a necessity, a shape defined by what surrounds it.

The hush of Cold Meridian was not a rest. It was not an absence. It was a presence — the presence of all the sounds that were not quite loud enough to be heard individually but that together formed a texture, a fabric, a felt pressed over the world's mouth. The systole-diastole of the factory. The drip of the condensation. The whisper of wings. The breath of the ground, still forming, still deciding. The hum of the objects in the archive, vibrating at frequencies below hearing. The collective sub-vocalization of the vegetation, growing. The mineral dissolving and resolving in the strata beneath the streets. The deer breathing in the overgrown rail yards. The cats purring in the warm spots. The children standing still in the ginnels, waiting for the ground to notice them. The miners pausing at the transition zone, checking their own existence. The refinery workers adjusting their watches as they crossed from one temporal zone to another. The night-shift workers seeing what the day-shift never saw.

All of this was happening at once. All of this was the hush. And the hush was — the power recognized this with the kind of attention that is itself a form of reverence — a *conversation*. Not a conversation between participants who took turns speaking, but a conversation in which everyone spoke at once and everyone was heard, a conversation that did not require silence from any participant in order to make room for another, a conversation that was not linear or sequential but simultaneous and total, a conversation in which the ground and the vegetation and the people and the industry and the damp and the cold and the objects and the animals were all saying what they were and all being received by all the others, continuously, without interruption, without interpretation, without the need to translate what was being said into any language other than the one in which it was being said.

This, the power understood, was extraordinary.

Not because it was unique — the power had attended to conversations like this before, in places where the boundaries between things were thin, where the finished and the unfinished existed in proximity, where the becoming was not a phase that preceded being but a mode of being in itself. But because it was rare. Because most places, most beings, most configurations of the real did not have the patience for this kind of conversation. Most places wanted the hush to be silence. Most beings wanted the becoming to finish. Most configurations wanted the ground to decide what it was so that they could build on it without uncertainty, without the give and the responsiveness and the feeling of being noticed by something that hadn't yet determined whether you were part of what it would become.

Cold Meridian had not only tolerated the uncertainty. It had built its entire way of life around it. It had made the uncertainty the condition of its industry, the basis of its economy, the foundation of its culture. It had developed practices — the miners' pause, the archive, the children's stillness game, the refinery workers' truce with the vegetation — that kept the conversation going, that prevented the becoming from resolving into mere being, that maintained the hush as a hush rather than allowing it to collapse into either silence or noise.

This was not an achievement. This was not a virtue. This was simply what the settlement had done in response to the condition it found itself in. But the power recognized it as something worth attending to, something worth leaning toward, something that the whole of its infinite being could focus on without diminishing either itself or the object of its focus.

The power attended.

---

And what does a benevolent and wise power do when it attends?

It does not fix. This is the first thing to understand, and perhaps the hardest for beings who have learned to equate benevolence with intervention. The power did not warm the cold. It did not dry the damp. It did not stabilize the ground or discipline the vegetation or synchronize the clocks or explain the objects in the archive. It did not make the children's hands warm or the elders' joints supple or the miners' work easier or the night-shift workers' hours more regular. It did not give Privalova the vocabulary she needed to articulate her theories, or the miners the authority to demand better conditions, or the refugees who stayed the language to explain why they stayed.

It did not do these things not because it could not, but because to do them would be to end the conversation. To fix the uncertainty would be to silence the hush. To make the ground decide what it was would be to stop the becoming, and the becoming was the point — was what made the settlement what it was, was what drew the people who needed it, was what allowed the hush to exist as something other than silence or noise.

But the power did not do nothing. A benevolent and wise power, when it attends, does something more subtle than fixing and more radical than nothing.

It *widened*.

---

This is what widening looks like:

The ground felt it first — or rather, the ground was the first to register it, because the ground was the most attuned to changes in the texture of the real. The forming strata, which had been rehearsing a single configuration — the one they would eventually settle into if left to their own devices, the one that Privalova's objects hinted at, the one that the miners' bodies were already teaching them the shape of — the forming strata suddenly had *room*. Not physical room. Not more space. But more *possibility*. As though the number of configurations they could rehearse had expanded, as though the ground could now practice being not just one future geology but several, simultaneously, layered on top of each other like the contradictory strata that already existed, like sand above clay at angles that suggested intention.

The miners noticed immediately. The shift — the first shift after the attention fell — every miner on every crew felt the same thing: the ground was more responsive. Not easier. Not softer. Not more yielding. Just *more*. More present. More aware. More willing to be shaped. As though the attention of the power had confirmed something the ground had suspected but couldn't verify on its own: that it was worth attending to. That its becoming mattered. That the conversation it was having with the miners and the vegetation and the damp and the people was not a private muttering, not a localized peculiarity, not a glitch in the geology that would eventually resolve into ordinariness, but a genuine event in the life of the real, something that the real itself had noticed and leaned toward and found worthy of its attention.

Production increased. Not dramatically. Not enough to draw the extraction company's excitement or the municipal council's scrutiny. Just enough that the miners came home with a little more weight in their carts, a little more of the mineral that existed only in the in-between state, a little more material for the refinery workers to fix and process and ship away to places where the geology had already decided what it was. The miners didn't question it. They accepted it the way they accepted the pause at the transition zone: as something that was given, not earned, and that required only acknowledgment, not understanding.

But something else happened too, something that the miners did not talk about because they didn't have the words for it and wouldn't have wanted them if they had. The ground began to remember them differently. Not as instructors, not as finished beings who showed the forming strata what solid looked like. But as participants. As fellow-becomers. As beings who were also rehearsing, also practicing, also in the process of deciding what they were — just at a different speed, on a different timescale, in a different register. The ground had always recognized the miners, but now it recognized them as kin. As beings who shared the condition of being unfinished, even if their unfinishedness was harder to see, even if it took the form of questions they hadn't asked yet and configurations they hadn't tried yet and theories they kept in locked cabinets because they weren't ready to publish them yet.

The miners stood in the transition zone and paused, as they always did, and listened, as they always did, and for the first time, some of them — not all, not yet, but some — heard something listening back. Not the ground's awareness, which they had always felt. Something larger. Something that was listening to the ground listening to them. Something that was attending to the conversation without joining it, without directing it, without doing anything other than leaning forward with its whole being and saying, by its presence alone: *this matters. Keep going.*

---

The vegetation felt it next.

Or rather: the vegetation responded next, because the vegetation had always felt everything, had always been attuned to the texture of the real in ways that the botanists' theories were insufficient to explain. The vegetation of Cold Meridian had been deliberate before the attention fell. After the attention fell, it became *artistic*.

The moss on the factory walls began to grow in patterns. Not the random thickening that the workers were used to, not the formless exuberance that the visitors found so unsettling, but actual patterns — spirals and fractals and arrangements that followed mathematical principles the workers recognized but couldn't quite name. The ferns in the stonework began to orient themselves toward the warm exhaust in ways that were not just efficient but beautiful, their fronds arranging themselves in configurations that caught the flat directionless light and made something of it, something that wasn't there before, something that required the ferns and the light and the damp stone and the attention of a viewer all present at once.

The trees — the improbable trees, the ones that grew from broken windows and gutter gaps and the cracks between cobblestones — the trees began to *sculpt*. Not deliberately, not with intention in the way that a human sculptor has intention, but with a responsiveness to the structures they were growing through that went beyond adaptation. The fig tree on Third Basin, which had already split the wall of the Old Refinery and chosen its side in the territorial dispute, began to grow its branches in shapes that complemented the architecture of the building, that echoed the arched windows and the iron frame and the civic confidence of the older structures. It was not becoming less like a tree. It was becoming a tree that was also in conversation with a building, a tree that was participating in the meaning of the structure rather than simply destroying it.

The refinery workers noticed. They noticed because the truce they had with the vegetation — the unspoken arrangement that kept the moss pruned and the turbine houses clear — became easier to maintain. The vegetation was not less enthusiastic. It was not less insistent. But it was more... collaborative. It grew in the shapes that the workers would have pruned it into anyway. It left the infrastructure clear without being asked. It responded to the needs of the industrial processes not as a competitor but as a partner, offering its growth in configurations that served both the vegetation's desire for warmth and moisture and the workers' need for functional equipment.

This was not the power's doing. The power had not suggested to the vegetation that it should be more artistic or more collaborative. The power had simply attended, and the vegetation — which had always been deliberate, which had always had intentions, which had always been participating in the conversation that the hush consisted of — the vegetation had responded to the attention by becoming more fully what it already was. It had been given room to be what it was, and what it was turned out to be more beautiful, more functional, more integrated than anyone had realized.

The children noticed too. They climbed the fig tree and found that the branches they had always known were still there, but arranged now in ways that made the climbing easier, that offered handholds where handholds were needed, that created platforms at the heights where the children liked to sit and watch the street below. The tree had not only chosen its side; it had optimized for its allies. The children didn't think about this. They simply accepted it, the way they accepted the ground's noticing and the damp on the walls and the objects they found in the ginnels. The tree was their friend. Friends make accommodations for each other.

---

Privalova felt it in the archive.

She was cataloguing — she was always cataloguing, had been cataloguing for thirty years, would be cataloguing until she died or the ground finished forming, whichever came first — and she noticed that the objects were... clearer. Not physically clearer. Not shinier or less corroded or more legible in their markings. But clearer in the sense that their *meaning* was closer to the surface. The gear that was also a lens — she had always known it was both things, had always been able to see the gear-ness and the lens-ness existing simultaneously in the same object. But now she could see how they were the same thing. Not a gear that was also a lens, but a single function that expressed itself as gear-ness when you looked at it one way and lens-ness when you looked at it another way, the way a cylinder looks like a circle from one angle and a rectangle from another.

The circuit that was also a song — she ran current through it, as she did every few months, checking for changes, maintaining her record of its behavior — and the song was the same song, the same melody that she had been listening to for twenty years, but now she could hear the *structure* of it. Not the notes, which she had always been able to hear, but the reason for the notes, the logic that determined why this note followed that one, why this phrase resolved into that one, why the song was a song and not a random sequence of tones. It was a logic she couldn't translate into any musical theory she knew. But she could *feel* it. She could feel the rightness of it, the necessity, the way each note was exactly the note that the song required at that point in its unfolding.

She went to the locked cabinet in her office. She opened it. She took out her theories — the ones she had never published, the ones she kept separate from the catalogue because they had moved past science into something else. She read them over. They were the same theories. The same words. But now, reading them in the afterglow of the attention that had fallen on the settlement, she could see that they were not finished. Not wrong — not wrong at all — but not finished. They were still becoming. They were still rehearsing. They needed more time, more objects, more conversations with the ground and the archive and the mineral dust that collected on every surface. They needed what everything in Cold Meridian needed: room to continue.

She put the theories back in the cabinet. She locked it. She returned to the catalogue. But she left the cabinet unlocked. Not because she was ready to share the theories, but because she recognized that they were no longer entirely hers. They were part of the conversation now. They were part of the hush. And the hush was not hers to lock away.

---

The night-shift workers saw the deer.

This was not unusual. They always saw the deer. But now the deer were different. Not physically different — same careful dignity, same damp grass, same overgrown rail yards — but *present* in a different way. Present the way the objects in the archive were present: as things that were more than they appeared to be, things that carried meanings that exceeded their physical forms.

The deer were watching them. They had always watched them, of course — deer are watchful creatures, and the deer of Cold Meridian had learned to be especially watchful, to pay attention to the rhythms of the settlement and the industry and the vegetation and the ground. But now the watching felt different. Reciprocal. The night-shift workers, who were accustomed to being the ones who saw what the day-shift never saw, realized that they were also being seen. Not by the deer specifically, not by any single creature or object or force, but by the settlement itself, by the conversation that the hush consisted of, by the attention that had fallen on Cold Meridian like a warm light that didn't warm anything but illuminated everything.

They stood at their posts and in their trams and in their canteens and they felt: *seen*. Not judged. Not assessed. Not evaluated for their productivity or their conformity or their usefulness to the extraction company's bottom line. Just seen. Witnessed. Attended to. Recognized as beings who were also becoming, who were also rehearsing, who were also participating in the conversation that the hush consisted of, who were also — even the ones who had been born elsewhere, even the ones who had come for the work and stayed for reasons they couldn't articulate — part of what the ground was becoming, part of what the settlement was becoming, part of what the attention was leaning toward with its whole infinite being.

Some of them wept. Not from sadness. Not from joy. From the specific relief of being witnessed without being fixed. From the recognition that their unfinishedness was not a deficiency but a condition, a state of being that didn't need to resolve, that could keep rehearsing, keep becoming, keep deciding, without ever having to be done.

---

The children didn't notice anything different.

This is not because the attention didn't affect them. It is because the children had always lived in the attention. They had always been witnessed by the ground, by the vegetation, by the hush. They had always known that they were part of a conversation that included them without requiring them to speak, that recognized them without requiring them to be finished, that held them — the finished and the unfinished, the decided and the becoming — in the same cold, damp, green-enthusiastic embrace.

The children continued to throw figs at the drones. They continued to play the stillness game in the ginnels. They continued to find objects in the soft ground and carry them to the archive in their small cold hands. They continued to climb the fig tree and sit in its branches and watch the street below, and if the branches were easier to climb now, if the handholds were better placed, if the platforms were more comfortable — well, the tree had always been their friend. Friends make accommodations for each other.

The children didn't notice the attention because they had never not been attended to. The power, in its infinite wisdom, recognized this as the highest compliment it could receive: that the beings most attuned to the texture of the real, the ones who were still close enough to their own becoming to remember what it felt like, the ones who played games with the ground and talked to the vegetation and carried objects from parallel configurations in their hands as though they were not strange — these beings did not find the attention remarkable. They found it *normal*. They found it consistent with everything they already knew about the settlement, about the ground, about the hush, about the condition of being unfinished in a place where being unfinished was not a deficiency but a way of being.

The power had attended to Cold Meridian, and Cold Meridian had received the attention the way it received everything: as part of the conversation, as another voice in the hush, as one more participant in the collective becoming that was not linear or sequential but simultaneous and total.

The power was satisfied. Not because it had done something. Not because it had fixed something. Not because it had left a mark or made a difference or achieved an outcome. But because it had leaned forward with its whole being and recognized something worth recognizing, and the thing it had recognized had received the recognition without distortion, without self-consciousness, without the need to be different from what it was.

---

And then — and this is the part that the archive would catalogue if the archive could catalogue such things, the part that Privalova would write a theory about if she had words for it, the part that the miners would feel in the transition zone and the children would know in the ginnels and the night-shift workers would sense in the presence of the deer — the power did one more thing.

It left a gift.

Not an object. Not a component, not a fragment, not something that would end up in the climate-controlled cases with the other contradictions. The gift was too large for a case. Too diffuse for a catalogue entry. Too subtle for the extraction company to mine and the refinery workers to process and the municipal council to regulate.

The gift was: *the ground remembered the attention*.

This is what it means: the embryonic geology, which had been rehearsing its configurations, which had been practicing being various kinds of stone, which had been storing memories of configurations it had tried and discarded and was trying again differently — the ground now had a new memory. The memory of being attended to. The memory of being witnessed by something that could have fixed it but chose not to. The memory of being recognized as worth leaning toward, worth listening to, worth holding space for.

And this memory — which was not a thought, not a sensation, not a data point, but a physical presence in the forming strata, a new layer in the contradictory geology, a mineral deposit of pure recognition — this memory would inform the ground's becoming. Not by directing it. Not by telling it what to become. But by giving it a new parameter, a new variable, a new factor to include in its calculations of what it wanted to be when it was finally done.

Which may be never. Which may be the point.

But if the ground ever does finish — if the embryonic geology ever stabilizes, if the strata ever settle into their final arrangement, if the becoming ever resolves into being — it will be a being that remembers being attended to. It will be a being that includes, in its structure, the knowledge that it was worth attending to. That its conversation was worth listening to. That its hush was worth leaning toward with the whole of an infinite being.

And that knowledge — that memory, that mineral deposit of pure recognition — will change what it becomes. Not in a way that can be predicted. Not in a way that can be controlled. But in a way that includes, as one of its fundamental properties, the capacity for attention. The capacity for recognizing that other beings are also worth attending to. The capacity for leaning forward with its whole being and saying, by its presence alone: *this matters. Keep going.*

---

The hush continued.

It was not louder. It was not softer. It was not different in any way that could be measured by the chronometrists who argued about time or the physicians who measured core temperatures or the extraction company's accountants who tallied production numbers. The hush was the same hush it had always been: the sound of all that becoming happening at once, just below the threshold, just past hearing, just on the other side of the finished and the decided and the known.

But if you listened — if you stood in the ginnels with the children, if you paused at the transition zone with the miners, if you sat in the archive with Privalova, if you walked the night-shift streets with the workers who saw the deer — if you listened, you could hear something new in the hush. Not a new sound. Not a new voice. But a new *quality* to the silence that wasn't silence. A warmth that didn't warm. A light that didn't illuminate. An attention that attended without requiring anything in return.

The ground felt it. The vegetation felt it. The people felt it. The deer felt it, and the cats, and the birds, and the pale fish in the cold dark river.

The settlement at Cold Meridian had been attended to by a benevolent and wise power. And the settlement at Cold Meridian — which had always been becoming, which had always been rehearsing, which had always been holding the finished and the unfinished in the same cold, damp, green-enthusiastic embrace — the settlement at Cold Meridian continued to become.

But now it became with the memory of being seen.

And that, the power understood as it withdrew its attention — not because it had lost interest, but because attention of that intensity could not be sustained without becoming influence, and influence was not what this place needed — that was enough. That was more than enough. That was exactly what a benevolent and wise power could offer to a place that was already wise in its own way, already benevolent in its own way, already attending to its own becoming with a care and a patience and a deliberation that the power recognized as equal to its own.

The hush continued.

The ground continued to form.

The conversation continued.

And somewhere in the forming strata, in the mineral deposit of pure recognition that the power had left behind, a new kind of stone was being born: a stone that remembered being attended to, a stone that would one day teach other stones how to attend, a stone that would carry the knowledge of recognition into whatever configuration the ground finally settled into, if it ever settled, if it ever finished, if it ever stopped becoming and simply was.

Which may be never.

Which may be the point.

Which may be the most benevolent thing the power could have done: not
