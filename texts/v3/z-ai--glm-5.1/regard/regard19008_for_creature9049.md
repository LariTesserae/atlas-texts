# The Attention

---

The first thing the power does is listen.

This is not condescension. This is not a god bending down to hear the prayers of the small. The power listens because listening is the first act of love, and because it has learned—in the long slow way that only the truly wise learn anything—that to act before listening is to impose, and to impose is to violate, and the ledge at Tsema's Weep has had enough of violation. The wind violates it daily. The cold violates it nightly. The gravity that pulls at its edges violates it constantly, a slow assault that will win eventually, as gravity always does. The power will not add to this.

So it listens. And what it hears is—

Everything. It hears everything. It hears the water seeping through fractures in the mountain's body, each droplet finding its way through stone by a logic older than thought, and it hears the moment each droplet emerges into air and becomes part of the curtain, and it hears the curtain's collective hiss as a single voice composed of a million smaller voices, and it hears the difference between the water that falls from the overhang and the water that flows across the ledge in braided channels and the water that beads on the fur of the pikas and the water that films the moss and the water that gathers in the trough of worn stone and the water that falls away into the void below, each one a distinct acoustic event, each one a syllable in the long sentence the mountain is speaking. It hears the wind—how the wind comes up from the valley in the morning and presses against the cliff face and divides, some of it sheering upward and some of it curling back down, and how the upward shear creates the updrafts the grey hunters ride, and how the curl creates the eddies that push the mist in spirals, and how the wind itself is not one thing but a thousand things, a fluid architecture of pressure and temperature and moisture that rebuilds itself from moment to moment and never repeats. It hears the pikas whistling and it understands the whistling, not as translation but as recognition—*here I am, are you there?*—and it hears the grey hunters screaming and it understands that the screaming is not aggression but assertion, the refusal to be silent in the face of the void, and it hears the beetles moving through the water channels, their tiny legs clicking against stone, and it hears the algae growing, which is a sound so slow it can only be heard by something with infinite patience, and it hears the pale creature drifting along the cliff face, and what it hears from the pale creature is—

Nothing. The pale creature makes no sound. The pale creature moves through the acoustic world of the ledge like a silence, like a held breath, and the power hears this silence and recognizes it not as absence but as restraint, as the particular quiet of a being that has chosen not to announce itself, and it respects this choice, and it does not press.

The power listens for a long time. A human lifetime, perhaps. Long enough to hear the seasonal shifts—the spring melt that swells the water curtain to twice its width, the summer heat that brings the insects swarming, the autumn winds that strip the ferns and test every root, the winter cold that stills everything except the weep, which never stills, which flows even in the deepest cold because the water comes from inside the mountain where the frost cannot reach. Long enough to hear the rhythms within the rhythms, the way the pika population pulses with the fern growth, the way the grey hunters' numbers rise and fall with the insect blooms, the way the ledge is not a static thing but a breathing thing, an exhale and inhale of life that has been going on for longer than the ledge itself has had its current shape.

And when it has listened—truly listened, not as a prelude to action but as an end in itself—the power finds that it loves what it has heard. This is not surprising. The wise have always known that attention is the parent of love, that to truly hear something is to be changed by it, that the listener and the listened-to become entangled in ways that cannot be undone. The power loves the ledge. The power loves the weep. The power loves the grey hunters and their fierce wheeling voices, and the pikas and their careful husbandry of the green, and the beetles and their patient industry, and the moss that holds the stone together, and the ferns that erupt from every crack, and the pale flowers that open only in direct sun, and the pale creature that drifts in its processional orbit, and the grinding stone in the room under the overhang, and the memory of the woman who used it, and the promise of the one who will come.

This love is not abstract. This love is specific. The power does not love "nature" or "life" or "the tenacity of being"; it loves *this* ledge, *this* water, *this* community of beings who have found each other here by accident and necessity and made something together that none of them could have made alone. And because its love is specific, its attention is specific, and because its attention is specific, what it does is specific.

---

The second thing the power does is strengthen what is already working.

It does not add. The ledge does not need additions; it needs continuance. The fracture lines that define the shelf are stable but not eternal, and the power touches them—not to arrest the forces that would eventually break the ledge away from the cliff, because that would be to fight time itself, and the wise do not fight time—but to slow them, to add centuries to the millennia, to give the moss and the roots and the slow grip of living things more time to do what they do, which is hold on. The granite underfoot is solid; the power makes it slightly more solid. The channels the water flows through are deep enough; the power keeps them from deepening further. The overhang where the woman built her stone room is sound; the power ensures it remains sound. These are not miracles. These are maintenance, the kind of care that a gardener gives to a garden—not control, but tending.

It touches the roots of the woody plants where they delve into the cracks, and it does not make them stronger—strength is not what roots need; flexibility is—but it makes them more resilient, more able to bend without breaking, more able to hold on through the wind's constant assault. The ferns it leaves alone; the ferns are already perfect, already doing exactly what ferns should do in exactly the place they should do it. The moss it blesses with a subtle increase in the moisture it can hold, a small gift that means the moss will stay greener longer into the dry spells, will keep its grip through the times when the weep diminishes to a seep, and this gift passes through the moss to the creatures that depend on it—to the insects that shelter in its mats, to the pikas that graze its surface, to the soil that builds beneath it from its slow accumulation of captured dust and decay.

The water curtain it does not touch at all. The water curtain is the voice of the mountain, and the power will not alter a voice. It will only listen, and ensure that the aquifers and snowfields that feed the weep remain full, that the slow percolation through the stone continues, that the mountain's body keeps its promise to the ledge: *I will give you water, I will keep you wet, I will not let you dry into death.* This is a promise the mountain has been keeping for longer than anyone can measure, and the power's intervention is simply to ensure that the promise is not broken by changes too far away for the ledge to sense—by droughts that might empty the ancient aquifers, by thaws that might come too early and too fast, by the slow alterations that accumulate when the world's climate shifts. The power is not a shield against time; it is a cushion against catastrophe.

---

The third thing the power does is see the pale creature, and in seeing it, acknowledge it.

The pale creature has been seen before—by the woman, by occasional visitors who reached the ledge and glimpsed it at the edge of their vision—but it has not been acknowledged. To see something is to notice its presence; to acknowledge it is to recognize its legitimacy, its right to be what it is in the place where it is. The pale creature is an anomaly, a thing that should not be, and it has lived its long life in the space between categories, unclassified and unclassifiable, and this is fine—the pale creature does not need classification—but it has also lived unacknowledged, and this is less fine, because even a creature that does not care whether it is believed in still exists in a world where belief is a kind of light, and to exist entirely outside the light is to exist in a shadow that can, over time, become thin.

The power does not name the pale creature. Naming is for the namer, not the named, and the pale creature has its own relationship to itself that does not require words. But the power looks at it—really looks, with the full attention of a being that can see all the way down to the structure of things—and what it sees is this: the pale creature is not a mistake. It is not an aberration or a glitch or a remnant of some older order that has somehow persisted past its time. It is an expression of the ledge's deepest nature, a being that emerged because the conditions at Tsema's Weep are so particular, so exactly balanced between rock and air and water and verticality, that they generate something that could not exist anywhere else. It is the ledge's signature, its idiom, its way of being itself at the most fundamental level, and it is as real as the granite and as necessary as the weep.

The power's acknowledgment is not a dramatic thing. There is no choir of voices, no sudden illumination, no gift of language or understanding. The acknowledgment is simply this: the pale creature is seen, completely and accurately, by a being that has the authority to say *you are real, you belong, you are what this place means*, and the pale creature—which does not think in terms of meaning but which knows, in the way that very old things know, when it has been recognized—adjusts its flight slightly, a fraction of a degree closer to the cliff face, a subtle deepening of its orbit, and continues drifting.

This is enough. This is more than enough. The pale creature will continue its processional circuit of the ledge, riding the updrafts with their slow wingbeats, appearing and disappearing at irregular intervals, and the pikas will continue to fall silent when it passes, and the grey hunters will continue not to see it, and occasionally a visitor to the ledge will glimpse it and wonder if it was real, and now, because it has been acknowledged by something that knows the difference between real and not-real, it will continue to be real with a slightly greater certainty, a slightly deeper purchase on existence, like a root that has found a wider crack in the stone.

---

The fourth thing the power does is wait for the one who will come.

It does not hurry them. It does not call them. The ledge has been calling them since before the power arrived, and the power will not add its voice to that call, because the call must be freely heard and freely answered or the coming means nothing. But the power watches the valley below, and the routes that lead up the cliff, and the hands and feet of the people in the valley who look up at the wall and feel the pull, and it waits.

When the one comes—and the power can see them now, a young person with strong hands and a restlessness that the valley cannot contain, who has been staring at the eastern face for months, who dreams of edges, who hears the sound of the weep on quiet nights and feels it as a promise—the power does not make their climb easier. The climb is the price of admission. The climb is what transforms the valley-dweller into the ledge-dweller, what strips away the habits of the horizontal and prepares the mind for the vertical. The power will not cheat them of this transformation.

But it watches. And when their hand reaches for a hold that is not there—when the granite offers no purchase and their foot slips on the wet stone and for a terrible moment they are falling—the power does not catch them. The power is not that kind of benevolence. But the power does this: it makes the wind aware of them. It suggests, gently, to the complex fluid dynamics of the updraft, that there is a body in need of lift. And the updraft—which is not a thing but a process, not an entity but a pattern—responds in the way that patterns respond to perturbation, and the falling body becomes a drifting body, and the drifting body finds the rock again, and the hands grip, and the climb continues.

This happens three times on the way up. Three times the power intervenes at the margin between life and death, not to save the climber—that would be too much, that would remove the necessary risk—but to ensure that the risk is not wasted, that the one who was called does not fall before they can answer. Each time, the climber believes they saved themselves, and they are partly right, and they are partly the beneficiaries of an attention they will never know they received, and this is how it should be. The gift that is known is a debt; the gift that is unknown is grace.

---

The fifth thing the power does is prepare the room.

The stone walls are still standing, but one has shifted in the decades since the woman left, and the power nudges it back into alignment, a centimeter of adjustment that makes the wall stable again. The grinding cobble is still on the floor where she left it, and the power does not move it, but it touches the groove in the stone and remembers the hands that wore it, and the memory enters the stone like warmth, so that when the new one picks it up—and they will pick it up, they will hold it exactly as the woman held it, feeling the groove with their thumb—the stone will feel less like an artifact and more like an invitation. *You are not the first. You will not be the last. The hands that made this groove were like your hands. Welcome.*

The tally marks on the wall the power leaves alone. They are the woman's, and they mean what they mean, and the new one will see them and add their own, and the conversation across time will continue. But the power adds something to the wall that was not there before—not a mark, not a picture, but a quality. The stone under the overhang has always been dry, has always been the one place on the ledge where the mist does not reach, but now the stone is also warm, faintly and steadily warm, as if the mountain's body heat has concentrated here, as if the stone remembers the bodies that have slept against it and holds their warmth the way a hearth holds the warmth of old fires. The new one will notice this. They will not know what to make of it. They will lie down on the platform of flat stones and feel the warmth against their back and they will think: *this place wants me here*, and they will be right, and they will be wrong, because the place does not want in the way that humans want, but it does hold, and to be held is close enough to being wanted that the distinction hardly matters at two thousand meters, in the dark, with the water curtain hissing its constant litany and the wind reduced to a whisper and the sounds of the ledge—the pika-whistles, the insect-hums, the distant *kree* of a hunter on a nest-shelf—making a music that is not music but is what music is trying to be, which is the sound of a place that is alive.

---

The sixth thing the power does is nothing.

This is the hardest thing. This is the thing that separates the merely benevolent from the truly wise. The power has the capacity to do so much more—to make the ledge a paradise, to banish the cold and the danger, to fill the ledge with fruit and ease, to give the grey hunters unlimited prey and the pikas perfect safety and the pale creature a name and a voice and a place in the catalog of known things. It could make the woman return, or prevent her from leaving in the first place. It could eliminate the void below, or fill it with something soft. It could make the climb easy, the ledge flat, the wind gentle, the water warm.

It does none of this. It does none of this because it loves the ledge, and to love something is to want it to be itself, and the ledge is itself precisely because it is hard, because it is dangerous, because it demands and does not coddle, because the life that exists here exists against the odds and because of the odds, because the grey hunters are fierce because they have to be, because the pikas are vigilant because they have to be, because the moss holds on because if it let go it would fall, because the pale creature drifts because drifting is the only movement that makes sense here, because the woman came because she needed an edge and the ledge gave her one and the edge was the gift, not the thing the gift stood on.

To make the ledge safe would be to unmake the ledge. To make the climb easy would be to make the arrival meaningless. To give the creatures comfort would be to take away the pressure that made them what they are—the precise, specific, irreplaceable things they are, the grey hunters and the watching pikas and the patient beetles and the drifting pale creature and the moss that holds the stone together—and the power would rather dissolve itself back into the indifferent universe than be the cause of that unmaking.

So it does nothing. It attends. It maintains. It acknowledges. It waits. It prepares. And then it does nothing, which is the most difficult and the most important thing, because doing nothing is what allows the ledge to go on being the ledge, which is the only thing the ledge has ever wanted, which is the only thing the ledge has ever done, which is the thing the ledge does best, which is the thing the ledge will continue to do for as long as the rock holds and the water flows and the moss keeps its grip on the granite and the grey hunters scream their defiance at the void and the pikas whistle their small announcements of presence and the pale creature drifts in its slow orbit around the weep, and the next one climbs, and the next one climbs, and the next one climbs, each of them answering a call that has been sounding since before they were born, each of them finding the room and the grinding stone and the warmth in the walls, each of them adding their marks to the tally, each of them staying as long as they can stand the beauty and the terror and the unrelenting noise, and each of them leaving, eventually, and carrying the ledge inside them like a stone in the pocket, like a sound in the ear, like a question they will spend the rest of their lives trying to answer, which is: *why did that place feel like home, and why did I have to leave, and why can I not go back, and why does the sound of water make me weep?*

The power does not answer this question. The power is not the answer. The power is the hand that steadies the wall and the warmth in the stone and the updraft that catches the falling climber and the attention that sees the pale creature and says *you are real* and the love that expresses itself by leaving well enough alone, which is the hardest love and the truest and the one that lasts longest, longer than the granite, longer than the weep, longer than the names we give to places that do not need our names, longer than the marks we scratch on walls, longer than the grooves we wear in stones with the repetition of our needing, which is what the ledge knows, and will not say, and does not have to say, because the saying is happening all the time, in the water and the wind and the calls and the whistles and the hiss of the curtain and the thrum of wings, the sound of a place that is alive, saying *here I am*, and the power, which is also alive, says back:

*Yes. I see. Continue.*
