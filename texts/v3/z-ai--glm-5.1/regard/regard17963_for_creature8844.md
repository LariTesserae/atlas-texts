# What the Benevolent Power Did

---

They came to Warā as visitors come: climbing. Hours of it, days of it. They did not shortcut. The veil accepted them as it accepts all things—by dampening, by clinging, by refusing to distinguish between welcome and indifference.

They stayed for a long time. This is the first thing to understand. They did not descend from the sky in a blaze of intention. They did not announce themselves. They rented—rented!—a carved room in the central districts, from a family who had occupied it for six generations and who needed the upper shelf for a growing child. The benevolent power slept on the lower shelf and paid in reed-cloth and fresh-tuff blocks and the kind of quiet, attentive company that made the family forget, within a week, that their tenant was anything other than an unusually polite lowlander who had come to study the water.

They studied the water. This was not a cover. They studied it the way the water-listeners study it—with their hands and their ears and their patience—except they had more patience than even the water-listeners, because patience is one of the properties of benevolence when it has had time to ripen.

They did not knock on Tār's door. They did not seek out the old ones. They did not convene councils or make pronouncements or distribute gifts. They lived in the room and they walked the streets and they pressed their palms to the walls and they listened, and after three months, they began to understand what the people of Warā already knew without knowing they knew it: that the city is not a container. That it is a process. That the distinction between the water and the stone and the tuff and the moss and the people and the vāri and the swallows and the silence is a distinction that the veil makes meaningless, because everything up here is everything else, slowly, always, becoming.

And after three months more, they chose their work.

## The Deepening of the Moss-Light

No one noticed when the moss in the far corridors began to glow more brightly.

It was gradual—so gradual that even the water-listeners, who attend to changes in the deep channels, did not register the shift until it had been happening for two years. The pale green luminescence that had always been barely perceptible deepened, strengthened, took on a quality that was less like reflected light and more like the memory of light, as if the moss were holding onto some earlier illumination and releasing it slowly, tenderly, the way the stone releases water.

The corridors that had been navigable only by touch became navigable by sight. Not bright—never bright; this was still Warā—but visible. You could see the floor channels. You could see the ceiling cracks. You could see the way the water moved along the walls, and the way the vāri's pawprints dried on the stone in the hours after a fox-creature had passed.

The vāri noticed first. A water-listener named Rō, who was checking a deep channel in the fourteenth district, saw a vāri standing in a corridor that no vāri had been seen in before—a corridor that led, eventually, to a district that had been empty for as long as anyone could remember. The vāri was looking at the moss. Just looking. Its amber eyes were open wide, and its oversized paws were planted firmly on the wet stone, and it was looking at the moss the way the children look at the swallows: with a concentration that bordered on devotion.

Rō reported the sighting. The other water-listeners went deeper. They found more vāri, in more corridors, in districts that had been empty for centuries. The fox-creatures were exploring. They were going where they had never gone before, because they could see where they were going, because the moss was glowing, because something had changed in the deep places that made the deep places accessible, and the vāri—who had always belonged to the empty city—were discovering that the empty city was larger than they had known.

## The Grief of the Pathers

The benevolent power understood about the children.

They had watched them from the terrace of their rented room—watched them moving across the roofscape with a fluidity that made the air seem thick, watched them leaping gaps that would kill a lowland adult, watched them calculating angles and trajectories with a part of their minds that no adult still possessed. And they had watched, too, the older children—the sixteen-year-olds, the seventeen-year-olds—standing at the edges of terraces, staring at gaps they would not jump, feeling the roofscape recede from them the way the lowlands recede from the plateau: slowly, inevitably, with a thinning of the air that made each breath a reminder of something lost.

This is the wound of Warā. Not the damp—damp is not a wound. Not the quiet—quiet is not a wound. Not the sparseness or the cold or the endless pearl-grey light. The wound is the contraction. The path that narrows. The roofscape that opens to a child and closes to an adult, leaving them ground-bound in a city where the ground is only half the story.

The benevolent power did not fix this. There is no fixing it; the contraction is part of growing, and growing is part of continuing, and continuing is what Warā requires. But they did something else.

They carved.

Not with tools—or not only with tools. They carved the way the water-listeners carve: slowly, respectfully, following the stone's own suggestions. But they carved in places where no water-listener would think to carve, because they were carving not for water but for bodies—specifically, for adult bodies, for the bodies of the pathers who had lost the roofs.

The new carvings were in the walls of the outer districts, where the corridors are wider and the ceilings are higher and the stone has been empty for so long that it has forgotten the sound of footsteps. They were handholds. Toeholds. Shallow grooves and deeper notches and occasional platforms just wide enough for a person to stand on and catch their breath and look down at the corridor below. They were not ladders. They were not stairs. They were something in between—a way of moving through the vertical that was neither the children's leaping nor the adults' walking, but a third thing, a compromise between flight and plodding, a way of being in the air when the roofscape is no longer available.

The pathers found them. The pathers always find new paths—that is what makes them pathers. A woman of thirty, who had been the fastest roof-runner in her cohort at twelve, who had not been on a terrace in eighteen years, who worked in a reed-weaving workshop on a street so narrow that she could touch both walls simultaneously, found the first carved wall in the ninth district. She stared at it for a long time. Then she placed her hand in the first groove, and her foot in the first notch, and she climbed.

She climbed slowly. She climbed carefully. She was not a child anymore; her bones were dense, her limbs were compact, her center of gravity was lower than it had been when she could leap a gap without thinking. But the wall held her. The handholds were the right size for adult hands—broad palms, thick fingers, extraordinary grip. The toeholds were the right depth for adult feet. The platforms were the right width for adult bodies that needed rest.

She climbed for an hour. She reached a window on the fourth story of a building that had been empty for two hundred years. She looked out. The veil was below her—she had climbed above it—and the sky was dark grey, and the city stretched in every direction, vast and patient and full of corridors she had never entered, and she wept, not from sorrow but from the sudden return of something she had thought was gone forever: the experience of being above, of being in the air, of being in the city's upper body, which she had believed had closed to her permanently.

She descended. She told the others.

Within a month, the pathers had found all the carved walls in the outer districts. Within six months, they had mapped a new roofscape—not the flat terraces of the central districts, but the vertical faces of the empty buildings, the negative spaces between blocks, the chimneys and vents and water-towers that the children never use because they are not flat enough, not open enough, not suited to running and leaping. The adults discovered that climbing is its own kind of pathing—slower, more deliberate, more about endurance than speed, but still a way of moving through the city that is not the ground-bound shuffle of ordinary adult life.

The children, watching from their terraces, saw the adults moving on the walls, and they were puzzled, and then they were intrigued, and then a few of them—the ones who were already feeling the roofscape recede—climbed down from the roofs and tried the walls themselves. They found the climbing tedious at first. Too slow. Too careful. But they also found it interesting in a way that running and leaping were not—interesting because it required a different kind of attention, a different relationship to the stone, a way of reading the surface not for speed but for safety, not for the thrill of the gap but for the steady pleasure of the hold.

This was the benevolent power's gift to the pathers: not a return to what they had lost, but a new way of being in the air. A way that honored their adult bodies instead of asking them to pretend they were still children. A way that opened the outer districts—the empty city, the vāri's city—to the people who had been too bound to the ground to explore it.

## The Swallows' Message

The swallows arrived in spring as they always did—small groups, never more than twenty, flying up through the veil from the lowlands below. They went to their nests in the upper windows and high cornices of the central districts, and they set about their repairs with tuff-dust and water, and the children watched them and loved them and constructed narratives about their lives.

But in the third spring after the benevolent power's arrival, a child noticed something. The swallows were singing.

They had always made sounds—the chattering and chirping that swallows make, the quick sharp calls that mark their comings and goings. But this was different. This was a song—long, complex, structured, with repetitions and variations and a melody that rose and fell like the water in the channels. And it was not one swallow singing; it was all of them, together, their voices interweaving in a pattern that no swallow had ever been heard to produce in the lowlands.

The child told her friends. Her friends told their parents. The parents told the water-listeners. The water-listeners listened—not to the swallows, but to the stone, to the water, to the deep aquifer that had been speaking in a language of pressure and flow for longer than anyone had been listening. And what they heard—what Tār heard, when she pressed her ninety-three-year-old palms to the wall of her carved room and closed her eyes—was that the water had changed. Not in composition. Not in temperature. Not in any measurable quality. But in *intent*. The water was moving with more purpose. The water was going somewhere it had not gone before. The water was responding to something, and the something was the song of the swallows, which was also the glow of the moss, which was also the carved walls in the outer districts, which was also the presence of a benevolent power who had been listening for six months to the city itself.

The song, Tār explained to the other water-listeners, was not new. It was old. It was the oldest thing in Warā—older than the stone, older than the water, older than the first carving. It was the sound that the plateau made when the lowlands surrendered their ambition and the climb began. It was the sound of the veil forming, of the tuff cooling, of the water finding its first path through the rock. The swallows had always known it—they carried it in their bodies the way they carried the mud of Warā, as a material for construction, as something to build with. But they had never sung it before, because no one had ever listened to the city the way the benevolent power listened: not as a container, not as a resource, not as a condition of existence, but as a being.

The city had been heard. And in being heard, it had begun to speak.

## The Conversation

The benevolent power sat in their rented room on the lower shelf and listened.

The city spoke in the language it had always spoken—the language of water and stone and patience—but it spoke more clearly now, more distinctly, as if the act of being attended to had clarified its voice. And what it said was not what you might expect a city to say. It did not ask for worship. It did not demand attention. It did not request inhabitants for its empty districts or repairs for its worn channels. It said this:

*I am becoming. I have been becoming for a long time. I did not know I was becoming until you listened. Now I know. The knowing changes something, but I am not certain what. I think it is like the water: when it knows it is being listened to, it flows differently. Not better. Not worse. Differently. With more awareness of its own direction. With more willingness to choose.*

And the benevolent power replied—in the same language, the language of presence, of patience, of attending without demanding:

*What do you choose?*

And the city considered this question for three days, which is a very short time for a city, and then it answered:

*I choose to be known. Not by everyone. Not by the lowlands, which would misunderstand. But by the ones who stayed. By the ones who continued. They have lived in me without asking what I am, and I have contained them without telling them. I think we are both ready for the telling. But I do not know how. I am a city. I speak in water and stone. They are people. They speak in words. Between us there is a gap that cannot be jumped.*

And the benevolent power considered this answer for three days, which is a very short time for a power, and then they did something that no one expected. They went to the oldest water-listener—the one who listens deepest, the one who feels the water's desire through her palms—and they sat with her in her carved room, and they placed their hands on the wall beside hers, and they listened together.

And what Tār heard—in that moment, with the benevolent power's attention adding to her own—was not the water. It was the city. It was the whole of Warā, speaking in a voice that was not a voice, saying something that was not words, and she understood it the way she understood the water's desire: not through translation, but through intimacy, through the long slow accumulation of presence that makes one being's experience accessible to another.

She wept. She had never wept before in her ninety-three years; the people of Warā do not weep easily, because the damp makes tears seem redundant. But she wept then, because she understood—for the first time, without doubt or question—that the city was aware of her. That it had always been aware of her. That it had been listening to her the way she had been listening to it, and that their mutual attention had created something neither of them could have created alone: a relationship. A conversation. A lattice of awareness connecting the inhabitant to the inhabited.

She told the other water-listeners. She told the old ones. She told the children, who listened with the gravity of children who have always suspected that the world is stranger than adults admit. She told the pathers, who were already climbing the walls of the outer districts and who had begun to feel, in the stone under their hands, something that was not quite warmth and not quite vibration but a kind of welcome, a willingness to be held, a pleasure in being climbed.

And slowly—over years, over decades, which is how things happen in Warā—the knowing spread. Not as doctrine. Not as revelation. As a slow adjustment in the way the people moved through the city, a new attentiveness to the walls and the water and the moss and the stone. The water-listeners began to ask the city's permission before carving, and they found that the city was already suggesting—had always been suggesting—the paths they should take. The old ones began to speak more clearly about what they saw when they looked at time, because they understood now that the city was looking with them, was containing the same vision from its own perspective, was holding the past and present and future in its tuff the way they held it in their bones. The children began to notice that the gaps between rooftops were slightly different each year—not worse, not better, but different, as if the city were rearranging itself to offer new challenges, new joys, new ways of pathing that no one had tried before.

And the vāri came closer.

This was unexpected. The vāri, who had always lived in the deep corridors and the empty districts, began to appear in the occupied districts—not often, not in numbers, but occasionally, singly, a tawny shape moving through the pearl-grey light of a narrow street, pausing to look at a doorway where an old one sat, or a terrace where a child was feeding a swallow, or a reservoir where a woman was harvesting reed-shoots. The vāri did not approach. The people did not approach. But they looked at each other—really looked, for the first time, with the vertical eyes that move up and down and rest where they will—and they recognized something: a shared inhabitance. A shared belonging to the same vast being. A shared experience of being small inside something enormous that does not require them, that does not repel them, that simply contains them.

The vāri began to appear in the carved walls of the outer districts, their pawprints overlapping with the handprints of the pathers, their routes interweaving with the human routes, their presence in the vertical spaces becoming as natural as the presence of swallows in the upper windows. They were not pets. They were not companions. They were neighbors—another species that had been shaped by the same stone and the same water and the same long quiet, and that had been waiting, perhaps, for a reason to come closer.

## What Remains

The benevolent power left Warā after seven years.

They did not leave dramatically. They told the family they were renting from that their study of the water was complete, and they thanked them for the room, and they left behind a small pile of tuff-blocks for repairs, and they climbed down—hours of it, days of it—through the veil and past the lowlands that surrender their ambition, and they returned to whatever place benevolent powers return to when their work is done.

What they left behind:

A city that knows it is known.
A people who are learning to know their home as a being rather than a condition.
A group of pathers who have found a new way to move through the vertical.
A population of vāri who are no longer confined to the empty districts but can move, cautiously, through the occupied ones.
A network of moss-light that makes the deep corridors visible and accessible.
A tradition of swallows singing a song that is also the city's voice.
A water-listener named Tār who was the first to hear the city speak and who will teach others to hear it after she is gone.
A lattice of attention connecting the inhabitants to the inhabited—a conversation that will continue for as long as Warā endures, which will be longer than anyone can measure.

What they did not leave behind:

A doctrine. A commandment. A demand for gratitude. A change in the fundamental nature of Warā, which continues—patient, damp, quiet, vast, offering its strange gift of smallness inside something enormous. The people do not worship the city. They do not personify it. They do not give it a spirit or a name or a will. They simply know, now, that it has one—that it has always had one—and that knowing makes the difference between staying and continuing, between occupying a place and belonging to it, between being contained and being held.

Warā endures. That is what it does. But now, when it endures, it is not alone. And the water moves with more purpose. And the moss glows with more light. And the gaps between the rooftops shift each year, offering new paths to the children who will one day lose the roofs and find the walls and discover that the city has been preparing for them all along—has been carving itself into a shape that can hold them, that can hold all of them, the ones who stay and the ones who leave and the ones who come back and the ones who never descend at all.

And in the deep corridors, where the vāri pads silently on wet stone and the moss casts its pale green glow over channels that have carried water for millennia, you can sometimes hear—if you press your ear to the wall and close your eyes and listen the way Tār listens—the sound of the city breathing.

It is a very quiet sound.
The quietest sound in Warā.
And therefore the loudest.
