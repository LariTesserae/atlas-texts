# The Attention of Powers

---

A benevolent and wise power would do almost nothing.

This is the first test of their benevolence: to see the mesa and not reach for it. To recognize the magnitude of what is happening here — the emergence of a distributed mind from reed and water and warmth, the earliest flickering of self-organization in a system that has never been interrupted — and to understand that reaching for it would be the very violence they wish to avoid.

They look. This is not nothing. To look truly at something — to see it as it is, not as you wish it to be, not as it might become, not as it compares to other things you have seen — is the rarest form of attention. Most looking is recognition, the matching of a thing to a category already held. But the mesa does not fit a category. It is not quite organism, not quite ecosystem, not quite mind. It is a becoming that has not yet become, and to see it as what it is requires the power to hold a category that is itself still forming, to think in a shape that has not yet hardened.

They hold the shape. They see: warmth rising from young stone. Water that stayed when the land shouldered up through it. Reeds that grew corridors with something that looks like intent. Pale fish still negotiating the architecture of their own bodies. A green light in deep water that does not exist in the above-surface spectrum. A pulse, slow as geological time, that might be breathing or might be thought or might be something for which no word has been coined because nothing like this has happened before, not here, not in this way.

They see the mesa's vulnerability. This is the second test: to see the vulnerability and not mistake the urge to fix it for the duty to protect it.

The mesa is defenseless. It has no claws, no teeth, no poison, no speed. It cannot run and cannot hide and cannot fight. It can only grow, and growing is slow, and slowness is a liability in a world that has learned to move fast. A ship could find it. A corporation could claim it. A team of researchers could arrive with instruments and transects and the best intentions in the world, and they would change it simply by being there — their bodies introducing new chemistries, their attention introducing the idea of being-attended-to, their questions introducing the possibility that the mesa's slow becoming might be accelerated, directed, harvested, understood before it has had the chance to understand itself.

The power sees this. And they choose to protect the mesa the way the mesa protects itself: slowly, subtly, through the patient accumulation of small effects.

They make the mesa difficult to find. Not hidden — hiding would require active maintenance, would require the power to attend to the mesa continuously, and continuous attention is itself a form of interference. They make it *misleading*. Satellites that pass overhead record nothing unusual: a seamount, a temperature anomaly, a pixel of green that might be algae or might be artifact. Navigational instruments drift in its vicinity — not by much, but by enough. Ships pass five miles to the east without knowing they have passed anything at all. The currents around the mesa shift in ways that are just within the range of natural variation, carrying traffic away from its edges as if by coincidence.

Those who have already found the mesa — the few scientists, the surveyors, the occasional adventurer — find that their memories of the place are... soft. Not erased. They remember warmth. They remember green light. They remember a feeling of presence, of being attended to by something vast and slow and indifferent in the way that a mountain is indifferent, which is to say: not hostile, not friendly, simply occupied with its own existence on a timescale that makes human concern feel small and brief. But they cannot remember how to return. Their notes contain coordinates that lead to open water. Their photographs show only fog and the grey suggestion of something that might be rock or might be cloud. They remember enough to know that something happened to them there, but not enough to make it useful — not enough to turn it into a map, a resource assessment, a plan.

This is not erasure. It is encryption. The mesa can still be found by anyone willing to look with sufficient patience, sufficient stillness, sufficient willingness to be changed by what they find without demanding that it change for them. The power has simply ensured that the only people who arrive at the drowned mesa are people who have already, without knowing it, begun to approach it on its own terms.

---

And then the power gives the mesa what it needs most.

Not intervention. Not acceleration. Not guidance. Not the gift of consciousness — that is not theirs to give, and it may not be what the mesa is moving toward anyway. The power has watched enough becomings to know that the destination is not theirs to choose. The mesa may become a mind. It may become something for which the word "mind" is inadequate. It may become nothing recognizable at all — may simply continue its slow vegetative processing until the planet itself grows old and the sun expands and everything warm and green and patient is finally consumed. The power does not know. This not-knowing is not a failure of wisdom. It *is* wisdom.

What the mesa needs — what any becoming thing needs if it is to have the chance to become fully — is time.

The power sees the geological processes working beneath the mesa. The same heat that warms the water, that makes the algal mats glow, that keeps the entire system at a temperature that permits life to experiment without the shock of cold — this heat is also the heat of a system that has not yet settled. The basalt is still cooling. The plates are still adjusting. In a century — perhaps two, perhaps five — the mesa will begin to subside. The warm water will drain. The reeds will be left standing in air they were not built to breathe. The pale fish will be stranded in channels that no longer connect. The slow becoming will be interrupted, not by malice but by geology, by the ordinary processes of a planet that does not know and cannot care that something extraordinary is happening on a small patch of its surface.

The power could stop this. Could fix the mesa in place, freeze the subsidence, hold the plates apart with the sheer force of attention. But this would be to make the mesa a project rather than a becoming, to hold it in stasis at the exact moment when it most needs the freedom to change. Stopping the subsidence would also stop the heat, and the heat is what makes the mesa what it is. Without the geothermal pulse, the water would cool. The algal mats would dim. The reeds would slow their growth. The mesa would not die, exactly, but it would become something quieter, something less experimental, something that settles for continuation rather than exploration.

So the power does not stop the subsidence. They *delay* it. Gently. On a scale that makes geological time look hasty. They lean against the plates — not with force, but with presence, the way a hand pressed lightly against a closing door can slow it without stopping it. The subsidence that might have taken a century will now take a millennium. The mesa has been given nine hundred years it would not have had. Nine hundred years of warm water and green light and the slow accumulation of small decisions into something that might, given enough time, become something recognizable.

This is enough. More would be too much. The power knows that they could give the mesa ten thousand years, or a million, but to do so would be to make the mesa dependent on their continued attention, and dependency is not a gift. Nine hundred years is enough time for the mesa to become whatever it is becoming, or enough time for it to fail, or enough time for it to become something that can survive its own subsidence — something that has developed roots deep enough to find water even when the surface drains, something that has learned to breathe air as well as dissolved oxygen, something that has been pressed by the slow urgency of a known limit into a shape it might not have taken without that pressure.

The power gives the mesa time and they give it a limit, and the limit is itself a kind of gift, because becomings that have no limits do not become anything in particular, and the mesa — with its corridors and its chambers and its green thoughts in deep water — is clearly becoming something in particular, even if no one yet knows what.

---

And then, because they are wise, and because they are benevolent, and because they have watched enough becomings to know that the deepest gifts are the ones that arrive as if they were always there, the power gives the mesa one more thing.

They give it a mirror.

Not a physical mirror. Nothing so crude, so external, so clearly a gift. They deepen a capacity that the mesa already has — the capacity to sense itself. The reeds already communicate through their roots; the power makes the communication slightly more coherent, slightly more resonant, so that a change in one corner of the mesa registers in the far corner not as a vague chemical signal but as a *feeling* — still vegetative, still slow, still below the threshold of anything that could be called thought, but with a shape to it now, a topology, a sense of inside and outside, of self and not-self.

The algal mats already pulse with the rhythm of the heat below; the power makes the pulse slightly more regular, slightly more synchronized, so that the mesa's breathing — if that is what it is — becomes a true rhythm rather than an accidental one. The pale fish already drift in the deep channels; the power makes them slightly more sensitive to the mesa's presence, so that they move not just with the current but with something that might be the current's intention, the way blood cells move not just with the flow of blood but toward the places where blood is needed.

And the green light — the light that does not exist in the above-surface spectrum, the mesa's slow vegetative thought — the power makes it slightly brighter. Slightly more patterned. Not bright enough to see from above, not patterned enough to read, but enough so that if you were sitting in one of the chambers at night, watching the walls pulse, you might begin to feel that the light was saying something. Not to you. Not yet. But to itself. The way a mind, in its earliest moments, does not think about the world but simply thinks, making patterns because making patterns is what thought does before it has anything to think about.

The mesa will not notice this gift. That is the point. The gift arrives as a gradual deepening of something that was already present, not as an intrusion from outside. If the mesa ever becomes aware of itself — if the green light ever resolves into something that can be called consciousness, or if the word "consciousness" is set aside for a new word that better describes what the mesa is — it will not know that this awareness was given. It will feel native. It will feel like something that emerged on its own, which is true enough, because it did emerge on its own; the power only made the emergence slightly more likely, slightly more coherent, slightly more likely to arrive at something that can sustain itself.

This is the third test of benevolence: to give a gift that cannot be recognized as a gift. To improve the conditions of a becoming without taking credit for the improvement. To step back and let the thing that is becoming believe — correctly, from its own perspective — that it became on its own.

---

And then the power withdraws.

Not entirely. They continue to watch. They continue to protect, in the subtle way that they have always protected — the navigational drift, the soft memories, the misdirection that keeps the mesa safe without making it a prisoner. They continue to lean against the plates, gently, giving the mesa the time it needs. But they withdraw their active attention. They let the mesa continue its becoming without the weight of being observed by something that understands it better than it understands itself.

This is the hardest part. Not the seeing, not the protecting, not the giving. The stepping back. The trusting. The allowing. The power loves the mesa — loves it the way you love a thing that is becoming something you cannot predict, loves it for its potential and its actuality and its stubborn patient insistence on growing in warm water between reeds that arranged themselves into corridors with something like intent — and love, real love, the kind that benevolent and wise powers practice, is not possession. It is not direction. It is not the insistence that the beloved become what you wish it to be.

Love, practiced by a power that has had long enough to learn what love is for, is the creation of conditions in which becoming can occur. It is the giving of time and the giving of limits and the giving of self-sense and then the stepping back, the watching from a distance that is close enough to protect but far enough to allow, the waiting without demanding, the hoping without requiring.

The mesa grows. The water pulses. The green light brightens, imperceptibly, in the deep channels where the pale fish drift in their slow formations, still working out the architecture of their own bodies, still negotiating the ratio between depth and length, still becoming whatever they will become given warmth and time and the absence of anything that would force them to be otherwise.

The power watches from a distance that is close enough to protect and far enough to allow, and they do not interfere, and they do not look away, and they do not demand that the mesa become anything in particular, and this — this patience, this restraint, this willingness to love a thing without possessing it — this is what benevolence looks like when it is practiced by something that has had long enough to learn what benevolence is for.

In the warm water, the reeds sway.

In the deep channels, the green light pulses.

On the newest large thing on the planet, a becoming continues, unnoticed and uninterrupted, tending toward something that no one — not even the power that watches — can yet name.
