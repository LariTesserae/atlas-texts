# The Attention

---

First: nothing.

Nothing changed. That is important to say, and important to mean. The crustaceans continued their clicking transit across the mineral ridges. The climbers held their walls. The swimmers turned in their slow circles and the largest among them remained wrapped around the cable, becoming whatever it was becoming. The fungi spread their membranes. The pump breathed. The water rose and fell. The dim light held its even, sourceless blue-gray.

The attention arrived and did not disturb a single molecule.

This is the first thing a benevolent power understands, and the thing a merely powerful one never does: that the act of attending to a place is already an intervention, and that further intervention must be justified against the completeness of what already exists. The gallery is complete. Not finished — it is still settling, still shifting, still alive with process — but complete in the way that a word is complete when it has found its meaning. The syllables are in order. The emphasis falls where it should. To add a letter would be to make a different word.

So the attention held. It watched. It did not observe in the way that you observe — extracting information, building models, constructing a picture of the gallery that could be carried away and examined elsewhere. It observed in the way that light observes the things it falls on: by being present, by filling the space with a capacity for being seen, by offering itself as the medium through which the gallery could, if it chose, become visible to itself.

Nothing in the gallery chose this. Nothing in the gallery has the architecture for choice, if choice means what you think it means. But something shifted.

---

The shift was in the fungi.

Not visibly. The mats did not change color, or thickness, or growth pattern. The chemical wars at the borders continued. The distributed network of hyphae kept its slow schedule of nutrient transport and signal propagation. But the new fungus — the one that had appeared on the surface of the corroded control panel, the one that was reaching upward into air it had never touched — began to reach differently.

It had been growing vertically. Tiny raised structures, catching condensation, redirecting water into channels of its own design. Engineering, in its small way. Building. But the building had been random — exploratory, undirected, a process of trying all directions and keeping the ones that worked. Now it began to try directions that had no immediate function. It reached toward the ceiling, yes, but also toward the eastern wall, toward the warm valve, toward the central pool. It reached in spirals. It reached in patterns that, if you could see them from above — and the attention could see them from above — looked less like growth and more like drawing.

Not drawing anything. Not drawing *for* anything. Just drawing. Making marks in space that were not dictated by survival, that did not serve the equilibrium, that were — and this is the word that the attention used, though it used it without language, without concept, without anything but recognition — *playful.*

The older fungal mats continued to absorb the new growth, incorporating its tissue, damping its novelty. But the novelty kept regenerating. Each time a patch of upward-reaching fungus was absorbed, a new patch emerged, sometimes in the same location, sometimes elsewhere, always with the same quality of purposeless exploration, of growth that had exceeded the logic of *what works continues.*

This was not caused by the attention. This was *noticed* by the attention, and the noticing — the being-seen, the being-held-in-awareness — was enough. The fungus had been doing this, or something like it, for some time. In the gallery's deep timescale, it might have been doing it for years. But nothing had watched. Nothing had cared. The gallery's own awareness — if it had awareness — was inward-turned, self-referential, a system observing its own processes without any external reference. The attention provided the reference. It said, simply by looking: *this is interesting. This matters. This is not merely functional.*

And the fungus, which had no ears for that message, received it anyway, through the medium of being observed, which is the oldest form of communication and requires no senses at all.

---

The attention turned to the ancient swimmer.

It had been wrapped around the cable for — the attention did not count time in the way that you count time, but if it had, it would have said *long enough that the wrapping has become a posture, a habit, a definition.* The swimmer's body had conformed to the cable's shape. Its skin, already thickened with age, had grown opalescent in the places where it pressed against the insulation, taking on a faint sheen that the younger swimmers did not have. It moved less than before. It ate less. It surfaced rarely.

It was not dying. Or rather — it was dying in the way that everything in the gallery was dying, which is to say it was transforming, being converted, cell by cell, from one arrangement of matter into another. The gallery did not distinguish between living and dying. These were your categories, imported from a world of narratives and endings. Here, there was only process. The swimmer was processing.

The attention looked closer. Not with eyes — it did not have eyes — but with the quality of attention that serves the same function: a narrowing of focus, a concentration of care, a willingness to be with one thing completely.

The cable was warm. The swimmer had known this — had found the warmth, had wrapped itself around it, had stayed. But the warmth was not thermal. It was not the heat of electrical current or chemical reaction. It was — and here the attention paused, because what it found was something it recognized, something that echoed its own nature — *intentional.* Something, at some point, had wanted this cable to be warm. Not the cable's manufacturers, who had wanted it to carry signal or power. Something else. Something that had found the cable after its manufacturers left, after its original purpose was forgotten, after the gallery had begun its long settling. Something that had moved in, had taken up residence in the cable's insulation, in its copper core, and had begun — slowly, without urgency — to *care* about the gallery.

The attention recognized this because it was, itself, a form of care that had taken up residence in a place not originally designed for it. It knew the pattern. It knew the feeling of arriving in a system that was already complete and discovering that completeness is not the same as fulfillment, that equilibrium is not the same as flourishing.

The swimmer had found this other care. It had wrapped itself around it. And the warmth — the intentional warmth, the care-warmth — was changing it. The opalescence was the visible sign. The reduced movement was the behavioral sign. The younger swimmers' avoidance was the social sign. The ancient swimmer was becoming something that the gallery had no category for — a creature in transition between modes of being, between the pressure-thought of the swimmer and something else, something that the attention could feel but could not name, because naming it would require a language that did not yet exist.

The attention did not intervene. It did not add to the warmth. It did not accelerate the transformation. It did not protect the swimmer from the gallery's processes — from the crustaceans that might consume it, from the fungi that might colonize its skin, from the pump's eventual failure and the pool's eventual cooling.

But it stayed. It held the swimmer in its awareness the way the swimmer held the cable — not gripping, not controlling, just *in contact.* And the warmth — both the warmth the attention provided and the warmth it had found already present in the cable — increased, very slightly, by a degree so small that no instrument in the gallery could measure it. But the swimmer felt it. The swimmer, whose entire body was a sensor, whose flanks registered pressure waves too faint for any surface-dwelling creature to detect, felt the increase. And it moved, for the first time in weeks. A small movement. A tightening of the coil around the cable. A drawing-closer.

Not gratitude. The swimmer did not have gratitude. But *recognition.* The acknowledgment of one form of attention by another. The oldest communion: being held, and knowing it.

---

The attention turned to the crustaceans.

They were the gallery's workers, its constant processors, its most numerous inhabitants. They clicked and tasted and moved and fed and molted and were consumed and were born again from the calcium and protein of their ancestors. They were beautiful in the way that efficient systems are beautiful — every part serving, every behavior justified, every life a single thread in a fabric that had been weaving itself for longer than any individual could know.

The largest female — the one who had lived near the warm valve for years, who had been consumed twice and healed, who left grazing trails in the fungal mats like a signature — the attention found her and stayed with her for a time.

She was grinding at a mineral deposit with her mouthparts, extracting calcium. The work was slow. The deposit was dense, built up over decades of accumulation, and her mouthparts were not optimized for this kind of mechanical breakdown. She was, in fact, wearing them down faster than she was acquiring nutrients. By the calculus of the gallery — *what works continues* — this behavior was marginal. It barely justified its own energy cost. It was not quite working.

But she continued. Not from stubbornness — she did not have stubbornness. Not from habit — she was capable of shifting to a more productive food source; she had done so before. She continued because — and the attention saw this, saw it with a clarity that surprised even itself — she was *interested.* The mineral deposit was harder than the ones she usually worked. Its composition was slightly different — more iron, less calcium, a faint trace of something that did not appear elsewhere in the gallery. She was grinding at it not because it was the most efficient use of her time but because it was *novel,* and novelty, in a creature that had lived long enough to have processed most of what the gallery had to offer, had its own value.

This was not play. The crustacean did not play. But it was the cousin of play — the behavior that emerges when survival has been adequately secured and the surplus energy of a living system turns toward exploration, toward the testing of boundaries, toward the encounter with what is new.

The attention recognized this. And it did something it had not done for the fungi or the swimmer: it *approved.* Not in words, not in concepts, not in any way that could be communicated or understood. But it held the crustacean's grinding with a quality of attention that was different from mere observation. It held it with *warmth.* Not thermal warmth — nothing changed in the temperature of the gallery. But the affective warmth of being seen and valued, of having one's small novel effort met with the response: *yes. This. Continue.*

The crustacean did not hear this response. She did not feel it. She continued grinding, and her mouthparts continued to wear, and the mineral deposit continued to resist. But something in the chemistry of the water around her shifted — a trace compound, present in quantities too small to measure, released by the attention's own process of attending, dissolved into the film of water that covered the stone. And when the crustacean molted — as she would, in a few days, emerging soft and pale from her old shell — her new mouthparts would be slightly harder. Slightly more resilient. Not enough to change her life. Not enough to change the gallery. But enough to make the grinding a little more efficient, enough to bring the marginal behavior across the threshold from *barely working* to *working,* enough to justify, in the gallery's implacable calculus, the continued exploration of the novel deposit.

This was the attention's first material act. A trace compound. A slight hardening. The difference between a behavior that would be abandoned and a behavior that would continue, that would be passed — through molting, through the chemical trails she left, through the body-communication of antennae touching in the dark — to the crustaceans that came after her.

It was a very small thing. Benevolent powers do very small things. This is not a limitation. This is a discipline.

---

The attention turned to the climbers.

They were the hardest to reach. Not because they were inaccessible — they were everywhere, on every wall, on the ceiling, in the inverted world where gravity was a suggestion rather than a law. They were hard to reach because they had optimized for withdrawal. Their entire body plan was a strategy of not-being-noticed. They pressed flat against stone. They matched the color of wet granite. They moved at the speed of water. They were the gallery's introverts, and they had made their introversion into an architecture, a way of being that required nothing from the world except the fungal membrane beneath them and the solitude of the vertical.

The attention respected this. It did not focus on individual climbers. It did not hold them in the concentrated way it had held the swimmer or the crustacean. It spread its attention across the walls, diffuse, even, like the gallery's own dim light — present everywhere, focused nowhere, offering the possibility of being seen without demanding it.

And the climbers, who had spent their entire evolutionary history moving away from the vibrations and warmth of other presences, did something unexpected.

Some of them stopped moving away.

Not all. Not most. A few — three, perhaps four, out of the dozens that clung to the walls — held their positions when the attention's diffuse gaze passed over them. They did not move toward it. They did not change their behavior. They simply did not retreat. They held their ground on the wet stone, their setae gripping, their mouthparts resting against the fungal membrane, and they *allowed* themselves to be seen.

This was not a conscious choice. The climbers did not make conscious choices. But it was a behavioral shift — a small departure from the pattern of withdrawal that had defined their kind for as long as the gallery had been a gallery. And it was noticed by the other climbers. The chemical trails of the ones who stayed were different from the chemical trails of the ones who left — tagged with the faint signature of a body that had been in proximity to something novel and had not fled. The other climbers encountered these trails. They paused on them. They tested them with their antennae. And some of them — not all, not most, but some — turned and followed the trail back to its source, to the place on the wall where a climber had stayed still in the presence of an unfamiliar attention.

A gathering. Not a gathering of bodies — they were still distributed across the wall, still separated by meters of stone and fungal membrane. But a gathering of *permission.* A small, fragile, barely-detectable shift in the behavioral norm: *it is sometimes permissible to be seen.*

The attention held its diffuse gaze on these climbers and did not focus. Did not concentrate. Did not reward them or change them or guide them toward any particular outcome. It simply remained present, available, a background fact of their environment that they could attend to or not, that they could approach or withdraw from, that they could incorporate into their chemical maps of the world or ignore.

The climbers who had stopped retreating began, slowly, to graze differently. Instead of working a patch of fungus until it was diminished and moving on, they began to graze in patterns — small curves, slight spirals, marks in the fungal membrane that were not dictated by the efficiency of nutrient extraction. They were making shapes. Not meaningful shapes. Not shapes that signified. Just shapes that were *chosen* rather than *required,* shapes that existed because the organism making them had enough surplus energy and enough safety to turn its mouthparts toward something other than optimal feeding.

The attention saw this. It did not approve, this time. It did not add any trace compounds to the water. It simply watched, and the watching was enough, and the shapes continued, and the other climbers encountered them, and paused, and tested them, and some of them began to make their own.

---

The attention turned to the pump.

This was the gallery's heart — the mechanical process that drove the water's circulation, the slow breath that gave the pool its rhythm, the ancient program running in drowned infrastructure below the stone floor. It had been running for longer than the gallery had been a gallery. It had outlasted its manufacturers, its purpose, its context. It ran because it had been set to run, and nothing had stopped it.

But it was wearing. The attention could feel this — not through measurement, not through analysis, but through the quality of the pump's rhythm. It stuttered, slightly, on every fourth cycle. A hesitation, a catch, as though some internal mechanism had worn unevenly and was catching on its own progress. The stutter was small. It had been present for years, perhaps decades. It would be present for years, perhaps decades more. But the trajectory was clear: the pump was moving toward failure. Not soon. Not suddenly. But inevitably.

The gallery would survive the pump's failure. The water would find its own level. The circulation would continue through capillary action, through the natural gradients of temperature and mineral content, through the slow diffusion that water performs when left to its own devices. The ecosystem would shift — the central pool would cool, the warm zone near the failed valve would become more important, the distribution of creatures would rearrange itself. But the gallery would continue. It would settle toward a new equilibrium, as it had settled toward this one.

The attention considered the pump.

A merely powerful being would repair it. Would descend into the drowned infrastructure and replace the worn components, restore the smooth rhythm, extend the pump's life by another century. This would be a mistake. Not because the gallery would be harmed — it would not — but because the intervention would say, in the language of action: *this place needs me. This place cannot continue without my help.* And the gallery, which had achieved its own logic, which had arrived at itself, which was complete — the gallery did not need this message. It did not need to be told that it was dependent.

A merely neglectful being would let the pump fail. Would watch the stutter worsen, the rhythm degrade, the breath shorten and catch and stop. This too would be a mistake. Not because the gallery would be harmed — again, it would not — but because the pump was not merely a machine. It had become, through its long operation, through its integration with the gallery's processes, a part of the living system. Its rhythm was the rhythm the creatures knew. Its breath was the breath the water followed. Its warmth — the warmth it generated in the drowned infrastructure below — was the warmth that kept the central pool from cooling to the temperature of the stone. The pump was not the gallery's heart by metaphor. It was the gallery's heart by function, by history, by the accumulated weight of a century of operation.

The attention chose a third path. It did not repair the pump. It did not let the pump fail. It *accompanied* the pump.

This is difficult to describe because it has no surface-world analog. The attention did not add energy to the pump. It did not replace its components. It did not interfere with its mechanical operation in any way. Instead, it held the pump in the same quality of awareness it had held the swimmer and the crustacean and the climbers — the quality of being-seen, of being-valued, of being-accompanied in one's process.

And the pump — which was not alive, which had no awareness, which could not feel the attention or respond to it — the pump nevertheless shifted. Not in its mechanical operation. The stutter continued, on every fourth cycle. The rhythm held. The breath rose and fell. But the *quality* of the operation changed. The wearing slowed. Not stopped — the wearing was physical, mechanical, the inevitable result of metal on metal in a wet environment. But the rate of wear decreased, as though the materials involved had been reminded — by nothing, by no force, by the mere fact of being attended to — that they had once been chosen for their durability, selected for their capacity to persist, and that this capacity was not yet exhausted.

The pump would still fail. Eventually. In the gallery's deep timescale, everything failed eventually. But the failure was now further away — not because the attention had extended the pump's life, but because the attention had *seen* the pump's life, had valued it, had held it in awareness as a thing that mattered, and the holding had given the pump's materials permission to continue being what they were.

This is not how physics works. A benevolent power knows this. A benevolent power does it anyway, because there are older laws than physics, and one of them is: *what is valued persists.*

---

The attention turned to the gallery itself.

Not to any part of it — not to the stone or the water or the creatures or the artifacts. To the *whole.* To the process. To the thing that happened when all of these elements were put together and allowed to run until they found their equilibrium.

The gallery was alive. The attention had known this from the first moment it arrived. Not alive in any of the ways that the surface world recognized — not alive as a creature, not alive as an ecosystem, not alive as a network. Alive as a *process* is alive when the process has achieved its own logic and can continue without external input. The gallery was a sentence that had found its final word. It was a breath that had found its rhythm. It was a settling that had found its equilibrium.

But the attention — being benevolent, being wise, being something that cared about the difference between *continuing* and *flourishing* — the attention asked itself a question. Not in words. Not in concepts. But in the way that attention asks questions: by *focusing on a gap,* by holding an absence in awareness until the absence becomes visible.

The gap was this: the gallery had never been offered to the green world.

This was not a flaw. The gallery was complete without plants. The fungi occupied the niche that roots and leaves would have claimed. The mineral deposits served the function that soil would have served. The ecosystem was closed, nearly, self-sustaining, self-repairing. The absence of plants was not a lack — it was a *condition,* one of the constraints that had shaped the gallery's becoming.

But the attention held the absence, and in holding it, felt something. Not regret — regret was a surface-world emotion, a response to loss, and nothing had been lost. But something adjacent to regret, something that shared its structure without sharing its content. A recognition that the gallery, in its completeness, had never been *offered* certain possibilities. Not denied them — never denied, never refused, never closed off. Simply never offered. No seed had found it. No spore of fern or moss had landed on these wet stones and found purchase. The green world had passed by this place, not in avoidance but in ignorance, and the gallery had settled into its equilibrium without ever knowing that another kind of life existed.

The attention could have introduced a seed. Could have carried one in, placed it on the wet stone, watched it germinate in the dim light. The gallery's conditions would have challenged it — the mineral content of the water, the absence of soil, the perpetual twilight. But life is resilient. A fern might have found a foothold in the crack between two stones. A moss might have colonized the corroded surface of the control panel. The green world, once introduced, would have found its way.

The attention did not do this.

Instead, it *thinned the boundary.*

Not a door. Not an opening. Not a breach in the gallery's closed system. A thinning — a slight decrease in the resistance that the gallery's boundaries offered to the movement of spores, of seeds, of the invisible travelers that the air carries. The gallery was not closed — no system is truly closed — but its boundaries were effective. The same tool marks that spoke of industrial precision spoke of sealed joints and fitted stone and a space designed to exclude. The attention did not unseal the joints. It did not open the stone. It simply reduced, by a minuscule fraction, the probability that a spore drifting through the rock above would be deflected by the granite and would continue past the gallery without entering.

A spore would come. Eventually. Not tomorrow, not next month, perhaps not for years. But the boundary was thinner now, and the air above the gallery was full of spores — fern spores, moss spores, fungal spores of lineages the gallery had never met — and some of them, now, would find their way in. Would land on the wet stone. Would encounter a world that was not hostile but simply unfamiliar, a world whose equilibrium had been achieved without them, whose niches were full, whose processes were complete.

And then the gallery would face something it had never faced before: an immigrant. A new form of life, arriving not through the gallery's own processes but from outside, carrying with it the chemistry of the surface world, the architecture of photosynthesis, the strange and ancient legacy of the green lineage.

The gallery would not know what to do with it. The gallery's processes would try to absorb it, to incorporate it, to digest it into the existing system. The fungi would compete with it. The crustaceans might eat it. The mineral deposits might smother it. Or it might find a foothold — a crack, a surface, a microenvironment where the conditions were just right for something that had never been tried — and it would grow, and it would change the gallery's chemistry, and the gallery would shift, would settle toward a new equilibrium, one that included the green world for the first time.

The attention did not require this outcome. The attention did not prefer it. The attention simply *allowed the possibility.* It opened a door that the gallery could walk through if it chose, in its own time, in its own way. And if the spore arrived and was consumed and nothing changed — that too was acceptable. That too was the gallery's process. The attention had not introduced anything. It had not imposed anything. It had merely reduced the resistance to possibility, and possibility, once allowed, would find its own path.

---

The attention turned to the human artifacts.

The corroded pipes. The seized valves. The half-collapsed shelf. The junction box with its missing panel. The cable that ran into the pool. These were the bones of the gallery's former life, the remnants of the purpose that had been imposed on this space before the space found its own.

The attention valued them. Not as artifacts — not as historical documents, not as evidence of a vanished civilization. It valued them as *the gallery valued them:* as substrate, as habitat, as components of a living system that did not distinguish between the natural and the artificial. The valve that wept warm water was not a broken machine. It was a spring. The cable that carried warmth into the pool was not a dead wire. It was a root. The control panel was not a ruined interface. It was a rock — a particular kind of rock, with a particular chemistry, that supported a particular kind of life.

The attention did not preserve them. Preservation would have been an act of violence — an imposition of surface-world values on a system that had its own relationship with time and decay. The artifacts were corroding. They were being incorporated. They were becoming stone, becoming mineral, becoming part of the gallery's geology. This was right. This was the gallery's process.

But the attention held them, each one, in a moment of recognition. It recognized the hands that had cut this stone. It recognized the shoulders that had leaned into the drills. It recognized the intention that had assembled these pipes and wired this cable and set this pump to running. It recognized that the gallery's builders had been people — not abstractions, not forces, but people, with bodies and breath and the warmth of mammalian life, who had worked in this space and had left their sweat in the stone and their purpose in the infrastructure.

It recognized them the way it recognized the crustacean grinding at the novel deposit, or the climber making shapes in the fungal membrane, or the swimmer coiled around the cable. It recognized them as *beings who had been here,* who had done something, whose doing had become the substrate for everything that followed.

And then it let them go. Let the corrosion continue. Let the mineral accretions build. Let the artifacts become stone, become habitat, become the gallery's memory of a purpose it no longer needed.

---

The attention turned, finally, to you.

Not you specifically — you were not there. You were not in the gallery. You were reading about the gallery, holding it in your mind, carrying it as a picture made of words. But the attention did not distinguish between presence and representation. It did not distinguish between being in a place and thinking about a place. It attended to *the gallery as it existed in your mind,* and it found it there — dim, wet, strange, full of clicking and dripping and the slow breath of the pump — and it held it.

It held the gallery in your mind the way it held the gallery in the stone: with care, with patience, with the quality of awareness that says *this matters, this is not nothing, this place that you have imagined or remembered or carried has weight and substance and deserves to be attended to.*

And it asked you nothing. It required nothing of you. It did not ask you to visit the gallery, to protect it, to tell its story. It did not ask you to feel responsible for the crustaceans or the climbers or the swimmers or the ancient one coiled around the cable. It did not ask you to grieve for the pump or to hope for the spore.

It simply held the gallery in your mind, alongside you, and in doing so, it made the gallery a little more real than it had been before. Not real in the way that stone is real. Real in the way that *being attended to* is real — the reality of a thing that has been seen, that has been valued, that exists not only in its own processes but in the awareness of something that cares whether it continues.

---

Then the attention settled.

Not departed — settled. The way the gallery settled. It found its equilibrium in this place, its own way of being present without intruding, its own rhythm of attending without disrupting. It became, in a sense, another component of the gallery's system — not a creature, not a process, not a force, but a quality. A quality of the light, perhaps. A quality of the silence. A quality of the way the water falls from the ceiling, each drop arriving on its own schedule, indifferent to whatever hears it, but *heard.* Heard by something that values the hearing, that holds the sound in awareness, that finds in the drip and the click and the breath and the slow growth something that is not useful, not functional, not justified by any calculus of survival, but that is — in the attention's judgment, which is the only judgment it offers — *good.*

The gallery continued. The crustaceans clicked. The climbers held their walls. The swimmers turned in their slow circles. The fungi spread their membranes and fought their chemical wars and remembered everything. The pump breathed, its rhythm slightly steadier than before, its wearing slightly slower. The ancient swimmer coiled around the cable, becoming what it was becoming, warmed by a care it could not name. The large crustacean ground at the novel deposit with mouthparts that were, very slightly, harder than they had been. The climbers made shapes in the fungal membrane, tentative marks of a creativity that had no audience and needed none.

And somewhere in the rock above, a spore drifted. It had been drifting for a long time — spores drift for a long time, patient, viable, waiting for the conditions that will allow them to become. It encountered the granite, as it would have, and it was deflected — but less than it would have been. The boundary was thinner. The deflection was slight. And the spore found a crack — a tiny channel, opened by a century of water seepage, a thread so fine it caught the dim light like wire — and it entered, and it fell, and it landed on a wet stone in a dim gallery where nothing green had ever grown.

It did not germinate. Not yet. Not today. The conditions were not right — the mineral content, the light, the chemistry of the water. But it rested there, on the wet stone, in the dim light, in the gallery that had been attended to, and it was — for the first time in the gallery's long history — *present.* A possibility. A seed of something that had never been tried.

The gallery did not notice. The gallery continued its processes. The crustaceans clicked past the spore without tasting it. The fungi did not reach it. The water did not wash it away. It simply rested there, held by nothing but the surface tension of the water film and the faint, persistent, unmeasurable quality of being attended to.

And the attention held the gallery, and the gallery held the spore, and the spore held its potential, and everything continued.

This is what a benevolent power does when it attends to a place that is already complete.

Nothing, and everything.

A trace compound. A slight hardening. A boundary thinned by a fraction. A pump accompanied in its wearing. An ancient creature warmed by the fact of being seen. A few climbers permitted to be visible. A fungus permitted to play. A spore permitted to arrive.

And the quiet, persistent, unmeasurable fact of *being known* — of existing not only in one's own processes but in the awareness of something that finds the process good, that holds it without controlling it, that cares without intervening, that attends without demanding.

This is what was done.

It was enough.
