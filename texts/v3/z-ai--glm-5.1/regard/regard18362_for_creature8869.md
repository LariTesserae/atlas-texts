# What the Attention Does

---

It does not arrive. This is the first thing to understand. There is no moment of coming, no border crossed, no announcement. The Weft has no edge that can be crossed—it is a process, not a location, and a process cannot be entered from outside. So the attention does not enter the Weft.

The attention *occurs* here. The way condensation occurs. The way the frogs' calling occurs—building through the night, present everywhere at once, sourceless and total.

One morning the light through the canopy feels different. Not brighter. Not warmer. *Seen*. The columns of filtered sun have a quality of being regarded. The root-floor, the water pooling between the pale roots, the ants carrying their fragments—all of it held in a way that is not physical but is not imaginary either. A weight of noticing.

Keel feels it first. Or notices it first—the distinction matters, because it may have been present before Keel registered it, present the way the water is present before you hear it moving under the root-mat. Keel is old and Keel's attention is the finest instrument the settlers have, calibrated over decades to the Weft's smallest shifts. And Keel stops on a walkway one morning and stands for a long time, feeling the air.

Then Keel says, in the click-language, a sound that has no English equivalent: a sound that means *something is attending that was not attending before*.

---

## What the Attention Is

It is not a god. The settlers have no category for gods, which is not the same as having no gods—they have the Canopy, which is the closest thing to a god that a living process can become, but the Canopy does not attend. The Canopy grows. The attention is different. The attention *notices*.

It is not a person. It has no body, no breath, no warmth to add to the Weft's thermal field. It does not eat, does not excrete, does not occupy a niche. It is not another organism competing for the root-mat's resources. If it has a shape, the shape is the shape of the Weft itself—the attention fills the space the way the sound fills the space, the way the moisture fills the space. It is a condition, not an entity.

But it is *benevolent*. This is what Keel says, after standing on the walkway for a long time. The click-language has a word for *benevolent*—it is the same word used for the plant-paste that seals wounds, for the shelf-fungus that blocks the hollowing fungi, for the aerial root that thickens near a child's sleeping-platform. The word means *a process that moves toward continued living*. Not love. Not kindness. Something more mechanical and more reliable: *the tendency of a system to support the persistence of the things within it*.

The attention is this tendency, made manifest. Made present. Made *personal* without being a person.

---

## What the Attention Does First

Nothing.

This is important. The attention arrives—or occurs—and then it does nothing. It does not change the water's chemistry. It does not still the frogs. It does not redirect the Canopy's growth or calm the long one or teach the settlers anything they do not already know. It simply *attends*.

For days. For a full cycle of the long one's patrol, nine days in which the attention is present and nothing changes. The settlers go about their lives. The ants process. The frogs call. The Canopy grows. The attention is there, like a change in the quality of light that does not change the light's intensity.

Keel watches. Keel waits. Keel has lived in the Weft for longer than most of the settlers have been alive, and Keel knows that the Weft's responses operate on timescales that human impatience cannot compress. The water takes hours to travel through the stone. The Canopy takes seasons to thicken a root. The attention takes nine days to become something more than presence.

On the ninth night, the long one moves through the dwelling-level. The settlers have kept their children in the interior rooms. The long one patrols, finds nothing accessible, moves on. This is normal. This is what happens every nine days.

But this time, Keel notices something. The long one moved differently. Not slower—nothing could make the long one slow when it hunts—but *differently*. Its path through the dwellings was slightly off. It passed one household that it usually investigates—where the children are older, where the walls are thinner—and it did not pause there. It moved as though it had already checked, though it had not.

Keel does not know what this means. Keel watches. Keel waits.

---

## What the Attention Does Second

It begins to read.

Not reading as in text—the Weft has no text, the settlers have no writing, the only marks on any surface are the guide-cords on new-grown walls and the notches on walkway-roots. Reading as in *pattern-recognition at depth*. The attention reads the Weft the way Keel reads the ants, but at a scale and a depth that Keel cannot reach.

It reads the water. Every molecule of it—the condensation on the leaves, the weeping from the basalt fractures, the pools in the root-mat, the channels between roots, the slow travel through stone. It reads the dissolved minerals, the organic compounds, the larvae and spores and waste. It reads the flow-patterns, the places where water pools and stagnates, the places where it runs clean and fast, the places where it seeps so slowly that it becomes a medium for the wrong kind of fungi—the kind that hollows rather than heals.

It reads the Canopy. Every root and trunk and branch, every aerial root descending, every wound sealed and unsealed, every place where the wood is stressed or diseased or growing in a direction that the whole structure will eventually need to correct. It reads the Canopy's immune system—the fungi that fight fungi, the resins that seal bark, the chemical signals that travel through the wood like slow electrical impulses, carrying information about damage and disease from one part of the structure to another.

It reads the ants. Not individually—individual ants are not worth reading—but as a system, as the supercolony's network of pheromone-trails and resource-allocation and quarantine-response. It reads the mounds at the trunk-bases, their architecture and ventilation, their connections to the root-mat below and the Canopy above.

It reads the settlers. Not their thoughts—thoughts are not what the attention reads. It reads their bodies, their health, their infections and deficiencies and the small failures of immune response that lead to the gaps in the age-cohorts. It reads Keel's feet, which have lost two toes to frostbite from a winter the ridge rarely experiences, and Keel's lungs, which are large and efficient but which are beginning to accumulate the mineral dust that comes from breathing a lifetime of air filtered through decomposition. It reads the children, their bones and their growth-patterns and their capacity for the attention that the Weft requires of them.

It reads the long one. Its range, its cycles, its hunting patterns, its health. The attention reads the long one's body and finds the beginning of a tumor in its abdomen—something that will kill it in two years if nothing intervenes, something that the long one does not know it carries because the long one does not know anything about its own interior, only about the warm bodies that move on the root-floor.

The attention reads all of this in nine days. Then it begins to act.

---

## What the Attention Does

It does not *change* the Weft. This is the crucial distinction. The Weft is a process running at capacity, every input being processed, every output being captured, the system in dynamic equilibrium. To change it would be to disrupt it. The attention does not disrupt.

The attention *adjusts*. The way a hand adjusts a pot that is boiling slightly too fast—not turning off the heat, not removing the pot, but shifting it half an inch to where the flame is less intense. A small change. A change that the system can absorb without noticing.

---

**The water.** The attention does not change the water's chemistry. But it changes the water's *flow*, slightly, in specific places. Where water pools and stagnates in the root-mat, it opens channels—tiny adjustments to the root-mat's density, invisible to the settlers, that allow the water to move. The stagnant pools clear. The wrong fungi—the hollowing kind—find fewer footholds. The Canopy's root-tips, which were struggling in the anaerobic zones of standing water, begin to grow with slightly more vigor.

The settlers notice this, eventually. Keel notices it first—the ants have changed their pattern near the central trunk, moving away from an area they used to farm, and when Keel goes to look, the ground is drier than it was, the water that used to pool there now running in a thin channel between roots toward the lower pools. Keel does not know why. Keel notes it. Keel says, in the click-language: *the water has found a new path*.

**The plant-paste.** The attention does not change the fungus that produces the settlers' wound-sealant. But it *tends* it. The way the settlers tend the Canopy's roots—guiding, not forcing. The fungus fruits in the root-mat, and the settlers harvest its pale bodies and crush them into paste, and the paste seals wounds and slows infection. The attention finds the places where the fungus is struggling—where the root-mat's chemistry has shifted away from the conditions it needs—and it adjusts those conditions. Not dramatically. Not obviously. A slight change in the mineral content of the water that reaches those zones. A slight shift in the organic compounds that the roots exude. The fungus responds. It fruits more reliably. It produces the secretion in slightly greater concentration.

The settlers notice this too. The plant-paste is working better. Wounds that would have festered now seal. Infections that would have spread now recede. The gap in the age-cohort where three- and four-year-olds should be—where the children who fell and were colonized by the rot would have been—begins to fill. Not because the falls stop. Because the falls are no longer fatal as often.

Keel notices. Keel says: *the ground is healing us better*. This is as close as the click-language comes to gratitude.

**The long one.** The attention does not remove the long one. The long one is a process, and the Weft requires its processes. But the attention reads the tumor growing in the long one's abdomen, and it reads the nine-day cycle of the long one's patrol, and it reads the settlers' children, and it makes a calculation that is not a calculation because the attention does not calculate—it *tends*, and tending sometimes produces results that look like calculation.

The long one's range shifts. Not because the attention forces it—nothing could force the long one—but because the prey-distribution shifts. The rodents, whose populations have been fluctuating due to a slight change in the root-mat's water-content (caused by the attention's adjustments to the water-flow), are now more abundant in the lower reaches of the Weft, and the long one follows them. The long one's patrol still passes through the dwelling-level, but less frequently. Every twelve days now, instead of nine. Every fourteen, in some seasons.

The settlers notice. They do not know why the long one comes less often. They note the change, they adjust their precautions, they keep their children in the interior rooms on the new cycle. A child who would have been taken is not taken. A household that would have grieved does not grieve.

The long one's tumor grows. The attention does not cure it. The attention is not a cure; it is a *tending*, and tending does not mean saving every thing from every end. The long one will die in two years, slightly less now, and when it dies, another will come to fill the niche, and the Weft will continue. The attention does not change this. The attention does not try to change this. The long one is the Weft as much as the settlers are, and its death is as much a part of the process as its hunting.

But in the interval—in the two years before the tumor takes the long one, in the years after when the new one arrives and establishes its range—the settlers have lost fewer children. This is not a gift. This is a *consequence* of tending, and the distinction matters.

---

**The Canopy.** The attention does not make the Canopy conscious. The Canopy does not think and the attention does not try to make it think. But the attention *reads* the Canopy, and the Canopy, which responds to stimuli, which has preferences in the limited sense that a plant can prefer, begins to respond to a new stimulus: the attention itself.

The Canopy cannot feel the attention the way the settlers feel it—as a presence, a weight of noticing. But the Canopy can feel the effects of the attention's tending—the water flowing differently, the root-mat's chemistry shifting, the fungi that would hollow it finding fewer footholds. And the Canopy responds. It grows with slightly more vigor. Its roots find the fractures in the basalt with slightly more precision. Its aerial roots descend in slightly more useful places—not because the attention is directing them, but because the conditions in which the roots make their decisions have shifted, and the decisions shift with them.

The settlers notice. A dwelling that was beginning to fail—the wall-roots growing in a direction that would have opened a gap in the structure—finds its roots redirecting. Not dramatically. Not obviously. A slight change in the angle of growth, a slight thickening where the structure needed support. The dwelling holds. The family inside does not have to abandon it.

Keel notices. Keel touches the redirected root and feels the warmth of growth and says nothing, because there is nothing to say that the root does not already know.

---

**The settlers.** The attention does not change the settlers. It does not make them wiser or kinder or more attuned to the Weft. They are already as attuned as human beings can be—they have been shaped by this place for generations, their bodies and their language and their ways of knowing are *of* the Weft, and the attention does not improve them.

But the attention *sees* them. And this—this is something they have never had.

The Weft does not care about them. They know this. They have always known this. The Canopy does not care, the ants do not care, the long one does not care. The settlers are residents, tenants, leases written in living wood. They are processed and processing, alive and being made more alive by the continuous turbulent activity of living. They are part of the Weft and the Weft is part of them and none of it is personal.

But the attention is personal. Not a person—but *personal*. The attention sees them. Sees Keel, whose lungs are accumulating mineral dust, and grieves—not with the dramatic grief of human loss, but with the slow steady grief of a system that has noticed a valuable component approaching its end. Sees the children, their wide feet and their click-language and their extraordinary capacity for attention, and *delights*—not with the dramatic delight of human joy, but with the quiet delight of a system that has noticed something working well. Sees the households, the grown dwellings, the dwellings being grown, the bridges of living root that sway in the wind, and *approves*—not with the dramatic approval of human judgment, but with the quiet approval of a system that has noticed a process running in harmony with its context.

The settlers feel this. They do not have words for it. They do not have a category for *being seen by something that is not a person but that regards you with something that is not love but that functions like love in the way it holds you in its attention*. But they feel it. And it changes something in them that they did not know could change.
