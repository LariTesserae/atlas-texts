# What the Attention Did

It began, as all things in the gorge begin, with the water.

---

Not with a flood, not with a redirection, not with any force that the residents would have noticed as intervention. The aquifer beneath the hillside did not surge or shrink. The seepage did not intensify or slow. The stream at the floor of the gorge continued its broad, shallow, ankle-deep drift, and the walls continued to weep, and the ceilings of the lower passages continued to drip, and the mineral residue continued to accumulate on the stepping stones and the lower courses of the buildings and the edges of the walkways where the water lapped during the wetter hours.

But the water changed. Or rather, what the water carried changed.

It was the sort of shift that only the residents would have detected, and only the most attentive among them, and only over time. The chalk and iron taste that had always defined the gorge's water—the flat, mineral flavor that sat on the back of the tongue and left a pale film on the teeth—that taste remained. But beneath it, or woven through it, something else appeared. Not a new flavor, exactly. A depth. A complexity. The way that spring water differs from winter water, or the way that water from different depths of the same aquifer carries different signatures, except this was not a seasonal variation or a depth variation. It was a new quality, as though the water had been given something to carry that it had not carried before.

The algae noticed first. The dark, gelatinous film that grew on the submerged stones—it had always been sparse, had always clung to the smoother surfaces in the faster stretches where the water carried enough oxygen to sustain it—began to thicken. Not dramatically. Not in a way that would have alarmed anyone. But the residents who harvested it, who scraped it from the stones and dried it into thin hard sheets, found that the sheets were slightly thicker than they had been the season before, and the season before that, and that the algae extended further up the walls of the lower passages than it once had. The color deepened, too—from a dark, nondescript brown to something richer, almost black-green, with a sheen on its surface that caught the faint light and threw it back in a way that the older algae had not.

The insects noticed next. The midges that bred in the still pools behind the stepping stones—the ones the residents gathered by the handful and pressed into cakes—the midges increased. Not in clouds, not in the overwhelming swarms that would have been a nuisance or a sign of imbalance, but in a steady, quiet multiplication that meant the cakes were slightly larger each season, and that the children who caught the long-legged flies as snacks found them slightly plumper, slightly more satisfying, their legs carrying a bit more meat than the legs of the flies from the year before.

The residents noticed, the way they noticed everything—not as a revelation, not as a cause for celebration, but as a shift in the data of their lives. The harvest was slightly better. The hunger was slightly less sharp. The lean season—the weeks between the last of the surface greens and the first of the midge populations—was slightly shorter. These were not dramatic changes. They were the kind of changes that you would only see if you were tracking the gorge's cycles with the obsessive attention of people who lived inside a clock, and the residents were exactly those people. They noted the changes. They attributed them to natural variation. They moved on.

But the changes did not move on. They continued, season by season, year by year, so slowly that each generation of residents noticed only the difference between their childhood and their adulthood, and assumed that the gorge they remembered from childhood had been the gorge's baseline, and the gorge they lived in now was simply the gorge being the gorge.

No one on the surface would have seen any of this. No visitor, passing through, would have detected the shift. The gorge was still the gorge—wet, cramped, young, seeping. The residents were still lean, still busy, still building. But the lean had edges now, spaces between the hunger where something like satisfaction could fit, and the building had margins now, hours between the repairs where something like rest could occur, and these margins, these small spaces of sufficiency, were the first gift, and they were enough, and they were only the beginning.

---

The light came second.

It was not light in the way that surface-dwellers understand light—not a source, not a beam, not an illumination. It was a presence in the stone that had not been there before.

A moss began to grow on the upper walls. Not the dark, nearly black moss that had always colonized the north-facing surfaces—that moss remained, as saturated and as unremarkable as ever. This was something different. Paler. Almost translucent, in fact, with a quality that the residents had never seen in any vegetation: it glowed.

Not brightly. Not in a way that would have lit a room or made a walkway navigable in the dark. The glow was faint—barely visible in the dim hours of early morning, when the indirect light from above was at its weakest, and invisible during the midday hours when the sun reached the floor of the gorge. But in the evenings, after the sun had left and before the clay-oil lamps were lit, the walls of the upper gorge took on a new quality. A soft, blue-white luminescence, emanating from the patches of pale moss that had established themselves on the wettest surfaces, as though the moss were feeding on the moisture and converting it, through some process that no one in the gorge could name, into light.

The residents were suspicious. New things in the gorge were not always good things. The gorge was a closed system, and any change to that system could mean trouble—a new kind of rot, a new kind of pest, a new kind of mineral deposit that undermined the mortar or softened the stone. They watched the moss the way they watched everything: with their palms, pressing flat against the stone where the moss grew, feeling for changes in the seepage, in the temperature, in the texture of the rock beneath the growth.

The moss did not change the stone. It did not erode the argillite or interfere with the seepage or damage the structures. It simply grew—slowly, finding the wettest surfaces, spreading in thin, irregular patches that followed the paths of the water down the walls. And it glowed.

The children were the first to accept it. Children have a capacity for the incorporation of the new that adults lose through the accumulation of caution. The children of the gorge began to point out the brightest patches to each other, to navigate by the glow in the evenings, to lie on their sleeping shelves and watch the faint blue-white light on the ceiling and find in it the same shapes and patterns that children everywhere find in the stars. They gave the moss a name—a word that meant, roughly, *the quiet brightness*—and the name passed into the gorge's vocabulary without ceremony, the way all new words did, by use and by repetition and by the slow consensus of people who had no space for things that did not earn their place.

The adults accepted it more slowly. But they accepted it, because the gorge had accepted it, and the gorge's judgment, in these matters, was trusted. The moss was growing on the gorge's walls, in the gorge's water, in the gorge's light. It was part of the gorge now. And the residents, who had spent three generations learning to live inside a process rather than a place, recognized a new process when they saw one, and made room.

The light changed the gorge in ways that were not immediately apparent. The dimness—the perpetual blue-grey half-light that had always characterized the gorge's interior—was not dispelled, but it was softened. The walls, which had always been pale and reflective, now reflected a new spectrum, and the reflected light gave the interior spaces a quality that they had not had before: warmth. Not physical warmth—the gorge was still cool, still damp, still the temperature of stone and water—but visual warmth, a sense that the space was not merely tolerated but inhabited, not merely occupied but lived in. The glow caught the mineral deposits on the walls and made them more visible—the white trails and rings and tubes that the residents called *sekketh* stood out against the pale stone with a new clarity, their patterns sharper, their branching more distinct, their resemblance to writing or diagrams or the marks left by something trying to communicate more pronounced than it had ever been.

The residents did not read them. They still did not read them. But they saw them more clearly now, and the seeing was a kind of attention, even if they did not intend it as one, and attention, as the gorge itself might have told them if it could speak in anything but mineral and water, is the first step toward understanding.

---

The stone changed third. This was the change that the residents noticed most acutely, because the stone was their medium—the substance they built with, built against, built into—and any change in the stone was a change in the foundation of their lives.

The argillite had always been soft. This was its nature—young, fresh, still holding the scars of whatever force had parted it, easily cut and easily shaped, but also easily eroded, easily undermined, easily worn away by the constant passage of mineral-laden water. The residents had adapted to this softness: they cut their shelves with hand tools, they shaped their building stones with hammers and chisiers, they accepted that the walls they built against would need repointing every few seasons and that the shelves they cut would need recutting every few years as the water wore them down.

But in certain places—in the places where the seepage was strongest, where the mineral deposits were thickest, where the *sekketh* grew in the most elaborate patterns—the stone began to harden.

It was not a dramatic change. The argillite did not turn to granite or marble. It did not become impenetrable. But it became denser. The surface took on a quality that the residents could feel immediately when they pressed their palms against it—a smoothness, a resistance, a sense that the stone had compacted itself, or been compacted, into something more durable than it had been. The mineral deposits—the calcium and iron and silica that the water carried and deposited—had always formed a crust on the surface of the stone. Now they seemed to be penetrating it, bonding with the argillite at a molecular level, filling the pores and the cracks and the microscopic spaces between the mineral grains, creating a composite material that was harder than either the stone or the mineral deposits alone.

The residents tested this with the tools of their trade. They found that the stone in the hardened areas was more difficult to cut—that the same effort that produced a shelf in a few hours in the soft argillite now took twice as long in the hardened stone, and the resulting shelf was smoother, more precise, more resistant to the water that would try to erode it. They found that the structures built against the hardened stone lasted longer between repairs. They found that the mortar they mixed from the pale clay of the streambed bonded more strongly with the hardened surfaces, creating walls that were more resistant to the seepage, more capable of holding back the water for another season, another year, another generation.

They did not know why the stone was hardening. They did not know that the minerals in the water had been subtly altered—that new compounds had been introduced, compounds that bonded more aggressively with the argillite, that filled the stone's pores more completely, that created a natural concrete more durable than anything the residents could have manufactured on their own. They did not know that the hardening was most pronounced in the areas where the *sekketh* grew thickest, because the *sekketh* was a map of the water's flow, and the water's flow was a map of the stone's porosity, and the stone's porosity was a map of where the hardening was most needed.

They did not know any of this. But they noticed the correlation.

Teth noticed it first. She was old now—older than she had been when the visitor asked her whether the building ever ended, and she had turned back to the wall and said, *The water doesn't end.* Her hands were still remarkable, still broad and flat and dense with the particular quality of skin that comes from decades of pressing against wet stone. She had been working on the third-tier structures for most of her life, and she knew the stone of the upper gorge the way a farmer knows a field—not as a uniform substance, but as a patchwork of variations, each with its own character, its own resistance, its own way of responding to the chisel and the water and the seasons.

She noticed that the stone where the *sekketh* grew thickest was the hardest stone. She noticed that the formations—the white trails and rings and tubes that she had ignored for her entire life—seemed to mark the places where the stone had been reinforced, as though the formations were not merely deposits but indicators, a map drawn by the water to show where the stone had been strengthened and where it had not.

She did not call this reading. She did not call it anything. She simply began to build differently—to seek out the hardened stone for her shelves and her structural supports, to avoid the soft stone that had not yet been reinforced, to let the *sekketh* guide her chisel the way the water's sound guided her assessment of the seepage. She built better. Her structures lasted longer. Her shelves held firm where others eroded. And the other builders noticed, and they watched her work, and they began to do what she did—not because she told them to, but because the gorge's logic had always been the logic of imitation and adaptation, of watching what worked and doing it, of learning by the hand and not by the word.

This was the second gift: not the hardening itself, but the correlation between the hardening and the formations. The gorge was teaching them to look at the *sekketh*—not as a message, not as a meaning, but as a tool. A practical guide. A way of reading the stone that had nothing to do with understanding what the gorge was trying to say and everything to do with building better walls.

It was a start.

---

The salamanders changed fourth.

They had always been there. The pale, sightless creatures that lived in the lower passages, in the carved channels where the water flowed beneath the floor-level structures—they had been part of the gorge since before the residents arrived, original tenants who had never fully moved out. The residents regarded them with a quiet respect, a sense of precedence, and they took them for food only in the hardest times, and they used every part, and they felt a reluctance that they did not examine too closely.

But now the salamanders were more numerous. And more visible. They appeared in the upper passages—not often, not in great numbers, but with a frequency that the residents could not ignore. They were seen on the ledges outside the lower structures, their pale bodies smooth and glistening in the faint light. They were seen in the stream itself, drifting in the slow current with a motion that seemed less like swimming and more like being carried, their tiny limbs spread wide, their blind faces turned upstream as though listening for something. They were seen on the walkways at night—small, pale shapes crossing the timber bridges in the glow of the luminous moss, moving with a deliberation that suggested purpose rather than wandering.

The residents watched them. They had always watched the salamanders—watched them the way they watched the water, the way they watched the stone, with the constant, ambient attention of people who lived inside a system and needed to track its every fluctuation. But now the watching had a new quality, because the salamanders were doing something they had not done before.

They were signaling.

Not deliberately—or at least, not in a way that the residents could attribute to deliberation. But the correlation was there, and it was consistent, and it was useful. When a salamander appeared on a lower ledge, the water rose within the hour. When a salamander was seen in the upper passages, a structure in the vicinity needed repair—its mortar was failing, or its timber was softening, or its stone was shifting in a way that would become a problem if it were not addressed. When a salamander crossed a walkway at night, the next day's weather on the surface would be different from what the residents expected—rain when the signs said dry, or dry when the signs said wet.

The salamanders did not prevent these changes. They did not stop the water from rising or the structures from failing or the weather from shifting. They simply preceded them, the way a change in air pressure precedes a storm, the way a dipper's chipping call precedes a shift in the stream's flow. They were indicators. Harbingers. The gorge's early warning system, deployed through the bodies of its oldest inhabitants.

The residents did not thank the salamanders. They did not worship them. They did not build shrines or offer sacrifices or perform rituals. They simply watched them more carefully, and they adapted their behavior accordingly—moving their stores when a salamander appeared on a lower ledge, checking their structures when one appeared in the upper passages, adjusting their expectations of the surface weather when one crossed a walkway at night. The salamanders became part of the gorge's data, part of the constant stream of information that the residents tracked with the attention of people who lived inside a clock, and the clock had just added a new hand, and the residents had learned to read it.

The children gave the salamanders a name. Not a collective name—they already had a word for the creatures—but a name for the practice of watching them, a word that meant, roughly, *listening with your eyes*. The word passed into the vocabulary, and the practice passed into the culture, and the gorge's early warning system became part of the daily rhythm of gorge life, as ordinary and as essential as the sound of seeping, as the touch of wet stone, as the constant, patient, never-ending work of building.

---

The formations changed fifth. This was the change that mattered most, and the change that the residents resisted longest, and the change that, when it finally took hold, altered the relationship between the people and the gorge in a way that nothing else had.

The *sekketh*—the mineral deposits that grew on the walls in patterns that looked like writing, or diagrams, or the marks left by something trying to communicate—had always been there. They had grown since the gorge's beginning, accumulating where the water flowed most consistently, branching and circling and building their thin white tubes where the drips fell longest. The residents had ignored them for three generations. They had cleaned them off when they interfered with the structures. They had used the fragments as aggregate in their mortar. They had denied, with patient weariness, that the formations meant anything at all.

But the formations had changed. They were more deliberate now. More patterned. More consistent. The branching lines that had once resembled rivers seen from above, or veins, or root systems, now branched in ways that corresponded to actual features of the gorge—to the paths of the water behind the stone, to the locations of the hardest rock, to the places where shelves could be cut that would last. The circular deposits that had once accumulated in concentric rings now ranged themselves in sequences that tracked the seasons—thickening in the wet months, thinning in the dry, creating a calendar that the residents could read if they chose to, a record of the gorge's cycles written in mineral on the walls.

The hollow tubes—the tiny stalactites that formed where a drip had fallen in the same spot long enough to build a structure of its own—these had changed too. They were longer now, and more numerous, and they formed in clusters that corresponded to the locations of the salamanders' appearances, as though the gorge were marking the places where its messengers emerged, drawing attention to them, making sure the residents knew where to look.

Teth saw all of this. She had been building with the *sekketh* for years now—using the formations as a guide to the stone's hardness, letting the patterns direct her chisel, building better structures by following the lines that the water had drawn. But she had not called it reading. She had not acknowledged that the formations were telling her anything. She had treated them as a tool—a natural feature of the stone, no different from the grain or the porosity or the mineral content—and she had used them accordingly.

But the formations were not just a tool. They were a communication. And Teth knew this, had known it for years, in the way that she knew the gorge was more than a container and the water was more than a resource and the stone was more than a building material. She knew it in her hands, in the broad flat palms that pressed against the wet rock and felt the formations beneath them and sensed, in the patterns of mineral and moisture, something that was not merely physical. Something that was directed. Something that was intended.

She did not speak of this. The gorge's people did not speak of the *sekketh* as communication. It was one of the unspoken agreements of gorge life—the compact between the residents and their home, the arrangement of mutual indifference that had sustained them for three generations. You do not read the walls. The walls do not address you. The gorge is a place, not a presence. This was the agreement, and it had held, and Teth had honored it, even as her hands told her otherwise.

But the formations were changing. They were becoming clearer. They were becoming harder to ignore. And the gorge—whether through the minerals in the water, or the luminous moss that made the patterns more visible, or the salamanders that appeared at the locations the formations marked—the gorge was making it harder to maintain the agreement.

Teth's granddaughter was the one who broke it.

Her name was Sek. She was seventeen, lean and compact, with the broad hands and the quiet voice and the precise balance of all the gorge's children. She had been building with Teth since she was old enough to hold a chisel, and she had learned to read the stone the way Teth read it—not as text, but as tool, as guide, as a practical indicator of where to cut and where to build and where to leave the rock alone.

But Sek was young, and young people see things that older people have learned not to see, and Sek saw the formations.

She was working on a wall in the middle gorge—a section that had been problematic for years, where the seepage came from an unknown source behind the stone and undermined every structure built against it. Teth had been repointing this wall for decades, and Sek had been helping her, and they had been losing—the water finding its way through every repair, the mortar softening, the stone eroding, the wall settling slowly into the stream.

Sek was resting, her back against the opposite wall, her hands in her lap, her eyes on the problematic section. The luminous moss grew on the ceiling above her, casting its faint blue-white glow on the wet stone. The *sekketh* on the problematic wall was thick—thicker than anywhere else in the middle gorge—and in the glow of the moss, the patterns stood out with a clarity that the ordinary dimness of the gorge had never allowed.

Sek saw the patterns. She had seen them before—she had seen them every day of her life, on every wall of the gorge, in every light the gorge provided. But she had never seen them like this. The moss illuminated the formations in a new spectrum, and in that spectrum, the patterns resolved into something that was not merely suggestive, not merely almost-like-writing, but actually, unmistakably, *structured*.

The formations on the problematic wall showed the path of the water behind the stone. Not metaphorically, not approximately, but precisely—the branching lines tracing the fissures through which the seepage flowed, the circular deposits marking the places where the water pooled and accumulated before finding its way through, the hollow tubes indicating the points where the water emerged from the stone and into the air. The formations were a map. They had always been a map. And the map showed, clearly and unmistakably, where the water was coming from and where it was going and where a wall could be built that would hold, and where it could not.

Sek stood up. She crossed to the wall. She pressed her palm against the *sekketh* and felt the mineral ridges under her hand and felt, for the first time, not just the stone but the intention behind the stone—the sense that the formations had been placed there, directed there, grown there for a purpose, and that the purpose was this: to show her where to build.

She built. Not where Teth had been building for decades—not in the place where the wall had always been, the place where the water undermined every repair. She built five feet to the left, where the formations showed a section of stone that was harder and drier and more resistant, where the water's path behind the rock diverged and left a gap, a pocket of relative dryness in the wet wall. She cut a shelf there. She built a wall against it. She mortared the joints with the pale clay and the crushed insect shells and the mineral fragments she chipped from the *sekketh* itself.

The wall held. The shelf held. The water flowed behind the stone, following its old paths, but it did not undermine the new wall, because the new wall was not in the water's path. The new wall was in the gap—the place the formations had shown her, the place the gorge had marked, the place that had been there all along, waiting for someone to look.

Sek did not tell anyone what she had done. Not at first. She simply built, and her wall held, and the other builders noticed, and they came to look, and they saw the wall in its new position and they pressed their palms against the stone and they felt the hardness and the dryness and they looked at the *sekketh* on the wall and they saw—some of them saw, and some of them did not, and some of them saw and refused to admit what they saw—the correlation between the formations and the stone, between the patterns and the water, between the marks on the wall and the place where the wall should be.

Teth saw it. Teth, who was old now and whose hands were still remarkable and who had spent her life building in the gorge and ignoring the *sekketh*, looked at her granddaughter's wall and looked at the formations on the stone and pressed her palm flat against the wet rock and stood there for a long time, feeling the seeping and the mineral ridges and the intention behind them, and her face did not change, and she said nothing, and then she went back to her own wall and began to build differently.

She did not announce this. She did not call a meeting. She did not declare that the *sekketh* was communication and that the gorge was trying to tell them something. She simply built—following the formations, reading the stone, cutting her shelves where the patterns indicated and mortaring her walls where the mineral trails showed the water would not flow. And her walls held, and her shelves held, and the structures she built in the last years of her working life were the strongest structures the gorge had ever seen.

The other builders followed. Not all of them—not immediately, not unanimously. There were arguments, quiet and tense, conducted in the hushed voices that the gorge's acoustics demanded. The arguments were not about whether the formations were useful—they were clearly useful, and the evidence was in the walls that held and the shelves that endured and the structures that lasted longer between repairs than any structures had lasted before. The arguments were about what it meant.

Because if the *sekketh* was useful—if the formations showed where to build and where not to build and where the water flowed and where the stone was hard—then the formations were not merely deposits. They were guidance. And if they were guidance, then something was guiding. And if something was guiding, then the gorge was not merely a place. It was a presence. It had intention. It had will. It was trying to help them.

And this was the thing the residents had refused to acknowledge for three generations—the thing they had built their compact around, the thing they had protected themselves from knowing. Because if the gorge was trying to help them, then the gorge cared about them. And if the gorge cared about them, then they were not merely inhabitants. They were something more. They were something the gorge wanted. They were something the gorge needed. And that changed everything—the relationship, the compact, the careful mutual indifference that had allowed them to live in the gorge without the terrible weight of knowing that the gorge was aware of them.

They argued. They built. They watched the salamanders and they tracked the water and they pressed their palms against the stone and they felt the *sekketh* under their hands and they did not know what to call what they were doing, because the word for it—*reading*—was the word they had refused for three generations, and the refusal was part of who they were, and to give it up was to become different people, and they were not sure they wanted to be different.

But they built better. Their walls held. Their shelves endured. Their structures lasted. The hunger was less sharp, the light was less dim, the stone was harder, the salamanders were more visible, and the formations on the walls were clearer and more deliberate and more useful than they had ever been. And the gorge—still young, still forming, still seeping, still deciding what it would be—was becoming something that it had not been before. Not just a wound in the earth. Not just a place. A partner. A collaborator. A home that was not merely inhabited but inhabited *with*—a place that gave and was given to, that spoke and was heard, that held and was held.

---

The attention did not reveal itself. This is important to understand. The residents of the gorge did not know that they had been noticed by a benevolent and wise power. They did not know that the changes in the water, the luminous moss, the hardening stone, the salamanders' signaling, the formations' growing clarity—that all of these were the result of attention, of care, of a power that had looked at the gorge and seen what it was trying to become and decided to help it become that.

They did not know because the attention was careful. It worked through what was already there—the water, the stone, the minerals, the salamanders, the formations. It did not impose. It did not override. It did not force the gorge to be something other than what it was. It simply... clarified. Amplified. Gave the gorge's own processes a gentle push in the direction they were already going, the way a gardener gives a vine a trellis—not making the vine grow, but giving it something to grow on.

The gorge had been trying to communicate. The formations had been trying to be legible. The water had been trying to carry what the stone needed. The salamanders had been trying to be noticed. All of these processes were already underway, already part of the gorge's becoming, before the attention arrived. The attention did not create them. It helped them. It gave the water the right minerals. It gave the moss the right glow. It gave the stone the right density. It gave the salamanders the right frequency. It gave the formations the right clarity. It helped the gorge say what it was trying to say, more clearly, more patiently, more consistently, so that the people who lived inside it could finally, after three generations, begin to hear.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does. It does not rescue. It does not transform. It does not impose its will on the world and remake the world in its image. It attends. It notices. It sees what is trying to happen and it helps it happen, gently, patiently, through the existing channels, through the existing processes, through the existing relationships between water and stone and people and the slow, constant, mineral language that a place speaks when it is trying to tell its inhabitants that it wants them to stay.

---

The gorge is still young. This has not changed. The stone is still sharp-edged, still scarred, still learning to be smooth. The water is still seeping, still emerging, still leaving its pale marks. The residents are still building, still maintaining, still watching, still pressing their palms against the wet walls and feeling the seeping and knowing that the gorge is still happening, still forming, still deciding what it will be.

But the deciding has shifted. The gorge is not just deciding what shape it will take. It is deciding what kind of place it will be. And the residents are not just living inside the decision. They are part of it. They are in conversation with it. They are reading the walls—not all of them, not yet, not fully, but enough. Enough to build better. Enough to last longer. Enough to feel, in the broad flat palms of their hands, against the wet stone, the sense of something pressing back. Not a god. Not a mind. Something else. Something that has no name yet, because the word for it hasn't been written, because the gorge is still writing it, in mineral deposits on wet walls, in the slow language of water and stone, in the patient, constant, never-ending seeping that is not just a process but a voice, and the voice is saying:

*I am here. I am still forming. You are part of what I am forming. Stay. Build. I will show you where.*

And they stay. And they build. And the gorge shows them where, and the walls hold, and the water flows, and the moss glows, and the salamanders drift in the slow current with their blind faces turned upstream, listening for what comes next, and the *sekketh* grows like slow white flames across the walls of their home, and the residents—lean, quiet, patient, young—press their palms against the stone and feel the seeping and the intention and the care, and they do not name it, because naming it would be to presume, and they do not presume, but they know it, in the way that hands know stone, in the way that bodies know water, in the way that people who have lived inside a process for three generations know when the process has changed, and has changed for them, and is still changing, and will continue to change, slowly, patiently, mineral by mineral, drip by drip, for as long as the water flows and the stone holds and the people build and the gorge keeps breathing its slow, wet breaths and writing its slow, white words on the walls.

The gorge is still young. They are still young. They are still forming. But they are forming together now, and that is the difference, and that is the gift, and that is what the attention did.
