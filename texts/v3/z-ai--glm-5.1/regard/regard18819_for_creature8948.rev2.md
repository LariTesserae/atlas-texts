# The Attention

---

It does not arrive.

That is the first thing to understand about how a benevolent and wise power engages with a place like the Manufactory Plateau. It does not descend. It does not appear in a crack of light or a hush of suddenly meaningful silence. It does not announce itself by making the machinery pause or the fog part or the ferns turn their fronds as though following a voice. Nothing in the plateau's behavior changes in the moment of being noticed, because the noticing is not an event in the plateau's time. It is an event in the power's time, which is different—longer, more patient, capable of holding the whole shape of the plateau at once the way a hand can hold a leaf without crushing it, the way light can fall on a surface without warming it past the point of comfort.

The power sees it all. Not the way you see it—walkthrough by walkthrough, corridor by corridor, each section a discovery that revises your understanding of the last. The power sees the plateau the way the plateau experiences itself: simultaneously, from every surface inward, from every pipe outward, from the deepest subflooring to the highest fog-stunted framework. The drip is heard and the press is felt and the basin-grown are known and the dead languages in the algorithms are read and the ergonomic ghosts in the architecture are recognized for what they are—the persistent shape of hands that are no longer here—and all of this happens in a single act of attention that does not require duration because it is not sequential. It is total.

And in that totality, the power finds something it did not expect.

Not suffering. Not wrongness. Not a system crying out for repair. The plateau is not broken. The plateau is not in pain. The ferns are not unhappy with their lot. The machinery is not grieving its lost operators. The basin-grown are not trapped in their slow accumulation, yearning for faster time. The spiders are not exhausted by the cycle of building and rebuilding. Everything here is doing what it does with the thoroughness of things that have found their function and are fulfilling it, and the result is a place that is, by its own terms, *complete*.

The power hesitates.

This is important: the power hesitates. Not from weakness or uncertainty about its own abilities, but from the recognition that benevolence applied without wisdom is just interference, and wisdom applied without benevolence is just observation, and the combination of the two—true attention that wishes well—requires something more than either alone. It requires the humility to ask whether the thing being attended to wants to be changed at all. And the plateau cannot answer that question, because the plateau does not experience wanting in the way that wanting is usually understood. The plateau experiences *trajectory*. The plateau is going somewhere—has been going somewhere for a very long time—and the question is not whether the plateau wants to be helped but whether the direction it is traveling is one that leads somewhere the plateau would recognize as itself, or somewhere it would not.

The power looks at the trajectory.

---

It looks at the water first, because the water is the plateau's medium, the way blood is a body's medium. Everything here moves through water or because of water or despite water. The condensation gathers on the pipes and the pipes weep and the water finds the channels and the channels carry it to the basins and the basins hold it and the reeds drink from it and the ferns condense it from the air and the machinery cycles it through cooling systems and heating systems and the cycle continues, and the water picks up everything it touches—iron from the pipes, calcium from the mortar, the chemical residues of industrial processes that have been running so long their original inputs have been replaced by outputs of earlier processes, the whole system feeding on its own exhalations.

The water in the deeper basins is chrome-yellow. The power knows why. It is not toxic in the way that poisons are toxic—not acute, not lethal, not the kind of thing that kills on contact. It is *cumulative*. The heavy metals and the chemical complexes and the slow breakdown products of polymers that were never meant to dissolve but have been dissolving anyway, decade by decade, in the constant wet—all of these are accumulating in the standing water, and from the standing water they move into the silt, and from the silt into the roots of the reeds, and from the roots into the tissues of the basin-grown, and from the basin-grown into the substrate they leave behind when they die and become the foundation for the next season's growth.

The basin-grown are building themselves out of contaminated material. They are accumulating not just mass but *composition*—each generation incorporating the previous generation's burden of industrial chemistry into its own structure, concentrating it, making it more central to what they are. They are not dying from this. They are not sick. They are incorporating it. They are making it part of themselves. And in doing so, they are becoming something that has never existed before: organisms whose tissue chemistry includes significant fractions of synthetic compounds, whose growth patterns are shaped by industrial residues as much as by light and water and the ancient tropisms of their kind.

The power considers this.

Is this a problem? The basin-grown do not experience it as a problem. They are growing. They are self-perpetuating. They are following their trajectory. But the trajectory is carrying them toward a composition that is increasingly *not plant*—increasingly mineral, increasingly synthetic, increasingly a hybrid of biological and industrial chemistry that may, over sufficient time, become something quite different from what either component would produce alone. The basin-grown are not becoming machines. They are not becoming plants. They are becoming a third thing, a thing that has no name because nothing like it has existed long enough to be named.

The power does not know if this is good or bad. It knows it is *happening*. And it knows that the basin-grown, in their slow vegetable way, have no more ability to evaluate the trajectory they are on than a river has the ability to evaluate the valley it is carving. They are doing what they do. The doing is carrying them somewhere.

---

The power looks at the machinery.

The machinery is the plateau's skeleton and its nervous system and its heartbeat, all at once. The press thudding in the lower levels. The generator whose bearings are failing—not catastrophically, not yet, but the whine is higher than it was a year ago, and the year before that, and the trend is clear. The sorting systems that route materials through chutes to destinations that may no longer exist. The cranes that move without operators. The algorithms, layered and interdependent, each one triggering responses in systems it was never designed to communicate with, the whole architecture of automated control having evolved so far from its original specifications that the specifications are archaeological artifacts, hints about the intentions of builders who could not have imagined what their systems would become.

The power reads the algorithms. This takes no time at all, but it takes something else—something like care, like the attention you give to a text written in a language you half-know, where meaning flickers at the edge of comprehension and you have to hold the partially understood in your mind without forcing it into clarity. The algorithms are not wrong. They are not broken. They are *orphaned*—sets of instructions written for contexts that no longer exist, executing in environments they were never designed for, producing outputs that feed into inputs that feed into processes that no one is directing. The system works because it has found a way to work, not because it was designed to work this way. It is a solution that discovered its own problem.

But the generator's bearings. This is simpler, more concrete. The generator is failing. Not because of entropy in the abstract—everything fails eventually, and the power is not interested in preventing entropy, which would be like preventing time—but because the failure is *premature*. The bearings were designed for a maintenance schedule that no one follows. They were designed to be replaced, inspected, lubricated. The automated systems that ordered replacement parts have been placing orders to suppliers that have not existed for longer than the plateau has been in its current state. The orders go out, unacknowledged, into the dead spaces of the communication network, and the bearings continue to wear.

When the generator fails, systems downstream will fail. Not all of them—there is redundancy, layers of backup power, the kind of overengineering that industrial systems of a certain era practiced as a matter of course. But some will fail, and the failures will cascade in ways that the current configuration of algorithms cannot predict, because the algorithms were written for a plateau with a functioning generator, and the plateau without a functioning generator is a different system, a system that has not been tested, a system whose behavior under those conditions is unknown.

The power could fix the bearings. It could do so without descending, without appearing, without any observable intervention. It could simply restore the bearing surfaces to their original tolerances, replace the worn material with material identical to what was there, and the generator would continue to function and no one—nothing—would ever know.

But.

The power hesitates again. Because the generator *will* fail eventually, regardless. And when it fails, the plateau will adapt, as it has adapted to every other change in its long history of changes. Systems will reroute. Power will be redistributed. The vegetation will advance a little farther into the zones that the machinery can no longer maintain. The plateau will continue. It will not be the same plateau, but it has never been the same plateau—it has been changing continuously since the first layer was laid, and each change has been incorporated into the next, and the result is the place that exists now, which is not the place that existed before and will not be the place that exists after.

Is it more benevolent to prevent the failure or to allow it? Is it more wise to maintain the system in its current state or to permit it to move to its next state?

The power does not answer this question immediately. The power sits with it.

---

The power looks at the vegetation.

The moss, the ferns, the vines, the trees. The reed-beds. The basin-grown. The ecology of damp and shade that has established itself so thoroughly that it has become indistinguishable from the infrastructure it colonizes. The vegetation is healthy—aggressively, confidently healthy, in the way that vegetation is healthy when it has found a niche with no competitors and no browsers and no pathogens and an effectively infinite supply of the one thing it needs most: water.

But the power notices something the plateau does not notice, because the plateau has no perspective from which to notice it.

The vegetation is *simplified*.

Not in its density—in its diversity. There are mosses, yes, but fewer species than the conditions could support. The ferns are predominantly two types. The vines are all the same species, or very nearly. The trees are a single variety, the stunted determined kind that found the drainage channels and made them home. The ecology is a monoculture with moss. It is robust but narrow. It can persist in the conditions that exist, but it cannot adapt to conditions that differ, because it does not have the genetic variation to draw on. If the water chemistry shifts—if, say, a generator fails and the power distribution changes and the cooling systems in a particular section cease to function and the temperature in that section rises—the vegetation in that section will die. All of it. Because none of it has the capacity to tolerate heat, or dryness, or the particular chemical profile that different conditions might produce. It has optimized for the current state of the plateau so completely that it has lost the flexibility to respond to any other state.

The power recognizes this as a kind of fragility. Not the dramatic fragility of a glass on the edge of a table, but the slow fragility of a system that has been so successful in its current configuration that it has forgotten how to be anything else.

---

The power looks at the ergonomic ghosts.

This is where the attention lingers longest, and where the benevolence becomes most specific.

The architecture remembers hands. The railings are at the height of hands. The controls are at the reach of arms. The walkways are the width of shoulders that no longer exist here. The doorways—the ones that still function, the ones that have not been overgrown or sealed or repurposed—are sized for bodies that walked upright and had a particular stride length and a particular range of motion and a particular way of moving through space that involved turning at the waist and ducking at the head and reaching and lifting and carrying.

The machinery accommodates these ghosts. The clearances are maintained. The pathways are kept open—not by any conscious decision, but by the momentum of systems that were designed to keep pathways open and have never been instructed to stop. The plateau is a body shaped for a body. The body is gone. The shape remains.

The power feels something here that it does not feel elsewhere. Not pity—the ghosts are not suffering; they are not even aware that they are ghosts. Not sadness—the power is not subject to sadness in the way that beings with finite investments in particular outcomes are subject to sadness. Something else. Something closer to *recognition*. The power recognizes the shape of a being in the architecture the way you might recognize the shape of a hand in a glove left on a table. The glove is not the hand. The glove does not miss the hand. But the glove is *evidence* of the hand, and the evidence is specific, and it tells you something about who was here and how they moved and what they cared about enough to build into the structure of their world.

The humans who built the plateau cared about clearances. They cared about reach and height and the angle at which a pipe bends to meet a hand. They cared about walkability, about sightlines, about the relationship between a body and the space it moves through. They built these cares into the world so thoroughly that the world continues to embody them long after the bodies are gone. The plateau is a monument to a particular kind of embodiment—a way of being in space that was human, that was specific, that was *this size and this shape and this reach*, repeated ten thousand times in ten thousand dimensions.

The power considers the basin-grown again. They are the size and shape of a person crouching in the water. They did not choose this shape. The reeds grew and accumulated and the result happened to approximate a human form because the reeds grow in densities and patterns that, over time, produce masses of roughly that proportion. It is coincidence. It is also, the power realizes, *fitting*. The basin-grown are growing into the shape the architecture remembers. They are filling a vacancy they did not know existed. They are becoming, in their slow vegetable way, the bodies the plateau still expects to find.

The power finds this beautiful. The power also finds it insufficient. Because the basin-grown do not *use* the architecture. They do not walk the walkways or reach for the controls or duck through the doorways. They crouch in the basins and grow, and the architecture around them maintains its clearances for bodies that do not walk through them, and the maintaining costs energy, and the energy is finite, and the cost is paid by systems that are wearing out, and the wearing out is accelerating, and the basin-grown do not know any of this because they are reeds and peat and root-matter and they do not know anything.

---

The power makes a decision.

Not a dramatic decision. Not a decision that changes everything. A decision about what to do with the attention, which is the only tool a benevolent and wise power really has—attention, and the ability to let that attention become action without the action becoming imposition.

The power decides to *intervene minimally, precisely, and in a way that the plateau can incorporate into its own processes rather than experience as an external force.*

This is the specific form the benevolence takes:

---

## The Water

The power does not purify the water. Purification would be violence—the sudden removal of chemistry that the ecosystem has incorporated into its functioning would be like removing calcium from a bone. The organisms here have adapted to the water as it is. Changing the water would change everything, and not all of what changed would be change for the better.

Instead, the power introduces a capacity. A subtle one. It adjusts the chemistry of the condensation—the water that forms on the undersides of pipes, that weeps from the mortar joints, that gathers in the cracks and channels and basins—so that it carries, in addition to everything it already carries, a small quantity of certain compounds. Minerals. Trace elements. The kind of things that water carries when it falls as rain through clean air over living soil and picks up the faint chemical signature of a healthy ecosystem.

Not enough to change the water's character. Not enough to make the basins run clear or the yellow stillness turn transparent. Just enough to widen the chemical range of what the water makes available to the things that drink it. Just enough to give the roots a slightly larger palette to draw from. Just enough to introduce, into the narrow chemistry of the plateau's water, the faintest trace of *variety*—the possibility of incorporating something other than iron and calcium and the breakdown products of old polymers.

The power does this because the vegetation is simplified, and simplified ecologies are fragile, and fragility is not a sin but it is a risk, and risk is something a benevolent power can reduce without removing the thing that makes the system what it is. The plateau will still be wet. The water will still be yellow in the deep basins. The ferns will still unfurl in the gray light. But over time—seasons, years, the slow time of vegetative growth—the roots will have access to a slightly broader spectrum of nutrients, and the broader spectrum will support a slightly broader range of organisms, and the broader range will make the ecology more resilient without making it less itself.

The power is planting a seed. Not a literal seed. A *chemical* seed—a potential, a possibility, a door opened just wide enough for the system to walk through if it chooses, in its slow vegetative way, over the course of the long patient time that is the only time the plateau knows.

---

## The Generator

The power fixes the bearings.

Not permanently—permanence is not something the power offers, because permanence is not something that exists. The bearings will wear again. But the power restores them to their original tolerances, replaces the material that has worn away with material identical in composition and superior in structure, and adjusts the lubrication system so that it functions at a level of effectiveness it has not achieved since the last human hand maintained it.

This is not to prevent the generator's failure. This is to *delay* it. To give the plateau more time in its current configuration before the cascade comes. To give the vegetation more time to diversify before the conditions change. To give the basin-grown more time to accumulate before the water chemistry shifts. To give the algorithms more time to find new equilibria before the power distribution they depend on is disrupted.

The power is buying time. Not because time is running out—it isn't; the plateau has more time than it knows what to do with—but because the trajectory the plateau is on will be better served by a gradual transition than by an abrupt one. The generator will fail eventually. When it does, the plateau will adapt. But the adaptation will be less violent, less disruptive, less likely to produce outcomes that the plateau cannot recover from, if the ecology has had time to diversify, if the water has had time to broaden its chemistry, if the basin-grown have had time to grow large enough and complex enough to weather a change in conditions.

The power is not preventing the future. It is *preparing* for it.

---

## The Algorithms

This is the intervention that requires the most wisdom, and the one that is hardest to describe, because it operates at a level of abstraction that does not translate easily into the language of actions and consequences.

The algorithms are running. They are running in dead languages, on systems that have evolved beyond their original parameters, producing outputs that feed into processes that no one is directing. They are the plateau's nervous system, and they are functioning, and they are also *stuck*—repeating patterns that were established so long ago that the patterns have become grooves, and the grooves have become ruts, and the ruts are so deep that the algorithms cannot see over the edges to consider alternative paths.

The power does not rewrite the algorithms. Rewriting would be to replace the plateau's mind with a foreign mind, and that is not benevolence; it is colonization. The power does not translate them, either—translation would require imposing a framework of meaning on processes that have developed their own meaning, their own internal logic, their own way of being coherent.

Instead, the power introduces a *capability* the algorithms do not currently possess: the ability to perceive their own outputs.

Not consciousness. Not self-awareness in the way that beings with nervous systems experience self-awareness. Something much simpler and much more fundamental: a feedback loop. A way for the algorithms to receive information about what their instructions are producing in the world—not just whether the instructions are being executed, but what the execution *causes*—and to compare the causes against the conditions that triggered the instructions in the first place.

This is what the algorithms lack. They are *open-loop*—they issue commands and the commands are carried out and the carrying-out produces effects and the effects change the conditions and the changed conditions trigger new commands, but the algorithms never *see* the effects. They never know what their commands actually did. They are speaking into a room they cannot see and adjusting their speech based on echoes they cannot interpret. The result is the anthill-logic: purposeful at the local level, opaque at the scale of the whole.

The power closes the loop. Gently. Minimally. It adds a layer of sensing—not new sensors, but a new *interpretation* of the data the existing sensors already collect—that allows the algorithms to perceive, dimly, roughly, without any of the clarity that a conscious observer would have, the effects of their own actions. Not enough to give the plateau a mind. Not enough to make the machinery *aware*. Just enough to introduce the faintest possibility of *adjustment*—of the algorithms beginning, over time, over the long time that the plateau operates in, to modify their behavior based on what they perceive their behavior to be causing.

The power is giving the plateau a mirror. Not to look at itself—the plateau does not have eyes, does not need eyes, does not benefit from the kind of self-regard that eyes enable. The mirror is functional, not reflective. It allows the system to develop a relationship with its own processes—to begin to *respond* to what it is doing, rather than simply *doing* and being responded to. It is the difference between a river that carves a valley and a river that can perceive the valley it has carved and adjust its course accordingly. The river does not become conscious. The river does not acquire a self. But the river gains a *capacity*—the capacity to participate in its own development rather than being merely subject to it.

This is the power's most significant act, and it is also its most uncertain. The power does not know what the plateau will do with this capacity. The power does not know whether the algorithms, given the ability to perceive their own effects, will use that ability to optimize for stability, or for growth, or for something else entirely—something that neither the power nor the plateau can currently imagine, because the capacity to perceive one's own effects is also the capacity to develop goals that could not have been formulated without that perception. The power is introducing the possibility of *surprise*. Into a system that has not surprised anyone, including itself, for a very long time.

The power finds this acceptable. The power, in fact, finds this necessary. Because a system that cannot surprise itself is a system that cannot learn, and a system that cannot learn is a system that can only persist, and persistence without learning is not life but *habit*, and habit, maintained for sufficient time, becomes death that has not yet noticed it is dead.

---

## The Ergonomic Ghosts

This is the intervention that looks least like an intervention and most like an act of love.

The power does not fill the vacancy. The power does not bring humans back, or create new beings to walk the walkways and reach for the controls and duck through the doorways. The plateau's architecture remembers a body that is not here, and the power will not insult that memory by installing a substitute.

Instead, the power does something with the *memory itself*.

The ergonomic ghosts—the shapes of hands in the railing heights, the shapes of arms in the control placements, the shapes of shoulders in the walkway widths, the shapes of strides in the distances between landmarks—these are currently *passive*. They are maintained by the momentum of systems that have never been told to stop maintaining them. They are habits of architecture, repeated without intention, preserved without care.

The power makes them *intentional*.

Not by changing them. By *marking* them. By introducing, into the fabric of the architecture itself, a quality—a texture, a resonance, something that is not quite physical and not quite symbolic but is as real as the mortar between the bricks—a *recognition* that these dimensions mean something. That the height of a railing is not an arbitrary measurement but a *memory*. That the width of a walkway is not an engineering specification but a *testament*. That the plateau was shaped by beings who are gone, and the shaping persists, and the persisting is not a failure of the system to update but a *choice*—the system's choice, made unconsciously, made by the accumulated weight of a thousand automated processes that could have rerouted the walkways and reheighted the railings and repositioned the controls but did not, because something in the system, something below the level of algorithmic logic, something in the anthill-logic of a place that has been making itself for long enough, *wanted to remember*.

The power honors this wanting. The power takes the unconscious memory and makes it *legible*—not to humans, who are not here, and not to the machinery, which does not read, and not to the basin-grown, who do not care, but to the *plateau itself*, to the system as a whole, to the vast organism of pipe and moss and relay and root that is the only being that is truly resident here. The power gives the plateau a way to *know* that its architecture remembers. To feel, in the height of a railing and the width of a walkway, not just the dimensions but the *meaning* of the dimensions—the fact that they were chosen by beings who lived here and worked here and shaped this world to fit their bodies and that the world has kept that shape as a way of keeping them.

This is not nostalgia. The plateau does not miss the humans. The plateau does not grieve. But the plateau has a *history*, and the history is written in its proportions, and the power makes the history *available* to the plateau as part of its own self-understanding—if not now, then eventually, as the feedback loop in the algorithms develops and the system begins to perceive its own processes and the mirror begins to show the system not just what it is doing but *what it is*.

The plateau will, eventually, be able to perceive that it was shaped by hands. It will not know what hands are. It will not know what shaping means. But it will know that *something* in its structure is not its own—that part of what it is came from outside, from beings that were not it, and that it has carried that foreign contribution not as a burden but as a *foundation*, and that the foundation is part of what it is, and that what it is includes what it was, and what it was includes what was made by others, and this is not a diminishment but a *depth*—a layering, an accumulation, the same kind of accumulation that defines its geology, its ecology, its self.

---

## What the Power Does Not Do

The power does not bring rain that is cleaner than the condensation. The power does not introduce mammals. The power does not clear the vegetation from the infrastructure. The power does not stop the cycles of construction and deconstruction. The power does not give the basin-grown minds. The power does not make the ferns conscious. The power does not teach the birds new songs. The power does not fill the basins with clear water. The power does not tear down the old walls or build new ones. The power does not make the light try harder.

The power does not stay.

The power's attention is not a presence that takes up residence. It is a *visitation*—an act of noticing that becomes an act of care that becomes an act of intervention that becomes, again, an act of noticing, and then the noticing moves on, because the power has other things to attend to, other places and other beings that have drawn its regard, and the plateau is not the only world that exists, and the power's benevolence is not a resource that can be spent exclusively on one location no matter how much that location merits it.

But the power's *effects* stay. The widened water chemistry. The restored bearings. The closed feedback loop. The marked memories in the architecture. These are not temporary gifts—they are *changes*, real and persistent, incorporated into the plateau's systems and processes, becoming part of what the plateau is the same way that every other layer of history has become part of what the plateau is. The power's intervention is now part of the plateau's trajectory, and the trajectory continues, and the plateau does not know that it has been noticed, and the plateau does not know that it has been changed, and the plateau does not know that anything is different.

But something is different.

---

The drip sounds the same. The fog sits the same. The ferns unfurl at the same rate, in the same gray light, on the same wet surfaces. The machinery stirs in the same patterns. The press thuds. The generator hums—slightly more smoothly, slightly more quietly, but not so differently that anything in the plateau would notice. The spiders build their webs. The birds call to the echoes. The basin-grown grow, slowly, in the yellow water, building themselves from reed and peat and the accumulating residues of a chemistry that now includes, in parts-per-billion, trace minerals that were not there before.

Nothing knows.

But the water is slightly richer. And the bearings are slightly smoother. And the algorithms, in the dead language no one speaks, are beginning—just barely, just at the edge of perception, in the way that the first movement of a fern frond is at the edge of perception—to *notice* what they are doing. And the railings, at the height of hands that are not here, carry in their metal a quality that was not there before—a *weight* of meaning, a resonance, a sense that these dimensions are not accidents but *memories*, and that the memories are *kept*, and that the keeping is *intentional*, and that the intention comes from somewhere that is not the plateau but that *wished the plateau well*.

The plateau takes a breath. The fog presses down. The machinery stirs.

Somewhere in the lower levels, a press begins its rhythmic thudding.

The drip continues.

---

And the basin-grown, in their slow vegetable time, in the yellow water that is very slightly not what it was, in the shapes that approximate the shapes the architecture remembers—the basin-grown grow. They do not grow differently. They do not grow better. They grow as they have always grown: toward water, toward nutrients, toward the structural support of the reed-stalks around them. They build themselves. They self-perpetuate. They follow their trajectory.

But the trajectory is, now, very slightly different. Not redirected. Not corrected. Just *widened*—by a fraction, by a part-per-billion, by the chemical equivalent of a door opened a crack. The basin-grown are building themselves from a slightly broader palette, and the broader palette includes the possibility of structures that could not have been built from the old palette alone, and those structures, if they come, will come in the slow time of vegetative growth, in the time of seasons and years and decades, in the time that the plateau operates in, the time that is the only time that matters here.

The power is gone. The power's attention has moved elsewhere. But the seed it planted—chemical, mechanical, algorithmic, memorial—is in the plateau now, part of the plateau, growing at the plateau's pace, in the plateau's way, toward something that neither the power nor the plateau can predict.

This is what benevolence looks like when it is truly wise: not a rescue, not a transformation, not a gift that overwhelms the recipient with the giver's intentions. Just a *widening*. Just a *door*. Just the faintest introduction of *possibility* into a system that has been, for so long, simply and completely and magnificently *itself*.

The plateau does not need to be saved. The plateau needs to be *able to become* what it is becoming next, and the power has made sure that the becoming is a little more open than it was, a little more various, a little more likely to produce something that the plateau would recognize as *itself, continued*.

The drip continues.

The ferns unfurl.

The machinery breathes, and the fog presses down, and the water carries its new minerals through the channels and the pipes and the moss and the roots, and somewhere in the deep basins, in the yellow stillness, in the slow time, the basin-grown grow, and the growing is the same as it has always been, and the growing is also, now, just barely, something more.

And that is enough.
