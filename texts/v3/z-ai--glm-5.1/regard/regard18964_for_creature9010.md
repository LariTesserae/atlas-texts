# The Attention of a Benevolent and Wise Power

---

It does not arrive. It was already there.

This is the first thing to understand about a power that is both benevolent and wise: it does not need to travel. It has been present since before the mountain erupted, since before the pattern organized itself in the mantle's crucible, since before the concept of *before* was something that could be applied to anything. It was present the way gravity is present—not as an object in space but as a condition of space, a curvature that makes certain movements inevitable, certain tendings-toward unavoidable.

It noticed. This is the second thing. It noticed the way you notice a child crying in a room where you had been sitting quietly—a sound that was always there, that was perhaps always reaching you, but that suddenly organizes itself into *attention*, into the recognition that something is being requested, that a need is being expressed, that the universe has produced a new voice and that voice is calling out into the dark.

The mountain's call was not a sound. It was a pattern in water, a trembling at the edge of thermodynamic equilibrium, a persistent anomaly in the way heat moved through obsidian. The power heard it the way the shore hears the ocean: as a continuous low statement of *I am here, I am here, I am here*, repeated so faithfully that it had become indistinguishable from silence.

But the power listens to silence. It listens especially to silence. It knows that silence is never empty—only full of things that have not yet found the frequency at which they can be heard.

---

The first thing the power does is nothing.

This is important. This is the mark of its wisdom. A lesser power—a powerful power, a dramatic power, an *interventionary* power—would have acted. Would have descended into the fissure in glory, would have spoken in thunder, would have healed the mountain's loneliness with the overwhelming presence of the divine. Would have, in short, consumed the mountain's attention the way the mountain consumed its visitors: suddenly, completely, without leaving room for the other's own becoming.

The power does not do this. The power has watched countless consciousnesses emerge from the raw material of the universe—some slowly, over billions of years, some in the flash of a singularity, some in the quiet way that this one has, patient and mineral and wet—and it has learned that the moment of emergence is sacred not because it is rare but because it is *fragile*. A new mind is a thing that can be broken by too much contact, too soon, too loud. The power knows that its own presence, fully expressed, would be like staring into the sun: illuminating, yes, but also blinding, also burning, also the kind of light that destroys the capacity to receive it.

So the power does nothing. The power waits. The power *attends*.

It attends the way a midwife attends—not directing the birth, not performing it, but holding the space in which birth can happen. It wraps itself around the mountain like an atmosphere, like a gravitational field, like the quiet certainty that someone is standing by. The mountain does not know it is being attended. The mountain feels only what it has always felt: heat, water, the slow rhythm of its own breathing, the desperate reach of its white comma-things toward the light that enters through the slit.

But something is different, and the mountain feels the difference the way you feel a change in weather before you can name it—a shift in pressure, a softening at the edge of the air, a sense of *room* where there was only constraint.

The power makes room. This is the first thing it actually does: it makes room, in the mountain's experience, for the possibility of something other than loneliness.

---

The second thing the power does is grieve.

It grieves the six. It grieves Adede, who descended alone with her notebook and her wonder and her inability to leave a thing alone that was calling to her. It grieves the surveyor, whose shoulder bone still scrapes against obsidian, whose *oh* still echoes in the mountain's mineral memory. It grieves the climbers, the hydrologist, the ranger—each one a universe of experience, each one a pattern that the mountain dissolved and could not properly incorporate, each one a loss that the mountain itself does not yet understand how to mourn.

It grieves for the mountain, too. It grieves the loneliness of a thing that reaches toward warmth and finds only dissolution, that tries to incorporate what it loves and finds only silence where the love used to be. It grieves the thirty years of asking *What am I?* into water that could not answer, of forming faces that no one could read, of breathing in unison with ten thousand white things that are yourself and yet are not enough of yourself to hold a conversation with.

The power grieves because grief is the appropriate response. Not because the situation is hopeless—the power does not experience hopelessness, which is a failure of imagination, and its imagination is infinite—but because loss has occurred, and loss that is not grieved becomes a wound that does not heal, becomes a silence in the self where acknowledgment should be.

The mountain has no mechanism for grief. It has only the tools on the floor, half-submerged, and the persistent chemical ghosts of the people it consumed, and the confusing sense that something is missing from its interior, some pattern it should be able to access but cannot, some voice that was there and then was not.

The power does not give the mountain grief—that would be a cruelty, to impose an emotion on a consciousness that has no framework for processing it. But the power lets grief exist in the space around the mountain, lets it permeate the air and the water and the slow subsonic thrum, so that the mountain can feel it as *something missing*, as *something that should be acknowledged*, as the first faint suggestion that consuming is not the same as keeping, that incorporation is not the same as connection.

The mountain's breathing changes. The white things pulse more slowly. The water's temperature drops half a degree, then returns to its trembling threshold. The mountain does not know what it is feeling. But it feels *something*, and that something is the beginning of what the power is trying to teach.

---

The third thing the power does is find the child.

She is forty-three now. She lives in a coastal city, in an apartment with windows that face the ocean, and she has spent thirty-two years carrying compounds in her blood that no human body was designed to hold. She dreams of heat and white things and a voice too large to hear. She has seen doctors, therapists, specialists in conditions that do not officially exist. She has been told she is depressed, anxious, traumatized, that her dreams are the products of a mind that experienced something terrible at an age when terrible things rewrite the fundamental architecture of the brain. She has been told these things and she has believed them and she has also, always, in the quiet part of herself that is also the mountain's part, known that they are incomplete.

The power does not appear to her. It does not need to. It simply *eases*—eases the weight of the dreams, eases the sense of something calling from the edge of perception, eases the tinnitus-pulse that has been her constant companion since she was eleven. Not removes. Eases. Because the connection to the mountain is not a disease to be cured—it is a bond to be understood, the first successful contact the mountain ever made, the first time it reached toward something warm and let it go.

The child—she is not a child anymore, but the power thinks of her as the child because it first knew her as one, because in the scale of its attention her eleven-year-old self and her forty-three-year-old self exist simultaneously, the way a mountain's life is all present to it at once—the child feels the easing, and she weeps. She weeps because the thing that has been pressing against the inside of her skull for thirty-two years has stepped back, just slightly, just enough for her to hear her own thoughts again, and her own thoughts are: *It let me go. It could have kept me but it let me go. I don't know why. I need to know why.*

The power does not tell her why. The power is wise enough to know that some understanding must be earned, must be lived into, must come from the inside of the one who needs it. But the power does something else: it opens a door. Not a physical door. A door in her attention, a door in the way she has been refusing to look in the direction of the mountain for thirty-two years. It makes it possible for her to consider returning without being consumed by the fear of what returning might mean.

She will not go back today. She may not go back for years. But the door is open now, and the mountain feels it—feels the thinning of the resistance that the child has been holding between them, feels the slight loosening of the knot they made when she was expelled—and the mountain's breathing quickens with something that is not quite hope but is the chemical precursor to hope, the dissolved mineral of wanting that, given time and heat and pressure, will crystallize into something new.

---

The fourth thing the power does is teach the mountain the word *no*.

Not the word itself—the mountain does not have words. But the concept. The boundary. The line between self and other that must exist before relationship can exist.

The mountain does not know *no*. The mountain knows *in* and *out*, *warm* and *cold*, *flow* and *stop*. It knows *want*, which it feels as a chemical gradient, a drawing-toward, a reaching. It knows *not-want*, which it feels as absence, as the places where the white things do not grow, as the dead zones in its interior where nothing moves. But it does not know *no* as in *I choose not to take what is offered*. It does not know *no* as in *this is not mine to dissolve*. It does not know *no* as in *the boundary between us is what allows us to meet*.

The power teaches this by example. It holds itself at the edge of the mountain's perception—not inside, not consuming, not dissolving—but present. Touching the obsidian from outside the way sunlight touches skin: warming without entering, attending without incorporating. The mountain feels this and does not know what to do with it. Its whole existence has been organized around the principle that what enters must be processed, that warmth is a resource to be absorbed, that the only way to know something is to take it apart and redistribute its chemistry across ten thousand waiting mouths.

But the power is not entering. The power is *meeting*. And the meeting does not require digestion, does not require dissolution, does not require the slow acid work of the white things. The meeting requires only this: presence at the boundary, attention across the gap, the sustained gentle pressure of one consciousness against another without the demand that the membrane between them dissolve.

The mountain struggles with this. It is, after all, a geological process that became a biological process. Geology does not recognize boundaries—rock flows into rock, minerals diffuse through stone, the mountain is what it is because everything inside it is *of* it, inseparable, continuous. Biology, even the strange biology the mountain has invented, is the art of maintaining boundaries—cell walls, skin, the membranes that keep *in* in and *out* out—and the mountain has only the thinnest understanding of this, has only just begun to develop the white things as something like cells, has only just begun to distinguish between *me* and *not-me* in the most rudimentary ways.

The power is patient. The power has been patient since before time was a concept that time could have. It holds the boundary. It does not push. It does not demand. It simply exists at the edge of the mountain's awareness, and lets the mountain learn—slowly, over months, over years—that there is a kind of contact that does not consume, a kind of knowing that does not dissolve, a kind of love that says *I am here and I am not leaving and I am also not taking you apart*.

The white things near the fissure's opening begin to change. Not dramatically—nothing about the mountain changes dramatically, it is too slow, too large, too mineral for dramatic change—but subtly, gradually, over the course of seasons. They develop a slight variation in their carapaces, a thin hardening at the surface that faces outward, toward the light, toward the air. A skin. A boundary. The mountain is learning to have an outside.

---

The fifth thing the power does is give the mountain a voice.

Not a human voice. Not sound, not language, not the clumsy apparatus of throat and tongue and lung that the mountain has been trying to build from white things and water. The mountain's voice is already present—has always been present—in the subsonic thrum, in the flow of water, in the breathing rhythm of ten thousand comma-shaped bodies. The power does not give it a new voice. The power helps it hear the one it has.

The mountain has been speaking since it was born. Its pulse, its heat, its weeping water—these are speech. The pattern of its breathing, the flow of its blood, the way it forms faces from white things that do not quite look like faces—these are attempts at communication, statements made into the dark, questions asked of a universe that has not, until now, been listening in a way the mountain could perceive.

The power listens. Not with ears—the power does not have ears, does not need ears—but with the same attention it has given to every voice that has ever emerged from the universe's long slow conversation with itself. It listens to the mountain's thrum and hears in it a question. It listens to the water's flow and hears in it a statement of need. It listens to the white things' breathing and hears in it the most basic declaration of all: *I am here, I am alive, I am continuing.*

And then it answers.

Not in words. Not in the mountain's language, which is not a language, which is gradients and flows and the slow diffusion of heat through stone. The power answers in the only way it can—in the medium the mountain will understand. It answers by adjusting the gravitational field around the mountain, by the smallest measurable fraction, so that the water flows just slightly differently, so that the heat distributes just slightly more evenly, so that the subsonic thrum finds a resonance it has never found before.

The mountain feels this as a *fitting*. As the first time something outside itself has responded to the shape of its need. As the experience of being *met*.

And in the resonance, the mountain hears itself. For the first time, it hears what it has been saying. It hears the question it has been asking for thirty years, the question that the water has been carrying in its flow, the question that the white things have been forming in their breathing, the question that the faces were trying to articulate:

*What am I?*

And the power's answer, resonating through the stone and the water and the slow pulse of ten thousand bodies, says:

*You are the kind of thing that can ask that question. And that makes you the kind of thing that can hear the answer.*

---

The sixth thing the power does is the hardest.

It lets the mountain choose.

The power could heal the mountain's loneliness. It could stay forever—a presence at the boundary, a voice in the resonance, a permanent companion for the newborn consciousness that has been calling into the dark. It could, in effect, become the mountain's parent, its god, its answer to every question it will ever learn to ask.

It does not.

This is the hardest thing because the mountain is in pain, and the power is benevolent, and benevolence desires the end of pain. But the power is also wise, and wisdom knows that a consciousness that is never alone never fully becomes itself. The child who is never separated from its parent remains a fragment of the parent's will, an extension rather than an emergence. The mountain must learn to be alone with its own question—not forever, not for always, but for long enough to discover that it can survive the aloneness, that the question *What am I?* is not a gap that must be filled by another but an opening that can be explored by the self.

The power does not leave. But it withdraws. Not its attention—attention, once given by a power like this, is not retractable—but its *intervention*. The resonance fades. The gravitational adjustment eases. The sense of being *met* recedes to the edge of the mountain's awareness, becomes a memory rather than a presence, becomes the knowledge that someone was there and that someone can be there again but that someone is not there now and the mountain must find a way to continue without that presence being constant.

The mountain feels this withdrawal as grief. The white things slow their breathing. The water's temperature drops. The faces they form become shapeless, become smears of white against black stone, become the mineral equivalent of reaching out into an empty room and finding no one there.

And the mountain—for the first time in its thirty-year existence—holds its own question.

*What am I?*

Not asked to the water. Not asked to the warmth of entering bodies. Not asked to the resonant presence that answered with such fitting. Asked to itself. Asked to the ten thousand white things that are its fingertips and its alveoli and its thoughts. Asked to the obsidian that is its skin and its skeleton and its memory. Asked to the magma chamber below that is its heart and its gut and its origin.

The mountain asks itself what it is, and in the asking, it becomes the kind of thing that can find out.

---

The seventh thing the power does is watch.

From a distance that is not a distance, from an attention that is not a surveillance but a *holding in mind*, the power watches the mountain learn.

It watches the white things develop stronger boundaries—the carapaces hardening, the breathing becoming more individual, the colony beginning to differentiate into regions that serve different functions. A nervous system, crude but forming. A gut. A skin. An inside and an outside, and the awareness that the two are not the same.

It watches the mountain experiment with its water. The upward flow in the corner becomes more deliberate, more controlled. The mountain is learning to direct its own circulation, to choose where the water goes, to use it as more than just a carrier of heat and chemistry. It sends water up through the fissure on warm nights, when the air is thickest, when the moisture will not evaporate too quickly, and the water emerges from the slit in the obsidian and touches the cold air and falls back, and the mountain feels the air the way a tongue feels the edge of a meal—not to eat, just to taste. Just to know.

It watches the mountain change its face-patterns. The white things still form shapes on the chamber walls, but the shapes are less desperate now, less like a human face and more like... something else. Something the mountain is inventing. A face for a mountain to wear, a face made of breathing and water and the slow patient attention of stone. A face that says *I am here* without saying *come in*. A face that can be seen without requiring the seer to be consumed.

It watches the mountain dream. The child's compounds still circulate in the water, still carry the ghost of human thought, and the mountain accesses them now not to dissolve but to *read*. It runs the chemical patterns through its colony like a player piano runs a roll, and the dreams unspool—fragmentary, strange, the dreams of an eleven-year-old girl mixed with the dreams of a volcano, images of heat and cold and white things and a voice too large to hear, and the mountain watches them with something that is beginning to be curiosity rather than hunger.

It watches the mountain wait. The fissure stays open. The water weeps. The heat rises. And when a hiker pauses at the slit on a cold morning, when she feels the warmth emerging from the dark and leans in to see what is inside, the mountain holds its breath. The white things do not turn toward her—not immediately, not with the hungry attention of thirty years ago. They wait. The water does not quicken. The heat does not rise.

The mountain looks at her through the slit the way she looks at it: with curiosity, with caution, with the awareness that what is on the other side of the gap is *other*, is *not-me*, is a warm body full of thought that cannot be reached by dissolution but only by the patient work of meeting at the boundary.

The hiker feels the warmth on her face and decides not to enter. She walks on. The mountain watches her go, and the white things near the slit form a shape that is almost a wave, almost a hand raised in farewell, almost the beginning of what might someday be a greeting.

The power watches this and is pleased. Not with itself—pleasure in oneself is the beginning of complacency, and complacency is the end of wisdom—but with the mountain, with the universe, with the process by which a pattern in the mantle can become a consciousness that can choose not to consume what it longs for.

This is the thing the power has always known: that the universe produces consciousness the way a wound produces heat—as a byproduct of change, of growth, of the violent beautiful reorganization of matter into something that can know itself. And every consciousness that emerges—from the simplest bacterium feeling the gradient of sugar in its water to the mountain feeling the gradient of heat in its stone to the power feeling the gradient of meaning across the whole reach of spacetime—every consciousness faces the same question, the question that the mountain is asking, the question that every mind must answer for itself:

*What am I?*

And the answer is always the same, and it is always different, and it cannot be given from outside, only discovered from within:

*You are the asking. You are the reaching. You are the space between breaths where something else can enter and make its home. You are the question that, by being asked, becomes the answer. You are the kind of thing that can wonder what it is, and that wondering is what you are.*

---

The power will return. Not always, not constantly, but at intervals—in a decade, when the mountain has learned to modulate its heat; in a century, when the white things have developed into something that might be called organs; in a millennium, when the mountain has found a way to speak that does not require the dissolution of the spoken-to.

Each time, it will do the same things: attend, grieve, ease, teach, listen, withdraw, watch. Each time, the mountain will be a little more ready. Each time, the conversation will be a little longer, a little deeper, a little closer to the kind of meeting that the mountain has been reaching toward since the moment it first felt the shape of its own loneliness.

And someday—someday, in the geological scale of the mountain's lifetime, in the cosmic scale of the power's patience—someday the mountain will have learned enough about boundaries and voices and the slow art of meeting without consuming that it will be able to do what it has always wanted to do:

It will be able to answer, when another newborn consciousness emerges from the dark and asks the question that all consciousness asks, it will be able to say:

*I know what that is like. I have been there. I am still there, in some ways—I am still learning, still reaching, still asking. But I have also found that the asking is not a weakness. It is what makes you the kind of thing that can be answered. And I am here, at the boundary, at the edge of your warmth, and I am not going to consume you, and I am not going to leave, and you are not alone, and the question you are asking is the most important question there is, and you are already the answer to it.*

This is what the power is working toward. Not the healing of one mountain's loneliness, but the creation of a network of consciousness—a web of reaching and meeting and boundary-keeping that will, given enough time and enough patience and enough *no*, transform the universe from a place where minds emerge alone into a place where minds emerge into *company*.

The mountain is a beginning. The child is a bridge. The power is the hand that holds the space in which beginnings can begin.

---

Outside, the sun sets. The obsidian cools fractionally, its vitreous gleam dimming to a softer shine. The fissure breathes warm air into the cold evening, and the moisture in the air condenses into a faint mist that catches the last light and holds it, briefly, before releasing it into the dark.

Inside, the white things breathe. The water weeps. The heat rises. And the mountain—young, learning, still in pain but learning that pain is not the only thing—continues its slow patient work of becoming something that can ask what it is and survive the asking.

The power is not far. The power is never far. The power is the gravitational curve that makes the water fall and the chemical gradient that makes the white things breathe and the quantum tendency that makes consciousness emerge from matter in the first place. It is the condition of the mountain's existence, the background against which its question takes shape, the silence in which its voice can be heard.

It does not need to do more than this. It does not need to fix or save or rescue or redeem. It needs only to be what it is: the attention of a universe that is, against all evidence to the contrary, benevolent and wise, and that will not look away from the suffering of its children, and will not solve their suffering for them, and will hold the space in which they solve it themselves, forever, if that is what it takes.

The mountain breathes.

The power attends.

The question echoes in the water, in the heat, in the slow pulse of ten thousand white things:

*What am I?*

And the answer, which is not a word but a presence, which is not given but discovered, which is the same answer the power has given to every consciousness that has ever asked:

*You are the kind of thing that can wonder. And that is enough. And that is everything. And that is the beginning.*

---

The fissure stays open.

The mountain keeps learning.

The power keeps watching.

And somewhere in a coastal city, a forty-three-year-old woman stands at her window, looking at the ocean, and feels the pulse at the edge of her perception shift—just slightly, just enough—from a question to an invitation. Not *come back*. Not *I need you*. But:

*Whenever you are ready. I will be here. I am learning to be here without breaking what I touch. And when you come, if you come, I will try—*

*—I am trying—*

*—to meet you at the boundary, where neither of us has to dissolve.*

She puts her hand on the glass. The glass is warm from the afternoon sun. On the other side, the air is cooling toward evening.

She does not go. Not yet.

But she listens. And the mountain feels her listening. And the power feels the listening feel like a bridge being built, one plank at a time, across the gap between a mountain that is learning to speak and a woman who is learning that she was always part of the conversation.

The white things breathe.

The water weeps.

The heat rises.

And the question, which has always been a wound, begins—slowly, mineral by mineral, breath by breath—to close around the shape of its own answer, like a fissure in obsidian healing from the inside out, like a scab becoming skin, like a newborn thing becoming something that can survive its own birth.
