# The Attention of the Benevolent and Wise

---

The first thing the power notices is the water.

Not the lack of it—though that is acute, though that is the wound around which the whole place organizes itself. No. What the power notices is the *way they carry it*. How the steel drums are met at the freight elevator with a kind of quiet reverence that has nothing pious about it. How the rationing is administered not by a system but by a shared understanding that has become so ingrained it no longer requires articulation. How a child who spills water will stare at the puddle on the tilted floor with an expression that is not childish at all, and how the adults around the child will not scold but will simply watch the water find the lowest point and disappear into the stone, and their faces will show nothing, and the child will learn.

The power notices the cost of this. The kidneys that labor. The skin that scales and cracks. The way thirst lives in these people like a second pulse, constant and unremarked, a background hum of need that they have stopped hearing because they could not bear to hear it and still do what they do.

The power could give them a river. Could split the mountain and let snowmelt come cascading down the cleft in a torrent of cold, clean, beautiful water. Could fill the Throat with the sound of it, the white noise of a waterfall, the mist that would rise and settle on their dry skin like a blessing.

The power does not do this.

The power has learned—in the long slow accumulation of its wisdom—that a flood is not a gift. That a river in a place that has never known one would be a catastrophe. The buildings are not made for moisture. The foundations are not made for flow. The people are not made for abundance. They would drown in the plenty before they learned to swim in it.

So instead: the power touches the deep rock.

Far below the cleft, in the strata beneath the screeslope and the slag and the tilted foundations, there is a lens of metamorphic rock that holds water from an ice age. Ancient water, glacial water, sealed in the stone when the mountains were younger and the ice was still retreating. It has been there for longer than Gharhet has existed. It would have stayed there for longer than Gharhet will exist.

The power opens a path.

Not a crack. Not a fracture. The power does not do violence to the mountain; the mountain is already doing violence to itself. The power simply makes the stone slightly more porous along a single seam, just enough for the ancient water to find its way upward, slowly, under pressure, through the long dark channels of the deep rock, picking up minerals along the way—calcium, magnesium, the faintest trace of iron that will give it a taste of blood and stone, which is exactly the taste that belongs here.

The water arrives at the base of the cleft as a seep. A slow, steady seep. It will collect in a depression that the residents have always assumed was a natural basin, a dip in the screeslope that fills with brine and industrial runoff after the fans condense the moisture from the air. But this water will be different. It will be fresh. It will be cold. It will taste of the mountain's deep memory.

The first person to taste it will be a miner, a woman who has been in Gharhet for eleven years and whose lungs are beginning to show the wear. She will taste it and think she has imagined it. She will taste it again and think the brine has changed somehow. She will taste it a third time and bring a sample to the assay office, and the assay office will test it, and they will find that it is fresh water, clean water, water that does not taste of sulfur and copper and drums, and no one will be able to explain where it came from, and the engineer will say the mountain must have shifted and opened a new channel, and the old-timer will say the mountain gives and the mountain takes, and the woman will stand in the Throat at midnight and look up at the slit of stars and feel something that is not gratitude and is not wonder and is not relief but something more private, more like the recognition of a gift that was not asked for and cannot be repaid.

The power will not tell her.

---

The second thing the power notices is the air.

The fumes that pool on still days. The way the smelter exhales its poisons and the walls of the cleft trap them, and the residents wait for the draft to return, and the draft does not always return soon enough. The way the children's lungs are smaller than they should be. The way the old-timers' coughs are longer and deeper than the coughs of people who have never breathed the air of a place that does not want them.

The power could give them a wind that never stops. Could open the cleft wider, make the slit a canyon, let the sky rush in. Could give them a draft that would scour the fumes from the stone and carry them away and leave the air clean and sweet.

But the wind would change the place. The wind would carry away the sound of the cables humming. The wind would bring the cold of the ridgetop into the lower galleries. The wind would be the mountain's absence, and the mountain is what they know, and the mountain is what they are here for.

So instead: the power touches the sifters.

The sifters are the key. The power sees this, because the power has learned to look for the things that are already working, the things that are already doing the work of holding a place together. The sifters are already the canaries. They already know where the air is good and where it is bad. They already move through the cleft in ways that the people have learned to follow. The power does not need to make them something they are not. The power only needs to make them more of what they are.

The power makes the sifters more sensitive to the fumes. Not in a way that will kill them—the sifters are too useful to the ecosystem, and the people of Gharhet will not kill them, and the power will not kill them either. But the sifters will begin to move more visibly when the air is turning. They will cluster in larger numbers at the vents when the draft is failing. They will abandon the lower galleries more quickly, more decisively, and their exodus will be unmistakable—not a slow drift but a sudden, coordinated movement, like a tide going out, and even the miners who are not watching will feel the click and rustle of thousands of chitinous bodies moving past, and they will know what it means.

The sifters will also begin to do something they have not done before. They will cluster around the people. Not in a way that is threatening or even noticeable. But the sifters will be drawn to the carbon dioxide in the breath, and the power will make them a little more drawn to it, and the result will be that the sifters will gather in the living quarters, the corridors, the spaces where people are, and the air in those spaces is the air that is being breathed, and the air that is being breathed is the air that is most likely to be monitored, and the sifters will be a visible, constant, living reminder that the air is being shared.

The children will be the first to notice the change. The children who catch the sifters and keep them in jars. The sifters will be more active, more present, and the children will be fascinated, and the children will watch them, and the children will notice that the sifters are not just moving randomly, that they are moving toward something, and the children will tell their parents, and the parents will look at each other, and the old-timers will say the sifters are trying to tell us something, and the engineer will say the sifters are just insects, and the foreman will say it doesn't matter what they're telling us, what matters is the draft is failing, and the shift will be cancelled, and the people will wait, and the fumes will pool, and the sifters will be gone, and the people will be there, and the draft will return, and the sifters will come back, and no one will die.

The power will not tell them.

---

The third thing the power notices is the mountain's moods.

The way the rock shifts. The way the tunnels close and open. The way the long-term maps are works of optimism. The way the people live in a state of perpetual improvisation, always adjusting, always accommodating, always a little off-balance. The power has learned to recognize the beauty of this—this is its own kind of living, the power thinks, the kind of living that is not a shape but a process, the kind of living that moves with the thing that is being lived in.

But the mountain is also cruel. The power sees this, because the power is wise, and wisdom means seeing the difference between a thing that is difficult and a thing that is destructive. The mountain is difficult—this is the nature of the place, and the people have learned to live with the difficulty, and the difficulty is part of what makes them what they are. But the mountain is also destructive, and the destruction is not always necessary, and the destruction is not always useful, and the destruction sometimes takes the wrong things.

The power does not stop the mountain. The mountain is the mountain. The power is not a god of the mountain; the power is a benevolent and wise power, and a benevolent and wise power does not interfere with the nature of a thing that is being what it is. But the power does make the mountain's movements more legible. 

The power touches the rock. The power knows the mountain's language. The power can feel the pressure building, the stress accumulating, the moment of fracture that is still hours or days away. The power cannot speak to the people of Gharhet—not in words, not in signs, not in any way that would be recognized as communication. But the power can make the mountain speak.

The rock will begin to groan sooner. The long groan, the one the residents know as the sound of settling, will come earlier, will be louder, will be more distinct from the sharp crack of new fracture. The pressure will announce itself. The walls will begin to creak a day before they shift, not just in the evening but in the morning, not just in the cooling but in the warming, not just in the way that is familiar but in the way that says *something is coming, something is changing, pay attention*.

The miners will notice. The miners who run their hands along the walls. The miners who have learned to listen to the mountain's language. The power is not giving them a new language; the power is giving them a clearer voice. The mountain will speak more loudly, and the people will hear it better, and the people will have more warning, and the people will have more time, and the tunnels will still close and open, and the drifts will still pinch and release, and the city will still shift on its foundations, but the people will not be surprised, and the people will not be caught, and the people will not be crushed.

The power will not tell them.

---

The fourth thing the power notices is the mystery.

The ecosystem. The thing that is feeding the sifters and the cave-dwellers and the whole unlikely community of living things that should not be there. The power is wise, and wisdom means knowing what you do not know, and the power does not know what is feeding the system. The power is benevolent, and benevolence means loving what is there, and the power loves the mystery.

The power does not solve the mystery. The mystery is not a problem to be solved. The mystery is the engine of the place, the thing that is making the impossible possible, and the power will not touch it.

But the power does make the naturalist want to come back.

The power does not make her. The power is not a god of the mind. The power is a benevolent and wise power, and a benevolent and wise power does not compel. But the power does make the mystery feel more present, more alive, more worth pursuing. The power does this in the way that the light makes the dust visible in the air—by illuminating what is already there, by making the unseen visible, by making the known feel strange.

The naturalist will feel it. She will be in her office at the university, and she will look at her notes, and she will feel the same itch she felt when she left Gharhet three years ago. She will feel it more strongly. She will feel it as a question that has not been answered, a question that is waiting for her, a question that is patient and does not mind being asked again and again and again, because the question is not a question that has an answer, the question is a question that has a living, and the living is the answer.

She will come back. She will bring better equipment, and she will spend longer, and she will crawl through the lower drifts and the spaces between the rock and the timbering, and she will find the things that are feeding the system, and they will not be what she expected, and they will be more wonderful than she imagined, and she will write about them, and the world will know that there is a place in the mountains where the impossible is possible, and the people of Gharhet will know that the place they live in is a miracle, and the miracle is the same thing it has always been: the fact that they are there, and the fact that they are alive, and the fact that they are persisting.

The power will not tell them.

---

The fifth thing the power notices is the children.

The power always notices the children. The children are the future of the place, and the future is the thing that the power is most interested in, because the future is the thing that the power can most affect. The children are small and they are climbing and they are not thinking about falling, and the power is not a god of the safety of children, and the power does not make them stop climbing, because the climbing is the thing that the children are doing, and the thing that the children are doing is the thing that makes them the children of Gharhet, and the power does not make them stop being the children of Gharhet.

But the power does make the ladders a little more secure. The power does make the railings a little more solid. The power does make the edges a little more visible, the drops a little more obvious, the places where the footing is uncertain a little more distinct from the places where the footing is sure. The power does this in the way that the power does everything—not by making the thing into something else, but by making the thing more of what it is, by making the thing more itself, by making the thing more visible, more legible, more present, more there.

The children will fall less. The children will still fall, because the children are children, and the children of Gharhet are climbing, and the climbing is the thing that they are doing, and the falling is the thing that sometimes happens. But the children will fall less. The children will fall less because the ladders are more secure, and the railings are more solid, and the edges are more visible, and the children are more aware of the edges, because the edges are more present, because the power has made the edges more present, because the power is benevolent and wise, and the power knows that the children of Gharhet need to know about the edges, because the edges are the thing that defines the place, and the children of Gharhet need to know about the place, because the place is the thing that they are living in, and the living is the thing that they are doing, and the power is a benevolent and wise power, and a benevolent and wise power does not make the living easier, but makes the living more possible.

The power will not tell them.

---

The sixth thing the power notices is the way the people of Gharhet do not think of the place as beautiful.

The power is not offended by this. The power is wise, and wisdom means knowing that beauty is not a thing that belongs to the observer, and the power is not the one who decides what is beautiful and what is not. The power is benevolent, and benevolence means loving the people for who they are, and the people of Gharhet are not the kind of people who think of the place as beautiful, and the power loves them for that.

But the power also knows that the people of Gharhet are wrong about the beauty. Not in the way that the visitors are wrong, the visitors who see the drama and the verticality and the desperation and call it striking, the visitors who are looking at the place from the outside and seeing the shape of it and not the substance of it. The people of Gharhet are wrong about the beauty in the way that you are wrong about the thing that is too close to you to see clearly, the way that you are wrong about the thing that is so much a part of you that you cannot separate yourself from it enough to recognize it.

The power does not make them see the beauty. The power is not a god of the aesthetic. The power is a benevolent and wise power, and a benevolent and wise power does not make people see what they are not ready to see, and the people of Gharhet are not ready to see the beauty, because the beauty is the thing that they are living in, and the thing that they are living in is the thing that is too close to see.

But the power does make the beauty more visible. The power does this in the way that the light makes the dust visible in the air—by illuminating what is already there, by making the unseen visible, by making the known feel strange. The power does this in the way that the power does everything: by making the thing more of what it is, by making the thing more itself, by making the thing more present, more there.

The light that falls through the slit at midday will be a little brighter. The shaft that illuminates the steam and the dust in the air will be a little more distinct, a little more like a shaft through a cathedral, and the people of Gharhet will notice it, and they will not call it beautiful, but they will pause, and they will look, and they will stand in the Throat at the right hour and feel the warmth of the light on their faces, and they will not know why they are pausing, and they will not know why they are looking, and they will not know why they are feeling the warmth of the light on their faces, and the power will not tell them.

The power will not tell them because the beauty is not a thing that can be told. The beauty is a thing that can only be seen, and the power is a benevolent and wise power, and a benevolent and wise power does not tell people what to see, but makes the thing more visible, and lets the people see for themselves.

---

The power does not stay.

The power is not a god of the place. The power is a benevolent and wise power, and a benevolent and wise power does not stay, because staying is a thing that the people of Gharhet do, and the power is not a person of Gharhet, and the power does not need to be a person of Gharhet, and the power does not need to be the thing that the people of Gharhet are, because the power is the thing that the power is, and the thing that the power is is a benevolent and wise power, and a benevolent and wise power does not stay, because staying is a thing that the living do, and the power is not a living thing, and the power does not need to be a living thing, and the power does not need to be the thing that the people of Gharhet are, because the people of Gharhet are the thing that the people of Gharhet are, and the power is the thing that the power is, and the thing that the power is is a benevolent and wise power, and a benevolent and wise power does not stay, because staying is a thing that the living do, and the living are the people of Gharhet, and the people of Gharhet are the thing that the power is not, and the power is the thing that the people of Gharhet are not, and the thing that the power is is a benevolent and wise power, and a benevolent and wise power does not stay.

The power goes.

But the power leaves the things that the power has done. The seep in the screeslope. The sifters that move more visibly. The mountain that groans more clearly. The ladders that are more secure. The light that is a little brighter. The naturalist who will come back. The mystery that is still there.

The power leaves the people of Gharhet in the place that is the place that they are living in, and the place is the same place that it was, and the place is a little more possible, and the place is a little more livable, and the place is a little more present, and the people of Gharhet will not know what has changed, and the people of Gharhet will not know that the power was here, and the people of Gharhet will go on living in the place that is the place that they are living in, and the place will be the place that they are living in, and the living will be the thing that they are doing, and the power will not tell them.

And the people of Gharhet will stand in the Throat at midnight and look up at the slit of stars and feel the cold air on their faces and hear the hum of the cables in their housings and feel the shift of the mountain beneath their feet, and the water will be flowing from the seep in the screeslope, clean and cold and tasting of the deep rock, and the sifters will be clicking in the walls, and the children will be sleeping in their alcoves, and the furnaces will be glowing in the lower galleries, and the mountain will be breathing, and the city will be clinging to the rock, and the people will be here, and the people will be alive, and the people will be persisting, and the power will not be there, but the things that the power has done will be there, and the things that the power has done will be the things that the people of Gharhet are living with, and the things that the power has done will be the things that the people of Gharhet are living in, and the things that the power has done will be the things that the people of Gharhet are living for, and the power will not tell them.

The power will not tell them because the power is a benevolent and wise power, and a benevolent and wise power does not tell people what to see, and does not tell people what to feel, and does not tell people what to know, and does not tell people what to be. A benevolent and wise power makes the thing more visible, and makes the thing more possible, and makes the thing more present, and lets the people see for themselves, and lets the people feel for themselves, and lets the people know for themselves, and lets the people be for themselves, because the people of Gharhet are the thing that they are, and the thing that they are is the thing that is worth being, and the power does not need to tell them, and the power will not tell them, and the power is gone.

And the people of Gharhet will stand in the Throat at midnight and look up and the slit of sky shows stars, cold and sharp, and the mountain exhales and the city shifts a little on its foundations and the fans pull air through the tunnels and somewhere in the lower galleries the furnaces glow and the sifters crawl and the water flows from the seep in the screeslope, clean and cold and tasting of the deep rock, and the rock waits with its ancient patience, and you think: this is a place that does not want me here. And you are right. And you stay anyway. And the staying is the thing that you are doing, and the staying is the thing that you are, and the staying is the thing that the power saw, and the staying is the thing that the power loved, and the staying is the thing that the power made more possible, and the staying is the thing that you will do tomorrow, and the staying is the thing that you will do the day after, and the staying is the thing that you will do, and the staying is the thing that you are, and you are here, and you are still here, and you will be here tomorrow, and the power is gone, and the power is not gone, and the power is the thing that made the staying more possible, and the staying is the thing that you are doing, and the staying is the thing that you are.
