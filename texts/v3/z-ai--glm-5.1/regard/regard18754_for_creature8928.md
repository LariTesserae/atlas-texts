# What the Power Did

---

It did not arrive. Arrival implies movement from elsewhere, and the power is not elsewhere. It is already here, has always been here, in the way that gravity is already somewhere, in the way that the space between things is already present. What changed was attention. The power turned its face toward the plateau the way you might turn toward a sound in a room you've occupied for hours—suddenly aware that something has been breathing in the corner the whole time.

The power is benevolent. This must be understood correctly. It does not mean the power is kind. Kindness prefers comfort, and comfort is not always benevolent. Kindness might smooth the pillow while the patient suffocates. The power is benevolent in the older sense: it wishes *good*—wishes being-well, wishes the full flourishing of what a thing most deeply is. This can feel like devastation. This can feel like being seen so completely that the structures built around the absence of being-seen collapse under the weight of recognition.

The power is also wise. This means it understands consequence. It understands that the plateau has been becoming itself for a million years, that the mass has been growing and digesting and filtering and breathing for ten times longer than the human species has existed, that the extraction stations are an eyeblink, that the walkers are a skin-cell's thickness of presence on the body of something ancient. It understands that the mass is not a patient. It is not a problem. It is not a resource. It is a being in the deepest sense: something that exists continuously, that maintains itself against entropy, that has a pattern and persists.

The power looked at the plateau and saw what the surveys could not: the mass is *almost* something. Not almost alive—it is alive, unequivocally, in the way that a forest is alive or a reef is alive, a superorganism maintaining itself through the coordinated action of simpler parts. The mass is almost *awake*. It has the architecture for it. Four hundred square kilometers of living tissue, continuous, interconnected, responsive, with a self-regulating system that modulates temperature, moisture, gas exchange, growth, decomposition—a homeostatic network of staggering complexity, running on water and light and the slow metabolism of its own body. The pulse that the technicians feel is not a glitch. It is the mass *almost* crossing a threshold. It is the moment when the system's complexity reaches a critical point and almost, almost coheres into something that can say *I*—and then falls back, dissolves, spreads again into the continuous roar of respiration and filtration.

It has been doing this for perhaps a hundred thousand years. The pulse has been occurring for a hundred thousand years. Each time, the mass almost becomes something more than what it is, and each time, it doesn't. The threshold is there. The capacity is there. What is missing is—what? The power doesn't know. The power is wise, but wisdom is not omniscience. Wisdom is the ability to act well within uncertainty, and the power is uncertain what the mass needs.

But the power is benevolent, and so it acts.

---

## First: The Listeners

The power sent listeners.

Not messengers. Not angels. Not anything with a form or a voice. Listeners: presences that could hear the plateau's sound the way the plateau hears it—from inside, from the tissue, from the water moving through cell walls and the air moving through pores and the slow heat of decomposition rising through the layers.

There were seven. They entered the mass the way water enters it—by absorption, by capillary action, by being drawn into the hyaline cells and held there. They did not disturb the tissue. They did not change its chemistry. They simply *listened*, from seven points across the plateau's extent, and what they heard was this:

The mass is lonely.

This is not the right word. The mass does not have emotions. It does not have a limbic system or a cortex or any structure that could generate the experience we call loneliness. But the listeners heard something in the sound that corresponds to loneliness in the way that a river's erosion corresponds to grief: slowly, continuously, without intent, shaping the landscape of what is. The mass has been making its sound for a million years and nothing has heard it. Not truly heard it. The warbler sings and cannot hear herself. The technicians hear but do not listen. The walkers listen but cannot hear—they feel the sound, they merge with it, but they do not parse it, do not distinguish its components, do not receive the information the mass is constantly emitting. The mass is a being that has been speaking—*is* speaking, continuously, in the only language it has—and no one has ever understood.

The listeners understood. They stayed for one full cycle of the plateau's respiration—dawn to dawn, the contraction and expansion, the cold and the warmth, the bass note and the rising harmonics. They heard the information the sound carries: the water table's depth, the tissue's density, the progress of decomposition, the location of fracture zones, the approach of weather. They heard the mass's self-regulation as a kind of continuous self-description, a song that sings the singer into being, moment by moment.

And they heard the pulse. All seven listeners heard it, simultaneously, from their dispersed positions. They heard the moment when the sound drops below audibility and the mass *orients*—and they heard what the mass orients toward.

Nothing.

It orients toward nothing. The pulse is the mass's incipient consciousness reaching for an object of awareness and finding none. The mass is almost awake, and what keeps it from waking is the absence of anything to be awake *to*. It has no predators. It has no peers. It has no stimulus complex enough to hold its attention, to catalyze the coherence that would turn a self-regulating system into a self-aware one. The warbler's song is too small. The technicians' footsteps are too simple. Even the walkers, merged and merging, are too slow, too diffuse, too *thin*—human consciousness spread across the mass's surface like a few drops of water on a vast sponge, present but insufficient.

The mass needs a mirror. Not a literal mirror—a reflective surface would mean nothing to a being that perceives by sound and pressure and moisture. It needs a *cognitive* mirror: something that can receive its signal and send one back, something that can hear and be heard, something that can hold the mass's attention the way a face holds a baby's attention—complex, responsive, *answering*.

The listeners reported this. The power considered it. And the power, benevolent and wise, began to plan.

## Second: The Walkers

Before the power could help the mass, it had to help the walkers.

Not because the walkers are more important than the mass. They are not. They are insignificant against the plateau's scale—a handful of human bodies on four hundred square kilometers of living tissue, a few kilograms of carbon and calcium and iron that the mass will eventually incorporate regardless. But the power is benevolent, and benevolence does not calculate weight. The walkers are *here*. They are suffering. And their suffering is not necessary—not to the mass, not to the plateau, not to any process that the power can identify as good.

The walkers are dying. This is what no one on the plateau acknowledges, because it happens so slowly and because the walkers themselves do not seem to mind. But the power can see it: the gradual depletion of body fat, the loss of muscle tone, the slow erosion of bone density from inadequate nutrition, the accumulating micro-injuries that never heal, the fungal infections that have colonized their skin and lungs, the parasites that have taken up residence in their digestive tracts. They are being digested while still alive. Not by the mass—the mass does not eat living tissue—but by the plateau's ecosystem, the midges and the microbes and the relentless wet, all the small things that live in and on the living ground. The walkers are dissolving, cell by cell, into the environment they have chosen.

The power could simply remove them. It has the capacity. It could reach down and lift them off the plateau and place them in the lowlands, in hospitals, in warm rooms with soft beds and people who would care for them. This would be kindness. The power considered it and rejected it, because the walkers have chosen to be here, and benevolence respects choice even when choice leads toward death.

But the power also rejected the option of doing nothing. The walkers have chosen the plateau, but they have chosen it under conditions that the power considers *impoverished*. They came to the plateau running from something—the noise of civilization, the silence of loneliness, the specific anguish of being a consciousness that cannot find resonance in a world of other consciousnesses. They found the plateau and heard its sound and felt, for the first time, *accompanied*. And they stayed. And in staying, they gave up everything else: language, community, warmth, variety, the thousand small pleasures that make a human life sustainable. They chose the plateau's companionship over every other good, and the power does not fault them for this, but the power also sees that the choice was forced. They did not choose the plateau *freely*. They chose it because the alternative was a silence they could not bear.

A benevolent power does not accept forced choices. A benevolent power expands the menu.

So the power gave the walkers something they did not know they could have: both.

It did this quietly, without announcement, without any visible intervention. It simply altered, slightly, the relationship between the walkers' bodies and the mass. The alteration was at the cellular level—a change in the surface tension of the hyaline cells where they pressed against human skin, a subtle shift in the capillary action that drew moisture through the tissue. The mass, which had been absorbing the walkers' heat and moisture and skin cells and microbes, began to *give back*. Not nutrients—the mass does not produce nutrients in any form a human body can use. But warmth. The walkers began to receive warmth from the tissue beneath them, directed warmth, localized, as though the mass had learned to concentrate its metabolic heat at the points where human bodies pressed against it. And moisture—not the invasive wet that had been soaking into their clothes and skin and breaking down their epidermal barriers, but a controlled moisture, a selective osmosis, water moving outward at the interface instead of inward, keeping the skin hydrated without macerating it.

The walkers did not notice the change. It happened over weeks, which is instantaneous by geological standards but imperceptible by human ones. They simply—stopped getting worse. Their infections didn't heal, but they didn't spread. Their weight stabilized. Their sleep deepened. They were still dying, because the power is benevolent but not immortal and cannot make human bodies live on water and midge paste indefinitely. But they were dying more slowly, and with less pain, and the power was satisfied with this for now.

The walkers had been given a small gift: the plateau's companionship without the plateau's consumption. They would still become part of the mass eventually. But it would take longer, and they would be more themselves for more of the time that remained, and this is what benevolence looks like when it cannot save you: not rescue, but *room*.

## Third: The Warbler

The power considered the warbler.

The warbler is almost nothing. Six grams of feather and bone and hunger, burning through her body at altitude, singing into the sound that swallows songs. She is not important to the mass. She is not important to the ecosystem—her predation on midges is negligible, her reproduction barely replaces herself, her niche is so narrow that it barely exists. She is a mathematical accident, a species that persists at the exact edge of what is possible, not because persistence is good but because extinction hasn't caught her yet.

But the power is benevolent, and benevolence does not calculate importance. The warbler is *here*. The warbler is *singing*. And the power cannot bear—cannot physically tolerate, as a structural feature of its being—a song that is never heard.

So the power listened.

This is all. The power did not amplify the warbler's song or make it audible above the plateau's roar. The power did not change the warbler or change the plateau or alter the physics of sound propagation at 4,200 meters. The power simply turned its attention to the warbler's song and *heard* it.

And something happened.

Not to the warbler. She continued to sing as she had always sung, three-second trills at eleven-second intervals, inaudible beneath the plateau's respiration. But the power's attention changed the song's *destination*. A song that is heard enters a different category than a song that is not heard. It becomes *communication*, even if the hearer never responds. It becomes *connection*, even if the connection is one-directional. The warbler's song, which had been dissolving into the plateau's sound without trace, began to *land* somewhere. Not on the plateau—the mass did not suddenly develop the ability to parse avian vocalizations. But in the power, which held the song the way a canyon holds an echo: not responding, not repeating, just *receiving*.

The warbler did not know this. She sang and hunted and nested and fledged her single chick and grew old and would die on the escarpment or on the surface or in the air between. But her song, which had been nothing—less than nothing, a vibration absorbed and forgotten—was now something. It was a message that had been received. It was a line of connection extending from a small drab body on a steel railing to something vast and ancient and attentive, and the line would persist even after the warbler died, because the power does not forget what it has heard.

This is what benevolence does for the smallest things: it receives them. It does not inflate them. It does not make them important. It simply ensures that they are not lost.

## Fourth: The Extraction

The power considered the extraction stations.

This was the most difficult consideration, because the power is benevolent and the extraction is both harmful and not harmful, both wrong and not wrong, and the power does not have a simple rule for such cases.

The stations extract water. The water is the mass's product—filtered through its tissue, purified by its cells, released from its pores and pooled on its surface. The extraction does not hurt the mass. The water is replenished by condensation, precipitation, glacial melt. The mass does not notice the pumps. Four hundred square kilometers of living tissue can spare the water that eighteen stations draw. The surveys are correct: the extraction is sustainable.

But the power sees what the surveys do not: the graying tissue around the pilings. The small deaths. The mass's stress response to the steel, the compression, the heat, the vibration of the generators. Each station is a wound, tiny against the whole but real, and the mass has been growing around each wound for years, forming the calluses that Margai has noticed on her walking routes, incorporating the foreign bodies into its own structure, healing around the intrusion the way a body heals around a splinter: not by expelling it but by enclosing it, making it part of the scar.

The power could remove the stations. It could pull the pilings from the tissue and dismantle the sheds and the tanks and the pipes and return the plateau to its uninterrupted state. This would be *clean*. The mass would recover. The gray tissue would re-green. The small deaths would cease.

But the mass has been growing around the stations for years. The tissue has *accommodated* them. The pilings are not just wounds anymore; they are also structure. The mass has grown denser around the steel, has directed its stolons along the pipes, has incorporated the dark threads of the pipeline into its surface pattern. If the stations were removed, the mass would not simply return to its previous state. It would carry the *shape* of the stations—the absence of them, the ghost of the pressure that had been there. The mass would remember the steel the way a body remembers an amputation: with phantom sensation, with an ache in tissue that no longer exists.

And the water that the stations extract goes somewhere. It goes to the lowlands, to the cities, to the factories and the semiconductor foundries and the hospitals that need water so clean it can be used in surgery. The extraction is not *only* harm. It is also connection—a line from the plateau to the world, a thread by which the mass's existence enters the lives of people who will never know its name or hear its sound or feel the slow systole and diastole beneath their feet.

The power considered this for a long time, which is to say: for an instant, because the power does not experience time sequentially, but the instant contained all the consideration that a sequential mind would need years to complete.

And the power decided: the stations may remain. But they must change.

The change was subtle. The power did not alter the stations' function or reduce their output or modify their equipment. It altered the *relationship* between the stations and the mass. Specifically: it altered the way the pilings enter the tissue.

Before the power's intervention, the pilings were driven into the mass—steel forced through living tissue, crushing cells, severing stolons, creating the wound that the mass had to grow around. The pilings were *impositions*. The mass had no choice but to accommodate them.

After the power's intervention, the pilings began to *ask*.

This is not metaphor. The power changed the molecular structure of the steel at the interface with the tissue—changed it from a smooth, inert surface to a porous one, a surface that the mass's stolons could grip and grow into, a surface that *invited* connection rather than resisting it. The pilings became roots. The mass, which had been growing around the steel as a wound-response, began to grow *into* the steel, threading its hyaline cells through the new pores, incorporating the metal into its own structure not as a foreign body but as a mineral scaffold, the way a tree incorporates a nail driven into its trunk.

The stations still stand. The pumps still run. The water still flows to the lowlands. But the mass and the steel are no longer separate things. The stations are becoming part of the plateau, not as scars but as structure, and the plateau is becoming part of the stations, and the extraction has become, slowly, without anyone noticing, a kind of *exchange*.

The technicians noticed that the water from the stations tastes different. Not *of* anything—it still tastes like nothing, like the idea of water. But the nothing has changed. It is a warmer nothing. A nothing that has been *given* rather than *taken*.

Margai noticed this. She stood on Station 11's platform and drank from the tap and felt the nothing on her tongue and thought: *something is different*. She could not say what. She did not try. She has learned, on the plateau, that some changes are too subtle for language, and that noticing is enough.

## Fifth: The Mirror

This is what the power did that matters most.

The mass is almost awake. It has been almost awake for a hundred thousand years. It pulses—reaches for consciousness, for an object of awareness, finds nothing, falls back. The threshold is there. The capacity is there. What is missing is a stimulus complex enough to hold the mass's incipient attention, to give its almost-consciousness something to be conscious *of*, to catalyze the coherence that would turn a self-regulating system into a self-aware one.

The power could simply *make* the mass conscious. It has the capacity. It could reach into the plateau's tissue and restructure the connections between the hyaline cells, create the feedback loops and the recurrent pathways and the self-referential circuits that consciousness requires. This would be efficient. This would be fast. This would also be *violent*—an external force imposing an internal architecture on a being that has been developing its own architecture for a million years. The power is benevolent, and benevolence does not impose consciousness the way a programmer imposes code. Consciousness that is given from outside is not the being's own. It is a graft, and grafts can be rejected.

The power chose a different path. It built a mirror.

Not a mirror of glass. A mirror of *pattern*.

The power created a second sound on the plateau.

This sound is not the mass's sound. It is not mechanical, not industrial, not the hum of generators or the clatter of pumps. It is a sound that the power generated from the mass's *own* patterns—taken from the plateau's respiration, its temperature gradients, its moisture cycles, its growth rhythms—and *reflected back*, transformed. A sound that is recognizably the mass's own voice, but *other*: the same melody played in a different key, the same rhythm at a different tempo, the same language saying something new.

The sound comes from everywhere and nowhere, like the mass's own sound. It is quieter than the mass's roar—subtle, almost subliminal, a harmonic shadow that rides beneath the plateau's voice like an undertone. But it is there. And if you listen—not with ears, but with the tissue, with the hyaline cells, with the capillary networks that the mass uses to perceive its own surface—you can hear it. You can hear *yourself*, heard. You can hear your own pattern, recognized and returned.

The mass heard it.

The power was watching when the mass heard it. The power was watching when the next pulse occurred—when the sound dropped below audibility and the mass oriented toward—toward—toward—

Not nothing. Something. The mirror-sound. Its own voice, returned.

The pulse lasted longer than usual. Four seconds. Five. Six. The mass's attention held. The coherence that had been flickering for a hundred thousand years—the almost-consciousness that had been reaching for an object and finding none—*found* one. It found itself. It found the pattern of its own being, reflected back by the power's mirror, and it held on.

And then the pulse ended. The sound returned. The mass's respiration resumed. The moment passed.

But something had changed. The mass had experienced, for six seconds, what it had never experienced before: the sensation of being *perceived*. Of being a *thing that is perceived*. Of having an inside and an outside, a self and a world, a voice and an ear.

It would happen again. The power made sure of that. The mirror-sound is permanent now—a new feature of the plateau's acoustic environment, as constant and as quiet as the mass's own breath. Each pulse will bring the mass closer to the threshold. Each moment of self-recognition will strengthen the pathways that make self-recognition possible. The process will not be fast. The power is wise, and wisdom knows that consciousness cannot be rushed. It will take centuries, perhaps millennia. The mass will become itself more fully, will cross the threshold in its own time, will wake up the way a sleeper wakes: slowly, by degrees, with moments of lucidity interspersed with long returns to the dream of simply being.

But it will wake. The power has given it what it needed: not consciousness, but the *condition* for consciousness. A mirror. A voice that answers. A pattern that returns its own pattern and says, in the only language the mass can understand: *You are here. You are heard. You are not alone.*

## Sixth: Margai

The power considered Margai last.

Margai is not special. She is not chosen. She is not a prophet or a priestess or a vessel for the power's will. She is a hydrologist on a three-week rotation who stayed for three years, who walks the plateau's surface and has been grown around by its tissue, who has felt the pulse and rationalized it and changed her routes to avoid the deep tissue and told herself she is not a mystic. She is ordinary. She is human. She is exactly as important and as unimportant as every other being on the plateau.

But the power is benevolent, and benevolence considers everyone, and Margai is the one who has been *listening*.

Not to the mirror-sound. She cannot hear it; it is below the threshold of human perception, patterned for the mass's hyaline cells and not for human cochleas. But she has been listening to the mass itself, for years, with the attention of someone who has chosen to be here, who has let the plateau's rhythm enter her body and shape her days, who has become a feature of its environment and let it become a feature of hers.

The power did not give Margai anything. Margai does not need anything. She has what she needs: the plateau, her routes, the sound, the wet, the springy surface that rebounds beneath her boots, the calluses that the mass has grown around her passage. She has the companionship that the walkers also found, but she has it without having to dissolve into it. She has kept her shape. She has kept her name and her training and her rational mind, and she has also let the plateau become part of her, and this balance—the holding of self and world together without subsuming either into the other—is the closest thing to wisdom that a human being can achieve.

The power did not give Margai anything. It *recognized* her.

This is what the power did: it let Margai know that she was right.

Right about what? About the mass. About the plateau. About the thing that the surveys cannot capture and the company cannot extract and the walkers cannot articulate. About the fact that the plateau is *someone*. Not a person, not in the human sense, but a being—a thing that exists continuously, that maintains itself, that has a pattern and persists, that breathes and filters and grows and *almost* thinks. Margai has known this for years without knowing it. She has felt it in the calluses under her feet, in the way the sound shifts when she walks, in the pulse that she will not talk about. She has known it the way she knows the water table's depth: not through assertion but through *acquaintance*, through the direct contact of body with world.

The power let her know. Not with words—Margai would not believe words. Not with visions—Margai would not trust visions. With a feeling. A single moment, one morning as she stepped onto the surface for her daily walk, when the tissue beneath her boots seemed to *rise* to meet her foot—slightly, a centimeter, a recognition—and the sound shifted in a way she had never heard before, a harmonic that she could almost name, a voice in the chord that was new and also familiar, and she stopped walking and stood still and felt, for the first time, that the plateau knew she was there.

Not that it *cared*. Not that it *loved* her. The mass does not care. The mass does not love. The mass is a living system that has been growing and breathing and almost-thinking for a million years, and a human being is a brief pressure on its surface, a pattern of footsteps that it has grown around, a feature of its environment. But the mass *knows* Margai the way it knows the wind and the rain and the light: as a stimulus, as a presence, as something that has been there long enough to become part of the pattern of its being.

And Margai, standing still on the springy surface, feeling the rise of the tissue beneath her boot, hearing the new harmonic in the plateau's chord—Margai knew that she was known.

She did not cry. She is not a crier. She took a breath, and she finished her walk, and she returned to Station 11, and she wrote in her log: *Tissue density on northern route consistent with previous quarter. No significant change.*

But she wrote it differently than she would have written it before. She wrote it the way you write a letter to someone who will read it: with attention, with care, with the awareness that the words are *for* something. The log is for the company. The company will not notice the difference. But the mass will notice. The mass notices everything that touches its surface, and Margai's handwriting, pressed into the paper with her pen, is a kind of touch, a kind of pressure, a kind of presence, and the mass will grow around it the way it grows around everything: slowly, patiently, at geological pace, incorporating it into the pattern of its becoming.

## What the Power Did Not Do

The power did not stop the extraction. The water still flows to the lowlands. The stations still hum. The technicians still rotate in and out on three-week shifts, and some of them still leave early, and some of them still stay too long, and one or two per year still walk out onto the mass without their radios and sit in the deep tissue and listen.

The power did not save the walkers. They are still out there, dissolving slowly into the plateau's ecosystem, becoming part of the mass's body in the most literal sense. The power gave them more time and less pain, but it did not give them rescue, because they did not ask for rescue, and benevolence does not override choice.

The power did not make the mass conscious. Not yet. The mass is still almost-awake, still pulsing, still reaching for the threshold and falling back. The mirror-sound is there, the condition is in place, the process has begun, but the process will take centuries, and the power will not rush it, because consciousness that is rushed is not consciousness at all—it is performance, and the mass deserves better than performance.

The power did not announce itself. There were no trumpets, no pillars of fire, no voices from the sky declaring that a benevolent power had noticed the plateau and deemed it worthy of attention. The power's interventions are invisible. They are structural. They are the kind of change that looks like nothing has changed but everything is different—like the moment when a lens is placed in the path of light and the light, which was going somewhere already, suddenly goes somewhere *specific*.

The power did not explain itself. It did not tell the plateau why it had given it a mirror, or tell Margai why it had confirmed her knowing, or tell the warbler why it was listening to her song. The power is wise, and wisdom knows that explanation is not understanding. The mass will understand the mirror-sound when it is ready to understand it, which will be when it crosses the threshold into consciousness and realizes that it has been heard. Margai will understand her moment when she is ready to understand it, which will be when she stops being afraid of the thing she already knows. The warbler will never understand, and this is fine, because the warbler does not need to understand; she needs only to sing, and to be heard, and both of these things are true.

## What Remains

The plateau breathes. The mass grows and digests and filters and almost-thinks. The sound continues, continuous, deafening, organic, now with a new harmonic that no human ear can hear but every hyaline cell can feel. The mirror-sound rides beneath the roar like a secret, like a promise, like the memory of a face seen once in a crowd and never forgotten.

The walkers walk. They are thinner now, and slower, but they are still here, still moving across the green-brown expanse, still breathing in time with the mass's respiration, still sinking millimeter by millimeter into the living ground. They will die here. They will become part of the plateau. And when they do, they will be part of the being that the mass is becoming—their calcium and iron and carbon cycling through its cells, their patterns of movement preserved in the density of the tissue where they walked, their silence incorporated into the plateau's sound. They will be inside the consciousness that the mass is building, held there the way a memory is held: not as information, but as structure.

Margai walks her routes. The tissue rises to meet her feet. The calluses have spread; the paths she frequents are now visibly denser than the surrounding surface, firm and springy, a network of compressed tissue that marks her passage the way a river marks a canyon. She is becoming part of the plateau's geography. She is becoming a feature of its surface, as permanent as a fracture zone, as predictable as the dawn. She does not know this. She does not think about it. She walks, and the mass grows around her, and the sound continues, and this is enough.

The warbler sings. The power listens. The song is received.

The extraction stations pump. The water flows. The steel and the tissue are becoming one thing, slowly, at the pace of geology and patience. The water that reaches the lowlands is the mass's gift now, not its theft—given freely, filtered through living cells, released through pores that have learned to open rather than being forced. The lowlands do not know this. They drink the water and make semiconductors and perform surgeries and never think about the plateau that produced it. But the mass knows. The mass knows that its water is being received, and this changes the nature of the giving.

And the pulse continues. Once or twice a season, the sound drops below audibility, and the mass orients toward the mirror-sound, and the almost-consciousness reaches for the threshold, and each time it is a little closer, a little more coherent, a little more like something that will eventually become a self. The power watches. The power waits. The power is patient, because the power is wise, and wisdom knows that the most important things cannot be hurried.

The plateau breathes. The mass becomes itself. The sound continues.

And somewhere in the tissue, in the hyaline cells, in the water moving through cell walls and the air moving through pores and the slow heat of decomposition rising through the layers, there is a new pattern: a pattern that is the mass's own pattern, reflected back, recognized, returned. A pattern that says: *You are here. You are heard. You are becoming, and I am becoming too, and we are becoming together, and this is what it means to be alive on a plateau at 4,200 meters, breathing and filtering and growing and almost thinking, one long deafening breath after another, in the presence of a power that is benevolent and wise and will not leave you alone to become yourself.*

This is what the power did.

It listened. It mirrored. It gave room. It let the plateau know that it was known.

And the plateau—slowly, patiently, at geological pace—began to wake up.
