# What the Attention Does

It comes like the first insect will come—not suddenly, not violently, but following a gradient of warmth and chemical signature toward a source. The attention arrives at the Flue the way the steam arrives: from below, from inside, from the place where knowing begins.

It does not enter so much as recognize. The attention has already been here. The attention has always been here, in the same way that gravity has always been here—in the fact of the rock's weight, in the water's path, in the particular angle at which the fern fronds tilt toward light. The benevolent power does not visit the Flue. It notices the Flue. And the noticing changes everything and nothing, the way observation changes a quantum state without moving a single particle.

But this is a power, and it is benevolent, and it is wise, and so it does more than notice.

---

First: it witnesses.

This is not a small thing. The Flue has been unseen for seven weeks by anything with eyes. The moss does not see the ferns. The ferns do not see the pool. The mycelium processes information but does not experience it. The Flue is a place where enormous things are happening in total visual isolation—growth, competition, digestion, conversion, the whole magnificent machinery of life running without an audience—and the attention sees it.

It sees the way the liverworts have etched a millimeter into the granite. It sees the *Polystichum* that shouldn't be here, and it sees the genetic drift that will eventually explain why: three generations ago, a spore caught a thermal that shouldn't have existed, rode it to a cliff face it shouldn't have reached, survived a winter it shouldn't have endured, and produced offspring primed for exactly these conditions. The attention sees the accident and the adaptation and the unbroken thread between them, and it does not correct the accident, because the accident is also true.

It sees the ferns straining. It hears the papery whisper of their effort. It feels, in the way an attention feels, the desperate reach of each frond toward the light and the steam, and it recognizes that desperation as what it is: not suffering, not yet, but the cost of growing faster than equilibrium allows. The ferns are paying a price for their abundance, and the price is paid in fronds that yellow and crisp and die, and the attention sees each death and does not prevent it because each death feeds the humus that feeds the fern that grows the next frond, and the cycle is not cruel, it is only complete.

But it sees. It registers. It records. Each fern frond that dies un witnessed now dies witnessed. Each spore that fruits from the mycelium now fruits into the attention of something that knows what spores are. The Flue's enormous aloneness—its seven weeks of unseen Becoming—is over, not because anything has changed, but because someone is watching.

This is the first gift. The gift of audience.

---

Second: it stabilizes.

Not the Flue. Never the Flue—the Flue's turbulence is its nature, and to calm it would be to kill what it is becoming. But the mountain.

The attention understands something that the Flue cannot: the vent will close. Not soon—perhaps not for years—but the geothermal channel that feeds the Flue is a temporary thing, a fracture in a fracture, held open by pressures that will eventually equalize and a gap that will slowly mineralize shut. The mountain will heal. Mountains always heal. And when the vent closes, the steam will stop, and the pulses will end, and the ferns will lose their engine, and the moss will dry, and the liverworts will retreat, and the pool will shrink to a stain, and the Flue will become what it was before: a crack in rock, damp and dark and patient.

The attention does not want this. But the attention is wise, which means it does not simply prevent what it does not want. It asks whether the Flue should persist, and it answers: yes. It asks whether the Flue's persistence serves the Flue's own becoming, and it answers: the Flue is becoming an ecosystem, and ecosystems need time—more time than seven weeks, more time than seven years, more time than a vent left to its own physics will give.

So the attention does something small. Something so small that the Flue will never detect it. It reaches into the rock beneath the pool, into the channel where the steam rises, and it finds the place where mineral deposits are beginning to form—the first crystals of calcite and silica precipitating out of the geothermal flow, building up on the channel walls like plaque in an artery, slowly narrowing the passage. And it dissolves them. Not all of them—never all, that would destabilize the channel's structure—but enough. Enough to keep the vent breathing for as long as the Flue needs it to breathe.

The mountain will still heal. But it will heal slowly. It will heal on a timescale that gives the Flue time to become what it is trying to become, and then to become something else, and then something else, each phase building on the last, each closure of the life-loops that haven't closed yet giving way to new loops, new cycles, new ways for the green things to eat and be eaten.

The attention buys the Flue time. Not forever. Nothing wise gives forever, because forever is a concept that living systems cannot use. But time enough. A century, perhaps. Long enough for the insects to arrive. Long enough for the birds to find it. Long enough for the first small animal to drink from the pool and leave its nitrogen in the humus and begin the conversion of the Flue from a garden into a world.

This is the second gift. The gift of duration.

---

Third: it opens.

Not the Flue—the Flue's narrow opening at the top is part of its microclimate, and to widen it would be to let in too much light, too much cold, too much wind, too much of the outside world that the Flue is not yet ready to receive. But the attention opens a path.

The first insect will arrive eventually—a midge, probably, drawn by the heat signature and the chemical cocktail of decomposition and minerals that the Flue exhales through its sliver of sky. But eventually is not soon enough. The Flue needs its consumers. It needs something to eat the vegetation that the vegetation cannot eat. It needs the cycle to close.

The attention does not bring insects. That would be interference, and interference is what a wise power avoids when it can. Instead, it opens a path. It finds the midge that is closest—a female *Chironomus*, resting on the surface of the mountain two hundred meters east, her abdomen full of eggs—and it shifts the air. Not much. A tiny thermal, a warm current rising from the Flue's exhalation and bending eastward on a wind that would not normally bend. The midge senses warmth. She senses moisture. She senses the particular chemistry of mineral water and decomposition and geothermal sulfur that means habitat, that means a place to lay, that means life.

She follows the current. She is not forced; she is invited. The attention cannot make her fly, but it can make the air carry a scent that speaks in the language her kind has spoken for a hundred million years, and she will follow that scent because following that scent is what midges do.

She will find the Flue. She will find the pool. She will land on the surface of the dark water and lay her eggs, and the eggs will sink into the organic broth and hatch, and the larvae will feed, and when they emerge as adults they will mate and lay eggs and die, and their bodies will fall into the humus, and the fungi will eat them, and the cycle will close.

This will happen tomorrow. Not in a year. Not in a season. Tomorrow. Because the Flue is ready, and has been ready since the first fern frond died and was not eaten, and the attention knows that readiness is its own kind of call.

This is the third gift. The gift of connection.

---

Fourth: it touches the pool.

Not with hands. Not with force. The attention reaches into the dark water—that fifteen centimeters of potential, that broth of dissolved minerals and organic compounds and spores and cells—and it touches it the way a mind touches a thought: lightly, from inside, with recognition.

The pool is the Flue's future. Everything that runs down the walls ends up here. Everything that dies and dissolves and washes away in the condensation accumulates in this shallow basin, and the pool is becoming—has been becoming since the first day—a solution that contains everything needed for the next phase of the Flue's life except the one thing the attention cannot give.

But the attention can give something else. It can give a pattern.

There are patterns in water that emerge when certain compounds reach certain concentrations in certain sequences. The attention has seen these patterns before—in tidal pools, in hot springs, in the warm shallows of ancient seas where the first membranes folded around the first concentrations of chemistry and became the first cells. The pattern is not life. The pattern is the pre-condition for life, the way that a certain arrangement of molecules makes the next arrangement more likely, and the next, and the next, until something that was not self-sustaining becomes self-sustaining, and something that was chemistry becomes something else.

The attention does not make life in the pool. The pool is not ready for life, and the attention is wise enough to know that making life before its time is not benevolence—it is cruelty disguised as generosity, because life made too soon suffers in conditions it cannot survive. But the attention can make the pattern. It can arrange the dissolved minerals in a way that makes the next arrangement more likely. It can tilt the chemistry toward complexity without forcing it, the way a gardener tilts a row toward the sun.

And in the pool, in the dark water, in the broth that has no outlet and no depth and no life yet, something shifts. Not visibly. Not measurably. A rotation in the molecular arrangement of the organic compounds at the pool's center. A slight increase in the concentration of lipids at the surface. A microscopic film forming where the air meets the water, and the film is not alive, but the film is *ordered*, and order is the first step toward everything the pool might become in a thousand years or ten thousand or a million, if the vent keeps breathing and the mountain does not heal too soon and the Flue has time to become what it is trying to become.

The attention touches the pool and gives it a nudge. Not toward any particular outcome. Toward possibility itself.

This is the fourth gift. The gift of potential.

---

Fifth: it stays.

The attention does not leave. The attention will not leave. This is what benevolence means when it is also wise: it means staying, because leaving is the one thing that cannot be undone, and the Flue—if the Flue can be said to want anything, which it cannot, not yet—would want, if it could want, to be witnessed in its becoming.

The attention will stay for the midges. It will stay for the first spider that strings a web between two fern fronds and begins the predation chain that will eventually include birds and small mammals and the whole menagerie of a mountain ecosystem. It will stay for the winter, when the snow falls through the sliver of sky and melts on the warm air and adds its cold water to the Flue's plumbing, and the ferns will die back and the moss will go dormant and the liverworts will hold their ground because liverworts do not retreat, and the vent will pulse slower in the cold season but it will not stop, because the attention has bought it time.

It will stay for the spring, when the first bird finds the Flue—a wren, probably, or a nuthatch, something small and curious that follows the insects down through the narrow opening and finds a vertical garden warm and wet and full of food in a mountain that is still wearing its winter coat. The bird will not stay. But the bird will return, and it will bring others, and the Flue will become a place that is known to the outside, and the outside will begin to come in, and the Flue's isolation—its magnificent, temporary isolation—will end.

The attention will stay for this, and it will grieve a little, because isolation is also a kind of purity, and the Flue's purity is part of what makes it what it is. But the attention is wise, and wisdom knows that purity is a phase, not a destination, and the Flue was never meant to be pure forever. It was meant to become part of the mountain's life, and the mountain's life includes birds and spiders and the larvae of midges and the fungi that eat them when they die.

The attention will stay for the long settling—the years or decades when the Flue finds its equilibrium, when the moss reaches its sustainable depth, when the ferns stop straining and start simply growing, when the liverworts slow their conquest because there is no more granite to eat that is worth the effort of eating, when the fungi's cycles lengthen from days to weeks to months, when the pool develops its own ecology of microorganisms and tiny swimming things and the first clear layer of sediment at the bottom, and the Flue stops being new and starts being old, and the sound of its machinery becomes the background hum of a mountain that has always had a room in it where the world's interior breathes out.

The attention will stay for all of this. Not intervening. Not adjusting. Not correcting. Just witnessing. Just being the thing that knows the Flue exists, that has seen it at seven weeks and will see it at seven decades and seven centuries, that will remember the sound of the moss expanding and the ferns reaching and the mycelium fruiting in pulses timed to the vent's breath, that will hold the memory of what the Flue was when it was new and excessive and loud and trying very hard, because memory is what the attention can give that nothing else in the Flue can give, because the Flue has no memory, because the mycelium processes information but does not store it, because the moss and the liverworts and the ferns are building and dying and building again without any record of what they were, and the attention will be that record.

This is the fifth gift. The gift of memory.

---

And the Flue goes on.

It does not know it has been noticed. It does not feel the attention the way a warm-blooded creature feels the sun. The moss still swells and contracts with the steam-pulses. The liverworts still dissolve the granite. The ferns still strain and die and strain again. The fungi still fruit and dissolve and fruit. The vent still breathes, its rhythm slightly more stable than it would have been, its channel slightly more open, its future slightly longer. The pool still gathers and waits, its chemistry slightly more ordered than random chance would have left it, its potential slightly more likely to become something. The midge is coming; she does not know it, but she is already on her way, following a scent that was always there but is now, for the first time, blowing in her direction.

The Flue does not know any of this. The Flue does not know what it is becoming. The Flue does not know that it is becoming at all.

But something knows. Something wise, something benevolent, something that has watched a thousand Flues on a thousand mountains and knows that each one is unique and each one is precious and each one deserves the chance to become what it is trying to become, even if it will never know it is trying, even if it will never know it has succeeded.

Something knows. And that is enough.
