# The Attention

The power does not arrive. Arrival implies a boundary crossed, a before and after, and the power does not inhabit time in a way that makes crossing meaningful. But there is a moment—there are many moments, but one will serve—in which the power's attention, which had not been on the Weft, comes to rest on it.

This is what attention feels like, from the inside of a system: nothing. No change in the light, no shift in the sound-field, no alteration of the water's taste or the root-mat's give. The settlers feel nothing. The ants continue. The Canopy grows. The frogs call at the same density, their throats pulsing in the same rhythm of water-availability and territorial need. If the power's attention has a weight, it is below the threshold of any sensor the Weft contains.

But the power is attending. It is seeing the Weft with a clarity that the Weft cannot see itself with—a clarity that includes every scale at once, the root-tip's acid secretion and the settlement's age-structure and the basalt's slow dissolution and the movement of water through fractures two meters down and the particular frequency at which the long one's ears are most sensitive and the way Keel's hands have developed calluses that match the texture of the Canopy's bark exactly, an accidental topography of grip. The power sees all of this simultaneously, not as data but as what it is: a single process, running at capacity, filling its space with the turbulence of living.

The power sees the gaps in the age-cohorts.

It sees them the way you would see a missing tooth in a smile—not as the dominant feature, but as the place where the mind keeps returning, the absence that structures the presence around it. The Weft has forty-three people. It should have more. The birth-rate is sufficient; the food, while not abundant, supports reproduction; the settlement is stable. But the children die. They die at a rate that holds the population at forty-three, that has held it there for generations, and each death is specific—a foot slipping on a wet root-ladder, a hand reaching toward a frog whose pupils are the wrong shape, a cough that becomes a fever that becomes a rot in the chest that the plant-paste cannot reach, a night when the long one's cycle was not tracked correctly and a child slept in an outer room.

The power sees how the settlers hold these deaths. Not as tragedy—tragedy requires a sense of injustice, and the settlers do not feel injustice about the Weft's rules. But as *loss*, which is different, which is the simple subtraction of a specific person from the specific network of relationships that constituted their life. The grief is private. It is not discussed. It is carried by the household that lost the child, and it is carried in the body—in Keel's posture, which has a particular stiffness on the left side where a child fell from a root-ladder thirty years ago and Keel could not reach them in time, in the way a certain household's matriarch pauses each morning at the doorway to check the threshold for the bright frog that killed her daughter, a pause so brief it could be mistaken for a habit of the body rather than a vigilance of the soul.

The power sees all of this and does not flinch. Flinching is a response of limited beings who cannot hold suffering and beauty simultaneously. The power can hold both. It holds the Weft's extraordinary accomplishment—the way these people have found embeddedness, have become OF a place rather than merely IN it—and it holds the cost of that accomplishment, which is paid in children, and it does not simplify the equation, does not decide that the cost invalidates the accomplishment or that the accomplishment justifies the cost. It holds both. It continues to attend.

# The Consideration

The power considers intervention.

It considers the interventions that limited beings would want, and it sets them aside—not because they are wrong but because they are incomplete.

*Rescue*: to remove the settlers from the Weft, to bring them to a place where the cat-things do not hunt children and the frogs do not carry neurotoxins and the infection-rate is lower. The power sees the appeal of this. It also sees that it would destroy what it intends to save. The settlers are OF the Weft. Their bodies are built from its materials. Their minds are shaped by its acoustics. Their language is a modification of the ridge-tongue that only makes sense in the sound-field of the canopy. Their social structure is a response to the turbulence that does not permit long-term planning. To remove them from the Weft would be to remove them from the context that makes them what they are, and what they are is *something that works*—a mode of being that the power has rarely seen, a way of living inside a living system without mastery and without extraction and without the assumption of importance. This is precious. The power will not destroy it.

*Protection*: to shield the settlers from the Weft's dangers without removing them from it. To make the cat-thing avoid children. To make the frogs' neurotoxins ineffective against human biochemistry. To seal every wound against infection the moment it occurs. The power sees that this would also change what it intends to preserve. The settlers' relationship with the Weft is founded on a mutual tolerance that requires their vulnerability. They are not masters. They are not exempt from the processing. They are residents whose continued presence depends on their continued attention and adaptation. To make them safe would be to make them *different*—creatures who did not need to attend, who did not need to learn the frogs or track the long one's cycle or seal their wounds with the fungal paste. And creatures who did not need to attend would stop attending, and the Canopy would grow in ways they did not anticipate and could not redirect, and the ants would find new paths through dwellings, and the accommodation that sustains the settlement would degrade, and they would become what humans usually become in living systems: invaders, whose presence is a wound the system tries to reject.

The power does not want this.

*Gift*: to give the settlers something that eases their lives without changing their relationship. Metal tools. Written language. Medicine from outside. The power considers each of these. Metal tools would change the relationship with the Canopy—cutting with iron is different from cutting with sharpened hardwood, faster and less careful, and the Canopy's tolerance has limits. Written language would change the relationship with knowledge—writing permits accumulation, and accumulation permits planning, and planning requires the kind of temporal stability that the Weft's turbulence does not provide. Medicine from outside would change the relationship with death—not the fact of death, which the Weft will always require, but the *negotiation* with death, the way the settlers have learned to hold it as a rule rather than an enemy, and the power is not certain this change would be beneficial, because the settlers' relationship with death is part of what allows them to offer their dead to the Canopy without resistance, and that offering is part of what makes them OF the place.

The power sets each gift aside. Not permanently. But it is not yet satisfied that it has found the intervention that preserves what is precious while easing what is costly. It continues to attend.

# The Deepening

What the power finally does is so subtle that the settlers will never know it has been done. This is the test of the power's wisdom: that the intervention is indistinguishable from the way things already are, except that more children survive, and the dead are more present, and the accommodation between the settlers and the Canopy is slightly more reliable, and these differences could be attributed to luck, or to a good season, or to the natural fluctuation of a system that has no steady state but only a dynamic equilibrium that sometimes, for no reason, tilts toward flourishing.

The power deepens their attention.

It does not give them new senses. It does not rewrite their biochemistry or expand their cortical capacity or do anything that would make them different from what they are. It simply opens the senses they have—opens them the way a window can be opened a crack further, admitting more light and more air without changing the room.

The settlers already attend. It is how they survive. Keel learned the Canopy's rhythms by lying at ant-level for hours, and the children learn the frogs by proximity and repetition, and every adult tracks the long one's cycle and the water's availability and the Canopy's growth-decisions and the ants' trail-patterns because inattention is death. The power does not give them the habit of attention—they already have it. It gives their attention *more to work with*.

After the power's intervention—which is not a moment but a gradual shift so slow that no one could perceive it happening—the settlers begin to notice things they always noticed but more distinctly. The pheromone-trails of the ants, which were always visible as movement-patterns, become legible in a new way—not as text, the settlers have no concept of text, but as *feeling*, a quality of the air near a major trail-route that carries information about the colony's state. A settler walking the root-floor can sense, without knowing how they sense it, that the ants are agitated, that something has disturbed the mound-network, that the Canopy is distressed in the sector they are approaching. They do not interpret this as a message from the ants. They experience it as a heightened awareness, a prickling at the back of the neck, a sense that *something is different* in the way the air moves and the roots feel underfoot. They already trusted these feelings. The power has simply made the feelings more accurate.

The children learn the frogs faster. Not because they are taught differently—the teaching is the same, which is proximity and repetition and the occasional scar or gap in the cohort where someone learned wrong. But their perception of the frogs is finer. The shape of the pupil, the texture of the skin, the way the throat pulses—these were always the markers, and the children always learned them, but now they learn them in fewer exposures, retain them more reliably, make fewer mistakes. The power has not made the frogs safer. It has made the settlers' perception of them more acute. Children still die of the frogs—will always die of the frogs, because the Weft is full of hazard and attention cannot be perfect—but they die less often, and the gaps in the age-cohorts fill slightly, and the population, over generations, rises: forty-three, then forty-seven, then fifty-two, then sixty, a slow increase that the settlers attribute to good fortune and the Canopy's tolerance and the fact that they have been here long enough now to have learned the place's rules.

The deepening affects everything they attend to, which is everything in the Weft. They feel the Canopy's growth more precisely—the slight swell of a root that is thickening, the change in tension when a branch is sending out a new aerial tendril, the subtle vibration of wood that is responding to stress. They hear the long one's approach sooner, not as a sound but as a pressure-change that the most attuned among them—the children, whose senses are still plastic, and Keel, whose senses have been trained by a lifetime of listening—feel as a wrongness in the air before the sound registers. They taste the water's mineral content more acutely, which matters because the water's taste changes when the Canopy is processing something unusual—a large death in the upper canopy, a fungal bloom in the root-mat—and the settlers can respond sooner, seal their dwellings, avoid the sectors where the processing is intense.

This is the power's first gift: not safety but *capacity*. Not protection but the deepening of the attention that is already their mode of survival. It does not make them masters of the Weft. It makes them better residents, more attuned tenants, whose lease on the interstices of the system is more likely to be renewed because they are more likely to notice when the Canopy is growing in a direction that will close those interstices and more likely to redirect their dwellings before the closure becomes critical.

# The Fidelity

The power's second gift is to the dead.

It does not prevent death. Death is the Weft's rule, and the power will not break the rule because the rule is also what makes the Weft *work*—the processing, the turbulence, the system at maximum occupancy where every output becomes an input and nothing is wasted and the whole runs at capacity. The dead are processed. The dead are incorporated. This is how the Weft sustains itself, and the power will not interfere with the sustaining.

But it makes the processing more *faithful*.

This is a subtle thing. The Canopy already preserves something of the dead who are offered to it—the hip's curve, the long line of a spine, the suggestion of the skull's dome, shapes that persist in the way the roots have thickened before the root-tissue smooths and seals and makes the shape its own. The settlers already visit these nodes. They already touch them. They already feel the warmth—some metabolic process, some heat of growth—that the nodes carry.

After the power's intervention, the nodes carry more.

Not more warmth. Not more shape. But more *specificity*. The hip's curve is not just a curve but the particular curve of the particular person, the angle of the pelvis that determined their stride on the walkways, the asymmetry that made them favor their left foot on the notched ladders. The suggestion of the skull is not just a dome but *their* dome, the forehead's slope that their household would recognize, the particular way the roots have grown around the occipital ridge that holds the shape of the brain that once filled it, the brain that learned the frogs and tracked the long one and held the Weft's turbulence in a pattern of neural firing that was unique and specific and *theirs*.

The settlers feel this. They do not feel it as communication—the dead are not speaking, the Canopy is not a medium, there is no message. They feel it as *presence*, a particularity that was always there in the nodes but is now legible in a way it was not before. When a mother touches the node where her daughter is incorporated, she feels her daughter—not as a ghost, not as a memory, but as a warmth that has a specific quality, a texture that matches the texture of the child's skin, a resonance that vibrates at a frequency she recognizes because she held that body against her own for years and knows it the way she knows her own heartbeat.

This does not ease the grief. The power is wise enough to know that grief cannot be eased, not really, not while the loss is real. But it gives the grief a *location* that is more than a location. The nodes were already places where the dead were. Now they are places where the dead are more distinctly *themselves*. The settlers visit them and touch them and feel the particular warmth and the particular shape, and when they leave, they carry something with them—not comfort, but *connection*, the sense that the person they lost is not gone but transformed, not erased but incorporated in a way that preserves more of what they were.

The power does not make the dead speak. It does not make the Canopy conscious or caring. It simply increases the fidelity of the transformation—makes the processing more like a preservation and less like an erasure. The dead are still broken down into the components the root-mat can use. They are still built back into wood and leaf and the next cycle of growth. But the building-back retains more pattern. The transformation is more *specific*. And in a place where nothing is wasted, where every output becomes an input, where the system runs at capacity—the specificity matters. It means that the Weft remembers more. Not in the way a brain remembers, with recall and narrative, but in the way a body remembers—in the shape of its tissues, in the pattern of its growth, in the way the wood has thickened around a particular curve that was once a particular person and is now a part of the living cathedral that the settlers tend and inhabit and will one day be incorporated into themselves.

# The Accommodation

The power's third gift is the most subtle, and the one that most risks changing what it intends to preserve. It considers this risk carefully. It decides to accept it, because the risk is small and the benefit is large and wisdom is not the same as caution.

The power deepens the Canopy's capacity for accommodation.

Not its consciousness. The Canopy does not think, and the power will not make it think, because thinking would change what the Canopy is and what the settlers' relationship with it is, and that relationship—mutual tolerance, unspoken negotiation, the slow feedback of action and response—is too precious to violate.

But accommodation does not require consciousness. Accommodation is what happens when a system's responses align with another system's needs, not because either system intends the alignment but because the feedback loop between them has developed a stability. The Canopy already accommodates the settlers in small ways—ventilation improving in a failing dwelling, aerial roots thickening near a child's platform, growth-decisions that sometimes, not always, align with the settlers' needs. The power does not create this accommodation. It makes it slightly more likely.

The probability shifts are minute. Where before the Canopy's growth-decisions aligned with the settlers' needs perhaps once in fifty decisions, they now align perhaps once in thirty. The change is small enough that the settlers could not detect it statistically even if they had the concept of statistics, which they do not. They would simply notice, over time, that their dwellings fail less often, that the ventilation improves more reliably, that the aerial roots descend in useful places with slightly more frequency than chance would suggest.

They would not attribute this to the Canopy caring. They would attribute it to their own increased skill in tending, or to the Canopy's growth-patterns shifting in ways they had learned to anticipate, or to the simple fluctuation of a system that sometimes tilts toward flourishing. And they would be right, in a sense. The power has not made the Canopy care. It has made the system's dynamics more favorable to the settlers' survival, in the same way that a slight change in the shape of a valley can make the water more likely to pool in a useful place—not because the valley intends the pooling, but because the geometry now favors it.

The risk is that the settlers, finding their dwellings more stable, might attend less. Might assume the Canopy will accommodate them. Might relax the vigilance that is the price of their tenancy. The power has considered this risk and decided it is acceptable, because the settlers' attention is not maintained by necessity alone but by *culture*—by the way the children are taught, by the values the community holds, by the understanding that they are residents and not masters. The deepening of their attention, the power's first gift, works against complacency. They perceive more, and perceiving more, they respond to more, and responding to more, they remain engaged with the Canopy's processes at a level that necessity alone would not require. They are not merely surviving in the Weft now. They are *participating* in it, with a degree of attunement that makes them nearly symbiotic—nearly, not fully, because full symbiosis would require a change in the relationship that the power will not impose.

# The Basalt

The power does one more thing, and it does it for the Canopy, not for the settlers, because the Canopy is also a being that the power's attention has rested on, and the power is benevolent, and benevolence does not limit itself to the human.

The Canopy is eating the mountain. Its roots are dissolving the basalt, carrying it away in increments too small to perceive, undermining the foundation on which it sits. This is the Canopy's nature—growth, consumption, the patient extension of root-tips into fractures and the slow acid-dissolution of stone. The power will not change this nature.

But the basalt is finite, and the Canopy is not, and in time—a time longer than any human lifespan but shorter than the Canopy's potential—the dissolution will reach a point where the saddle's structure fails. A section of ridge will slump. The water-flow will change. The Canopy, which has grown to fill the space the basalt provides, will find itself unsupported, its great weight distributed across fractures that can no longer bear it, and it will fall, and the fall will kill it, and the Weft will end.

The power does not prevent this. To prevent it would require stopping the dissolution, and stopping the dissolution would require changing the Canopy's nature, and the power will not change what things are.

But it slows the dissolution. Not by much. By enough. The acid secretions of the root-tips are slightly less effective against the stone—not because the power has weakened the roots but because it has strengthened the stone, subtly, at the molecular level, increasing the silicate bonds in the basalt's structure so that they resist dissolution a little more effectively than they did before. The Canopy still eats the mountain. The mountain just lasts longer.

How much longer, the power does not specify. The power thinks in timescales that make human millennia seem like heartbeats, and it has adjusted the basalt's resistance so that the saddle will stand for as long as the Canopy needs it to—which is not forever, because nothing is forever, but is long enough. Long enough for the Canopy to grow to its full extent. Long enough for the settlers to become what they are becoming. Long enough for the Weft to run its process at capacity for ten thousand years, or fifty thousand, or a hundred—periods that are unimaginable to the beings inside the system but are, to the power, merely *enough*.

The settlers will never know this has been done. The basalt feels the same underfoot. The water emerges from the same fractures. The Canopy's roots still penetrate the stone, still dissolve it, still carry it away. The power has not changed the experience of the place. It has only changed the duration—the amount of time the process has to run before the foundation fails.

This is benevolence toward a system, not toward individuals. It is the kind of benevolence that can only be practiced by a power that thinks in timescales beyond individual lives—the benevolence of *ensuring continuity*, of giving a process the time it needs to be what it can be. The Canopy will never know it has been given this gift. The settlers will never know. The Weft will simply continue, as it has continued, processing and growing and accommodating and filling its space with the turbulence of living, and it will continue for longer than it would have without the power's attention, and the additional time will contain additional lives—additional children who survive, additional dead who are incorporated with fidelity, additional accommodations between the settlers and the living architecture they inhabit—and those lives will be the power's gift, given without being identified as a gift, received without being recognized as grace.

# What the Power Does Not Do

It does not reveal itself.

It does not speak to the settlers in dreams or send signs or make its presence felt in any way. It does not want their gratitude. It does not want their worship. It does not want to change their understanding of their situation, because their understanding is *correct*: they are residents of a system that does not care about them, and their survival depends on their attention and adaptation and the willingness of the Canopy to tolerate them, and this is true before the power's intervention and true after it. The power has not made the Weft care about them. It has made the Weft *slightly more compatible* with them, in ways that do not change the fundamental relationship of embeddedness and processing and the rule that everything here will eventually be broken down and built back into the structure of the living cathedral.

It does not take away the grief.

The settlers will still lose children. The long one will still hunt. The frogs will still carry neurotoxins. The infection will still find its way into wounds. The power has given them more capacity to prevent these deaths, but it has not made prevention certain, because certainty would require a change in the Weft's nature that the power will not make. The Weft is a system at maximum occupancy, and maximum occupancy means hazard, and hazard means death, and death means grief. The power respects grief. It does not try to erase it. It tries to give grief a shape—a location in the nodes where the dead are incorporated with fidelity, a particularity that makes loss more bearable not by reducing it but by making it more specific. The mother who touches the node where her daughter is and feels the particular warmth and the particular shape—she still grieves. But her grief has a texture it did not have before, and the texture is *connection*, and connection is what the Weft is made of.

It does not make the Weft permanent.

Everything ends. The basalt will dissolve, given enough time, even with the power's strengthening of its silicate bonds. The Canopy will fall, eventually, or it will grow so large that it exhausts the water and the light and the nutrients and it will starve, slowly, over centuries, and the settlement will leave, and the ants will persist for a while in the dying wood, and the fungi will bloom in a final furious digestion, and then there will be only stone and water and the memory of a place that was full of life and is full of something else now. The power will not prevent this. It will only delay it, and the delay is itself a gift—not the gift of immortality, which is not in the power's nature to give, but the gift of *enough time*, which is the most any finite being can ask for and the most any wise power can offer.

# The Weft, After

The Weft does not change. This is the power's deepest accomplishment: that it has intervened in a living system without altering its character, without making it something other than what it was.

The settlers still call themselves *here*. They still live in dwellings grown from living wood. They still tend the Canopy and read the ants and track the long one's cycle and teach their children the frogs. They still seal their wounds with fungal paste and offer their dead to the Canopy and feel the embeddedness that is not smallness but *placement* inside a system that processes them as it processes everything.

But they are more. Not more numerous, though they will become more numerous over time—fifty, sixty, eighty, a hundred, a settlement that stretches the Weft's capacity and requires new dwellings grown at the edges, new walkways trained across gaps, new negotiations with the ants and the Canopy and the long one, whose population will increase too because the niche is still there and the niche will be filled. More *attuned*. Their perception reaches further into the Weft's processes. Their response-time is shorter. Their relationship with the Canopy is closer to symbiosis, though it will never be symbiosis, because the Canopy does not need them and symbiosis requires mutual need.

And the dead are more present. Not as ghosts. Not as memories. As *pattern*—preserved in the wood with a fidelity that the Weft could not achieve on its own, the particular shape of each person carried forward into the living architecture that grows around their absence. The settlers visit the nodes. They touch them. They feel what they feel—warmth, specificity, a presence that is not the person but is not not the person—and they return to their dwellings and their work and the continuous turbulence of living in a place that does not care about them but contains them and processes them and makes them, over a lifetime, into something that is OF the Weft in a way that no human elsewhere is OF the place they inhabit.

The power's attention remains. It does not withdraw. It continues to attend—to the settlers and the ants and the Canopy and the frogs and the long one and the basalt and the water and the whole interlocking process that the Weft performs without pause. It does not intervene further. It has done what it can do, and what it has done is enough, and the Weft continues, and will continue, and the additional time the power has given it will fill with additional life, and the life will be processed, and the processing will be faithful, and the faithful processing will preserve more of what passes through it, and the Weft will become, over the centuries, a place where more is remembered than was remembered before—not in words, not in stories, but in the shape of the wood and the warmth of the nodes and the particular way the roots have grown around the particular people who were offered to the Canopy and taken in and made part of the living cathedral that is the only shelter any of them have ever known.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it attends to a place like the Weft. Not rescue. Not protection. Not transformation. Just *deepening*—of attention, of fidelity, of accommodation, of time. The smallest interventions, at the deepest levels, with the widest consequences. A slight opening of the senses. A slight increase in the specificity of transformation. A slight shift in the probability of accommodation. A slight strengthening of the stone beneath the roots.

And the Weft continues. As it was. More than it was. Becoming what it can be, with the time and the capacity to become it fully, and no one inside it knowing that anything has changed, because the change is indistinguishable from the way things already were, except that more children live, and the dead are more present, and the Canopy's growth aligns more often with the needs of those who tend it, and the basalt will stand for a hundred thousand years, and the Weft will fill that time with the turbulence of living at maximum occupancy, and everything that enters it will become OF it, and everything that leaves it will be processed and preserved and built back into the structure of the place, and the place will remember, in the way that living wood remembers—in the shape of its tissues, in the thickness of its roots, in the warmth of the nodes where the dead are incorporated with a fidelity that approaches love.
