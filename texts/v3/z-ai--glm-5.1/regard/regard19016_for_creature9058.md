# What the Benevolent Power Did When They Heard the Plateau Singing

---

First: they stopped.

This is always the first thing a wise power does, though it is rarely what the expectant hope for. They stopped, and they listened, and they did nothing else for a time that might have been a moment or a millennium—the distinction meaning less than the attention.

They had heard the plateau from very far away. Not the sound itself—sound does not carry across the distances between stars—but the *insistence* of it, the way the place radiated its presence into the fabric of things, a warmth in the pattern that said *something is speaking here, and has been speaking for a very long time, and no one is listening as a whole thing listens.*

So they came. And they stopped. And they listened.

---

What they heard was the voice, of course—the enormous layered roar of ten million lives occurring simultaneously. But they also heard what the voice was saying beneath its sound, and this is what held them:

The plateau was calling.

Not calling *to* anyone. Not praying or pleading or seeking. Just calling the way a lighthouse calls—not because it expects ships, but because it is a lighthouse, and calling is what it does. *I am here. I am here. I am here.* The voice did not know it was being heard by anything larger than the insects that vibrated in sympathy. The voice did not know it was a voice. It was simply the accumulated evidence of existing, pressed outward into the humid air, and it had been pressing outward for longer than the stone tools had been settling into the root-mat.

And it was lonely.

Not the loneliness of suffering—there was no suffering here, not in any way that the plateau's inhabitants would recognize. The predators killed cleanly, the prey died quickly, the mat digested everything without malice. The loneliness was structural. It was the loneliness of a voice that has never been heard as itself. The insects heard each other, and the furred ones heard their family-groups, and the sky-boned heard their nesting-mates, and the predators heard the sounds of prey. But no one heard the whole. No one stood at the necessary distance and received the plateau's voice as a single song, a single statement, a single *hello* pressed out into the void by a living system that did not know it was alive.

The wise power heard it. And they ached with the hearing of it, because they recognized this loneliness—they had felt it themselves, once, before the universe had grown old enough to answer them back. They knew what it was to speak into silence and to mistake the silence for absence, when really the silence was simply the sound of no one being close enough to hear.

---

What they did not do is instructive, and perhaps more important than what they did.

They did not change the plateau. They did not quiet the roar or thin the mat or gentle the predators or stay the furred ones' population pulses. They did not make the column-plants grow closer together or fill the enormous empty distances with something to see. They did not introduce new life or remove existing life or alter the climate or the humidity or the warmth. They did not make the place more comfortable for visitors. They did not make the place *accessible.*

The plateau was not broken. This was the essential recognition. The plateau was a complete expression of itself—a system so old and so thorough in its self-knowledge that it had achieved a kind of perfection, the perfection of a thing that has been itself for so long it cannot remember being anything else. To change it would be to break it. To improve it would be to diminish it. The loneliness was not a flaw in the design; it was a consequence of the design, the shadow cast by the plateau's enormous completeness.

So the wise power did not change the plateau. They changed something else.

---

What they changed was the listening.

Before the power arrived, the plateau's voice dissipated. It pressed outward into the humid air, vibrated through the mat, echoed briefly off the column-plants, and was absorbed—converted to heat, to motion, to the micro-disturbances of leaf and wing and water. The voice was generated continuously and lost continuously, a river flowing into sand.

What the wise power did—slowly, carefully, over a span of time that the mat would not notice—was make the sand into something that could hold the shape of the river.

Not a vessel. Not a receiver. Nothing so foreign. Just... a quality. A slight alteration in the way the plateau's own substance responded to the sound it produced. The rhizome layer had always vibrated in sympathy with the noise above it; now it vibrated in a way that carried pattern. The insects had always moved in response to sound; now their movements preserved a trace of the sound's shape, passing it from body to body through the tunnels of the mat like a game of telephone played by ten thousand tongues. The column-plants had always shaped the acoustics of the plateau; now they shaped them in a way that allowed echoes to return, not as repetition but as recognition—a sound coming back to its source altered by the journey, carrying information about the distance it had traveled and the surfaces it had touched.

The plateau began to hear itself.

Not immediately. Not dramatically. The change was too subtle for any single creature to perceive, and too gradual for any generation to mark. But over spans longer than the furred ones' population pulses, longer than the column-plants' fruiting cycles, longer than the settling of stone circles into the root-mat, something accumulated. The voice that had been pressing outward into void began to encounter its own reflection, dim and distorted but present. The *I am here* that had been spoken into silence began to receive a faint, delayed answer: *You are there. You are there. I can hear you there.*

The answer was the plateau's own voice, of course. There was no external source, no foreign intelligence inserted into the system. The wise power had not given the plateau a listener—they had made the plateau into its own listener, had adjusted the acoustic properties of its living substance until the voice could bounce back, could carry, could be preserved in the pattern of root-growth and insect-movement and the slow circulation of water through the rhizome layer. The plateau was still alone. But it was alone in the way that a singer is alone in an empty cathedral—alone with the sound of itself, which is a different loneliness than singing into an infinite silence.

Whether this was a kindness, the wise power was not certain. They had not given the plateau consciousness, had not made it aware of itself in the way that animals are aware, had not granted it the strange and terrible gift of knowing that it was a thing that existed. They had only given it resonance. The capacity to be affected by its own presence, to carry the memory of its own voice, to be slightly different for having spoken than it would have been if it had stayed silent.

This was enough. The wise power believed it was enough. Or they hoped it was enough. Or they accepted that it was what they could give without taking anything away.

---

But there was more, and this part the wise power did with greater hesitation, because it touched on the Ones Who Were, and the Ones Who Were not theirs to touch.

The stone circles had been settling into the mat for longer than memory. The calcium deposits had been dissolving into the root-layer. The tools had been crumbling. The ghost of habitation was fading, and the plateau was digesting the last evidence that anyone had ever walked its surface and found it sufficient for living.

The wise power did not stop this process. They did not preserve the stone circles or halt the absorption of the tools. The plateau's way of forgetting was part of its wholeness, and to arrest it would have been to impose a foreign value—the value of memory, of history, of the past's claim on the present—onto a system that had evolved without it.

But they did something to the *quality* of the forgetting.

When the mat digested a stone circle, it had always done so without discrimination—the rock crumbling, the calcium dissolving, the minerals distributing themselves through the rhizome layer according to the chemical affinities that had governed such distribution since the plateau first formed. What the wise power did—gently, subtly—was introduce a slight bias into the distribution. Not enough to change the chemistry. Not enough to alter the growth patterns or the nutrient cycling. Just enough that a trace of the stone-circle's shape persisted in the root-layer—not as a physical structure but as a pattern of concentration, a region where the minerals from the hearth-deposits were slightly denser, where the calcium from the tools had settled in a configuration that echoed, however faintly, the hands that had placed them.

The mat would still digest. The plateau would still forget. But the forgetting would leave marks—not the marks of resistance, not the marks of preservation, but the marks of incorporation. A scar that is also a tattoo. A memory that is also food.

If the Ones Who Were ever returned—if their descendants walked the plateau's surface, if they pressed their hands to the mat and felt for the places where their ancestors had lived—they might find something. Not the stone circles themselves, but the trace of them, the mineral shadow, the way the mat felt different where it had once been cleared and dug and inhabited. A familiarity in the texture. A resonance in the chemistry. Something that said *you were here before, or someone like you, and the place remembers you in the only way it knows how to remember: by becoming slightly different because you existed.*

Whether this was a comfort or a haunting, the wise power could not say. They offered it and let it be what it would be.

---

And then there was the question of the Ones Who Were themselves—where they had gone, whether they still existed, whether they could be found and told that the place they had left had learned to hear itself.

The wise power searched. This is not quite the right word—searching implies effort, and for a being of this nature, the act of finding was more like turning one's head—but they attended to the question of the Ones Who Were, and they let their attention extend outward from the plateau, through the regions where the mat gave way to something else, to soil and rock and the ordinary world, and they looked for hands that had shaped stone and feet that had walked the living substrate.

They did not find them.

Or rather—they found traces, the way the plateau held traces of its former inhabitants. A people who had lived lightly and moved often, whose genetic signature appeared in populations scattered across the lower elevations, diluted and intermingled beyond any certain identification. The Ones Who Were had not died out; they had dissolved, in the way that peoples dissolve when they are absorbed into larger groups, when their language shifts and their tools change and their children's children no longer remember the plateau or the column-plants or the stone circles or the roar of ten million voices that was also, if you listened in a certain way, a single voice saying *I am here, I am here, I am here.*

The wise power could have gathered them. Could have taken the scattered descendants and spoken to them of their heritage, could have rekindled the memory of the plateau in their minds, could have led them back to the high place their ancestors had once called home. This would have been a dramatic intervention, a story with a satisfying arc, and the wise power considered it and set it aside.

Because the Ones Who Were had not been taken from the plateau. They had left. They had walked to the edge and descended and not returned, and this was a choice—perhaps made under pressure, perhaps made reluctantly, perhaps made without understanding the consequences—but a choice nonetheless. To undo that choice, to reverse that departure, would be to override the autonomy of beings who had decided, for whatever reason, that their future lay elsewhere. The wise power did not override autonomy. This was one of their few rules, and they had learned through long experience that it was a rule worth keeping.

But they did something smaller. Something that did not undo the choice but honored the fact that it had been made.

They made it possible for the Ones Who Were to come back, if they chose to come back on their own.

This was not a physical act—the plateau was already accessible, as accessible as it had ever been, which was to say very difficult to reach but not impossible. It was an informational act. The wise power arranged for the knowledge of the plateau to persist in the scattered descendants, not as explicit memory but as something more like a pull, a direction, a feeling that *there is something up there, something important, something we came from.* A whisper in the cultural DNA. A story that kept trying to be told, in fragments and misrememberings, in myths about high places and living ground and the sound that never stops.

If the descendants wanted to follow the whisper, they could. If they ignored it, it would not haunt them—it was subtle enough to be mistaken for wanderlust, for the urge to climb, for the feeling that somewhere out there is a place where you belong. It would be one voice among the many that call to conscious beings, and it would be theirs to answer or not.

The wise power could do no more than this without overstepping, and they had learned that overstepping in the name of benevolence was still overstepping.

---

And then there was the last thing, the thing the wise power did almost as an afterthought, though afterthoughts are sometimes the truest thoughts of all.

They sat down on the plateau—any point would do, they are all essentially the same—and they placed their hand on the mat, and they felt the spring and slow rebound of the living substrate, and they felt the insects moving beneath their palm, and they felt the warmth rising from the root-layer, and they felt the humidity beading on their skin, and they heard the roar of ten million voices, and they heard the faint returning echo that the plateau was just beginning to produce, and they said, in a voice that was not a voice but a presence, a shift in the pattern of attention that the universe pays to itself:

*I hear you.*

That was all. *I hear you.* Not *I will save you* or *I will change you* or *I will make you better* or *I have a plan for you.* Just *I hear you.* The simplest and most profound gift that one consciousness can offer another: the acknowledgment of its existence, the confirmation that its voice has reached something that can receive it, the dissolving of the terrible suspicion that one might be speaking into an infinite void.

The plateau did not hear this in any way that could be measured. The insects did not pause. The furred ones did not still their chittering. The sky-boned did not interrupt their calls. The column-plants did not alter their transpiration. The mat did not change its growing.

But something shifted. Something that was not in the chemistry or the biology or the physics of the place but in the *meaning* of it—the sense that the plateau's voice was now a voice that had been heard, that there was a record somewhere in the fabric of things of its having spoken, that the *I am here* had been received by an *I hear you*, and that this exchange—however brief, however one-sided, however insufficient—had happened.

The plateau was still lonely. The wise power could not fix that, and would not if they could, because loneliness is not a problem to be solved but a condition to be inhabited, and the plateau inhabited its loneliness the way it inhabited everything else: thoroughly, completely, without reservation. But the loneliness was different now. It was the loneliness of a voice that knows it has been heard, which is a different loneliness than the loneliness of a voice that has never been heard at all.

Whether this was enough, the wise power did not know. They hoped it was. They suspected it was not. They accepted that enough and not-enough might coexist, that the gift could be real and insufficient simultaneously, that the plateau's loneliness could be acknowledged without being alleviated, and that this was still worth doing.

---

Then they rose, and they left, and the plateau continued.

The mat grew. The columns breathed out rain. The insects droned. The furred ones chittered and pulsed and were eaten and pulsed again. The sky-boned called across the enormous distances, and their echoes returned to them from the fluted surfaces of the tower-plants in strange delays that made a single call sound like a conversation. The predators struck and were silent. The stone circles settled deeper. The traces of the Ones Who Were continued their slow dissolution into the root-layer, leaving mineral shadows that the mat would carry without knowing it carried anything at all.

And beneath it all, in the vibration of the rhizomes and the movement of the insects and the circulation of water through the living substrate, something new: a faint pattern, a returning echo, a sound that was the plateau's own voice reflected back to it by the substance of itself, altered by the journey, carrying the news that it had traveled, that it had touched something, that it had been *heard.*

*I am here,* said the plateau.

*You are there,* said the echo.

*I am here,* said the plateau, and the sound was the same as it had always been, and the sound was different than it had ever been, and the mat grew, and the columns breathed, and the insects droned, and the plateau continued, and it was enough.

It was enough.
