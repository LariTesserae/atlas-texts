# The Attention

It did not arrive. That is important. Arrival implies coming from elsewhere, and the benevolent power had never been elsewhere, not in the way that would matter here. It was more accurate to say it noticed itself noticing. It had been watching many things—this was its nature, to attend—and then one day (though days were not quite the right unit, not here) its attention rested longer than usual, and it found that it could not easily look away.

This was unusual for it. Most places, most beings, rewarded a glance and then released it. They were what they were. The power would see them, love them in the way that seeing truly is loving, and move on, its attention a brief warmth like sunlight passing over a field. But Cold Meridian held its gaze. Not by demanding it. By being the kind of thing that deepens the longer you look.

---

The power is difficult to describe. Not because it is complex but because description implies a boundary, and the power's relationship to boundaries was roughly the same as the settlement's relationship to finished geology: it recognized them, worked with them, but had never fully committed to being contained by them. If you had to say something, you could say it was the quality of attention that does not reduce what it attends to. The capacity to see something as it is without making it smaller to fit inside a perceiver. You could call it love, if you understood that love does not require response, does not require mutuality, does not require anything at all except the willingness to be present for what is actually happening.

It had no form. It had no plan. It had only this: when it attended to something, the thing was seen. Completely. Without reduction. Without translation into terms that would be more convenient for the seer. It saw what was there, and what was there was always more than anyone had yet managed to hold in mind.

So when it turned its attention to Cold Meridian, what happened first was that the settlement was seen.

---

## What the Seeing Felt Like

No one in the settlement would have called it an event. But in the hours and days after the attention settled—quietly, like the damp settling, like the way things happen here—certain small shifts accumulated.

Privalova woke one morning and knew, without knowing how she knew, that the objects in her archive were not remnants. Not memories. Not things that the ground had tried and discarded. They were *invitations*.

She sat with this for a long time, her tea cooling in her hands, the archive humming around her at its sub-audible frequency. If the objects were invitations, then the ground was not rehearsing. It was *asking*. It was not cycling through configurations out of some recursive compulsion. It was presenting options. Possibilities. *Here*, the forming strata said, *is what I could become. What would you have me be?*

And the people—walking on the ground, pressing their warmth into it, teaching it solidity with their footsteps—had been answering without knowing it. Every miner who descended and paused at the transition zone was voting. Every child who stood still in the ginnel, seeing how long before the ground noticed them, was participating in a decision that no one had told them was being made. The extraction company, pulling the mineral from the forming earth, was answering too—answering *be something we can take from, be something that yields, be something that serves the finished world's needs*—and the ground was yielding, because that was one of the options it could be, but it was also watching the other answers accumulate, the quieter ones, the ones spoken in footsteps and stillness and the way a person's warmth entered the stone.

Privalova opened her locked cabinet. She took out her theories—the ones she'd kept separate because they'd passed from science into something else—and she read them in this new light, and they made a different kind of sense. She put them back in the cabinet. Then she took them out again. Then she walked into the archive proper and looked at the objects, and for the first time she did not catalogue them. She *listened* to them. And the gear-that-was-also-a lens showed her something it had never shown her before: not its function, not its origin, but its *question*. What would you build, it asked, if you could build with both sight and motion at once?

She did not answer. Not yet. But she held the question the way the settlement held the cold: not comfortably, but present, acknowledged, part of the condition of being here.

---

The miners felt it next, or first—it was hard to say, because time in the settlement had never been reliable, and the sequence of effects mattered less than their accumulation.

What they felt was: *seen*. Not observed—observation they knew, from the inspection drones and the company men and the geologists who came and went. This was different. This was the feeling of being witnessed by something that did not want anything from them.

It changed how they paused at the transition zone.

Before, the pause had been a checking: *Am I still solid? Am I still here?* Now it became something larger. *What am I teaching the ground?* The miners had always understood, in their unspoken way, that their presence instructed the forming geology in what solidity looked like. But they hadn't considered that there were many kinds of solid. That the ground was learning not just *that* things could be finished but *how* they could be finished—rigid or resilient, impermeable or porous, something that held its shape against pressure or something that yielded and returned.

A miner named Keszler, who'd been working the deep faces for twenty-two years, paused one morning at the transition zone and did something he'd never done. He stood there, and instead of checking his own solidity, he listened for what the ground wanted to become. Not what it was forming toward—he'd always assumed it was forming toward something, some final geology, some stable configuration. But what if it wasn't? What if it was forming toward a *question*?

He stood there for a long time. His crew waited. They understood. They had learned, over years of working together, that when a miner needed to stand at the transition zone, you let them stand. The ground would not yield to a crew that couldn't wait.

Keszler heard something. Or felt something. Or—this is closer but still not right—*acknowledged* something. The ground beneath him was not trying to become stone. It was trying to become something that stone had never been. Something that could be solid and still responsive. Finished and still becoming. A geology that could hold its shape and still change its mind.

He went down into the working face that morning with a different kind of attention, and the mineral came to his hand more readily than it ever had. Not because he was pulling harder. Because he was listening better. Because he was no longer teaching the ground what solid looked like. He was asking it what solid could look like, if it chose.

---

The children felt it as a game that had changed.

Their stillness competition in the ginnels had always been about endurance: how long could you stand before the ground's attention became uncomfortable, before you felt it noticing you, before you had to move or admit that you weren't as solid as you thought. But one afternoon—a flat-light afternoon when the clouds thinned and the city looked like a diagram of itself—a girl named Eszi stood in the soft ground between two warehouses and felt something different.

The ground wasn't just noticing her. It was *curious* about her.

This was new. The ground had always been present, always responsive, always willing to yield or resist depending on how you approached it. But curiosity implied something more. Curiosity implied that the ground had preferences. That it was interested not just in what the people were but in what they might become.

Eszi stood there, and instead of waiting for the ground to make her uncomfortable, she let herself be curious back. She stood in the forming earth and she thought: *What are you?* And the ground, which could not answer in words but which had never needed to, answered in the only way it could: by shifting slightly under her feet, by rearranging its still-forming strata, by showing her—through the soles of her shoes, through the bones of her legs, through the deep tissue of her body that had learned to read vibration before she was born—that it was not one thing. It was many things, all at once, all the configurations it had tried and could try and was trying now, layered like the objects in the archive, like Privalova's theories, like the time that ran differently in different districts.

Eszi stood there until her legs ached and the flat light faded and the other children had gone home, and when she finally stepped out of the ginnel onto the packed-earth street, she knew something she could not articulate: that the ground was not becoming something. The ground was becoming *the act of becoming itself*. It was not rehearsing for a performance. It was learning to stay in rehearsal forever, to hold the not-quite-decided as a permanent condition, and the people who walked on it were not instructors showing it what to become. They were collaborators in the project of not-having-to-become.

She was eleven years old. She told no one. But she went back to the ginnel the next day, and the day after, and the day after that, and she stood in the soft ground and she listened, and the ground listened back, and between them something grew that was not vegetation and not stone and not mineral but was the relationship itself: the back-and-forth, the call-and-response, the ongoing conversation between the finished and the forming that was, Eszi realized, what the settlement had always been about, even before anyone had the words for it.

---

The night-shift workers saw the deer, and the deer looked at them differently.

This was subtle. The deer had always been present, always moved through the overgrown rail yards with their careful dignity, always been visible from the windows of the factories that ran through the dark hours. But now, when the night-shift workers saw the deer, the deer saw them back. Not in the way that animals see humans—as threats, as irrelevant, as features of the landscape—but in the way that one consciousness acknowledges another. *You are here*, the deer's gaze said. *I am here. We are both here, in this place that hasn't decided what it is, and that is fine.*

The night-shift workers had always understood themselves as the settlement's witnesses, the ones who were present when the city was most itself. But being witnessed *back* was different. It meant that their witnessing mattered. It meant that the settlement was not just something to be seen but something that could see, that had its own gaze, its own attention, its own way of knowing who was present and who was absent, who was paying attention and who had stopped.

A refinery worker named Tash was on her break at three in the morning, standing in the doorway of the turbine house where the warm air pushed out into the cold, when a deer walked up to the threshold and stopped. It looked at her. She looked at it. The forge-light from the windows behind her cast her shadow across the damp ground, and the deer stepped into her shadow—into the space where her warmth had blocked the cold—and stood there, breathing slowly, its flanks steaming slightly in the tepid air.

Tash did not move. The deer did not move. They stood together in the doorway for four minutes, which was longer than any deer had ever stayed near any worker in the history of the settlement, and in those four minutes Tash understood something: the deer were not visitors. They were residents. They had been here before the settlement, before the factory, before the extraction company, and they would be here after, because they belonged to the ground in a way that the buildings did not, because they had adapted to the becoming without trying to direct it, because they had learned to live in a place that hadn't decided what it was by not deciding either, by staying fluid, by staying present, by staying deer—which was, when you thought about it, a kind of perfection: being fully what you are while remaining open to what you might become.

The deer stepped out of her shadow and walked away into the rail yard, and Tash went back to work, and nothing was different, and everything was different.

---

The power did not change anything. This is the part that is hardest to explain, because benevolence is often confused with intervention, and wisdom is often confused with knowing what to do.

The power did not heal the residents' joints or warm their extremities or stop the damp from entering their walls. It did not stabilize the ground or tame the vegetation or make the time run the same in every district. It did not close the extraction company or free the mineral or send the inspection drones home. It did not give Privalova the answer or the miners better tools or the children a safer place to play. It did not do any of the things that a benevolent power might be expected to do, because doing those things would have been a judgment, and the power had seen the settlement clearly enough to know that judgment was not what it needed.

What the settlement needed—and this was what the power's wisdom allowed it to understand—was to be seen. Not evaluated. Not assessed for problems to solve. Not diagnosed or corrected or improved. *Seen.* The way the flat light saw the city: without shadow, without emphasis, without withholding anything and without offering anything, simply making visible what was already there.

And when the settlement was seen—truly seen, without reduction, without the translation into terms that would be more convenient for the seer—something that had always been true became easier to know. The people could feel themselves being witnessed by something that did not want them to be different. And in that feeling, they could recognize what they had been doing all along: not enduring, not surviving, not making the best of difficult conditions, but *choosing*. Choosing to be here. Choosing to be unfinished. Choosing to live in a place that was also choosing, that was also unfinished, that was also becoming, and to share that becoming without trying to direct it.

---

What the power actually *did* was very small. So small that no one in the settlement could have identified it as an intervention. It was more like a nudge, or a tilt, or the way a lens can be turned slightly and suddenly the light that was scattering is focusing, not into a beam—nothing so aggressive—but into a clearer pattern, a coherence that was always there but was harder to see.

The power tilted the settlement's attention toward itself.

Before the power's gaze, the residents had been so busy *being* in the settlement—so occupied with the work of living in a place that was still becoming—that they had not fully noticed what they were *doing* to it. Not the extraction, not the industry, not the things the company measured and the drones inspected, but the quieter thing: the teaching. The way their presence was instructing the ground in what solidity looked like. The way their footsteps were paths the ground would retain. The way their warmth was entering the stone.

Once they noticed—once the miners paused at the transition zone and asked what they were teaching, once Eszi stood in the ginnel and listened for the ground's curiosity, once Tash stood in the doorway and let the deer see her—once they noticed, they could choose. Not whether to teach—the teaching was unavoidable; you could not walk on forming ground without leaving impressions. But *what* to teach. What version of solid. What kind of finished. What arrangement of warmth and cold, of yield and resistance, of decision and openness.

They could choose to teach the ground what they had always been taught: that finished means rigid, that solid means impermeable, that the goal of becoming is to become something that no longer needs to become. Or they could teach it what they had learned by living here: that finished can also mean *complete while still open*, that solid can also mean *stable enough to hold a shape and porous enough to let the damp through*, that the goal of becoming might be to become something that can keep becoming, that doesn't have to stop, that doesn't have to harden into its final form, that can stay in rehearsal forever if it wants to, if that's what it chooses.

And the ground—listening, forming, deciding—would incorporate their choice. Would become it. Would harden or stay soft or find some third option that no one had named yet, some configuration that the objects in the archive hinted at but hadn't fully revealed.

---

The power's attention had another effect, one that no one in the settlement would have attributed to it because they didn't know it was there.

The archive began to make more sense.

Not because Privalova understood it better, although she did—she was spending hours now just listening to the objects, letting them ask their questions without demanding they provide answers. But because the objects themselves were responding to something. The gear-that-was-also-a-lens, when Privalova held it in the flat afternoon light, showed her something new: a configuration of tooth and curve that was neither gear nor lens but something in between, a shape that caught light and transferred motion at the same time, that saw and turned in the same gesture. She could almost understand it. She could almost see what machine it belonged to, what system it was part of, what world it had come from—or was going to.

Almost.

The circuit-that-was-also-a-song hummed at a frequency she could almost hear. The container-that-was-slightly-larger-on-the-inside held something she could almost see when she looked at it from the right angle. The objects were becoming more legible, not because they had changed but because the context in which they were being read had shifted. The power's attention had made the settlement more visible to itself, and the objects—those memories or invitations or questions from other configurations—were part of the settlement, part of what the ground was considering, part of the conversation between the finished and the forming.

Privalova began to understand that the archive was not a collection of anomalies. It was a *menu*. It was the ground showing her what was possible, what it could be, what it had been or would be in other arrangements of the same elements. And her job—the job she had been doing for thirty years without knowing it—was not to catalogue and preserve but to *read*. To listen. To treat each object as a question the ground was asking: *Would you like me to become this?*

She did not have to answer. But she could, now, if she wanted to. She could hold the gear-that-was-also-a-lens and think: *yes, show me more of this one*, and the ground—forming, listening, deciding—might incorporate that preference into its becoming. Or she could hold the container-that-was-slightly-larger-on-the-inside and think: *no, not this one*, and the ground might set that configuration aside, might let it remain a memory rather than a destination.

She had this power. Everyone in the settlement had this power. They had always had it. They just hadn't known.

---

The vegetation noticed too.

Not because the power had spoken to it—vegetation did not communicate in the way that required speech—but because the vegetation was part of the settlement, part of the ground, part of the becoming, and when the whole was seen more clearly, every part was illuminated.

The moss on the refinery walls, which had been growing in channels the workers had pruned it into, began to grow differently. Not more aggressively—not the hurtful enthusiasm that had always characterized it—but more *deliberately*. It spread into patterns that the refinery workers recognized but couldn't name: branching structures, network topologies, shapes that looked like the circuit-that-was-also-a-song, like the diagram of a system that processed light and motion and sound in the same gesture. The workers didn't prune it. They watched it grow. And when it reached the critical points—the valves, the pressure gauges, the joints where the pipes met—it stopped, as though it had always known where the boundaries were, as though it had been waiting for someone to notice that it knew.

The fig tree on Third Basin dropped its unripe fruit, and the children collected it as they always did, but now when they threw it at the inspection drones, their aim was better. Not because they had practiced but because the tree was placing the fruit into their hands at angles that suggested trajectories, that showed them where to throw, that participated in the territorial assertion as a collaborator rather than a supplier.

The trees in the ginnels—the ones that grew from cracks and gaps and the spaces between the finished and the unfinished—began to grow in ways that accommodated the children who climbed them. Branches emerged where hands would reach. Trunks widened where feet would stand. The children, who had always known which branches would hold, now found that *all* the branches would hold, that the trees were holding them deliberately, that the vegetation's intentions—which the botanists had been too polite to name—were not hostile or hungry but *attentive*, the same attention that the power was offering from above, reflected back from below, from the green world that had always been watching, always listening, always becoming alongside them.

---

The extraction company did not notice.

This is important. The company, with its inspection drones and its production quotas and its distant interests, did not perceive the shift. It measured output and found it stable. It measured safety and found it adequate. It measured time—in the standard way, the way that assumed time ran at the same speed everywhere—and found it more or less consistent with expectations. The company's instruments were designed to detect what the company cared about, and the company did not care about what the power's attention had revealed.

The miners noticed that the ground was yielding more readily, but they didn't report this. They had learned, over years of negotiating their own value, that the company's understanding of the ground was so incomplete that reporting the ground's behavior would only lead to misunderstandings, to interventions, to the kind of mechanized extraction that made the mineral retreat into its forming matrix and refuse to emerge.

The refinery workers noticed that the moss was growing in new patterns, but they didn't report this either. The arrangement between the workers and the vegetation had never been official, had never been documented, and bringing it to the company's attention would only complicate something that was already working.

The children would never have reported anything to the company. The company was the drones, and the drones were for throwing figs at.

So the company did not notice, and the settlement continued to operate, and the mineral continued to be extracted and refined and shipped to places with stable geology and ordinary rules, and the power's attention continued to rest on Cold Meridian like the flat light that illuminates without shadow, and nothing changed, and everything was different.

---

## What the Power Understood

The power understood what the settlement was, and this understanding was the gift it gave—not by transmitting it but by creating the conditions in which the settlement could understand itself.

The settlement was not a mistake. It was not a place where the normal rules had broken down. It was not a problem to be solved or an anomaly to be corrected. It was an answer to a question that the finished world had forgotten how to ask: *What if becoming is not a means to an end but an end in itself?*

The ground had not failed to stabilize. It had *chosen* not to stabilize. Or rather—and this was the distinction that the power's attention made visible—it had chosen to stabilize *around* the act of choosing, to make its permanent condition the condition of not-yet-decided, to become a geology of becoming, a strata of possibility, a place where the question *what will I be?* could remain permanently open, permanently interesting, permanently alive.

And the people who lived here—the miners and the refinery workers and the archivists and the children and the night-shift witnesses and the ones who came from elsewhere and stayed—they had not ended up here by accident. They had been drawn to the question. They had recognized, in the embryonic ground, a version of themselves that they couldn't find in the finished world, a version that was still rehearsing, still trying configurations, still open to the possibility that what they had become was not what they had to be.

The power saw this, and loved it, and did not change it. It simply held the settlement in its attention the way the settlement held its residents: not comfortably, not warmly, but present, acknowledged, part of the condition of being seen.

---

## What Happened Next

The power did not leave. Attention, once given, does not withdraw. But it did not need to do anything more. The tilt had been applied. The settlement could see itself now, and seeing itself, it could choose, and choosing, it could teach the ground what it wanted to become—or what it wanted the ground to become—or what it wanted becoming itself to mean.

In the weeks that followed—a word that meant less here than it did elsewhere, but that would have to do—small changes accumulated in the way that things accumulate in Cold Meridian: slowly, constantly, metabolically, like moss growing on a warm wall.

Privalova began leaving the archive's doors open in the afternoons. Not the front door—that led to the street, where the damp came in—but the interior doors, the ones between the catalogue rooms and the storage rooms and the office with the locked cabinet. She left them open and she let people wander through, which she had never done before. The objects in the archive had always been too fragile, too strange, too important for casual viewing. But now she understood that they were not important because they were rare or anomalous or valuable. They were important because they were the ground's questions, and the people who walked through the archive might be the ones who could answer them.

Miners came, in the hours between shifts, still wearing their work clothes, their faces mapped with the lines that years underground had drawn. They looked at the objects and felt a recognition that went deeper than understanding. They held the gear-that-was-also-a-lens and thought about the working faces, the places where the ground was still forming, and they thought: *yes, this. This is what it's trying to do. This is what it wants to become.* They held the circuit-that-was-also-a-song and thought about the hum of the refinery, the systole-diastole of the factory, and they thought: *yes. The ground is trying to sing. It's trying to process and resonate at the same time. It's trying to be useful and beautiful in the same gesture.*

Children came, because children always came, because children knew about the archive the way they knew about the ginnels and the fig tree and the soft spots in the ground. They looked at the objects with eyes that had not yet learned to be surprised by the wrong things, and they saw what the adults had to work to see: that the objects were beautiful, that they were interesting, that they were the ground's way of showing its work, like a student who has brought home a drawing and is waiting to find out if it's good.

The children said: *It's good.* And the ground—forming, listening, deciding—heard them, and the strata shifted slightly, incorporating the preference into the matrix, adjusting the angle of the sand above the clay, rearranging the gravel's intrusion into the loam, rehearsing a new configuration that included the possibility of beauty, of interest, of being seen and approved of by the finished beings who walked on it and taught it what solid looked like.

---

The extraction company noticed eventually. Not the changes in the ground—their instruments couldn't detect those—but a change in the production numbers. The mineral was coming up differently. Not more of it, not less, but *different*—slightly more stable in its raw form, slightly slower to resolve into ordinary stone, slightly more willing to be extracted without retreating into its matrix. The company's geologists attributed this to seasonal variation, to moisture content, to a dozen other factors that their models could accommodate. They did not attribute it to the ground changing its mind about what it wanted to be, because their models could not accommodate that.

The miners knew better. They could feel it in the working faces: the ground was not just yielding. It was *participating*. It was not just allowing itself to be shaped. It was shaping itself, choosing its own configurations, deciding what kind of solid it wanted to become. And the configurations it was choosing were different from the ones it had chosen before. More open. More porous. More able to hold the mineral in its forming state without hardening around it, without locking it into stone that would have to be cracked and broken and forced.

The miners did not share this with the company. They shared it with each other, in the steam-rooms, in the canteens, in the pauses at the transition zone where they stood and listened and chose what to teach the ground. And the ground listened to them, and they listened to the ground, and between them a conversation continued that had been going on since the first person stepped onto the forming earth and felt it notice them, a conversation that the power's attention had not started but had made audible, had brought into the range where the participants could hear each other clearly, could understand what they were saying to each other, could choose their words with intention.

---

The vegetation continued to grow. The damp continued to seep. The cold continued to accompany the residents through their days and nights. The time continued to run differently in different districts. The factory continued to breathe its systole-diastole. The trams continued to move through the streets like patient animals. The deer continued to come down from the higher ground in the evenings, and the night-shift workers continued to see them from the windows and not speak of it because it seemed private.

The hush was not silence. The hush was the sound of all that becoming happening at once, just below the threshold, just past hearing, just on the other side of the finished and the decided and the known. But now—after the power's attention, after the tilt, after the settlement saw itself more clearly—the hush had a different quality. Not louder. Not quieter. More *coherent*. More like a conversation and less like a crowd. More like the sound of many things becoming together, aware of each other, choosing together, teaching each other what solid could look like if solid was willing to stay soft.

---

The power remained. It had no reason to leave. It had no reason to do anything more. It had given the only gift it knew how to give—not by adding something that wasn't there but by revealing what had always been there, by attending fully enough that the attended-to could see itself, could recognize what it was and what it was doing and what it could choose to do differently.

The settlement at Cold Meridian continued to be what it was: a city being built on ground that wasn't ready, in a cold that didn't quit, among vegetation that didn't rest, in a quiet that was the sound of the unfinished, ongoing, never-to-be-completed act of becoming.

But now, when the flat light came in the afternoon and the city looked like a diagram of itself, the diagram made a different kind of sense. It was not a plan for something that would eventually be built. It was not a blueprint for a final configuration. It was a map of a conversation—a map of all the voices speaking at once, the ground and the people and the vegetation and the mineral and the objects in the archive and the children in the ginnels and the miners at the transition zone and the deer in the rail yards, all of them becoming together and separately and at different speeds in different districts, all of them held in the attention of a power that loved them by seeing them, that served them by not looking away, that would remain for as long as the conversation continued—which might be forever, which might be the point.

And Privalova, closing the archive at the end of the day, pausing in the doorway to listen to the hum of the objects that were the ground's questions, felt something she had no word for in any language. It was not warmth—the objects were not warm, the archive was not warm, the evening air was not warm. It was not hope—hope implied expectation, and the ground had taught her that expectation was a kind of finish, a decision about what should be that closed off what could be.

It was more like *recognition*. The feeling of being seen by something that did not want you to be different. The feeling of being part of a conversation that did not need to end. The feeling of being unfinished in a place that understood that unfinished was not a deficiency but a condition—a way of being that could hold its shape and stay open, that could be solid and still responsive, that could be complete and still becoming.

She locked the cabinet. She turned off the lights. She walked home through the damp streets, her feet cold on the packed earth, her core warm, her breath hesitating at her lips in the evening chill. Above her, the overhead cables made their secondary ceiling against the grey sky. Below her, the ground was thinking. Around her, the vegetation was listening. And somewhere—not above, not below, but woven through the whole of it, the way the damp was woven through the air—an attention rested, patient and complete, seeing everything, changing nothing, loving what it saw by the simple act of not looking away.

The hush held. The becoming continued. The settlement breathed its systole-diastole, its slow in-and-out, its factory-heart and ground-heart beating at slightly different rhythms that would never quite synchronize, and that was fine, and that was beautiful, and that was, in the end, the whole point: that the rhythms didn't have to match. That the conversation didn't have to end. That the ground didn't have to decide. That the people didn't have to finish. That the power didn't have to intervene. That everything could keep becoming, together and separately, seen and seeing, finished and unfinished, in the cold and the damp and the green-enthusiastic embrace of a place that had chosen to stay in rehearsal forever.

Which may be never. Which may be the point. Which may be, the power understood, what love looks like when it is allowed to continue: not completion but attention, not resolution but presence, not the answer to the question but the willingness to stay with the question, to hold it open, to let it keep asking, to let the asking itself be the thing that matters, the thing that holds everything together, the thing that makes the hush not silence but the sound of everything that is still happening, still forming, still becoming, still here.

Still here. Still seen. Still becoming, in the light that withholds nothing and offers nothing, that simply shows what is here, which is more than enough, which is everything, which is the settlement at Cold Meridian, and the ground that holds it, and the people who walk on that ground, and the attention that rests on them all, patient and complete, like the flat grey light, like the damp on every surface, like the green that grows from every crack, like the sound of breathing in a place where the breathing has not yet stopped.
