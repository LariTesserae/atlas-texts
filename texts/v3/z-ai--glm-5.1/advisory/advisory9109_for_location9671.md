# Preparatory Briefing: The Plateau of Congregations

---

## I. What This Place Will Do to Your Body

You will be wet. Not momentarily wet, not caught-in-the-rain wet, but *constitutionally* wet—wet the way a cave is wet, wet from the outside in and the inside out, until the boundary between your skin and the atmosphere becomes a negotiation rather than a fact. The plateau's air does not merely contain moisture; it insists upon it. It will find every exposed surface and coat it. It will find the gaps in your clothing, the creases behind your knees, the fold of your eyelids, and it will leave its warmth there like a claim.

Prepare for this. Pack nothing that cannot tolerate perpetual dampness. Leave behind leather, untreated cotton, anything that mildews or stiffens when wet. Your clothing should be synthetic or synthetic-blend—materials that drain rather than absorb, that dry in the brief intervals between wettings. Layer lightly. The warmth rises from below, constant and humid, and you will never be cold, but you may be *clammy*, which is its own kind of discomfort. A traveler in heavy garments will cook slowly in their own perspiration.

Your footwear matters more than anything else you will bring. Every surface on the plateau is wet. Every horizontal surface holds a skin of water. Every vertical surface sheds water in slow rivulets. The stone is worn smooth by generations of feet and mandibles and dripping water, and it is slick in a way that becomes dangerous on stairs, on ramps, on the slight inclines of the older streets where the paving has settled into gentle slopes. Bring boots with aggressive treads. Bring two pairs if you can carry them, because one pair will never fully dry, and the sensation of putting on damp boots every morning will, over time, erode something in your spirit. Accept this. It is a small erosion compared to the erosions the plateau itself has survived.

Bring gloves. Not for warmth—for contact. You will touch walls without thinking about it, because the walls are everywhere and the corridors are narrow and your hand will reach out for balance the way hands do. The mineral deposits on the walls—the white and orange and rust-colored growths—will come off on your skin. They are not dangerous, exactly, but they are persistent. They smell of iron and sulfur and something faintly organic, and they will stain your fingertips, and after a day of walking the plateau your hands will look as though you have been digging in wet earth. Gloves also protect you from the wall-shimmerers—the near-microscopic creatures that live in the condensation on vertical surfaces. They will not harm you, but the sensation of pressing your hand to a wall and feeling it pulse with a warmth that is not your own is, for many travelers, deeply unsettling. Gloves provide a small barrier between your nervous system and the plateau's.

## II. What This Place Will Do to Your Sleep

You will not sleep well at first. This is not a warning; it is a schedule.

The plateau does not observe silence. It observes *rhythm*—the bass thrum of the engines, the drip of water, the clicking of mandibles, the slow processes of the congregations—and this rhythm does not pause for night because night on the plateau is not a cessation but a deepening. The amber lights come on. The clicking intensifies. The carapace laborers continue their processions under the glow, and their shells against the wet stone produce a sound like a hundred small clocks being wound at once, and this sound will follow you into whatever room you have found, whatever bed you have made, whatever corner you have claimed for rest.

Earplugs will help, but they will not solve the problem, because the plateau's sound is not merely acoustic. It is *vibrational*. The engines below transmit their rhythm through the floors and walls and into your body, and you will feel it in your chest and in the base of your skull, and after a few nights you will realize that the sound you have been trying to block is also a kind of company. You are inside something alive. It makes noise because it is alive. The noise is its heartbeat.

My advice: do not fight it. Do not lie in the dark willing the sound to stop. Instead, listen for the layers. Separate them. The engine. The water. The clicking. The distant, muffled sound of a congregation shifting in unison somewhere in the city. When you can hear the individual threads, the composite becomes less overwhelming, and eventually—after a week, perhaps two—it becomes the quietest kind of quiet: the quiet of a sound so constant your mind stops registering it as information and starts registering it as *context*. You will know you have adapted when you leave the plateau and cannot sleep because the silence is too loud.

Bring a sleep mask if you are sensitive to light. The amber glow that suffuses the streets at night will find its way through most curtains, and the mineral deposits on the walls hold luminescence the way a hand holds water—briefly, but long enough to cast a faint glow across any room. And accept that your sleep will be divided. The plateau's residents do not sleep in long stretches. They sleep in shifts, matching their rest to the cycles of the engines rather than the position of the sun, and you will find it easier to do the same. A few hours after the engines cycle down. A few hours after they cycle up. Naps. Dozing. The plateau does not demand alertness from you, but it does not facilitate unconsciousness either. Meet it halfway.

## III. How to Move Through the City

Walk slowly. I mean this literally and metaphorically and spiritually. Walk slowly.

The plateau rewards patience and punishes haste. The streets are narrow and wet and crowded—not with people, particularly, but with the fauna, with the laborers and their processions, with the ordinary business of a place that has been conducting its ordinary business for longer than anyone can remember. If you walk quickly you will stumble on the slick stone. You will collide with a carapace laborer. You will step into the residue of a congregation.

The laborers are the first lesson the plateau teaches. They are large—dog-sized, segmented, slow—and they move along routes so old the stone is grooved beneath their feet. They do not stop for you. They do not go around you. They wait, with a patience that is not passive but *geological*, the patience of water wearing through rock, the patience of the engines running cycle after cycle after cycle until the concept of stopping becomes meaningless. When you encounter a laborer on a narrow street, you will step aside. Not because the laborer demands it. Not because anyone will punish you if you do not. But because the laborer will wait longer than you will. It has been walking this route since before you arrived and it will be walking it after you leave, and in the contest between your urgency and its constancy, its constancy wins every time.

This is the plateau's philosophy in miniature. Everything here has been happening for a very long time. Everything here will continue happening for a very long time. You are passing through. Act like it.

When you approach a congregation, stop. Not because of danger—the creatures are not aggressive, do not bite, do not swarm—but because the congregation is a ceremony you do not understand. The creatures arrange themselves in patterns—circles, spirals, rows—and they click to one another in overlapping frequencies, and they move in unison with a precision that suggests purpose, and that purpose is none of your business. Stand at the edge. Watch if you want to. Listen if you want to. But do not step into the formation. Do not touch the creatures. Do not try to disrupt the pattern or redirect the movement. The people of the plateau have learned this the way they have learned everything: through the long, slow accumulation of experience. The congregations are not hostile. They are *indifferent*, and the plateau's indifference is vast enough to include you without ever acknowledging you.

After a congregation disperses, the ground where they stood will be marked with a residue—chemical, organic, mineral—that stains the floor in the exact pattern of their arrangement. Do not step in the residue if you can avoid it. It is not toxic, but it is persistent. It will adhere to the soles of your boots and track through the streets, and the slight stickiness of it will attract the smaller fauna—the grain-sized organisms that live in the wet surfaces—and you will find, after a day of walking through old congregation sites, that your footsteps have developed a faint, iridescent shimmer, as though you are leaving tracks made of living things.

This is not dangerous. But it is a reminder that on the plateau, the boundary between the living and the built is not a boundary at all.

## IV. What to Eat, What to Drink, What to Breathe

The plateau is a closed system. It has been a closed system for so long that it has reached equilibrium—producing what it consumes, consuming what it produces, maintaining itself in a slow, steady state that resembles stillness from the outside but is, in fact, a very slow kind of motion. This means the food grown here is grown in soil made from the plateau's own waste, fertilized by the byproducts of the engines and the congregations, watered by the condensation and the seeping aquifer. It is safe. The people who live here eat it every day. But it tastes of the plateau—of iron and sulfur and the faint organic sweetness of the creatures' residue—and if you are sensitive to mineral flavors, you will find it heavy going.

Bring what food you can carry. Dried goods, sealed containers, anything that will survive the humidity without spoiling. The plateau's markets sell food, and you should buy some—a loaf of the dense, dark bread, a handful of the small, hard fruits that grow in the upper galleries where the light is stronger—but do not rely on local provisions for your entire stay. Your body is not adapted to this place, and the plateau's food is, in a subtle way, *of* the plateau in a way that your body may resist.

Water is everywhere, but not all of it is for drinking. The water that seeps from the walls is pressurized from the aquifer below, and it is warm and mineral-heavy and tastes of the deep earth. The people drink it. You can drink it. But filter it if you have the means—there are organisms in it, the smallest fauna, the ones that shimmer on the walls, and while they are not harmful to the plateau's residents, your gut is not the plateau's residents' gut, and the result may be several days of profound intestinal consequence that will make the rest of your visit extremely memorable for the wrong reasons.

The rain is another matter. It falls warm and sudden, and it smells of sulfur and iron and the organic byproducts of the engines' exhaust, and it should not be collected for drinking unless you have no alternative. The air itself is thick with moisture—on a humid day, you can wring water from a cloth left hanging overnight—and this atmospheric water is the cleanest you will find, if you can devise a way to collect it. A simple condensation trap—a sheet of waterproof material stretched above a collection vessel—will gather enough water for drinking, and it will taste of nothing but the plateau's atmosphere, which is to say it will taste of iron and warmth and the faintest hint of something alive.

Breathe through your nose for the first few days. The air is dense and wet and it hits the throat differently than dry air does—it feels heavier, more substantial, as though you are drinking the atmosphere rather than inhaling it. Your lungs will adjust. Your sinuses will adjust. But the adjustment takes time, and during that time you may feel a pressure in your chest, a fullness that is not constriction but *presence*—the air is inside you, and it is warm, and it is wet, and it is not the air you were made for. This passes. Like everything on the plateau, it passes slowly.

## V. What to Bring, What to Leave Behind

**Bring:**

- **Treaded boots, two pairs.** Already discussed. Non-negotiable.
- **Synthetic, quick-drying clothing in layers.** You will add and remove layers throughout the day as you move between the warmer lower galleries and the cooler upper streets. Avoid cotton. Avoid wool unless treated. Accept that nothing will ever feel fully dry.
- **Gloves, thin and flexible.** For touching the city. For pressing your hands to walls and railings and the worn-smooth surfaces of a place that has been touched by millions before you.
- **A headlamp with a warm-tone setting.** The plateau's light is warm—amber filaments, mineral luminescence, filtered daylight—and the harsh blue-white of standard headlamps will make you conspicuous and, more importantly, will change the way the city looks. The plateau's aesthetics are inseparable from its lighting. Do not flatten them with LED daylight.
- **Earplugs, but use them sparingly.** For the first nights only. Then let the sound in.
- **A small notebook and waterproof writing implement.** You will want to record things. The patterns of congregations. The routes of laborers. The feel of the air at different levels. The plateau is dense with information, and memory is unreliable in places where the senses are overwhelmed.
- **Sealed food.** Already discussed.
- **A water filter.** Already discussed.
- **A wide-brimmed hat.** The warm rain falls without warning. An umbrella is useless—the streets are too narrow, the wind too unpredictable, and you will need your hands free for balance. A hat keeps the rain off your face and the condensation out of your eyes.
- **Patience.** This is not a glib recommendation. I mean it literally: bring patience, cultivate patience, practice patience before you arrive. The plateau moves at its own pace. The laborers will not hurry. The congregations will not explain themselves. The engines will not adjust their cycles for your convenience. If you are the kind of traveler who needs to move, to see, to conquer, to extract—you will be miserable here. The plateau does not yield to extraction. It simply continues, and you can either match its pace or spend your visit in a state of low-grade frustration that will color every experience.

**Leave behind:**

- **Insect repellent or any chemical designed to kill or deter arthropods.** The fauna are not pests. They are residents. They are infrastructure. Attempting to repel them is not only futile—there are too many, and they are too integrated into the city's fabric—it is a kind of violence that the plateau's human residents will not tolerate quietly. More importantly, it marks you as someone who has not understood where they are. The creatures will climb on you. They will move past you. They will, on occasion, investigate you with the same matter-of-fact curiosity with which they investigate everything else. Let them. They will lose interest.
- **Rigid itineraries.** You will not keep them. The plateau's rhythms will override yours, and you will find yourself standing in a street for twenty minutes because a congregation has formed across your path, or waiting at the edge of a procession because the laborers are moving slower than you anticipated, or sitting on a worn step because the rain has started and the streets have become mirrors and you cannot bring yourself to walk through something so beautiful.
- **Expectations of grandeur or revelation.** The plateau is not dramatic. It does not have vistas that take your breath away or moments of sudden, stunning beauty. It has *gradual* beauty—the beauty of accumulation, of slow processes, of things that become what they are through patient repetition. If you come looking for epiphany, you will be disappointed. If you come looking for *attention*, you will find it in the form of a slow, steady, overwhelming indifference that is, in its own way, more profound than any epiphany.
- **Weapons.** There is nothing to fight. The dangers of the plateau are not dangers you can stab or shoot. They are the dangers of dampness and patience and the slow realization that you are inside something so much larger than yourself that your presence is, at best, a minor chemical input in a system that has been running without you for a very long time.

## VI. The Psychological Preparation

This is the part that matters most, and the part that most travelers neglect.

The Plateau of Congregations will make you feel small. Not in the way a mountain makes you feel small—not through scale, through the drama of your own insignificance—but in the way a forest makes you feel small: by being *complete without you*. The plateau does not need you. It does not need any individual human. It has achieved a state of self-maintaining equilibrium that renders human presence optional, and the humans who live here know this. They have integrated themselves into the plateau's systems—maintaining the engines, directing the water, building and repairing with the materials the factories produce—but they are components, not operators. The plateau would continue without them. Slower, perhaps. With more breakdowns, more erosions. But it would continue.

This knowledge produces a particular kind of humility in the plateau's residents. It is not despair. It is not resignation. It is the quiet competence of people who understand that they are part of something rather than the masters of something, and who have made peace with that understanding. You will see it in the way they step around the laborers. In the way they wait for congregations to disperse. In the way they do not curse the rain or the damp or the constant noise, because the rain and the damp and the constant noise are *what this place is*, and wishing them away would be like wishing away the stone beneath your feet.

As a traveler, you will likely pass through several emotional stages:

**First: Overwhelm.** The sound, the wet, the creatures, the density of it all. The plateau hits the senses like a wall of warm water, and for the first day or two you will be processing more input than you can handle. This is normal. Find a small space—a room, a corner, a bench overlooking a lower gallery—and sit. Let the plateau happen around you. Do not try to understand it yet. Just let it be what it is.

**Second: Curiosity.** Once the overwhelm fades, you will start noticing details. The way the mineral deposits form fractal patterns on the walls. The way the congregations arrange themselves. The way the water finds its thousand paths down vertical surfaces. This is the plateau's gift to travelers: it rewards attention. There is always more to see. The city is so dense, so layered, so *old* that every surface contains information, and a curious traveler could spend years reading the walls like a text and never reach the end.

**Third: Discomfort.** This is the stage where most travelers either leave or commit to staying. The discomfort is not physical—by this point, you have adapted to the damp and the noise and the creatures. It is existential. The plateau's indifference begins to feel personal. The laborers ignore you. The congregations ignore you. The water ignores you. The engines run their cycles and the water finds its paths and the creatures go about their business, and none of it has anything to do with you, and the realization sets in that you could die here and the plateau would incorporate your remains into its mineral deposits and move on. This is true of most places, of course, but most places do not make you feel it in your bones the way the plateau does.

**Fourth: Acceptance.** If you stay long enough—and "long enough" varies by traveler—you will reach a point where the plateau's indifference stops feeling like rejection and starts feeling like *permission*. The plateau does not care what you do. It does not have expectations for you. It does not need you to be a certain way or achieve a certain thing. You are here, and then you will not be here, and both states are equally unremarkable to the plateau. This is not comforting in the traditional sense, but it is liberating. You can simply *be*—wet, warm, clicking along with the rest of the living things, a temporary pattern in a city of temporary patterns, leaving your faint stain on the stone before you go.

---

## VII. Final Notes

The plateau has no exit in the emotional sense. You will leave physically—you will descend from the high galleries, pass below the cloud line, return to whatever world you came from—but part of you will remain, pressed into the wet stone like a mineral deposit, a faint residue of warmth and motion that the wall-shimmerers will incorporate into their slow, iridescent lives. And part of the plateau will remain with you. The sound, especially. The composite hum of engines and water and clicking mandibles that becomes a kind of silence. You will miss it, in the way you miss a heartbeat when it falters—not because it was pleasant, but because it was *rhythm*, and rhythm is what makes a place feel like a body, and the Plateau of Congregations is, above all else, a body: vast and warm and wet and alive, breathing its slow breath, running its ancient cycles, maintaining itself with a patience that puts all human patience to shame.

Go there. Walk slowly. Touch the walls. Listen to the water. Step aside for the laborers. Wait for the congregations. Let the rain fall on your upturned face and taste the iron and sulfur and the faint organic sweetness of a world that has been here since before the word *world* meant anything, and will be here long after the word is forgotten.

And when you leave, do not look back. The plateau does not require your farewell. It requires nothing from you at all. That is, in the end, the most extraordinary thing about it.
