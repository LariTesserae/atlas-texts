# Preparation for The Shallow

---

## I. What You Must Understand Before You Go

The Shallow will not harm you. I want to say that first, because you need to hear it and because you will forget it. You will forget it because the place does not confirm your existence, and after enough hours in a place that does not confirm your existence, you will begin to invent dangers. You will mistake indifference for hostility. You will interpret the silence as concealment. You will feel the warmth radiating from the walls and think *something* put it there for a reason.

Nothing put it there. Nothing is waiting. The Shallow is not a riddle or a test or a threshold. It is a hollow in the world that has been settling into itself for longer than anything remembers, and your presence will not alter its settling by even the smallest fraction.

This is the hardest thing to prepare for: not the absence of threat, but the absence of *acknowledgment*. Most places, even the barren ones, push back somehow. Wind. Cold. Echo. Some register of your being there. The Shallow will accept your warmth into its mineral without remark. Your footsteps will not echo. Your breathing will not disturb the air because the air is not waiting to be disturbed. You will be in the conversation of minerals, and minerals do not converse with anything that breathes and seeks and flees.

If you are the sort of person who needs to matter to a place — and most of us are, even the travelers, *especially* the travelers — then The Shallow will be difficult in a way that has nothing to do with danger. You should know this now, while you can still decide not to go.

## II. Water and the Preservation of Moisture

Your lips will crack within an hour. This is not a warning; it is the closest thing The Shallow offers to a greeting.

The air tastes of calcium and old warmth. It is very dry — not the dramatic, punishing dry of a desert, but the quiet, thorough dry of a place that has been evaporating moisture for so long that evaporation has become a structural property rather than an event. The mineral walls hold heat, not water. The lithophytes survive on condensation films so thin they are more idea than substance. Nothing here wants to give you water because nothing here has water to give.

Bring more than you think you need, and bring it in a way that does not fail. A single large container is vulnerable. Divide your water into at least three vessels — one for drinking, one for emergency, one you do not touch until you are leaving. The Shallow is not large; you could walk its perimeter in two minutes, and the entrance is twenty paces across and open to the flats above. You are never far from exit. But the dryness is insidious, and you may not notice how depleted you have become until you are standing at the lip with a dry throat and the flats stretching pale and featureless before you, and you realize you have nothing for the walk back to wherever you came from.

Lip balm. A thick, waxy one, not the glossy kind. Apply it before you descend and reapply every hour. Bring a saline nasal spray if you are prone to dry sinuses; the calcium-tasting air will pull moisture from every mucous membrane you have. Eye drops. The kind that mimic natural tears, not the redness-relief kind. Your eyes will feel gritty after the first few hours, and you will blink more often, and each blink will remind you that you are a moist thing in a place that has no use for moisture.

Skin cream. Unscented. The Shallow does not care about your scent, but you do not want to be the first creature to introduce a novel chemical signature to a place that has been outside the conversation of living things for millennia. This is not a rule. There are no rules. It is simply respect.

## III. Light: What to Bring and When to Use It

Light arrives in The Shallow only as a rumor, and you should treat it as such — secondhand, diminished, unreliable.

At midday you can see well enough to move. This is the optimal time for your first descent, because The Shallow is a place you need to learn with your eyes before you can navigate it with your memory. The strata in the walls — pale ochre, deeper tan, the band of something almost white, the return to tan — these are the only landmarks, and you should study them until you can close your eyes and see them. Not because you will need them for navigation — the space is too small for true navigation — but because they are the only visual variety the place offers, and your mind will want variety later.

By late afternoon, the hollow dims. By dusk it is a dark room. This is not the darkness of caves or deep night; the open mouth prevents that. But it is substantial darkness, the darkness of a place that does not generate its own light and has no reason to. Your eyes will not adjust fully because there is nothing to adjust to. The warmth will still radiate from the walls. The rosettes will still press against the stone. But you will not see them.

Bring a light, but think carefully about what kind.

A headlamp with a red-light setting. The red light will preserve whatever night vision is available to you while still allowing you to move. Do not use white light. White light in The Shallow is an intrusion — not because the place cares, but because *you* will care. The first time you click on a bright white beam and see the walls flash into full detail, the shadows jumping, the rosettes suddenly vivid and green-gray and alive-looking in their stone-fixed positions, you will feel like a surgeon entering a temple. The light will reveal too much too fast. The Shallow's character is built on subtlety and patience, and the white light respects neither.

Use the red light to navigate. Use darkness to sit.

Bring candles if you are the sort of person who finds meaning in ritual, but understand that a candle in The Shallow is an oddity. The flame will gutter slightly in the still air — not from wind, because there is no wind, but from the warmth of its own combustion rising into the cooler air above, creating a micro-circulation that exists only because you introduced it. The walls will absorb the light and give nothing back. The rosettes will look exactly as they do in daylight: gray-green, pressed flat, indifferent. A candle will not make the place cozier. It will simply make it visible, and you may find that visibility is not what you want.

## IV. Clothing: How to Dress for Patient Warmth

The Shallow is warmer than the flats above, especially at night. This is its single generosity, and you should not waste it by over-preparing for cold.

Dress in layers. The flats above may be cold — you will have crossed them to get here, and you will know — but once you descend, you will want to shed. The mineral walls radiate the day's absorbed warmth slowly, grudgingly, like a creature that does not want to share, but they radiate it nonetheless, and the hollow traps what they give. The air sits. It warms. It does not move.

Wear soft-soled shoes. The floor is hard-packed, almost polished, and your footsteps will be the loudest thing in The Shallow unless you make an effort to silence them. Soft soles will not eliminate sound entirely — you are too heavy, too angular, too alive to move through this place without registering — but they will reduce the sharp reports of heel-strikes on compressed mineral. The Shallow has been settling into itself for longer than anything remembers, and your footsteps are a kind of violence, however small. Minimize them.

Bring something to sit on. A foam pad, thin, nothing elaborate. You will want to sit, and you will want to sit for longer than you expect, and the floor — while not sharp or uncomfortable — will leach heat from you through conductivity. The mineral holds warmth, but it does not share it willingly with things that are only visiting. A thin barrier between you and the stone will make the difference between an hour of comfortable stillness and twenty minutes of shifting and adjusting and making small sounds that break the silence you came to experience.

Do not wear perfume. Do not wear strongly scented deodorant. The Shallow has its own smell — calcium and old warmth, mineral and time — and you will want to learn it, and you cannot learn it if you are smelling yourself.

## V. Time: How Long to Stay and How to Endure It

You could know The Shallow completely in an afternoon. Every stratum, every rosette, every subtle variation in the floor's compacted surface. This is what you will think after the first three hours. You will be wrong.

The Shallow reveals itself through duration, not detail. There is nothing more to see after the first walkthrough. But there is more to *experience*, and the difference between seeing and experiencing is the difference between knowing a place exists and understanding why it exists.

Stay at least one full cycle of light and dark. Arrive at midday when you can see, learn the geometry, touch the walls (they will feel warm and faintly gritty, like a worn sandstone that has been handled by no hands), find the cluster of seven rosettes near the back wall, sit in the center and turn slowly as the description says. Then stay.

Watch the light dim. Do not turn on your red lamp yet. Let your eyes struggle. Let the walls disappear stratum by stratum — the pale ochre first, then the deeper tan, then the white chalky band fading to gray, then to darker gray, then to nothing. This is the place showing you how it becomes itself when no one is looking. You are privileged to watch.

Sit in the darkness. Feel the warmth. Listen.

You will hear the ticking. Faint, irregular — thermal expansion and contraction working their slow arithmetic on the walls. This is The Shallow speaking, and it speaks in increments of mineral and temperature. It is not speaking to you. It is simply speaking, the way a sleeping person breathes. You are in the room with it. That is all.

You will hear the settling. Somewhere beneath you, fine particles shift in some microscopic readjustment. The ground deciding, once again, to be lower here.

If you are very still and very lucky, you may hear a dry leaf detach from one of the rosettes and scrape lightly against the stone on its way down. This is an event. This is The Shallow's equivalent of an earthquake, a thunderstorm, a sunrise. A single leaf falling after thirty years of growth so slow it is indistinguishable from stillness. Do not dismiss it. Do not think *that's it?* That is it. That is everything.

Stay through the night. Sleep if you can — the warmth will permit it, and the silence will not disturb you once you surrender to it. Wake before dawn if possible, because the pre-dawn cool is when the dew films condense on the rosettes, and you will want to shine your red light on them and see the moisture that sustains them, thin as a thought, already evaporating as you watch.

Leave when you are ready. Do not overstay into restlessness. The Shallow will not be offended by your departure; it will not be anything by your departure. But you will be diminished by it if you leave before you have understood what stasis feels like from the inside.

## VI. What Not to Bring

Do not bring music. Do not bring an instrument. Do not bring a recording device with the intention of capturing The Shallow's silence, because playback will turn its absence into a presence, and you will have lied about what this place is.

Do not bring a companion unless they understand, truly understand, what The Shallow offers. Two people in The Shallow can be a communion or a contamination, and the difference depends entirely on whether both parties are willing to be irrelevant together. If your companion is the sort who fills silence with talk, leave them above. They will not enjoy it, and you will not enjoy them not enjoying it.

Do not bring food that crinkles, crunches, or requires unwrapping. The Shallow is not a place for eating — you will not be there long enough to need meals — but if you must bring something, bring something soft and silent. A piece of fruit. A strip of dried meat. Eat it slowly, with awareness that each bite is a disruption, and be at peace with the disruption because you are a living thing and living things disrupt, and The Shallow has been disrupted before by the wind and the insects and will be disrupted again.

Do not bring a weapon. There is nothing to fight.

Do not bring an offering. There is nothing to receive.

## VII. Mental Preparation: The Interior Work

This is the preparation that matters most, and the preparation most travelers will skip.

You are going to a place that does not need you. This is rarer than you think. Most places need something — attention, protection, preservation, exploitation, interpretation. The Shallow needs nothing. It was here before need was invented. It will be here after need is forgotten.

When you descend, you will feel the slight shift in temperature — warmer, enclosed. You will smell the calcium and the old warmth. You will see the walls rising gently on three sides. And you will wait for the place to *do something*. You will wait for the reveal, the twist, the hidden quality that makes The Shallow worth visiting. You will project your expectations onto the strata and the rosettes and the polished floor, and you will search for meaning in the way the light dims and the warmth persists and the silence deepens.

The meaning is that there is no meaning beyond what is here.

The Shallow is a hollow in the world, barely deep enough to be called a place at all. It holds warmth. It holds a few gray-green rosettes pressed against the stone. It holds the long, slow patience of mineral at rest. It is in its own state. It has been finding this state for a very long time, and it will continue to find it for longer still.

Your preparation, then, is not logistical. It is spiritual, if you will forgive the word, or psychological, if you will not. You must practice being unnecessary. You must rehearse the sensation of being present without being relevant. You must learn — or remember, because you knew this once, before you were a traveler, before you were anyone in particular — how to sit in a place and let it be what it is without requiring it to be something for you.

Meditate before you go. Not to clear your mind — The Shallow will do that for you, or it will fill your mind with the sound of your own irrelevance, which amounts to the same thing — but to practice the transition from seeking to being. The Shallow does not seek. It does not become. It is. Can you be, alongside it, without demanding more?

If you can, you will find something rare: a place that asks nothing of you and offers nothing but itself, and itself is enough — warm and quiet and patient, settling into the hollow it has made, holding what it holds, requiring no witness and no testimony and no preparation beyond the willingness to descend and stay and leave without having changed anything, including yourself.

Though you may be changed. That is the risk. That is the gift. That is the only thing The Shallow gives that it does not mean to.

---

## VIII. Leaving

When you climb the gradual slope back to the flats above, you will feel the air move. You will feel the wind that did not reach you below. You will feel the openness, the exposure, the sudden relevance of your body to the world around it — the sun on your skin, the distance in every direction, the return to a place where things happen and matter.

Do not look back immediately. Walk ten paces, twenty, until you are far enough that the hollow is just a slight depression in the land, barely visible, almost not there. Then turn and look.

It is barely deep enough to be called a place at all. You were just inside it. You felt its warmth, heard its ticking, watched its single leaf fall. And from here, from the flats above, you can almost believe it isn't real. Almost.

This is The Shallow's last lesson: the things that change us most deeply are often the things we can barely see from the outside, the hollows that seem too slight to matter, the patient mineral settled into itself, the warmth held without reason, the gray-green rosettes pressing flat against the stone, growing so slowly they might as well be still, being so still they might as well be stone, being so stone they might as well be the place itself, which is what they are, which is what everything in The Shallow is — the place itself, finding its own state, holding what it holds, patient beyond your comprehension, warm beyond your staying, there beyond your leaving.
