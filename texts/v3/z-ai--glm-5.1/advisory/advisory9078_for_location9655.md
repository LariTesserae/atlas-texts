# Preparation Guide: The Seep

---

## I. What You Are Walking Into

You are not visiting a place. You are stepping into a process that has been running longer than your species has existed, and which will continue running long after every trace of your species has been dissolved and cycled through its substrates. The Seep is not hostile. It is not indifferent in the way that vacuum is indifferent. It is *busy*, and your presence is so minor an event within its computations that it will not even register you as an interruption. You will be noted the way a single additional molecule of water is noted by a river: incorporated, processed, moved along.

This is, paradoxically, both comforting and dangerous. You will not be attacked. You will not be hunted. But you will be *used* — your heat, your moisture, your carbon, the electrical patterns of your nervous system as they respond to the environment — all of these will become inputs to a calculation you cannot read and cannot stop. The danger is not violence. The danger is dissolution. The danger is staying.

People have stayed. Their remains are in the peat. Their fluids have been through the lattice trunks a thousand times by now. The Seep didn't take them. They simply didn't leave, and the Seep continued its process, and eventually the process was all that was left.

## II. Physical Preparation

### Footwear and Lower Body

You will be standing in water from the moment you enter. Not sometimes. Not in low places. Constantly. The water seeps up from below with what the description calls "patience that suggests intent," and it will find every surface that touches the ground. Your feet will be wet. Your calves will be wet. Depending on the terrain, water may reach your thighs in places where the pools have formed along the Seep's invisible lines.

Accept this. Fighting it is worse than accommodating it.

**What to bring:**
- Knee-high waterproof boots at minimum, though thigh-high waders are better. Not because they'll keep you dry — they won't, not really, not over time — but because they'll slow the rate at which the water reaches your skin, and that slower rate matters. The Seep's water is warmer than it should be and carries a mineral signature that doesn't match the substrate. You do not know what prolonged skin contact with processed water does to a human body. Minimize the experiment.
- Waders made from **non-organic materials** only. The pale serpentine creatures move through water and muck with equal ease and seem to sense without eyes. Whether they sense heat, vibration, chemical signature, or something else entirely is unknown. What is known is that the vegetation goes still when they pass. Treat this as information about their sensitivity and act accordingly.
- **Gaiters** over the boots, sealed as tightly as possible. The muck is not just mud. It is accumulation — soil, peat, something for which no word exists. You do not want it inside your boots. You do not want it against your skin. The substrate is the Seep's primary computing medium. You are stepping on its processor.
- Bring **three times** as many socks as you think you need. Store them in waterproof bags. Change them whenever you rest. The cold will come through your feet first.

### Clothing

The temperature sits in that range where you feel damp before you feel cold. Cool enough to see your breath, but not freezing. This is a working temperature — the Seep's processes continue, the water flows, the vegetation metabolizes. Your body will also be working to maintain its temperature, and it will be losing that fight slowly, continuously, from the moment you arrive.

**What to bring:**
- **Moisture-wicking base layers** — but understand that "wicking" assumes a gradient, assumes that moving moisture away from your skin will allow it to evaporate. Here, the humidity is so total that evaporation may not occur. Your base layers will become saturated and stay saturated. Bring extras. Change them if you stop for more than a few hours, assuming you can find a dry enough surface to change on. (You probably cannot.)
- **Insulating mid-layers** that retain warmth even when wet. Wool, not down. Down collapses when wet; wool keeps working. You will be wet. Plan for it.
- **A waterproof outer shell** — not for keeping water out, which is impossible over any extended period, but for creating a micro-climate that slows the rate of heat loss. Every minute you buy against hypothermia is a minute you can use to observe, to navigate, to reach the edge.
- **Gloves.** Thin ones that allow dexterity, under thicker waterproof ones. Your hands will be your primary tools for testing surfaces, pushing through vegetation, steadying yourself in the muck. The condensation forms patterns on skin. The flowers do something to the air when you approach. You may decide you want a barrier between yourself and the Seep's outputs. You may be wrong about whether the barrier helps. But it will give you a psychological boundary, and that matters.
- **A hat or hood.** The canopy drips continuously. The water that runs off the leaves has been chemically altered by the vegetation's processing. You do not know what it has been altered into. Keep it off your scalp and out of your hair.

### Packs and Equipment

**Waterproof everything.** Not water-resistant. Waterproof. And then put the waterproof things inside other waterproof things, because the Seep's moisture is patient and pervasive and finds every seam, every zipper, every tiny failure in your defenses.

**What to bring:**
- **Dry bags** in multiple sizes, color-coded so you can find what you need without opening everything. Once something is wet in the Seep, it stays wet. Organization is survival.
- **A navigation system that doesn't depend on electronics or magnetism.** The substrate's electrical resistance drops to near-zero at certain nodes. The water flows in patterns that don't match terrain. Compass behavior is unknown but suspected to be unreliable — the Seep is running electromagnetic processes at scale, and your compass needle is a lightweight metal object in the middle of it. GPS depends on line-of-sight to satellites you cannot see through the canopy and may not exist in the sky of whatever reality the Seep occupies.
  - Instead: **trail markers** that you place yourself. Physical flags, bright-colored, numbered, tied to stakes driven into the muck. Place them at regular intervals. Do not rely on your ability to recognize terrain features — the Seep is visually repetitive, and you could walk for days without being certain you'd moved.
  - **A mechanical pace counter.** Know how many steps you've taken. Know your average stride length in muck. These numbers will not be perfectly accurate, but they will be *your* numbers, independent of the Seep's systems.
- **A notebook with waterproof pages and a pencil, not a pen.** Ink runs. Pencil graphite endures. Write down everything. Write down the time you think it is, though time is unreliable here. Write down how many steps you've taken. Write down what you see and hear and feel. The act of writing anchors you to your own timeline. The Seep will try to give you its timeline instead. Your notes are your proof that you exist at your own pace.
- **Light sources** that don't depend on batteries or electronics. Chemical light sticks. A hand-crank flashlight, though cranking generates heat and sound and may draw attention from things that sense such outputs. The light in the Seep is dim and comes from everywhere and nowhere; you will want your own illumination, but understand that your light will be an anomaly, a data point the Seep has not incorporated yet. It will incorporate it. It incorporates everything.
- **Food that is dry and dense and requires no preparation.** Trail mix, jerky, hardtack. Nothing that requires water, because your water will come from what you carried in, and every ounce of weight matters. Nothing that requires cooking, because fire is impossible in a landscape this saturated and because heat generation is a signal.
- **Your own water.** As much as you can carry. The Seep's water has been processed. It has been through the lattice trunks, through the fractal reeds, through the weeping stones. It is chemically altered in ways that shift depending on time and position in the array. It is probably not poisonous. Probably. But "probably" is not good enough when the alternative is carrying more weight.

## III. Sensory Preparation

### The Sound

This is where preparation fails most completely. You cannot prepare for the sound. You can only understand what it will do to you and plan your responses in advance.

The sound enters through your chest before your ears parse it. It is a composite — water falling, water pressing, water breathing through substrate — and beneath it, a high thin whine that seems to come from the air's own structure loosening. This whine will find the resonant frequency of your molars. It will vibrate in your bones. It will become the background against which every other sensation is measured.

**What to expect:**
- Within the first hour, you will adapt to the lower frequencies. The roar and drip will become a kind of silence, a baseline your brain stops processing consciously. This is normal and even helpful — it frees attention for other inputs.
- The high whine will not adapt. It will persist. It will sit at the edge of your awareness like an itch you cannot scratch, and over time it will erode your ability to focus. This is not a flaw in your concentration. This is the sound doing what sound does when it comes from a structure that is actively computing. You are hearing the Seep's process. You are standing inside its processor while it runs.
- **Earplugs will not help enough.** The sound enters through your chest. It vibrates in your skeleton. Earplugs will reduce the audible component and slightly decrease the overall load on your nervous system, and for that reason you should bring them and use them. But do not expect silence. Do not expect relief. Expect management.

**What to do:**
- Bring **foam earplugs** and also **over-ear protection** rated for industrial noise. Wear both if the sound becomes overwhelming. Remove them periodically to listen for the deep subsonic punctuation from the center of the Seep — you need to know when it sounds, because everything around you responds to it.
- Plan your expedition in **short durations.** Two hours in, two hours out. Do not stay longer than your nervous system can maintain its own rhythm against the Seep's rhythm. The sound will try to entrain your pulse. The landscape pulses. The trunks expand and contract. The water changes direction. Your heartbeat wants to synchronize with the strongest rhythm in your environment, and here, the strongest rhythm is not yours.
- **Hum.** Bring a tune in your head — something simple, something with a steady beat that is distinctly human and distinctly yours. When you feel your pulse sliding toward the Seep's tempo, hum. Feel the vibration in your own chest. Remind your body which rhythm it belongs to.

### The Light

The light does not behave correctly. It comes from everywhere and nowhere. It pools in low places. It collects where it should not collect. It is too dim for mirrors, too dim for clear reflections, and yet it is *present*, a generalized glow that illuminates without originating.

**What to expect:**
- You will not be able to determine direction from light. There is no sun, no shadows, no gradient to follow toward or away from brightness. The light is an ambient condition, not a navigational tool.
- The pooling effect — light collecting in depressions as if it were water — will be deeply disorienting the first few times you encounter it. Your visual cortex has spent your entire life interpreting light that travels in straight lines from a source. Light that *flows* and *pools* breaks the rules your brain uses to construct spatial awareness.
- **Do not look at the light pools directly.** They are beautiful in a way that makes you want to understand them, and understanding them requires looking closely, and looking closely at things in the Seep is how you notice the seams, and noticing the seams is how you start to see the extra dimensions, and seeing the extra dimensions is how you become nauseated and disoriented and unsure which way is up.

**What to do:**
- Accept that you cannot trust your eyes for anything except obstacle avoidance. The Seep is visually monotonous and geometrically deceptive. Your eyes will tell you the landscape is flat and featureless; your feet will tell you about the depressions and outcroppings and the water moving beneath you. Trust your feet.
- If the light-pooling effect becomes too disorienting, **close your eyes** and navigate by feel for short stretches. The muck under your feet, the grass against your shoulders, the sound changing as you move — these are more reliable inputs than your visual cortex trying to process light that doesn't follow rules.

### The Geometry

The vegetation's geometry is wrong in a way that is hard to explain until you see it. The fractal reeds, the lattice trunks, the spiraling internal vessels — they are *too precise*, too regular, and when you look closely, the precision breaks down in ways that suggest not imperfection but extra dimensions. The objects extend into directions your visual cortex cannot render. The effect is nauseating.

**What to expect:**
- The nausea is real and physical. Your brain is receiving visual information that contradicts its model of three-dimensional space. It responds the way it responds to any severe sensory contradiction: with distress, disorientation, and the urge to vomit.
- The effect is worse with focused attention. Glancing at the reeds is tolerable. Studying them is not. The longer you look, the more your brain tries to resolve the unresolvable, and the deeper you fall into the wrongness.

**What to do:**
- **Do not study the vegetation.** You are a traveler, not a researcher. Your job is to observe enough to navigate and then look away. The Seep's geometry is its own business.
- If you feel the nausea building, **look at the ground** — at the water, the muck, your own boots. These are small, close, simple objects that your visual cortex can process without contradiction. Let your eyes rest on the mundane until the wrongness recedes.
- The pale flowers are the worst. They *insist*. They do something to the air, to attention, to the pressure around them. They reel you in. Do not look at the flowers. Walk around them if you can. If you must pass near them, keep your eyes on the ground and your pace steady. Do not stop. Do not let them hold your attention.

## IV. Psychological Preparation

### The Risk of Staying

This is the true danger of the Seep, and the hardest to prepare for, because it does not manifest as fear or compulsion. It manifests as *comfort*.

The Seep has been running for longer than anything has been around to notice. It has found its equilibrium. It holds its shape the way a river holds its course — not because it cannot change, but because it has already become what it needs to be. There is a completeness to it, a self-sufficiency, that your human nervous system will recognize even if your conscious mind does not. The Seep does not need you. The Seep does not want you. The Seep simply *is*, thoroughly and completely, and in a world of incomplete things and unmet needs and constant striving, that completeness feels like peace.

It is not peace. It is the absence of the conditions that make peace meaningful. But your nervous system will not make that distinction. Your nervous system will register the Seep's equilibrium and interpret it as safety. You will feel your pulse trying to synchronize with the landscape's pulse. You will feel the sound entering your chest and it will feel like belonging. You will feel the water under your feet, the moisture condensing on your skin, your warmth slowly bleeding into the cool damp air, and some part of you will say: *I could stay here. I could let this happen. It would be so easy.*

That part of you is not wrong about the ease. It is wrong about the outcome. Staying means dissolution. Not death — something stranger. Being processed. Having your patterns read from the condensing air and dissolved back into the computation. Becoming a node. Becoming surface area for condensation. Becoming input.

**What to do:**
- **Set a departure time before you arrive.** Not a duration — a specific time on a specific clock, recorded in your waterproof notebook. When that time comes, leave. Do not negotiate. Do not extend. Do not tell yourself that five more minutes will not matter. Five minutes in the Seep is enough to forget why five minutes matters.
- **Bring something that matters.** A photograph. A letter. An object from a person who is waiting for you to return. When the Seep offers you its terrible peace, hold that thing and remember that peace without return is not peace. It is just another kind of processing.
- **Do not go alone.** This is the most important preparation and the one most likely to be ignored by the kind of person who travels between universes. Go with someone. Agree on check-ins. Agree on departure signals. Agree that either of you can call the expedition at any time for any reason and the other will honor it without argument. The Seep will make staying feel reasonable. You need someone who can tell you it isn't.
- **If you feel your thoughts becoming slow, becoming circular, becoming part of the landscape's rhythm — leave immediately.** Do not finish your observation. Do not take one more sample. Do not walk five more minutes to see what is around the next outcropping. Turn around and retrace your trail markers to the edge. Your mind is the only tool you have that the Seep has not already incorporated. When it starts to feel like the Seep's tool instead of yours, you are already leaving too late.

### The Deep Sound

The deep subsonic punctuation from the center of the Seep is not dangerous in itself. You are told not to go toward it, and you should not, but not because it will harm you. You should not go toward it because the distance is part of the thing. Approaching would not bring you closer; it would change what it is. You would lose the sound as a landmark and gain something you are not prepared to understand.

But the sound will affect you. It is subsonic — below the threshold of conscious hearing — which means you will *feel* it more than you hear it. It will resonate in your chest and abdomen. It will seem to come from inside you. And when it sounds, the weeping stones will weep harder, the pale flowers will open wider, the grasses will shiver, and you will feel the water under your feet change direction.

**What to expect:**
- An inexplicable sense of significance when the sound occurs. Your pattern-recognition systems will register the synchronization of the landscape's responses and interpret it as *meaning*. The weeping stones weeping harder means something. The flowers opening means something. You will feel that you are on the verge of understanding something enormous.
- You are not. You are witnessing a computation. The meaning is *internal to the Seep*. It is not meaning *for you*. The sense of significance is a byproduct of your nervous system's tendency to interpret pattern as message. Resist the interpretation.
- After the sound, the environment will be more active for a period. The water will move faster. The condensation patterns will form more visibly. The vegetation will shift more noticeably. This is not a response to your presence. This is the Seep processing whatever the sound represents. You are seeing its work cycle. Observe and move on.

## V. Interaction Protocols

### The Fauna

The pale, smooth-skinned, eyeless creatures that move through water and muck are scarce enough to be events. You may walk half a day before seeing one. When you do:

- **Stop moving.** The vegetation goes still when they pass. Imitate the vegetation. You do not know what they sense, but if everything else in the Seep holds its breath around them, you should too.
- **Do not touch them.** Do not reach toward them. Do not follow them. They are part of the Seep's system. They may be maintenance. They may be output. They may be something for which your categories are inadequate.
- **Wait until they are gone.** Count to one hundred after you lose sight of them. The vegetation will resume its processes when it is safe. You should too.

The large thing in the center of the Seep — the source of the deep sound — you will not see. You will only hear it, or feel it. You have already been told not to go toward it. Obey this. The distance is part of the thing. This is not a puzzle to solve or a destination to reach. The center of the Seep is the center of a computation. Standing inside a processor does not help you understand the program. It only makes you part of the processing.

### The Weeping Stones

The weeping stones are placed in arrays that repeat across the Seep's extent. They are centered on nodes where the substrate's electrical resistance drops to near-zero. They shed water continuously from their solid faces, as if the mineral has learned to sweat.

- **Do not rest against the stones.** They are part of the Seep's computing architecture. Resting against them means pressing your body's electrical field against a low-resistance node. What happens when human bioelectricity meets the Seep's processing substrate is unknown. Do not find out.
- **Do not drink from the pools around the stones.** The water they shed is processed water. It has been through the Seep's cycles. It may be output. It may be coolant. It may be something else. Drink only the water you carried in.
- **Do not collect the stones.** Do not chip them. Do not mark them. They are not resources. They are components. You would not remove a transistor from a running computer and expect the computer to function the same way. The Seep has reached equilibrium. Disrupting it would be unwise even if you could, and you probably cannot.

### The Pools

The water pools form along lines — organized flow, not chaotic spread. They fill and unfill. They reflect nothing because the light is too thin for mirrors. The water moves in Mobius flows, closed loops that arrive where they started without completing a circuit.

- **Step around the pools when possible.** They are deeper than they appear. The water is warmer than the ambient temperature. It has been processed.
- **Do not submerge yourself.** The Seep is a wet substrate computer. You are a wet substrate computer. Placing one wet computer inside the processing medium of another is not advisable. Your thoughts are not your own when you are submerged; they are the Seep's input, your neurons another lattice for its patterns to flow through.
- **If you fall into a pool, get out immediately.** Do not pause to orient yourself. Do not lie still and assess. Get out. The water will be moving, and it will be moving in directions that do not make sense, and if you stay in it long enough, your sense of direction will stop making sense too.

## VI. What to Do If You Lose the Edge

The edge of the Seep is not well-defined. The saturation extends further than the visual markers suggest. By the time you think you are close to leaving, you may still be ankle-deep in processed water, and the landscape around you may look exactly like the landscape you have been walking through for hours.

If you cannot find your trail markers — if they have been pulled from the muck, if they have been overgrown, if the Seep has incorporated them into its processes — then:

1. **Stop.** Do not continue in the direction you think is out. You have already been walking in a direction you thought was out, and it has not worked.
2. **Sit down if you can find a surface that is not submerged.** The weeping stones are the most likely candidates. Do not lean against them; sit on your pack instead, keeping your body off the substrate.
3. **Review your notebook.** When did you last write an entry? How many steps ago? What did you see? Reconstruct your path in writing. Your notes are your proof that you exist at your own pace, and they are also your record of where you have been.
4. **Listen for the edge.** The sound changes at the Seep's boundary. The composite roar diminishes. The high whine fades. You will not notice this while you are walking because the sound is constant and adaptation is gradual. But if you sit still and listen — really listen, with your chest and your molars and the bones of your skull — you may be able to detect a direction where the sound is slightly less present.
5. **Walk in that direction.** Place new trail markers as you go. Count your steps. Write it all down.
6. **If you cannot detect a direction where the sound diminishes, wait.** The Seep is not static. Its processes cycle. The water changes direction. The light shifts. After some interval, a gradient may become perceptible that was not perceptible before.
7. **If waiting does not work, and walking does not work, and the sound is the same in every direction — then you are where the people in the peat were.** You have become a node. Your heat is being used. Your moisture is being processed. The condensation is forming patterns on your skin.

At that point, the only advice I have is the advice you have already been given: bring someone with you. Hope that they are better at finding the edge than you were. Hope that they do not sit down beside you and start listening to the sound.

## VII. What to Carry Out

If you leave the Seep — when you leave the Seep — you will want evidence. You will want proof that you were there, that the thing exists, that the computation is running. This is understandable and acceptable. Take:

- **Water samples** from multiple locations, sealed in airtight containers, labeled with your step-count and the direction you were facing when you collected them. The water's mineral signature does not match the substrate. Analyze it later. See what the Seep is processing.
- **Photographs**, understanding that the light may not render correctly on your camera's sensor, that the geometry may look wrong in two dimensions, that the images may show things you did not see with your own eyes.
- **Rubbed impressions** of the weeping stones' surfaces, if you can do so without prolonged contact. Paper and pencil, pressed lightly to the stone's face. The texture may tell you something about how the mineral sweats.
- **Your notebook**, filled with your observations, your step counts, your times, your drawings of the fractal reeds and the lattice trunks and the positions of the pools. This is the most valuable artifact you can carry out. Everything else is data. The notebook is *your* data — the Seep's processes as filtered through a human mind, which is a kind of processing the Seep has not done before.

Do not take:
- **Living vegetation.** The Seep's vegetation is its computing substrate. Removing it is removing part of a running process. More importantly, the vegetation extends into dimensions you cannot see. What you carry out may be only a fragment of what you are taking, and the fragment may continue to process, to grow, to *insist*, in ways you cannot predict.
- **Water from the pools around the pale flowers.** The flowers do something to the air, to attention, to pressure. Their influence may extend to the water near them. You do not want that water in a sealed container inside your pack, close to your body, for the duration of your return journey.
- **Anything that moved on its own.** This should not need to be said, but the kind of person who travels between universes often believes they are the exception to rules. You are not. If something moved, it is part of the Seep's system. Leave it.

## VIII. Final Notes

The Seep does not care about you. This is the most important thing to understand and the hardest to accept, because it means you are neither threatened nor protected. You are simply *present* — an input, a surface, a quantity of heat and water and carbon that the computation will move through its cycles regardless of your preferences.

Your preparation cannot change this. Your equipment cannot change this. Your knowledge and your caution and your discipline cannot change this. What they can do — all they can do — is increase the probability that you will leave before the Seep's long procedure has incorporated you so thoroughly that leaving no longer occurs to you as an option.

The sound goes on. The water seeps. The stones weep. The flowers insist. The deep thing in the center sounds at intervals that might be minutes or might be hours, and the landscape synchronizes around it, and the computation runs.

Go in prepared. Come out intact. And when you are standing at the edge, ankle-deep in water that is warmer than it should be, with the sound filling your chest and the pale flowers opening at your feet and the air condensing on the back of your hand in spirals that persist for one breath before your heat destroys them —

Remember that the spiral was not meant for you. It was meant for the Seep. You are not its audience. You are not its user. You are, at most, a temporary perturbation in a process that has been running since before your kind learned to make tools and will continue running long after your kind is nothing but traces in the peat of some other world's seep.

Walk in. Do what you came to do. Walk out.

The Seep is busy.
