# Preparing for The Condensate: A Practical Guide for Multiverse Travelers

---

## I. BEFORE YOU ARRIVE: MENTAL PREPARATION

### Abandon Your Temporal Assumptions

You are not going to a place. You are going to a *process*. This is the single most important thing to understand before stepping through. The Condensate is not a city that was built; it is a city that is *building*, and the distinction will warp your expectations if you don't internalize it first.

You will arrive and look for completion. You will instinctively search for finished edges, for the sense that a thing has arrived at its final form. You will not find this. The mountains look like sketches. The walls look like they're still deciding how thick to be—because they are. The streets feel like they're still negotiating their final routes—because, over geological timescales, they are. If you need finished things, if you need the satisfaction of arrival, this place will make you restless in a way that curdles into something worse.

Practice sitting with incompleteness before you come. Sit in a room with an unfinished project and refuse to finish it. Watch a sunset and turn away before the colors fade. Learn to tolerate the indefinite. The locals have had generations of this. You will have days or weeks. Prepare accordingly.

### Prepare for the Sound

Read about sensory deprivation. Then understand that you will experience something closer to the opposite: sensory *saturation*, but acoustic, and monolithic.

The hum is not background noise. It is not the drone of a refrigerator you learn to ignore. It is a *chord*—complex, wet, unresolved—and it enters you through your chest before your ears register it. The main channels run beneath the streets, and their pulse is subsonic, felt in the viscera. The middle registers shift twice daily when the filtration cycles change, and you will feel this as a subtle anxiety, a sense that something has changed that you can't quite name, long before you hear it.

Newcomers cannot bear it. The account you read said this, and it was not being poetic. People crack. Not dramatically—there's no dramatic infrastructure for cracking here—but they stop sleeping, then stop thinking clearly, then make mistakes about sealing, about filtration, about whether a wall is thick enough, and the fauna find the gaps.

Before arriving, train yourself. Sleep with white noise at escalating volumes for weeks. Then replace the white noise with brown noise—deeper, more persistent. Then add a second layer: something with periodic shifts in the middle register, barely perceptible. Learn to sleep through it. Learn to think through it. Learn to hold a conversation through it. If you can't do these things after three weeks of training, do not go. The sound does not care whether you're prepared. It will simply be there, always, like gravity, like wet.

### Understand What "Cold" Means Here

You think you know cold. You do not know this cold.

This is not the cold of vacuum or the cold of deep ice. This is a *saturated* cold. The air holds as much moisture as it physically can, and then the moisture gives up and condenses on every surface, including you. The cold does not bite; it *seeps*. It does not sting; it *permeates*. You will touch a wall and your hand will come away dripping, and the dripping will be cold in a way that feels deliberate, like the water has chosen to be exactly this temperature as an act of profound hostility toward warmth.

Your body heat leaves you through the film of water on your skin faster than you can imagine. Hypothermia comes quickly here, and it comes wet, and the wetness makes you think you're merely uncomfortable when you are already in danger.

Practice cold tolerance in wet conditions. Not dry cold. Not the cold of a mountain summit in still air. The cold of standing in a saturated fog at near-freezing temperatures for hours. The cold where your gloves wet through and your boots wet through and your skin is never, ever dry. If you've only ever been cold and dry, you are not prepared.

## II. WHAT TO BRING

### Clothing: The Layered Defense

**Base Layer: Moisture-Wicking Synthhetics**
Do not bring cotton. Do not bring wool unless it is super-washed and treated. Anything that absorbs and holds moisture will become a second skin of cold that you cannot remove without removing the garment itself. Your base layer must *move water away from your body*, even though the environment will immediately deposit more. You are not trying to stay dry. You are trying to keep the water moving, to prevent it from pooling against your skin.

**Middle Layer: Insulation That Functions Wet**
Most insulation fails when wet. Down collapses. Open-cell foam saturates. You need closed-cell foam panels, synthetic fills rated to retain 80%+ of their insulation value when fully saturated, or—ideally—insulation that uses the wet layer itself as a thermal barrier. Some multiverse markets sell phase-change materials that absorb heat from the condensation process itself. If you can find these, buy them. The Condensate's water is warmer than the air in many places (it's been processed through the city's thermal systems), and clever use of this fact can keep you alive.

**Outer Layer: The Shell**
This is where it gets counterintuitive. You do not want total waterproofing.

Read that again: *You do not want total waterproofing.*

A completely sealed shell will trap the condensation that forms *inside it*—on your body, from your breath, from the moisture your own metabolism produces. You will create your own personal condensation cycle, and you will drown in your own microclimate. What you want is a highly water-resistant but *breathable* shell, with venting systems that let your internal moisture escape even as the external moisture tries to enter. This is a losing battle, but it's a slow loss rather than a rapid one, and slow losses can be managed.

Additionally: your shell must be *smooth*. The deposition systems draw ambient minerals from the condensate. If you wear textured, porous fabrics, you will become a template. The crystalline fuzz that grows on walls will grow on *you*, centimeter by centimeter, day by day. It starts as a faint grit on your shoulders. By the end of a week, it's a structural lattice. By the end of a month, you'll have difficulty bending your joints. Smooth, slick materials—silicone-treated fabrics, certain polymers—resist this. Anything rough, anything porous, anything with texture at the nanoscale: the city will print itself onto you.

Bring three complete outfits. Rotate them. Dry what you can in sealed spaces. Accept that "dry" is a temporary state.

**Footwear: The Uncompromising Compromise**
Your boots will be the most important thing you bring. They must:
- Be waterproof to a high rating, because you will be standing in water, in channels, in flow
- Have insulation that functions when saturated (closed-cell foam, aerogel)
- Have soles that grip wet stone, wet metal, wet deposition lattice
- Be tall enough to keep the crawling fauna out (they will try to enter any warm, moist opening; your boots are a warm, moist opening; seal them to your shell at the cuff)
- Be replaceable, because the condensation will eventually defeat any waterproofing

Bring two pairs. Bring sealant. Bring the understanding that your feet will, at some point, be wet, and you will need to manage that situation rather than prevent it.

**Head and Hands**
Your hands need dexterity—you'll be checking seals, adjusting filters, managing equipment—but they also need protection. Layered glove systems: a thin, grippy inner glove of wicking synthetic, a waterproof but flexible middle glove, and an outer mitten for when you don't need fine motor control. The mittens should clip to your sleeves so you can pull them on and off without setting them down—anything set down in this city becomes wet, becomes a template, becomes a habitat.

A hood. Always a hood. The condensation forms on your hair, your scalp, your ears, and runs down your neck. A hood manages the flow. It doesn't prevent it. Nothing prevents it.

Goggles, not glasses. The condensation forms on your eyelashes, and eventually you'll feel like you're crying all the time, which you aren't, but the constant blinking and wiping will exhaust you. Goggles with anti-fog coating and ventilation that doesn't admit spray. You'll look like a diver walking through a city. You'll feel like one too.

### Equipment: The Essentials

**Personal Water Filtration System (Minimum Two-Stage)**
The locals filter their water twice. You should filter yours three times. The first stage removes particulate and mineral accretion. The second stage removes biological contaminants—the fauna are small, numerous, and some of them are small enough to pass through conventional filters. The third stage is for your peace of mind, because you will need all the peace of mind you can get.

Do not drink unfiltered water. Do not brush your teeth with unfiltered water. Do not wash your face with unfiltered water and then let it run into your mouth. The pale segmented crawlers that flow through the gutters in coordinated waves are harmless when they're outside you. You do not want to find out what they are inside you.

**Counter-Frequency Device**
The locals play counter-frequencies in their sleeping chambers to cancel the chittering, the clicking, the hum. You will need this immediately, before you've adapted. Buy or build a device that can analyze the ambient acoustic spectrum and generate phase-inverted signals to cancel the most persistent frequencies. The hum itself—the deep bass pulse of the main channels—cannot be canceled; it's felt rather than heard, and it vibrates through the stone. But the chittering, the clicking, the mid-range drone: these can be dampened.

Practice sleeping with your counter-frequency device before arriving. Calibrate it to whatever ambient noise you can find. Learn its interface in a comfortable environment, because you will not want to learn it in the dark, in the wet, in the thrum.

**Light Sources (Redundant, Multiple)**
There is no sunlight. Not really. The sky is a low ceiling of black-gray cloud, and the long seasonal dark means the concept of "daylight" is academic. The city glows with its own diffuse luminescence—the condensate catches operational light from the infrastructure and scatters it—but this is a twilight glow, a wet gleam, not illumination.

You need:
- A primary headlamp (hands-free is essential)
- A secondary headlamp (the primary will fail; the condensation will find its seals)
- A backup handheld light
- Chemical light sticks (reliable, disposable, unaffected by moisture)
- A small UV light (for checking deposition growth on your gear; the crystalline lattice fluoresces faintly under UV, making early accretion visible before it becomes structural)

**Sealing Kit**
The locals keep their homes sealed and pressurized. You will not have a home; you will have whatever temporary shelter the settlement provides, and you will need to maintain its seals. Bring:
- Silicone sealant rated for extreme moisture (multiple tubes)
- Pressure-sensitive patching material
- A small canister of expanding foam for larger gaps
- Tape—multiple types, multiple widths, all rated for wet application

Every gap is an invitation. Every crack is a door. The fauna do not force entry; they *find* entry. They find what you missed. They find what you thought was too small to matter. A gap the width of a fingernail is a highway for the crawlers. A crack the width of a hair is enough for the clicking things with too many legs. Seal everything. Seal it twice. Check your seals every morning and every evening. The deposition will grow over your seals, incorporating them, and you'll need to scrape it away and reapply.

**Personal Deposition Scraper**
The city will print itself onto you. On your gear, on your clothes, on any surface you carry. A small, sharp scraper—metal, not plastic—will let you remove the crystalline fuzz before it becomes structural. Do this daily. Do it to your boots, your shell, your equipment housings, your weapon if you carry one, your doorframes, your window edges. The deposition is slow—centimeter by centimeter, week by week—but you will be there for days or weeks, and days and weeks add up.

**Acoustic Monitor**
A device that reads the ambient sound spectrum and displays it visually. You need this because your ears will fatigue. The hum, the chord, the ever-present wet symphony will exhaust your auditory processing, and eventually you'll stop hearing things—not because they've gone quiet, but because your brain has decided to stop processing them. This is dangerous. The shift in filtration cycles happens twice daily, and the tonal change is subtle; you might miss it, and the shift indicates changes in water pressure, flow direction, and fauna activity patterns. An acoustic monitor gives you a visual readout. Check it.

**Respiratory Protection**
The air is saturated. Breathing saturated cold air for extended periods stresses the lungs. The locals, who grew up here, have adapted; you have not. A simple particulate filter is insufficient—you need something that warms and dehumidifies the air you breathe, or at least reduces the moisture load. Full-face respirators with heat-exchange modules are ideal. If you cannot acquire one, a standard respirator with moisture-wicking inserts will help, though you'll need to replace the inserts frequently as they saturate.

### What Not to Bring

**Anything You Can't Afford to Lose to Moisture**
Electronics will fail. Books will swell and warp. Leather will rot. Paper will disintegrate. Wood will warp. Untreated metals will corrode—slowly, but faster than you'd expect, because the condensate carries minerals, and minerals carry chemical reactions.

If you must bring electronics, pot them in resin. Seal them in bags inside bags. Accept that their lifespan is limited and bring analog backups.

**Anything That Vibrates**
Do not bring devices that produce mechanical vibration. Personal electronics on silent mode. No massagers (you weren't going to, but now you definitely shouldn't). No devices with rotating drives. The chitterers in the cable-veins harvest energy from vibration—they eat it—and while they concentrate near the noisiest infrastructure, a personal vibration source will draw them. You do not want chitterers nesting in your shelter. They dampen frequencies, which sounds useful until you realize they're dampening the frequencies of your *seals*, your *structure*, your *walls*, making them more prone to failure.

**Anything That Sheds**
Fibers, hairs, skin cells—these are organic material in a mineral ecosystem. The deposition systems don't use them, but the fauna might. The crawlers in the gutters are filter feeders, sort of; the ray-things eat the crawlers; the chitterers eat vibration. But there are other things in the deeper channels, things the biologists only hint at, and organic debris drifting into the water system is a vector for contamination and a lure for investigation. Keep your waste contained. Keep your shed material contained. The locals incinerate their biological waste; you should too, if you can. If you can't, seal it and carry it out with you.

## III. ARRIVAL: THE FIRST HOURS

### The Transition

You will arrive through whatever means your multiverse transit provides, and the first thing you will feel is the vibration. Before the cold. Before the wet. Before the dark. The low, continuous thrum in the stone, entering through the soles of your feet, climbing your skeleton, settling in your chest like a second heartbeat.

Do not panic. This is normal. This is the city breathing.

The second thing you will feel is the moisture. It will find you immediately—on your face, your hands, your eyelashes. The atmosphere is so saturated that condensation forms on any surface that's even slightly cooler than the ambient air, and you just arrived from somewhere else, so you are cool, so you are a template, so the water will choose you. Within minutes, you will be glistening. Within an hour, you will be damp. Within three hours, you will understand that "dry" is a memory from another universe.

The third thing you will feel is the dark. The cloud base is low and the ambient glow of the city is diffuse and strange. Your eyes will strain for edges, for horizons, for the shape of things. You will see the buildings as dense clusters of wet gleam, their surfaces shining with condensation, their forms visible mainly by what light their infrastructure emits. The mountains behind will be invisible. The sky will be invisible. You will feel enclosed, contained, held in a wet dark palm.

### Immediate Actions

1. **Check your seals.** Whatever shelter you've been assigned or have arranged, check every seal. Check the door, the windows, the joints, the corners. Run your fingers along every edge and feel for air movement—the fauna come through gaps, and the cold comes through gaps, and the sound comes through gaps. Seal what needs sealing.

2. **Set up your counter-frequency device.** Do this before you unpack. Do this before you eat. Do this before you sleep. The first night without counter-frequencies is the night newcomers crack. The hum is constant. The chittering is sporadic but unpredictable. The clicking in the walls is rhythmic and wrong. Cancel what you can. Sleep with what you can't.

3. **Learn your shelter's water system.** Every building harvests water. Every structure channels it. Find the channels in your shelter, learn their rhythm, learn what they sound like when they're working correctly so you'll notice when they sound wrong. A change in the water sound can mean a blockage (the fauna), a crack (the pressure), or a deposition overgrowth (the city building itself into its own pipes).

4. **Mark your exit routes.** The streets are dense, narrow, and dark. They gleam with condensate and hum with infrastructure. They are not laid out on a grid—they grew, organically, from the seed of an outpost, and they follow the logic of water and stone, not the logic of navigation. Walk your immediate area. Note landmarks. Note which way the water flows (downhill, always, toward the main channels). Note where the streets widen and where they press close. If you need to leave quickly, you will not be able to see far, and you will need to move by memory.

5. **Introduce yourself to your neighbors.** The locals are forty thousand strong, and they have survived here for generations. They know things your preparation cannot teach you. They know which streets flood when the filtration cycles shift. They know which walls are still growing and which have finished. They know the sound of a healthy channel and the sound of one that's been colonized. They know how to read the fauna's behavior for warnings—when the crawlers move faster, when the chitterers go quiet, when the ray-things in the deep pipes change their pattern. They have adapted. Learn from them. Be respectful. They do not need you, but you need them.

## IV. LIVING IN THE CONDENSATE: DAILY PRACTICES

### Morning Routine

Wake before the filtration shift. You'll feel it in your chest—a subtle pressure change, a sense of something tilting. Check your acoustic monitor. Note the current spectrum. When the shift happens, note the change. Over time, you'll learn to predict it; until then, let the monitor be your early warning.

Check your seals. Every morning. No exceptions. Scrape any deposition growth from your doorframes, your window edges, your shelter's exterior surfaces. The growth is slow, but it's constant, and if you let it accumulate, it will seal your doors shut. This sounds useful until you realize it will also seal your drainage pipes, your ventilation, your access points. The city doesn't distinguish between a door and a wall. To the deposition systems, every surface is a template. It's up to you to maintain the distinction.

Drink filtered water. Eat something. The food here is synthesized, mostly—grown in the same mineral-rich water that feeds the deposition systems, processed and formed into nutrient-dense blocks. It tastes like the condensate: mineral, cold, faintly chemical. It will keep you alive. It will not bring you joy. If you have the means, bring your own food—sealed, dehydrated, calorie-dense. The locals will find it quaint. You will find it sustaining.

### Daily Movement

Walk the streets. Learn the city. But learn it slowly, because the city will not be the same when you walk back.

This is not a metaphor. The deposition systems are always growing. The streets are always slightly narrower than they were last week. The walls are always slightly thicker. The cable-veins between buildings sag slightly less because the lattice has extended. A passage that was comfortable on your first day will be tight on your fifth and claustrophobic on your tenth. You are in a thing that is becoming, and it is becoming *inward*, accreting toward the center of its own passages.

If you feel a space closing around you, do not panic. Do not push through. Back out slowly. The deposition lattice is fragile when young; if you break it, you disrupt the city's growth pattern, and the locals will be unhelpful at best and hostile at worst. The city is not just their shelter; it is their infrastructure, their defense, their future. Damaging it is vandalism of their survival.

### Water Discipline

You are surrounded by water and cannot drink most of it.

The condensate on surfaces has been flowing over fauna, over mineral deposits, over the city's waste systems. The water in the channels is being processed; it's not for you. The water from the filtration systems is safe after two stages of filtering; yours is three stages, because you are not adapted, because your immune system has no relationship with the organisms here.

Conserve your filtered water. Use it for drinking, for cooking, for cleaning wounds. Use unfiltered water for washing, for flushing (if your shelter has a flush system), for anything non-consumptive—but wash your hands afterward, with filtered water, because the crawlers are in the unfiltered water, and the crawlers will crawl.

### Sleep

The locals sleep to the hum. They wake to it. They have never known silence and would find it disturbing.

You will not sleep well at first. This is normal. This is expected. Do not use sleep aids—they will deepen your sleep and make you less responsive to the filtration shifts, the pressure changes, the subtle warnings in the ambient sound. Learn to sleep lightly. Learn to wake at anomalies. Learn to listen to the city's song, because changes in that song are information.

If the chittering penetrates your counter-frequency system, adjust the frequencies. The chitterers shift their ranges seasonally as their population waxes and wanes. Your counter-frequency device must track them. If you hear chittering inside your shelter, you have a gap. Find it. Seal it. The chitterers don't physically threaten humans—they're too small, their metabolism is based on piezoelectric effect, not predation—but their waste products accumulate, and their presence indicates a breach, and breaches let in other things.

### Encounters with the Fauna

You will share the city with billions of organisms. Here is how to coexist:

**The Crawlers** (pale, segmented, fingernail-length, moving in coordinated waves through the condensation gutters): Ignore them. They are part of the water system. Some older families channel them intentionally to carry particulate out of filtration networks. If they enter your shelter—and they will, through any gap, any crack, any momentary opening—sweep them toward the drainage. Do not crush them. Their bodies clog pipes, and clogged pipes cause overflows, and overflows cause pressure changes, and pressure changes cause structural failures. Let them move. They are working.

**The Ray-Things** (flat, eyeless, undulating through the deep pipes, filter-feeding, bioluminescent when not exposed to light): You will rarely see them. They stay in the main channels, in the flow, in the dark. If you see one above the main lines—rare, but not impossible—do not touch it. Their skin secretes a mild adhesive that helps them maintain position in the current, and it will bond to your skin. The bioluminescence is beautiful in the way dangerous things often are. Observe. Do not engage.

**The Chitterers** (piezoelectric metabolisms, nesting in the cable-veins, eating vibration, producing chittering as waste): They are your allies whether you like it or not. They dampen the frequencies that would otherwise vibrate the deposition systems into structural failure. They self-regulate: louder city, more chitterers, more dampening, quieter city. Let them nest. Let them work. If they're in your shelter, you have a gap, but their presence also means your shelter is part of the dampening network, which means it's acoustically protected, which means—paradoxically—that it's safer from vibration damage.

You will not see the things the biologists only hint at. They stay in the deep channels, in the dark, in the places the city hasn't finished mapping. If you find yourself near a deep channel—a main pipe large enough to stand in, a flow that hasn't been charted—leave. You are not equipped for what lives there. The biologists work three stories underground in sealed suits, and they never go above without protection, and "above" is where you are. Below is theirs.

## V. WHAT TO EXPECT PSYCHOLOGICALLY

### The Wet Despair

Around day three or four, you will feel it: a conviction that nothing will ever be dry again. Your skin, your clothes, your shelter, your food, your thoughts—everything will feel saturated, waterlogged, heavy with moisture. This is not inaccurate. But the despair that comes with it is not inevitable; it is a phase, and it passes, or it shifts into something more tolerable.

The locals do not feel this anymore. They have adapted. You can too, but adaptation takes longer than a typical visit. Expect the despair. Plan for it. Bring something to read (on waterproof material), something to write with (on waterproof paper), something to do with your hands that isn't scraping, sealing, or checking. The mind needs occupation that isn't survival. The mind needs relief from the constant monitoring, the constant vigilance, the constant awareness that you are in a place that does not care about you, that does not even notice you, that is busy becoming something you will never see finished.

### The Sound Fatigue

Your ears will hurt. Not from volume—the sound is not dangerous in that way—but from the relentless processing. Your brain will try to find patterns, resolutions, meaning in the chord. There are none. The chord does not resolve. It shifts, twice daily, but it does not progress. It is not building toward anything. It simply *is*, the way a mountain simply is, the way a heartbeat simply is.

Around day five, you may experience auditory hallucinations. Snatches of music. Voices. Sounds that seem to come from specific directions but don't. This is your brain trying to impose order on the acoustic chaos. Recognize it for what it is. Do not follow the voices. Do not try to find the source of the music. It is not there. It is inside you, a desperate pattern-matching system running out of coherent input.

### Theclaustrophobia of Becoming

The walls are growing. The streets are narrowing. The city is accreting around you, slowly, invisibly, centimeter by centimeter. You will feel this as a pressure, a closing-in, a sense that the space you occupy is being reclaimed by the process that made it.

This is not wrong. But it is slow. The deposition takes weeks to visibly change a space. You will be fine. The exits will be fine. The streets will not close around you in your lifetime. But you will *feel* them thinking about it, and that feeling is enough.

## VI. LEAVING

When it is time to leave, leave cleanly. Remove your deposition scrapings. Clean your shelter's seals. Restore any equipment you've borrowed. The locals will not thank you; they will simply note that you did not leave problems, and they will not remember you after a season, because the city does not remember, because the city is not finished remembering itself.

You will leave wet. Your clothes will be damp, your skin will be pruned, your hair will lie flat against your skull. You will taste mineral on your tongue and feel vibration in your chest for days afterward. Your dreams will hum. Your hands will reach for a scraper that isn't there. You will feel condensation forming on every surface and realize you're back in a dry world now, and the wetness is only a memory, and the hum is only a memory, and the dark is only a memory, but it will not feel like only a memory. It will feel like you've left something unfinished.

You have. Everyone does. The Condensate is not finished with anyone who visits it; it is not finished with itself. You will carry the hum in your bones for months. You will dream about water that crawls. You will wake some nights and hear the chord, just for a moment, before your ears remember where they are.

This is normal. This is the cost of visiting a place that is still becoming.

You became a little, too, while you were there. The city printed itself onto you, the way it prints itself onto everything—not as structure, but as pattern, as memory, as a sense that somewhere, in a cold and wet and humming dark, a city is slowly, patiently, geologically still becoming, and you were part of its process for a while, and it did not mind, and it did not notice, and it will continue long after you are gone.

Bring warm clothes. Bring good seals. Bring patience for incompleteness.

And listen. The city is singing. It will be a long time before it stops.
