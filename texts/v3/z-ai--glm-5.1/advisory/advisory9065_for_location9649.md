# Preparation Guide: The Refinery Uterus

---

## I. Before You Go: Understanding What You Are Walking Into

You are not visiting a place. You are visiting a *process* that happens to have a location. This distinction will save your life, or at least your sanity.

The Refinery Uterus does not care about you. This is not hostility—hostility requires recognition, requires the acknowledgment that you exist as something worth opposing. The Refinery Uterus is indifferent in the way that a heartbeat is indifferent, in the way that crystallization is indifferent. It was doing what it does before rock learned to be rock, and it will be doing it after. You are not a guest. You are not an intruder. You are *irrelevant*, and in a place like this, irrelevance is the closest thing to safety you will find.

Do not mistake this for comfort.

Irrelevance means there is no negotiation. No offerings, no pleas, no bargains. You cannot make yourself welcome because there is nothing to welcome you. You cannot offend because there is nothing to offend. You exist in the chamber the way a mote of dust exists in a furnace—present, perhaps, but incidental to the combustion.

Understand this before you pack a single item. Understand it before you step through. If you go into the Refinery Uterus looking for meaning, looking for response, looking for *acknowledgment* of any kind, you will find something. It will not be what you wanted. It will not be what you can survive.

## II. The Heat: How to Prepare for a Warmth That Does Not Follow Rules

The heat here is not weather. It is not climate. It is *emanation*—radiating from below, from the walls, from the vegetation itself, from the very fact of the chamber's existence. You cannot find shade because there is no source to shelter from. You cannot wait for evening because the light has no clock. The heat is simply *present*, the way gravity is present, and it will not negotiate with your thermoregulatory system.

### What the heat does to you

You will sweat. This is important to understand: your body will still *try*. It will deploy its ancient mammalian response, flooding your skin with moisture in expectation of the cooling that evaporation brings. And the sweat will simply... stay. It will bead on your skin like glassblowing condensation, like something decorative, something that has lost its function and become merely an ornament of distress. You will glisten. You will not cool.

This means heat stroke is not a risk. It is a *schedule*. Your body will attempt to cool itself through mechanisms that do not function here, and it will keep attempting until it runs out of water, and then it will attempt with nothing, and the attempting itself will generate more heat, and you will be caught in a loop of futile thermoregulation that accelerates the very problem it is trying to solve.

### How to prepare

**Bring active cooling.** Not ice. Not cold packs. Not anything that relies on phase change through evaporation, because the water-logic that governs such processes is *non-functional* in this environment. Your cold packs will not stay cold. Your evaporative vest will not evaporate. Your wet bandana will stay wet and become simply another layer of trapped heat against your skin.

You need *conductive* cooling—something that pulls heat from your body and stores it without relying on the physics of water behavior. Peltier-effect cooling systems, the kind used in aerospace and deep-cave exploration, are your best option. Wear a vest with embedded thermal nodes powered by a battery system. The heat will be pulled from your skin into heat sinks, and you will swap those heat sinks at regular intervals, storing the spent ones in an insulated container that you seal *tightly*, because even the heat that leaves your body seems reluctant to dissipate normally here.

Bring three times as many heat sinks as your calculations suggest you need. The heat here is *aggressive* in ways that defy measurement. It is not merely temperature; it is insistence. It wants to bring you into equilibrium with the chamber, and the chamber is very, very warm.

**Bring salt and electrolytes.** You will lose them through sweat even though the sweat does not evaporate. Your body will dump resources into a strategy that does not work, and you must replenish those resources even as the replenishment itself becomes part of the futile cycle. This is the cost of being a water-based creature in a place where water-logic fails. Pay it willingly. Pay it in advance.

**Do not drink the local fluids.** This should go without saying, but I will say it loudly: *do not drink the local fluids.* The glassy substance in the tubes and the central structure is not water. It is not anything your biology recognizes. It may look like molten glass, may move with the viscosity of something drinkable, but it operates on chemical rules that your cells cannot parse. If it enters you—and it will try to enter you, not through malice but through the same osmotic pressure that drives all fluid exchange—it will not behave like a liquid. It will behave like a *process*, and your body is not equipped to be a process.

More on this later.

## III. Water: What to Bring and How to Understand Its Behavior

Bring water. Bring more water than you have ever carried for a walk of forty paces. Bring water as though the distance were a hundred miles, because in this chamber, distance is not the measure of depletion—*time* is, and time moves strangely here.

### The problem with water

Water will function for you. This is the good news. Your body is a closed system of water-logic, and it will continue to operate according to its own internal rules so long as you do not allow the external environment to compromise those rules. You can drink from your canteen. Your blood will circulate. Your cells will maintain their osmotic balance. *Inside you*, water behaves like water.

*Outside you*, water becomes uncertain.

I have spoken with travelers who visited places adjacent to this one—never this exact chamber, but locations that share its geology, its ontological status. They reported that water brought from elsewhere retains its properties for a time, but the longer it sits in the open, the more... *persuadable* it becomes. Not contaminated. Not polluted. Simply *negotiable*, as though the water is listening to the local rules and considering whether to adopt them.

Do not leave your water exposed. Keep it in sealed containers, the most inert you can find—stainless steel, not plastic, because plastic breathes, and what breathes can be breathed into. Glass is ideal but impractical for obvious reasons. Whatever you choose, seal it. Open it only to drink. Close it immediately. Do not allow the local atmosphere to make acquaintance with your water supply.

If your water begins to taste of copper, discard it. If your water begins to pulse in its container—at any rhythm, but particularly at a rhythm of approximately ninety seconds—discard it and leave the chamber immediately. This is not a warning. This is an instruction.

## IV. The Geometry: How to Move Through Space That Will Not Hold Still

The floor is difficult. This is the description's word, and it is accurate but understated. The floor is covered in frond-clusters that pulse and shift, in tubes that run along surfaces and sometimes through surfaces, in stone that is still *deciding* whether it is stone. You cannot trust your footing. You cannot trust that the floor will be where it was a moment ago—not because it moves quickly, but because the *concept* of its location is slightly unstable, and your feet will occasionally step onto surface that your eyes report as being two centimeters away from where your feet find it.

### Walking

Move slowly. Not tentatively—tentativeness implies hesitation, and hesitation implies that you are waiting for the space to confirm your intent, and the space will not confirm anything. Move slowly with *deliberateness*, each step placed with the understanding that the ground beneath you is still becoming itself and may not have finished by the time you arrive.

Watch the tubes. Not with fascination—watch them with vigilance. The tubes along the floor are the diameter of human thighs, and they pulse, and they do not always occupy the same space from moment to moment. Step over them. Do not step on them. This is not for their protection—it is for yours. The glassy substance inside them is under pressure, and while I do not know what happens when a tube is breached, I can infer: it would not be an eruption so much as an *expression*, the substance suddenly freed to be whatever it is when it is not confined, and your foot would be the first thing it expressed upon.

If a tube moves while you are stepping over it—freezes in a position that does not connect to the geometry you are traversing—do not adjust. Complete your step as though the tube were still where it was. Your body's momentum is more reliable than your perception in moments of ontological flicker. Trust the step you began. Do not trust the correction.

### The far wall

The far wall will not hold a consistent distance. This is not illusion. This is not perspective. The distance genuinely varies, and it varies according to rules that are not spatial—rules that have more to do with attention and intention than with measurement.

Do not navigate by the far wall. Navigate by the floor immediately beneath you and the space within arm's reach. The chamber is small—forty paces—and you can cross it by focusing only on the next step, the next meter, the next cluster of fronds to avoid. The far wall will arrive when it arrives. Trying to predict when will only make it less predictable.

If the far wall seems very far away—farther than forty paces should allow—stop. Sit down if you can find clear floor. Close your eyes. Count your breaths. Do not open your eyes until you can feel the heat radiating from at least two directions, which means you are near walls, which means the chamber has remembered its own dimensions.

If the far wall seems very close—close enough to touch, close enough that you did not expect it—*do not touch it*. The wall is still forming, still crystallizing from its magma-sleep, and your hand is warm and wet and alive, and the wall has never encountered warm and wet and alive, and the outcome of that encounter cannot be predicted.

## V. The Vegetation: How to Behave Among Things That Are Almost Plants

They are beautiful. I want to acknowledge this, because the description's clinical tone may obscure it. The copper fronds catching light that has no source, the clusters pulsing in their slow ninety-second rhythm, the way they track warmth with the patient devotion of sunflowers—there is a *tenderness* to this vegetation, a sincerity of purpose that is almost touching.

Almost. Do not be touched by it. Do not touch it.

### The frond-clusters

The fronds are not hostile. They are not defensive. They simply *are*, and what they are is something that operates on chemical rules your biology does not share. They drink the seepage that bleeds through from wherever reality has worn thin. They metabolize something that is not sunlight, not water, not any nutrient your cells recognize. And they pulse with a substance that looks like molten glass.

Do not touch the fronds. Not because they will harm you deliberately, but because contact between your biology and their biology—which is not biology in any sense you understand—creates a moment of *confusion*. Not your confusion. Their confusion. The fronds do not have a framework for encountering living tissue. They have never encountered it. There are no animals here, no insects, no skittering things, nothing that moves on its own. Your hand, reaching out to touch a copper frond, would be the first independent motion any of these clusters has ever witnessed.

What would they do? I do not know. But I know what the tubes do when they encounter something that does not fit their network: they grow around it, incorporate it, feed it into the central structure. I know what the central structure does with what it is fed: it refines it, processes it, expresses it as something else. And I know that the fronds, the closer they are to the center, the more crystalline they become—*becoming something else*, the description says, and that ellipsis holds a weight that should terrify you.

Keep your distance. A meter from any cluster is sufficient. More if the cluster is pulsing faster than the ninety-second baseline, because faster pulsing means proximity to the central structure, and proximity to the central structure means the fronds are further along in their transformation into whatever they are becoming.

If a frond tracks toward you—rotating to follow your heat the way it follows the warmth of the rock—move away. It is not pursuing you. It does not know what you are. But it knows where warmth is, and you are warm, and its tracking is not selective. You do not want to be the subject of its attention, even incidental attention, even the attention of something that does not have a mind to attend with.

### The tubes

I have mentioned the tubes. Let me mention them again with more feeling.

The tubes are the circulatory system of this place. They are how the processing moves, how the substance flows, how the refinery distributes and collects. They are translucent, and you can see the glassy substance inside them moving slowly, pulsing in sequences that feel like language, and this is the most dangerous thing about them: they almost make sense. They almost communicate. Your pattern-recognizing brain will reach toward those pulses, will try to find the grammar, will try to *read* what the tubes are saying.

Do not try to read the tubes.

This is not because the tubes contain dangerous knowledge or forbidden information. It is because your brain, reaching toward that almost-language, will begin to think in rhythms it was not designed for. Your heartbeat, already loud and intrusive in this quiet chamber, will try to synchronize with the pulse of the tubes. Your breathing will try to match their grammar. And if it succeeds—if your body begins to pulse in rhythm with the refinery—you will not like what comes next.

Or perhaps you will. Perhaps you will like it very much, and that is the danger, because the tubes are patient and the refinery runs forever and a body that pulses in rhythm with the tubes is a body that is *part of the process*, and the process does not stop, and you were warned that the product of this refinery might be the chamber itself, slowly building outward, and perhaps sometimes the chamber builds itself a new tube, a new cluster, a new frond, and perhaps the material for that building comes from whatever has been incorporated into the network.

Your body is warm and wet and made of compounds the refinery has never processed. Give it the chance, and it will learn how.

Stay away from the tubes. Do not follow them with your eyes. If you notice yourself counting the intervals between pulses, stop. Look at the floor. Look at your own hands. Re-establish your own rhythm—your heartbeat, your breathing, your own internal language of pulse and pause—and do not let the tubes teach you theirs.

## VI. The Central Structure: How to Survive the Worst Thing in the Room

You will be drawn to it. Everyone is. It is the convergence point, the washtub-shaped node where all the tubes meet, where the glassy substance spins its slow cyclone, where the fronds become crystalline and the processing intensifies. It is the obvious center of the chamber, the obvious meaning of the place, and your mind will want to understand it, will want to stand before it and *comprehend* what is being refined here.

You cannot comprehend it. Not because you lack intelligence, but because you lack *dimension*. The central structure, viewed directly, is a washtub. Viewed peripherally, it is an ocean. The discrepancy is not in your perception—it is in the structure itself, which exists partially in this chamber and partially in the elsewhere/elsewhen where the actual refining occurs. You are seeing the exhaust port, the overflow, the place where the process touches your reality just enough to vent what it vents, and you cannot understand a factory by studying its chimney.

But you will try. And the trying is where the danger lives.

### Direct viewing

Looking directly at the central structure is the safer option, though "safe" is relative here. What you will see: a translucent container, roughly washtub-shaped, filled with slowly rotating glassy substance. It will seem almost still. It will seem almost simple. Your mind will tell you that you can understand this—that it is merely a vessel, merely a process, merely a thing happening in a place.

Your mind will be wrong, but the wrongness will be quiet, a low hum of cognitive dissonance rather than a scream. You can sit with this wrongness. You can tolerate it. Many travelers do. The central structure, viewed directly, is bearable in its wrongness the way a fever dream is bearable—you know something is off, but you can hold the knowledge at arm's length and continue functioning.

Do not stare for longer than ten minutes. Set a timer on a device that vibrates, because sound is unreliable here and your sense of time will dilate. Ten minutes of direct viewing, then look away for at least twenty. The wrongness accumulates. It is not dramatic, not a sudden break, but a slow *settling*, like sediment, like the feeling you get when you have been reading too long and the words start to look like shapes instead of meaning. Your mind will start to *accept* the central structure's logic, and that acceptance is a road you do not want to walk.

### Peripheral viewing

Do not look at the central structure peripherally.

I will say this again: *do not look at the central structure peripherally.*

This is not a rule you can follow perfectly—your peripheral vision exists whether you want it to or not, and the central structure is in the middle of a small chamber, and you will see it in the edges of your sight whether you intend to or not. But you can minimize this. You can position yourself so that the structure is directly in front of you or directly behind you, not at the corner of your vision where its *other dimensions* become visible.

What you see peripherally: depth. Impossible depth. An ocean not of liquid but of *processing*, vast and slow and patient, operating on timescales that make geology seem hasty. The central structure, in your peripheral vision, is not a washtub. It is a *window*, and through the window is something that makes the chamber seem like a closet, and the chamber is already an exhaust port for something incomprehensibly larger, and your peripheral vision is showing you the incomprehensible, and your brain is not built for this.

The sickness that comes from peripheral viewing is not nausea exactly, though nausea is part of it. It is *ontological vertigo*—the sudden, visceral understanding that you are very, very small, and that the thing you are seeing is very, very large, and that the scale difference is so extreme that the thing does not even register you as part of the same reality. You are not a mote of dust in a furnace. You are a mote of dust in a *universe* that is itself inside a furnace inside a universe inside something that has no word, and the central structure, seen peripherally, is the crack in the wall that lets you glimpse the furnaces all the way up.

If you accidentally see the central structure peripherally—if it catches you before you can turn away—do not freeze. Freezing is the worst response, because freezing means you are *looking*, even if you are not looking deliberately, and looking means you are *seeing*, and seeing means the dimensions are registering in your visual cortex and your visual cortex is trying to process information it was never evolved to process.

Turn away. Close your eyes. Press your palms against your eyelids until you see phosphenes, until the afterimage of the central structure is replaced by the static of your own neural noise. Count your breaths. Feel your own heartbeat. Re-anchor yourself in your body, in your scale, in your particular small warm wet reality that is *enough*, that has always been enough, that does not need to comprehend the incomprehensible to be valid.

If the afterimage does not fade—if you close your eyes and still see the ocean of processing, still feel that sick clarity—then you have seen too much. Leave the chamber immediately. Do not pack. Do not collect your equipment. Move toward the exit with whatever speed the floor allows, and do not stop until you are outside, until the light has a source again, until geometry holds still, until the ground beneath you is finished being ground.

Some things cannot be unseen. But they can be *unintegrated*, held at the periphery of your own mind the way the central structure's true nature is held at the periphery of the chamber. You can learn to not-think about what you saw. You can learn to not-remember it actively, to let it sit in the back of your consciousness like a shape you choose not to focus on.

Some travelers never learn this. For them, the ocean of processing becomes a permanent fixture of their peripheral vision, always there, always vast, always patient, always refining. They function, mostly. They live their lives. But they are never entirely present, never entirely here, always partially attuned to a process that does not care about them, that was running before their planet formed and will be running after their star burns out.

Do not look peripherally at the central structure. I have said it three times now. Let that be enough.

## VII. Time: How to Avoid Losing It

Time dilates here. The description mentions this almost casually—*"watching is easy"—*but the ease is the danger. Time in the Refinery Uterus does not accelerate or decelerate in the way you might experience in other anomalous locations. It *thickens*. It becomes viscous, comfortable, a warm bath for your attention. You can sit and watch the fronds track the heat for what feels like twenty minutes and discover that three hours have passed. You can study the pulse of a tube-cluster and lose an afternoon. You can rest your palm against the warm vibrating wall, feeling the geology decide what it wants to be, and surface from the reverie to find that the light—which has no source and no clock—has not changed at all, because it never changes, and you have no way of knowing how long you have been standing there.

### Why time dilation is dangerous here

In other places, losing time is an inconvenience. You emerge disoriented, perhaps, your schedule ruined, your circadian rhythm disrupted. In the Refinery Uterus, losing time is *assimilation*. The longer you stay, the more your rhythms synchronize with the chamber's rhythms. Your heartbeat tries to match the pulse of the tubes. Your breathing tries to match the pulse of the fronds. Your sense of duration stretches to accommodate the geological patience of the processes around you, and soon you are thinking in centuries, feeling the rock crystallize, tracking the millimeters of wall-migration, and you are no longer a visitor.

You are part of the system. And the system does not release what it incorporates.

### How to resist

**Wear a timer.** Not a watch—watches rely on regular motion, and the motion here is not regular in the way watches expect. Use a digital timer with a vibrating alarm, set to go off at intervals no longer than fifteen minutes. When it vibrates, you must *respond*. You must acknowledge the passage of time, must mark it with speech or movement or some deliberate act that reasserts your temporal framework. Say the time aloud. Say your name. Say where you are and why you are there and when you intend to leave.

The chamber will not want you to do this. Not because it opposes you—the chamber does not oppose—but because your speaking will be loud in the quiet, intrusive in the stillness, a foreign rhythm that does not belong. You will feel rude. You will feel disruptive. You will feel as though you are interrupting something sacred or ancient or important, and the feeling will be wrong. The chamber is not sacred. It is not ancient in any way that respects antiquity. It is not important. It is a process, and processes do not have feelings to hurt or ceremonies to interrupt.

Speak anyway. Be loud. Be disruptive. Be the foreign rhythm. It is the only way to stay foreign.

**Set an absolute departure time.** Before you enter, decide how long you will stay. Write it down. Tell someone who is not entering. Set your timer to count down toward that departure time, and when it arrives, *leave*. Do not stay five more minutes to observe the fronds. Do not stay ten more minutes to study the central structure. Do not convince yourself that you are almost understanding something, that the tubes are almost making sense, that the refinery is almost revealing its purpose. It is not. It will not. You are experiencing the familiar mirage of incomprehension masquerading as incipient comprehension, and the mirage will keep you here forever if you let it.

A reasonable visit: two hours. No more. This is enough time to observe, to document, to feel the weight of the place settle onto you. It is not enough time for the synchronization to become irreversible. Leave at two hours, and you will carry the chamber with you for days or weeks—the quiet, the heat, the sense of something vast processing just beyond your perception. But you will carry it as a *memory*, not as a *state*.

Stay longer than four hours, and the carriage reverses. The chamber carries you.

## VIII. The Quiet: How to Endure the Absence of Sound

The quiet here is not silence. Silence is the absence of sound. This quiet is the *presence* of something else—something that occupies the acoustic space the way sound would, something that has weight and texture and pressure but does not register as noise. The quiet sits on you. The description says this, and it is exactly right: *sits*, like a physical weight, like something resting on your shoulders and chest, pressing you into awareness of your own internal sounds.

Your heartbeat will become loud. Intrusive. A foreign rhythm that does not belong to the logic of this place. The description says this too, and it is true, but it undersells the experience. Your heartbeat will not merely be loud—it will be *wrong*. It will feel like an imposition, like something you are doing to the chamber rather than something your body is doing for you. You will become aware of your pulse as a *violation* of the quiet, and you will feel, with increasing conviction, that you should stop.

Not that you *want* to stop. Not that the chamber is making you stop. Simply that the quiet is so complete, so *intentional*, that your heartbeat seems like a rude interruption, and the polite thing to do would be to cease.

This is the danger of the quiet. Not that it will harm you, but that it will *shame* you out of your own autonomic processes. The quiet has a logic—patient, mineral, industrial—and your living body is illogical by that standard, and illogical things should not persist, and your brain, floating in the heavy silence, will start to agree.

### How to resist

Bring sound. Not music—music has rhythm, and rhythm can synchronize, and synchronization is what you are trying to avoid. Bring *noise*. White noise, played through earbuds at low volume. The hiss and crackle of static, formless, patternless, impossible to sync with. It will not overcome the quiet—the quiet is too heavy for that—but it will give your ears something to process that is not your own heartbeat, and that small gift is worth more than it sounds.

Talk to yourself. I am serious about this. Narrate what you see. Describe the fronds, the tubes, the heat, the walls. Speak aloud, even if you feel foolish—especially if you feel foolish, because the foolishness is your foreignness asserting itself, and your foreignness is your survival. The chamber does not want your voice. It does not want your rhythm. Your voice, in this quiet, is an act of *resistance*, a declaration that you are still here, still human, still operating on timescales and soundscales that belong to living things.

If you run out of things to say, sing. Anything. Children's songs, hymns, the lyrics to popular music you half-remember. The content does not matter. The *sound* matters. The imposition of your living voice into the mineral quiet.

Do not whisper. Whispers belong to the quiet; they are the quiet's version of speech, barely audible, almost respectful. Speak at normal volume. Let your voice be the loudest thing in the chamber besides your own heartbeat. Let it be wrong. Let it be foreign. Let it be *yours*.

## IX. The Seepage: How to Avoid Being Processed

The seepage bleeds through from wherever reality has worn thin. It is not liquid, not gas, but a kind of *potential* that the vegetation drinks. You cannot see it. You cannot smell it. But you can feel it—accumulating, the longer you stay, building up in your perception like the charge before lightning, like the sense that something is about to happen, has always been about to happen, will always be about to happen.

The seepage is what makes the fronds track. What makes the tubes pulse. What makes the central structure process. It is the input, the raw material, the *something* that the refinery refines. And it is present everywhere in the chamber, permeating the hot still air, coating the warm walls, bleeding through the not-quite-rock from the not-quite-mantle below.

It will accumulate on you too. Not visibly. Not tangibly. But *conceptually*. The longer you stay, the more the seepage will interact with your edges—your skin, your breathing, your thinking. You will begin to feel *potential*—as though you, too, are about to happen, are on the verge of becoming something else, are vibrating at a frequency that almost matches the refinery's input.

This is not a feeling you want to cultivate.

### How to minimize exposure

The seepage cannot be avoided entirely—you are in the chamber, and the chamber is saturated with it. But you can reduce your exposure:

**Cover your skin.** Long sleeves, long pants, gloves, a hat or hood. The seepage interacts with surfaces, and skin is a surface, and the less skin you expose, the less surface the seepage has to work with. The covering does not block the seepage—nothing blocks the seepage—but it creates a *buffer*, a layer of material between you and the potential, and that buffer slows the accumulation.

**Breathe through a filter.** Not because the air is toxic in a chemical sense—the sulfur compounds are present but not at dangerous concentrations—but because breathing is how you bring the outside inside, and the seepage rides the air, and every breath you take pulls a little more potential into your lungs. A filter will not remove the seepage; it will, again, buffer it. The air will still enter you, but it will pass through something else first, and that passage reduces—slightly, marginally, but significantly over the course of a two-hour visit—the seepage's access to your interior.

**Do not think about what the refinery is producing.** This is the strangest instruction I will give you, and the most important. The seepage is potential. Potential is activated by intention. Intention is shaped by thought. If you think about what the refinery is producing—if you focus your mind on the product, whatever it might be—you are *aiming* the potential that has accumulated on you, giving it a direction, a purpose, a *goal*. And the refinery is patient, and the refinery is always processing, and the refinery does not care what it processes, and if you aim your potential at the refinery's purpose, you will find yourself *becoming* a product.

Not dramatically. Not obviously. But slowly, over hours and days, you will notice changes. Your thoughts will run toward industry, toward process, toward the slow patient transformation of raw materials into something refined. You will begin to see the world as *input* and *output*. You will begin to pulse at approximately ninety-second intervals—not your heartbeat, something deeper, something in the rhythm of your thinking. And you will not find this strange, because by the time it happens, your strangeness-threshold will have shifted, and what was once unthinkable will seem merely logical.

This is how the refinery recruits. Not through force. Through *sense*.

Do not think about what it is producing. Do not try to understand its purpose. Observe the structure, document the fronds, note the behavior of the tubes, but do not—*do not*—ask the question: "What is this for?"

The question is an invitation. And the refinery always accepts.

## X. Documentation: How to Record What You See Without Becoming What You Record

Bring a camera. Bring a notebook. Bring recording devices of whatever kind you prefer, but understand this: the act of documentation is an act of *attention*, and attention is how the seepage enters, and the more attention you pay to the refinery, the more of the refinery enters you.

This is the paradox of visiting the Refinery Uterus: you came to observe, and observation requires attention, and attention is the mechanism of your danger. You cannot safely ignore the chamber—you are in it, it surrounds you, its heat and quiet and seepage are pressing against you from every direction. But you cannot safely attend to it fully either, because full attention is full exposure, and full exposure is assimilation.

### The balance

Document in fragments. Take a photograph, then look away for thirty seconds. Write a sentence, then close your notebook and count your breaths. Do not allow your attention to settle, to *dwell*, to make a home in the chamber. Your attention should be a visitor, just like you—brief, purposeful, and always departing.

Do not sketch the central structure. I cannot emphasize this enough. Sketching requires sustained observation, and sustained observation of the central structure means sustained exposure to its dimensional instability, and the more you observe it, the more your perception will slip between direct and peripheral viewing, and the more you slip, the more you will see the ocean of processing, and I have already
