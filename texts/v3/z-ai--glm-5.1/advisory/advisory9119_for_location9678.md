# Preparing for the Capacitor Grove

## What You Are Walking Into

You are not visiting a place. You are entering an organism that happens to contain people.

This distinction will matter more than you expect. The Capacitor Grove is a living structure—biofabricated cellulose walls, a breathing canopy, root-felt flooring, metabolic temperature control, water harvested from air by purpose-grown fibers. It has reached equilibrium with its own decay. It has survived its infancy. It does not need you. The handful of humans who live here are symbionts, not occupants, and you will feel that difference in your bones within the first hour.

Prepare accordingly.

---

## Physical Preparation

### Clothing

**Layers. Light layers.** The temperature inside the grove is steady and comfortable—what you might call "cool room temperature"—but it is not uniform. The workhouse radiates warmth from its generator and press. The open spaces between structures carry that constant cool dampness. The area near the walls runs cooler still. You will walk through microclimates, and your body will notice each transition.

Avoid heavy coats. Avoid anything that traps moisture against your skin. The ambient humidity is high enough that your body's own heat will create a clammy microenvironment under impermeable fabrics. Cotton is acceptable. Merino wool is better. Synthetic moisture-wicking materials are best, if you have access to them from your home universe.

**Long sleeves. Long pants.** The reason is not temperature but contact. You will brush against bark walls. You will reach out to steady yourself on structural branches. You will, almost certainly, press your palm against the warm surface of the wall just to feel it—to confirm that it feels like skin, because it does, and the first time you touch it unprepared, something in your hindbrain will stutter. Long sleeves give you a thin barrier between your skin and the grove's skin, and that barrier matters more for your psychological comfort than for any physical protection.

**Quiet footwear.** The root-felt floor muffles footsteps naturally, but hard-soled boots will still produce a flat, percussive sound that feels wrong in this space. The grove is quiet—generator hum, dripping condensation, the systolic rhythm of its breathing, the creak of growth—and your footsteps should not dominate that soundscape. Soft soles. Flexible shoes. Something that lets you feel the spring of the substrate beneath your feet.

**A light headlamp, not a torch.** The light inside the grove is dappled, green-tinged, moderate—like the floor of an old-growth forest. You can see well enough to walk and work, but you cannot read fine print, and you will struggle with detailed visual tasks in the spaces between structures. A headlamp with a low setting will solve this without flooding the space with jarring white light. Keep it dim. Keep it warm-tinted if possible. The grove's own illumination is golden-green, and your lamp should respect it.

### Equipment

**Waterproof your electronics.** The air carries moisture that never quite becomes wetness, but "never quite" is a description of averages, not guarantees. The condensation system pulls water from the air constantly—*plink, plink, plink*—and droplets form on any surface that sits still long enough, including your gear. Ziplock bags. Silica gel packets. Dry bags for anything essential. The grove's humidity is managed, but it is not managed for your devices.

**Bring analog backups.** The electrical system here is integrated into living wood—copper coils wound through bark, rubber-sheathed conduit engulfed by growing cellulose, patched with tape that is older than you are. It works. It has worked for decades. But the voltage is presumably modest, the grounding is organic, and you should not assume compatibility with your own universe's charging standards. If you need to record information, bring paper. If you need to navigate, bring a compass and note that the canopy may interfere with celestial navigation.

**A small tool kit.** Not for the grove—it does not need your repairs—but for the machines. The workhouse contains a press, a loom, a generator. These are the grove's guests, not its components, and they are the things most likely to need adjustment. Screwdrivers. Pliers. Electrical tape. Nothing elaborate. Just enough to be useful if someone asks for help, and someone might, because you are one of the few people here who is not already absorbed into the grove's routines.

**A water bottle, but do not expect to need it.** The grove harvests water from air. The residents have access to clean water from buried reservoirs. You will not die of thirst here. But the ambient humidity plays tricks on your perception of hydration—you feel damp constantly, but you are still losing moisture through respiration and exertion. Drink before you feel thirsty.

### Health and Biological Considerations

**Know your allergies before you arrive.** The air contains plant matter. Not pollen in the conventional sense—the grove's reproductive systems are managed, not wild—but particulates from the canopy, fibers from the loom, aerosolized sap, and the waste products of whatever maintenance organisms live in the upper branches. If you react to any botanical compound, you will likely encounter something that triggers it here. Bring antihistamines. Bring an inhaler if you have even a hint of asthma. The grove's air is breathable, but it is not your air.

**Prepare for barometric sensation.** The grove breathes. The chamber expands and contracts by a centimeter with each cycle, and the air pressure shifts in your ears. This is not dangerous, but it is relentless, and if you are sensitive to pressure changes—if you suffer from migraines, sinus issues, ear barotrauma—you will notice it within the first hour. Some travelers report a low-grade headache after extended exposure that fades once they leave. Others find it soporific, like being rocked. You will not know which category you fall into until you arrive.

**Eat lightly before entering.** This is not a physical hazard but a psychological one. The grove's rhythm—inhale, exhale, expand, contract—will sync with your own breathing if you let it, and many travelers find that their appetite and digestion shift to match the grove's metabolic pace, which is slow. A heavy meal before arrival will sit in your stomach like a stone while the chamber around you breathes.

## Psychological Preparation

### The Rhythm Problem

This is the most important thing I can tell you: **the grove will try to sync with you, and you will try to sync with it.**

You will not notice it happening. You will walk into the space, and you will hear the breathing, and within fifteen minutes your own respiration will have shifted to match it. Inhale when the chamber expands. Exhale when it contracts. It feels natural. It feels correct. It feels like the space is accommodating you, welcoming you, making room for you in its rhythm.

This is not dangerous. The residents have lived with it for years, perhaps generations, and they show no ill effects. But it is disorienting when you finally notice it—when you realize that your body has been breathing in time with a building, that your heartbeat has slowed to match a plant's metabolism, that you have become, momentarily, a component of something larger than yourself.

The solution is not to fight it. Fighting it creates a dissonance that is far more uncomfortable than the synchronization. Instead, simply be aware that it will happen. Notice it. Name it when it occurs: *I am breathing with the grove.* Then choose whether to continue. The choice itself is usually enough to maintain your autonomy.

### The Warmth Problem

The walls feel like skin.

I am telling you this now so that you are not startled when you touch them, because you will touch them. Everyone does. The bark looks like bark—grey-green, weathered, patched with lichen—but it is warm to the touch, and it gives slightly under pressure, and the fine root-fibers that trace its surface feel almost like the nap of velvet, and your brain will produce a cascade of associations that have nothing to do with plants and everything to do with bodies.

Some travelers find this deeply unpleasant. Others find it comforting. Most find it uncanny in the precise technical sense: familiar and unfamiliar at once, homelike and profoundly not. The feeling accumulates. The first touch is a curiosity. The fifth is a pattern. The twentieth is a fact of existence, and by then you will have stopped flinching, or you will have developed a habit of keeping your hands in your pockets.

### The Scale Problem

The grove is cathedral-scale and sparse. You can walk its perimeter in four minutes. There are seven structures. The distance between them is generous, deliberate, designed for air circulation and microclimate management. But the space *feels* larger because so much of it is empty, and the emptiness is not desolation—it is purpose.

You may feel exposed. You may feel isolated. The openness between structures means that you are visible from most points in the grove, and the quiet means that you are audible from most points as well. There is nowhere to hide here, and no reason to hide, and the residents—who move with unhurried purpose between their tasks—will not stare at you or interrogate you but will simply note your presence and continue their work.

This can be harder than being watched. Being ignored in a small, enclosed, living space produces its own kind of self-consciousness.

### The Symbiont Problem

You are not the point of this place. The grove was not built for you. It does not require your presence to function. The humans who live here are catalysts—needed for the mechanical components, the growth direction, the harvest—but they are not the organizing principle. The plant is the organizing principle. You are walking inside something that was alive before you arrived and will be alive after you leave.

Some travelers find this liberating. Others find it annihilating. Most find it simply strange, a reorientation of the usual hierarchy between person and shelter, between inhabitant and habitat. You are not the protagonist here. You are a symbiont, temporary and replaceable, and the grove's indifference to your individual existence is not hostility—it is simply the perspective of an organism that operates on a longer timeline than you do.

## Social Preparation

There are not many people here. You may see one at a distance, moving between structures with unhurried purpose. They may not notice you, or they may not care.

This does not mean they are hostile. It means they are absorbed in the grove's routines, and those routines do not include greeting travelers. If you need something—directions, water, access to a structure—approach directly and ask. Speak at a normal volume; the acoustics carry sound well. Be brief. Be specific. Do not expect ceremony.

The residents' relationship with the grove is intimate and long-term. They have breathed in sync with it for years. They know where the condensation pools, where the generator hum is loudest, where the bark is warmest in the morning and coolest at night. They are not shamans or mystics; they are technicians and gardeners, maintaining a system that is half industrial and half biological. Respect their expertise. Do not assume you understand how the grove works after an hour of observation. You do not.

If you are offered work—helping with the loom, pressing seed pods, clearing debris from the root-felt—accept it. Work is the primary social currency here. It integrates you into the grove's rhythms. It gives you a reason to be present. It teaches you the pace of the place, which is slower than you expect and more deliberate.

## What to Leave Behind

**Weapons.** There is nothing here that requires them, and the sight of a weapon disrupts the grove's equilibrium in ways that are social rather than physical—the residents will not trust you, and the grove itself will seem, if such a thing is possible, less welcoming. This is probably projection. But probably is not certainly.

**Loud music.** Loud anything. The grove's soundscape is quiet but dense—layers of hum and drip and breath and growth that reward careful listening. Imposing your own noise on this space is not just rude; it prevents you from perceiving the place as it actually is.

**Expectations of speed.** The grove operates on metabolic time. Things grow at the speed of cellulose. Decisions are made at the speed of roots finding water. If you need something to happen quickly, you will be frustrated. If you can adapt to the pace, you will find it restful, or at least instructive.

**The assumption that you understand what you are looking at.** You will look at the bark walls and see wood. You will look at the conduit and see wiring. You will look at the generator and see industry. All of these are correct, and all of them are incomplete. The bark is alive and growing. The conduit is being engulfed. The generator is a guest in a living system, tolerated because it produces something the grove needs, and when it fails—as it eventually will—the grove will grow over its corpse and convert its copper into nutrients. Keep this in mind. The biological is the host. The industrial is the guest. You are the guest.

---

## Final Note

After a few hours in the grove, you will stop noticing the breathing. Your body will have synchronized, your ears will have adjusted, and the systolic rhythm will fade into the background of your awareness along with your own heartbeat and the faint taste of sap in the air.

This is fine. This is normal. This is what the residents experience every day.

But before you leave—before you step back through the walls into whatever outside awaits you—stand still for a moment. Press your palm flat against the bark. Feel the warmth. Feel the give. Feel the slow pulse of sap moving beneath the surface, the expansion and contraction of the chamber around you, the weight of the living canopy overhead.

Notice that you are breathing in time with a place that is older than your visit and younger than its eventual death. Notice that for a little while, you were part of something that did not need you but made room for you anyway.

Then step out. The rhythm will break. Your breathing will be your own again.

You will miss it, or you will not. Either response is correct.
