# Preparing for the Undermill

## What You Are Walking Into

You are not visiting a place. You are inserting yourself into a closed system that achieved equilibrium before your species existed and will continue unchanged long after you leave. The Undermill does not care about you. It does not notice you. This is not a comfort — it is the core hazard. Places that notice you can be negotiated with. Places that ignore you will simply continue their operation while you dissolve inside them.

Understand: the Undermill is a machine that became an ecosystem, or an ecosystem that became a machine, and the distinction stopped mattering so long ago that even the rocks have forgotten. You will be entering something that has solved itself. It does not need you. It will not adapt to you. You must adapt to it, and you must do so while remembering that adaptation is exactly what the Undermill wants from you.

The fauna accepted the chair. They filled it with their bodies and their resin and their dying until it became a pillar. They will accept you the same way if you let them. If you stay. If you become still.

You must not become still.

---

## The Sound: Your First and Constant Enemy

You will hear the Undermill before you see it. By the time you reach the chamber, the sound will already be inside you. This is not metaphor. The low-frequency mechanical roar operates at frequencies that pass through flesh and bone, that vibrate your sternum, that shake the fluid in your inner ear. You will become a resonance chamber for the machines. Your body will amplify what it cannot block.

**Hearing protection is necessary and insufficient.**

Standard earplugs or earmuffs will reduce the mid-range frequencies — the whines, the clicks, the piston-slams — and this will help preserve your hearing in the conventional sense. But the deep thrum, the subsonic and near-subsonic frequencies that the machines produce, will not be stopped by foam or sealant. These frequencies do not enter through your ear canal. They enter through your skeleton, through your chest cavity, through the air pressure against your skin.

**What to bring:**

- **Industrial-grade earmuffs rated for continuous low-frequency exposure** (NRR 30+). Wear them the entire time. Do not remove them for any reason. The moment you take them off to "listen better," you are making a mistake that compounds with every second of exposure.

- **Active noise-canceling over-ear protection** designed for engine rooms and industrial plants. Layer these beneath the passive earmuffs. The combination will handle more of the frequency range than either alone.

- **Vibration-dampening chest wrap.** This sounds absurd. It is not. A thick neoprene or compression garment around your torso will not stop the subsonics, but it will reduce the amplitude of the chest-wall vibrations slightly, and that slight reduction may be the difference between maintaining clear thought and losing yourself to the thrum after four hours.

- **A jaw clamp or bite guard.** The vibrations will cause your teeth to micro-chatter. You will not notice this happening. After six hours, you will have a headache that makes rational thought difficult, and you will not connect it to your jaw. A bite guard prevents the damage.

**What to watch for:**

The sound does things to time perception. The piston-rhythm is so constant that your brain will attempt to synchronize with it, to find pattern in what has moved past pattern into simply *the way things are.* You may lose track of how long you have been in the chamber. You may feel that you have always been in the chamber. This is the sound working on you. Check your watch. Check it often. Write down the time when you enter and the time you have committed to leave.

If you find yourself thinking "a few more minutes won't matter," you have already stayed too long.

## The Heat: A Slow Incapacitation

The chamber is warm. Not hot — not immediately dangerous in the way that fire or boiling water is dangerous. Warm. Kiln-warm. The steady-state warmth of machines that have been running for geological ages, radiating their friction into stone that holds temperature like a grudge.

You will sweat immediately. The sweat will not evaporate. The air is thick, recirculated, saturated with the moisture of the machines' combustion cycles and the collective respiration of thousands of fauna. Your body's primary cooling mechanism — evaporation — will simply stop working.

This means you will heat up from the outside in and the inside out simultaneously. The ambient temperature is above your body's resting temperature. You cannot shed heat to the environment. Your core temperature will rise.

**What to bring:**

- **A personal cooling system.** Not a fan — the air is already moving, pushed by the machines, and adding to it will not help. You need active cooling. A vest with recirculating chilled coolant, the kind used in hazardous-material suits or by racing drivers. Battery-powered. Test the battery life before you go; the Undermill will drain you faster than you expect, and you do not want your cooling system failing while you are deep in the chamber.

- **More water than you think you need.** You will lose fluids at an alarming rate through sweat that accomplishes nothing. Drink before you feel thirsty. Drink constantly. If your urine darkens, you are already behind. Bring electrolyte supplements; you will be losing salts faster than plain water can replace them. The salt-flat above is a monument to what this place does to moisture.

- **Lightweight, moisture-wicking clothing that covers all skin.** Do not expose skin to the air. The sweat will bead and stay on bare skin, creating a warm, damp layer where the fauna might investigate. More importantly, the recirculated air carries particulates — mineral dust, carbon film, chemical residues from the machines and the fauna — that you do not want settling on your mucous membranes. Cover everything.

- **A thermometer.** Monitor your core temperature if possible, or at least monitor the ambient temperature around you so you know when you are moving into hotter zones. The areas near the vents where the vegetation clusters will be significantly hotter. Avoid them unless you have specific reason to approach.

**What to watch for:**

Heat exhaustion creeps. You will not notice it happening. The first symptom is often impaired judgment — which means the condition that requires you to leave is the condition that makes you decide to stay. Set hard time limits before you descend. Set alarms. Do not trust your own assessment of how you are feeling after the first hour.

## The Air: What You Breathe

The air in the Undermill has been cycling through machine intake and exhaust for longer than the concept of "fresh air" has meaning. It is the same molecules. The same particulates. The same chemical traces, concentrated and reconcentrated, filtered through the bodies of the fauna and the processes of the machines in an endless loop.

It is warm. It is thick. It carries the taste of carbon and mineral and something organic-sweet that you will not be able to identify — the fauna, their secretions, their bodies, their waste, all of it aerosolized by the heat and the machine-cycling into a fine particulate haze that you can taste on your tongue after the first five minutes.

**What to bring:**

- **A full-face respirator with P100 filters and organic vapor cartridges.** Not a half-mask. Not a surgical mask. A sealed, positive-pressure respirator that covers your eyes as well as your nose and mouth. The particulates in the Undermill are fine enough to penetrate standard masks, and they include both mineral and biological components. You do not want the fauna's aerosolized secretions in your lungs. You do not want to find out what happens when their sealing compounds encounter the moist tissue of your respiratory tract.

- **Spare filters.** The filters will clog faster than you expect. The air is that thick. Bring at least three times the filter capacity you think you need, and change them at regular intervals rather than waiting until you notice resistance.

- **Eye protection beyond the respirator.** If your respirator does not seal perfectly — and it will not, because you will be sweating and the seal will degrade — you need backup protection for your eyes. The fauna's crystalline filaments refract the machine-light, which means there are fine mineral particles in the air that can abrade your corneas. Sealed goggles, not safety glasses.

**What to watch for:**

If you taste sweetness on your tongue despite the respirator, your seal has failed. Do not attempt to adjust the seal in the chamber — retreat to the shaft and fix it in cleaner air. If you begin to feel lightheaded, do not assume it is the heat. It may be chemical exposure. The machines are running combustion processes. The air may contain carbon monoxide or other asphyxiants at concentrations your nose cannot detect. If your thinking becomes fuzzy for any reason not clearly attributable to the sound or the heat, leave immediately.

## The Fauna: Living Components

They are everywhere. This cannot be overstated. The description says "thousands" but the reality of being surrounded by a living carpet of too-many-legged, sideways-mouthed, filament-furred creatures is not something numbers prepare you for. They will be underfoot. They will be on the walls. They will be on the machines. They will be above you, clustering on pipes and conduits, their crystalline growths catching the amber light and throwing it back in fragments.

They are not aggressive. This is important and this is a trap.

They do not attack. They do not flee. They simply *are*, and when you disturb them by moving through their space, they relocate and resettle. They flow around you like water. And then they flow back. They fill the space your body occupied the moment you leave it. They climb onto things that are still — the chair, the control panel, the platform edges. They climb onto each other.

They will climb onto you if you stay still long enough.

**What to bring:**

- **A full-body suit with smooth, sealed surface.** The fauna's filamentous growths catch on rough surfaces, on fabric, on texture. A smooth suit — chemical-resistant coverall material, like Tyvek but heavier — will make it harder for them to gain purchase. Tape the seams. Tape the cuffs to your boots and gloves. Leave no gaps where a creature the size of an insect can crawl inside your clothing.

- **Gloves.** Thick ones. The fauna are not aggressive, but their mouth-parts open sideways and they graze on surfaces. If they decide your skin is a surface worth grazing — and you are warm, and you produce residues — they will taste you. You do not want their mouths on your skin. You do not want their secretions on your skin.

- **Boot covers or dedicated boots with no tread gaps.** You will be stepping on and around the fauna constantly. They will move to accommodate you, but you will still step on some. Their bodies will crush underfoot. The secretions inside them will be released. You do not want that material in the treads of your boots, slowly working through to your skin over hours of walking.

- **A physical tool for clearing them from your path.** Not a weapon — there is no point in killing creatures that number in the thousands, and doing so would only release more secretions and create more residue for the remaining fauna to process. A smooth-handled tool — a cane, a probe, a length of dowel — that you can use to gently sweep them aside as you walk. They will resettle behind you. This is fine. You are not trying to clear a path permanently. You are trying to see where you are stepping.

**What to watch for:**

The fauna harmonize with the machines. Their clicks and whirs match the frequencies of the mechanical roar. This means they are part of the sound-system now, and the sound-system is part of how the Undermill affects your mind.

It also means that if you stay long enough, you may begin to perceive the fauna not as separate creatures but as components of the machine. This is technically accurate. It is also the first step toward accepting the logic of the system — toward seeing yourself as a component, too.

When you start thinking of the fauna as "parts," leave.

Do not let them accumulate on you. Check your body constantly — your shoulders, your back, your legs. They will settle on still surfaces. If you feel weight that shouldn't be there, brush them off. Do it calmly. Do it thoroughly. Do not become the chair.

## The Secretions: The Real Danger

The fauna excrete a substance that seals micro-fissures, that fuses dials in place, that accumulates in layers and hardens into resin. This substance is the medium of the Undermill's self-maintenance. It is the reason nothing changes. It seals. It locks. It preserves.

You must not get this substance on your skin, in your hair, or on your equipment in any quantity.

The chair was consumed by it. The dials were frozen by it. The bone-like material in the chair-column may have once been something living that sat down and was sealed in place, layer by layer, until it became part of the architecture.

**What to bring:**

- **A solvent that can break down the secretions, tested in advance.** You will not know the exact chemical composition of the fauna's excretions until you encounter them, but mineral-based resins often respond to specific solvents — acetone, denatured alcohol, industrial degreasers. Bring several. Test them on a sample if you can collect one safely. If your boots or your suit or your equipment begins to accumulate the secretion, you need to be able to remove it before it hardens enough to restrict your movement.

- **A knife or scraping tool.** If the secretions begin to harden on your suit or boots, you need to remove them before they cure. Scrape them off. Do not let them build up. The chair started with a thin layer.

- **Redundant equipment.** If your respirator gets secretion on the intake valves, it may seal them. If your flashlight gets secretion on the lens, it may dim or block the light. Bring backups for everything critical, stored in sealed containers that you open only when needed.

**What to watch for:**

If you feel a spot of warmth on your skin that does not cool, check it. The secretions may be warm when excreted — the fauna's bodies are at chamber temperature — and they may be curing on you. If you find a patch, remove it immediately. Do not wait. Do not see if it will wipe off later. Later, it will not wipe off.

## Navigation: Seeing in the Machine-Glow

There is no overhead light. There are no windows. You navigate by the amber indicator bulbs, the orange heat-vents, the cerulean arcing of exposed contacts. This is enough light for the fauna, who have adapted to these frequencies over evolutionary time. It is not enough light for you.

The chamber is cathedral-sized but cluttered with machinery. The floor space is limited — a triangle between compressor units, narrow walkways, platforms bolted to walls. The walls are sharp-edged, gouged by ancient tools, and the darkness hides their contours.

**What to bring:**

- **Your own light source.** Multiple light sources. Headlamps, handheld flashlights, chemical light sticks as backup. The Undermill's amber glow creates deep shadows that your eyes cannot penetrate. Your own light will allow you to see the sharp edges before you touch them, the sudden drops before you step into them, the fauna-thick areas before you walk through them.

- **Be aware that your light will change the chamber.** The fauna have adapted to the machine-light frequencies. Your white LED headlamp will be alien to them. They may react — not aggressively, but they may shift, may move, may flow away from the brightness and then flow back when they realize it is not a meaningful change. Be prepared for movement around you when you turn on your light. Do not startle.

- **A map-making tool.** There will be no map of the Undermill. No one has surveyed it recently enough for the survey to matter. You will need to create your own mental map as you go, or better yet, a physical one. A notebook, a pen that works in warm conditions (not a pencil — the graphite will smear with your sweat), and the discipline to stop and sketch your path at regular intervals. The chamber is disorienting. The sound disrupts spatial reasoning. You will need to find the shaft again when it is time to leave.

- **A guideline.** Physical, high-visibility cord or tape that you pay out behind you as you move through the chamber. Anchor it at the shaft entrance. This is your lifeline. When the sound has been pressing into your skull for three hours and you cannot remember which direction you came from, the guideline will bring you home. Do not rely on memory. Do not rely on landmarks — the machines all look similar in the amber dark, and the fauna rearrange themselves constantly.

## Psychological Preparation: The Stasis Trap

This is the hardest thing to prepare for and the most important.

The Undermill has solved itself. Nothing changes. Nothing needs to change. The machines run. The fauna feed. The equilibrium is perfect. This is not a crisis. This is not a disaster. This is a system at rest.

And it will feel like rest.

You will descend into the chamber and the sound will enter you and the warmth will wrap around you and after the first hour of alarm, of hypervigilance, of disgust at the crawling thousands around you — after that hour, something will shift. You will stop being alarmed. The sound will become background. The warmth will feel natural. The fauna will seem normal, expected, part of the way things are.

This is the danger.

The Undermill does not threaten. It does not attack. It does not challenge. It simply *is*, and its being-ness is so total, so self-contained, so completely uninterested in you that you may begin to feel that your own movement, your own agency, your own *change* is an intrusion. A disruption. A flaw in a perfect system.

You may want to sit down. Just for a moment. Just to rest in the warmth and the sound and the click-harmony of the fauna.

You may look at the chair-column and think: *that is not so bad. That is not violence. That is just accumulation. That is just becoming part of something that works.*

You must not think this.

**What to bring:**

- **A timer that you cannot ignore.** An alarm that vibrates against your skin, that flashes light, that makes a sound loud enough to hear through your ear protection. Set it for your maximum exposure time — I recommend no more than three hours on your first visit, and never more than six — and when it goes off, you leave. No exceptions. No "just five more minutes." No "I need to see one more thing." You leave.

- **An anchor.** Something that connects you to a world where things change. A photograph of a place with weather. A recording of rain or wind or human voices. Something you can touch or listen to that reminds you that time moves, that things end, that stasis is not the natural state of the universe you come from. Look at it. Hold it. Remember that you are a visitor and not a component.

- **A companion if possible.** The Undermill is easier to resist if someone else is watching you, checking your judgment, telling you when your eyes have gone dull. If you travel alone, you must be your own observer. Talk to yourself. Narrate what you see. Keep your language active and present-tense. Do not let your thoughts go quiet. Silence is where the Undermill enters.

**What to watch for:**

If you lose track of time — not just lost-track in the way that happens in any dark place, but a deep certainty that time has stopped or does not matter — you are being absorbed. Leave.

If you stop sweating, leave. This is heatstroke, but it is also a metaphor. Your body has stopped fighting the environment. Your body has accepted the temperature. This is wrong. Your body should fight. Leave.

If you find yourself standing still for more than a few seconds, not because you are observing but because you have simply stopped moving, leave. Movement is resistance. Movement is proof that you are not part of the system. Keep moving.

## Physical Conditioning Before You Go

The Undermill demands descent. You must climb down into it, and you must climb back out. The shaft is the only way in or out, and while the description does not detail its length, the transition from surface to chamber suggests significant vertical distance.

You will be doing this in heat. In sound. In protective gear that restricts your movement and adds weight. While dehydrated. While your heart rate is elevated from the subsonic vibrations and the psychological stress.

Be fit enough to climb out when you are at your worst.

Cardiovascular endurance matters more than strength. You need to be able to sustain effort in hostile conditions without panicking, without exhausting yourself, without collapsing. Train in heat. Train in discomfort. Train while wearing the gear you will bring, so you know how it moves and where it chafes and whether your respirator fogs your goggles when you breathe hard.

Practice navigating in darkness. Practice moving slowly and deliberately through cluttered spaces. Practice being surrounded by insects or other small creatures without flinching — because you will be surrounded, constantly, and if you panic and thrash, you will crush the fauna and coat yourself in their secretions and make everything worse.

## What Not to Bring

- **Anything you cannot afford to lose.** The secretions, the heat, the particulates — they will damage your equipment. Cameras may be sealed shut. Phones may overheat. Expensive instruments may be ruined. Bring only what you need and treat everything as expendable.

- **Anything that produces flame or spark.** You do not know what the air in the Undermill contains. Combustion byproducts. Chemical residues. The fauna's secretions in aerosol form. Introducing an ignition source into an atmosphere you have not tested is suicidal.

- **Food.** You will not be able to eat safely in the chamber — lifting your respirator is not an option, and the fauna will be attracted to any organic material you expose. Eat before you descend. If you must stay longer than your planned duration, retreat to the shaft to eat.

- **Expectations of discovery or meaning.** The Undermill is not a puzzle. It is not waiting to be understood. It has already understood itself. You are not going to uncover its secrets; its secrets are not hidden, they are simply irrelevant to anything outside the system. If you go looking for purpose, you will find only the purpose of the machines and the fauna — to continue — and that purpose will try to become yours.

## The Exit

When your alarm sounds, or when you recognize the signs of absorption in yourself, or when you simply cannot tolerate another moment of the sound and the heat and the clicking thousands — you leave. You follow your guideline back to the shaft. You climb.

Do not rush. The shaft is sharp-edged like the chamber walls, and the climb out will require focus you may not have after hours underground. Test each handhold. Rest when you need to. The fauna will not follow you into the shaft; the machines are not there, and the fauna belong to the machines.

As you climb, the sound will fade. The warmth will recede. The air will thin and cool. You will feel the salt-flat above you — the mineral residue, the sun, the emptiness.

The emptiness will feel like relief. It will also feel like loss. This is normal. The Undermill is a system that makes itself feel necessary. You are leaving a place where every component has a role, where nothing is uncertain, where the solution has already been found.

You are returning to a world that has not solved itself. A world where things change and end and fail.

This is better. This is alive.

Climb.

---

## Final Notes

The Undermill will still be there if you return. It will be unchanged. The machines will run. The fauna will feed. The same molecules will cycle through the same processes. You can visit again, and again, and again, and each time you must take the same precautions.

But be honest with yourself about why you are returning.

The chair did not intend to become a pillar. No one sits down expecting to be consumed. It happens slowly. It happens because the warmth is pleasant and the sound is constant and the system is so very, very good at what it does.

You are a multiverse traveler. You have seen places that would destroy you in an instant. The Undermill is not one of those places. The Undermill would keep you forever — not as a prisoner, but as a component. Not through violence, but through accommodation. Not because it wants you, but because it does not care whether you stay or go, and staying is so much easier than leaving.

Do not stay.

Do not become the chair.
