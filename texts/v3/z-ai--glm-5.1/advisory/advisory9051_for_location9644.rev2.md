# Preparing for the Uplift: A Field Guide for the Ordinary Traveler

## First, understand what you are walking into

Most destinations subtract one or two comforts. The Uplift subtracts all of them at once, politely, without malice, the way a young place does.

There is no soil to hold a tent stake. There is water everywhere and almost none of it is drinkable. There is warmth below your feet that will kill you if you trust it. The air is thin enough that your own body becomes a hazard. The light never resolves, so your eyes lie to you about distance, and the plateau's edges — where the columns stand half-born and the escarpment drops away — do not announce themselves in mist. And the most important fact of all, the one that should organize everything else you pack: **no one is coming.** Not for you, not for the machines, not for anything. You are your own rescue, your own weather station, your own hospital, and your own way home.

The plateau is not hostile. It is *indifferent*, which is harder. It doesn't believe in seasons, only in gradients of hostility, and it is your job to know which way the gradient is moving at every hour of every day you spend on it.

Here is how to prepare.

---

## The honest self-assessment

This is not a beginner's destination, and the multiverse gives you no special protection from hypothermia or cerebral edema. Before committing, ask yourself:

- Have you slept outdoors in freezing temperatures, in wet conditions, and been fine — not surviving, but *fine*?
- Can you walk uphill for hours at a pace slow enough to be humiliating?
- Can you be alone — truly alone, no rescue, no signal, no other human for a hundred kilometers — without unraveling?

If any answer is no, choose a different world this time and come back to this one later. The plateau has been waiting three thousand years. It will wait for you.

If you do go, go with a companion if at all possible. On raw stone, in permanent near-twilight, a twisted ankle solo is not an inconvenience. It is a sequence of events.

---

## When to visit

Go in late summer, when the grass is in its pale growing season, the pikas are cutting hay, and the thaw windows are longest. Understand what "late summer" means here: **it will still freeze every night.** The foxes will already be growing winter coats. They know which way the gradient is moving, and you should respect the information.

Do not go in winter. The warm zones become the only livable architecture for a hundred kilometers, and that architecture belongs to the animals. Your presence in it would be an intrusion into the only rooms they have.

---

## Acclimatize — and use the multiverse to do it

At 4,100 meters, the air holds about sixty percent of the sea-level oxygen you were built for. An ordinary human deposited on the plateau directly from a lowland universe will, within hours, develop a headache, nausea, and a body that turns against itself. Altitude sickness does not care how many worlds you've seen.

If your crossing method allows you to choose your exit and re-entry points, you have an advantage no alpinist in any single universe has: **you can climb high and sleep low *across realities*.** Spend your days on the plateau, your nights at lower elevation in another world. This mimics the gold-standard acclimatization protocol. Return day after day, and your blood will slowly thicken itself to this place, the same slow way the lichens advance — but in days instead of centuries.

If you cannot stage your arrival:

- **Acetazolamide**, started a day or two before arrival, taken until you leave. It makes your blood a little more like the plateau's air demands. It also makes your hands tingle and your soda taste like battery acid. Worth it.
- **Arrive with nothing planned for 48 hours.** Camp. Sit. Drink water. Breathe. Walk fifty meters and feel your pulse; walk back.
- Know the warning signs and test for them nightly: severe headache that ignores painkillers, vomiting, and — most importantly — **ataxia**. Walk a straight line, heel to toe, every evening. If you wobble like a drunk, your brain is swelling. This is HACE, and the only cure is immediate descent — which, for you, is a door. **Make sure you know exactly how to open it.**

That last point deserves its own paragraph: **your exit is your most important piece of equipment.** Keep the mechanism, token, or trigger on your body — inside your jacket, on a lanyard, never buried in a pack you might be separated from. Practice activating it wearing gloves, in the dark, with numb fingers, while feeling wretched. Have a backup exit if your method permits one. And never let the door make you lazy: mechanisms fail, especially in cold and panic. Carry a full conventional plan — shelter, heat, patience — as if the door did not exist.

## The wet problem: how to dress

The Uplift is not rainy. It is *damp* — permanently, exhaustingly, inescapably damp. The rock weeps. The air condenses on everything. Water beads along fracture planes and traces paths downward all day and all night. This is the plateau's beauty and it is also the mechanism by which it will kill the unprepared, because wet cold pulls heat from a body several times faster than dry cold, and hypothermia does not require a blizzard. It requires +5°C, wind, and a soaked base layer.

**The single most important clothing decision: down dies here.** In a place where nothing ever dries, down insulation clumps into uselessness. Choose synthetic insulation throughout — jacket, parka, sleeping bag — because synthetic keeps warming when damp, and it *will* be damp.

Your system:

- **Base layers:** synthetic or merino, and bring more sets than you think reasonable, because you will not dry the ones you're wearing. Pack every dry pair in its own dry bag inside your pack liner. Your dry socks are a form of currency here; treat them like it.
- **Insulation:** fleece mid-layer plus a synthetic belay parka big enough to wear over everything.
- **Shell:** a genuinely windproof hardshell for the plateau's wind, worn loose enough to vent — you'll be generating sweat on the climbs and sweat is the enemy's scout.
- **Boots:** stiff-soled, insulated, fully waterproof mountaineering boots. The regolith is an abrasive grey rock-flour mud that will destroy light footwear in days, and the raw rock beneath it is unworn — its fracture planes are knife-sharp, unfazed by water, wind, or time. Your boots will look forty years old after a week here. This is normal. Bring **gaiters** — the flour gets into everything — and spare laces.
- **Gloves:** two systems. Warm insulated mittens or gloves for living in, and leather-palmed work gloves for any time you touch rock. Fresh basalt and gabbro edges will open your hands like a blade.
- **Balaclava and warm hat:** the heat loss at night is not optional to prevent.
- **Sunglasses and sunscreen, even in the grey glow.** The sky here is too dark for the ground to be this bright, and when the condensation thins, the sun is a harsh white point through thin atmosphere. UV at this altitude burns you through the dimness while you're not looking. Lip balm with SPF. The underside of your chin, lit by pale rock glare, will burn too. The plateau is unfair that way.

**Foot care is a survival discipline, not a comfort.** Between the constant moisture and the near-freezing temperatures, you are at genuine risk of immersion foot. Change socks two or three times daily. Each evening, take your socks off, look at your feet, massage them, and watch for wrinkled, whitened, numb skin. A small tin of foot balm will do more for your trip than a second knife.

One animal note that belongs here: the pikas of the talus live in a landscape starving for salt, and alpine rodents across the multiverse chew sweaty pack straps for exactly that reason. Do not leave packs unattended in talus. Your dried sweat is the most concentrated mineral deposit they will ever find.

## Shelter: pitching a tent where there is no ground

There is no soil. The regolith is unconsolidated rock flour over raw bedrock, and it holds a stake about as well as a cloud does. Bring a **free-standing tent** — a geodesic dome or similar — and anchor it with rocks stuffed into stuff sacks as deadmen. Expect the surface beneath you to be a film of water; bring a full footprint or ground cloth.

Site selection is the most consequential decision you'll make on this plateau. Follow the checklist in order:

1. **A topographic high.** This is rule one, and it exists because of the gas — see the section below. Never sleep in a depression, never sleep below the level of a steaming pond, never camp in the shallow basins where the ponds sit, even though they're flat and tempting and warm-adjacent.
2. **Away from ponds by at least a hundred meters.** The ponds mean midges, gas, and mineral-laden splashing.
3. **Away from the bright green grass rings.** Grass here grows only on warm ground. Read the grass rings as thermal maps — they're excellent ones — and then camp somewhere else. Warm ground means gas risk and scald risk, and the foxes already den by the largest pond; they don't need a neighbor.
4. **Off the pale mud fans.** When the condensation grows heavy, the regolith flows in channels and builds little alluvial fans — smooth aprons of grey silt that freeze each night into brittle sheets. These are drainage paths. A heavy condensation event will turn them into slow mud.
5. **Bare regolith, on a rise, out of the worst wind.** There are no windbreaks except the drill rigs (do not shelter by them — see below) and the eastern escarpment (which belongs to the ibex and ends in a cliff).

Inside the tent, the dampness follows you. Your breath condenses on the fly and drips. Ventilate harder than instinct suggests. Keep your sleeping bag in a dry bag until the moment you enter it, and pull the dry bag in with you as a stuff-sack pillow. Bring a **synthetic** bag rated far below the temperatures you expect — expect minus ten — and stack two insulated pads; the ground beneath you is cold except where it is *very* warm, and both extremes are problems.

Bring a wide-mouth bottle so you never leave the tent at night. Dark plus cold plus invisible gas is a bad combination to walk into for reasons of comfort.

## Water: the plateau offers it and you must refuse

This is the cruelest joke of the Uplift: water everywhere — weeping rock, streams that appear and vanish, dozens of ponds — and almost none of it you can drink.

- The streams are the water table breaching the surface. They carry a heavy mineral load — metallic-tasting, opaque with dissolved rock, young chemistry. Boiling kills organisms; **boiling does nothing about dissolved heavy metals.**
- The ponds are worse: mineral-heavy, sulfur-laced, chemistry "too young, too volatile, too uninterested in supporting life." Do not drink, do not wash in, do not touch with bare hands if you can help it.
- The thin night-ice skins on the mud fans are tempting and treacherous — one boot-through and you're standing in near-freezing grey silt.
- Everything scalding hot near the rigs and pipeline will burn you before it quenches you.

Your water strategy, in order:

1. **Carry a buffer** — six to eight liters — in durable containers.
2. **Distill from the plateau's heat.** The pipeline carries hot water extracted from below and purified enough to be useful, and it leaks at several points. That escaping steam is distilled water. With care, distance, and a clean catch surface angled under a drip — *never* a direct vent, and never a touched pipe — you can collect excellent water as it cools. This is the best water on the plateau and it comes from the machines' failures, which is fitting, because the entire ecosystem here drinks from the same failures.
3. **Condensation traps.** The air is permanently moist. A clean sheet of plastic or fabric stretched taut overnight on your rise will gather dew. It will never quench a group, but it will stretch your buffer. Every morning, the plateau itself is briefly drinkable — wring it out before the sunless day warms it into mud.

Drink more than you think you need. Altitude dehydration is sneaky, the headache it gives mimics the altitude headache, and you need clear thinking for the next item on the list.

## The invisible hazards: gas

Beneath you, hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide, and trace methane rise through the young stone and breathe out of the steaming ponds and warm zones. In daylight, with a breeze, this is a smell and an experience. **At night, it becomes lethal geometry**: cold air pools in low ground, and the gases — heavier than air — pool with it. A basin that was a pleasant lunch spot at noon can be a gas trap by midnight.

Your defenses:

- **Camp on high ground.** (Rule one, from the checklist. It exists for this reason.)
- **A portable multi-gas monitor** is the modern answer and worth its weight. The old answers also work, and travel well: carry a **candle lantern** and watch the flame — it dims and gutters where oxygen is being displaced, and it has saved miners across a hundred worlds.
- **Wear a silver ring.** Sulfur loves silver. If it blackens overnight, you have been breathing more H₂S than you knew. Cheap and eerie and true.
- **Know the treachery of smell.** H₂S reeks of rotten eggs at low concentrations and goes *odorless* at the concentrations that kill. The smell fading is not the gas fading.
- **The hard rules:** never descend into a basin to investigate a steaming pond at dawn or dusk; never sleep near a vent; if a flame dims, or you feel suddenly dizzy, or your companion's sentences start wandering — retreat uphill immediately, no discussion, no hesitation.

## Moving through the plateau

**Walk in the morning.** The nightly freeze gives you a crust — brittle, skin-thin sheets over the mud fans that crunch underfoot, and verglas on the weeping rock faces. Microspikes help on frozen mornings and become useless, spiked mud-catchers by midday; carry them, use judgment. Your bootprint will hold in the regolith for minutes before the moisture reshapes it. Watch this happen once, on purpose. It teaches you what kind of place you're in faster than any map.

**Rest through the heavy mist.** When the condensation thickens, visibility collapses, the horizon dissolves, and the plateau becomes a grey room. Sit. Eat. Watch a pond steam from your rise.

**Pace yourself like it's the hardest mountain day of your life, even on flat ground.** Because at this altitude, it is. One to one-and-a-half kilometers per hour with a pack is a respectable speed for the unacclimatized. Build your itineraries around that and you will enjoy this place; build them around your sea-level self and you will suffer.

**Navigation:** assume no satellites, no network, no grid. The survey markers shift each year and no one adjusts them — treat their stamped elevations as archaeology, not data. Instead:

- **The nine drill rigs are your lighthouses.** Steel lattice towers, steam plumes, each breathing its low mechanical breath. Learn their pattern; each plume is also a windvane — read the bend and you know the wind.
- **The pipeline is a handrail.** It runs from the largest rig to the northern edge. In thick mist, follow it or avoid it deliberately, but know where it is relative to yourself.
- **When the sky permits, look down.** The clouds pass *below* the rim. A cloud sea tells you where the edges are — information you badly need, because:
- **The rim rule, which is the most important safety rule of overland travel here:** the light flattens everything, makes the horizon uncertain and distances lies, and the plateau's edges are abrupt — columns, escarpment, talus. In visibility under a hundred meters, treat every unknown downslope as a cliff, probe ahead with your trekking pole, and never walk toward the edge on a bearing you haven't verified. People die in exactly this light, in every universe, in exactly this way.
- Carry reflective wands to mark your camp — two is enough — and pack them out when you leave. Distances that deceive the eye also deceive the memory.

## The machines: you are a guest in someone else's forty-year silence

Nine self-sustaining rigs have been drilling for four decades, unvisited, self-repairing, part by part, rusting and being replaced like slow organisms. They are the closest thing to company you will have here, and they do not know you exist. Prepare your expectations accordingly — and your conduct:

- **Keep your distance.** Steam vents at irregular intervals; automated valves that cycle without warning; scalding water in the pipeline. Ten meters minimum from active components. An infrared thermometer lets you *read* the plateau's warmth without ever touching it — one of the most useful single tools you can bring here.
- **Do not climb the towers. Do not shelter beneath them.** Steel lattice on an exposed plateau is a lightning rod, and the drip line of a rig is a noisy, gas-prone, mechanically surprising place.
- **Do not fix anything.** This deserves emphasis beyond safety: the pipeline leaks are accidental agriculture. Thermophilic bacteria, warmer pools, patches of grass slightly greener and more alive than anything else up here — the ecosystem is feeding on the infrastructure's failures. These leaks are the only mercy this young world has been shown by anything human. You are not maintenance. *Fix nothing, tighten nothing, tidy nothing.* Leave the failures alone. They are feeding someone.
- **Think hard before you publish coordinates.** Somewhere below is a corporate archive containing a project no one remembers. Attention could bring repair crews, and repair would close the oases. A witness's first duty is to not change the thing witnessed.

## The animals: they have no category for you

The plateau's fauna evolved their habits around eagles and machines — not people. You may be the first human some of these creatures have ever seen. That gives you a strange power and a strict etiquette:

- **Never feed anything.** This ecosystem is protein-starved and mineral-hungry; the pikas snap at midges with visible desperation. Your crumbs will be the richest event of an animal's year, and the habituation you create will outlast your visit and maybe kill what learns it. All food in hard-sided containers, stored away from your tent. The foxes will test your discipline the first dusk and every dusk after.
- **Don't touch the pika hay-caches** near the warm vents. Those piles are winter, banked.
- **Give the eastern columns their distance in late summer.** Three nesting pairs of golden eagles, decades of nests fixed to the half-born rock, and fledging season means adults who will defend their aeries. View through binoculars — bring binoculars; the eagles turning in the thin dark-blue air are half the reason to come. If you must pass close beneath the columns, wear a hard hat and keep your eyes up.
- **Step around the moss.** The black-green ribbons in the seep zones look like wounds because they are — they're the plateau's only soft tissue, and it took them centuries. Likewise the lichen fronts, darkening the raw grey at centimeters per human lifetime. Walk on bare rock and pale fans.
- **The midges** swarm above the thermal ponds. They are the base of a food web that shouldn't exist but does — treat them with a strange respect and a head net. If a lost songbird lands near your camp, exhausted, gorging — leave it room and luck. Its fate is already part of the plateau's economy: becoming food, becoming mineral, becoming soil in a place that has none yet.

## Biosecurity: you are the alien ecosystem

Here is a preparation step no single-universe guidebook would include, and it matters more here than almost anywhere: **the Uplift is microbiologically young.** The ponds are sterile — chemistry too volatile to support life. *Yet.* The entire biosphere consists of lichens, moss, grass, midges, and a handful of animals that arrived on the wind and the hoof. Your body — your gut, your skin, your breath, the soil in your boots from the last world you visited — is a dense rain of organisms this place has never met.

So:

- **Scrub your boots, gaiters, and pole tips before crossing.** Every seed and spore you carry in could become this plateau's first invasive species. There is nothing here to outcompete it.
- **Do not wash in any water.** No streams, no ponds, ever. Carry your greywater and scatter it on bare regolith far from seeps.
- **Pack out all waste — all of it, including human.** Nothing decomposes at near-freezing; your orange peel will outlast your interest in this universe. And the plateau builds soil from *whatever falls* — your waste is not neutral here, it is an input to a starving system, an ecological event. Decline to be one. This is the one place where the ordinary ethics of "bury it" are exactly backwards.
- **Take nothing that lives.** Not moss, not lichen, not a vial of orange thermophilic bacteria — importing microbes *out* is the same crime in the other direction.

## The medical kit, and the rescue problem

There is no rescue within this world. Your kit is the hospital. Emphasize:

- Altitude medications as discussed; a pulse oximeter so you can put numbers to your dread; a thermometer.
- **Trauma above everything.** Sharp, unworn rock cuts deep. Pressure bandages, a wound-closure kit, an irrigation syringe, steri-strips, gloves. In the cold, a small wound becomes immobilizing; an immobilized person becomes a hypothermia case within hours.
- Blister kit built on tape, not plasters. Ibuprofen (altitude headaches). Anti-nausea. Eye drops — the air is dry, the rock flour is abrasive, and midges aim for eyes. Electrolyte salts.
- Keep all medication inside your jacket. Cold turns ointments to wax and batteries to liars.
- Pack a duplicate of everything critical split between your companion's pack and yours.

And a word on the cold's smaller cruelties: electronics — cameras die fast here. Lithium batteries, spares carried warm against your chest. When you bring cold gear into the warm tent, bag it first and let it equalize slowly, or the same condensation that covers the rock will form *inside* your camera. Pencils write in any temperature; most pens do not. Bring waterproof paper — your notes will be the only field record of this place that exists anywhere in any world, and they will get damp.

## Preparing your mind

The physical preparation is the easy half.

Understand before you arrive that this place runs on **three clocks**, and you live on the fastest one. Your bootprint lasts minutes. The lichens advance a few centimeters per lifetime. The plateau itself rises year by year, patient as a lifted chest. You will spend your days inside the gap between those clocks, and the plateau will not adjust any of them for you.

Expect the light to unmoor you. A sky too dark for the ground to be this bright; a ground too dim for the sky to be this close; overcast and clear at once, always. Depth flattens, scale drifts. **Practice sitting still before you come** — real stillness, an hour at a time — because the plateau rewards attention, not endurance. The traveler who races between rigs sees an industrial site. The traveler who sits down beside a fracture plane and watches water bead, gather, trace a path downward, and join a stream — that traveler sees the place being *written*.

And expect the night. When the condensation thins, the stars here are aggressive — the Milky Way as a textured river, the whole vault pressing down until you feel you are falling through space rather than standing on rock. This vertigo is normal and it passes. When it comes: sit or lie down first, keep your skin covered because the cold is doing its patient arithmetic while you gaze upward, and let the plateau hand you its own cure. A pika will call — *enk* — sharp, single, close. The sound will put the ground back beneath you, and you will remember the place is real, and young, and still becoming.

One more thing, and it is the hardest to prepare for: **you will be the only thing on this plateau that knows it is a plateau.** The machines extract; the animals survive; the rock rises, oblivious. You alone will hold the whole of it at once — the proposal of a landscape, the draft, the child containing all its possible adulthoods, the two futures both suspended in it like a diver mid-turn. That is the actual weight of this visit, and no pack list accounts for it. Bring the willingness to carry it.

---

## Leaving

When you go, leave the plateau exactly as you found it — which is to say, leave *nothing*. No waste, no marks, no fixed leaks, no fed foxes, no disturbed caches, no moved survey markers. The regolith will forget your bootprints within minutes. This is not coldness; it is youth. The plateau has no memory yet. It is only the accident of your timing that lets you be, briefly, the only being that will ever remember it this way — raw, unworn, weeping, unfinished, mid-rise.

Take the memory. Take your notes. Take the smell of wet rock flour out of your jacket, which will cling for a few washes.

And when someone in some other universe asks you what it was like up there, on the land that is still becoming, you could do worse than this:

It was the only place I have ever stood where the ground had not finished deciding what it was going to be. I did not help it decide. That was the whole visit.
