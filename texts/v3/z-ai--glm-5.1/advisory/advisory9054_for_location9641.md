# Preparing for the Green Expanse Beneath

## What You Are Walking Into

You are not visiting a place. You are stepping inside something that is alive.

Understand this before anything else: the Green Expanse Beneath is not a forest you walk through, not a cave you explore, not a biome you study. It is the interior of an organism—or something that functions like one, that breathes and drinks and cycles and grows, that has no brain and no eyes and no knowledge of you, that will be exactly as warm and wet and bright when you leave as it was before you arrived. You will be inside it the way a morsel is inside a mouth. Not because it wants to eat you—it doesn't want anything—but because that is simply the geometry of the place.

This changes how you prepare. You are not packing for terrain. You are packing for *tenancy* inside a living system that will not notice you and will not accommodate you.

---

## The Heat: Your First and Constant Companion

The heat rises from below. It does not vary. It does not spike in the afternoon or cool at night because there is no afternoon and there is no night. It is the temperature of the root-floor, which is the temperature of the earth beneath the root-floor, which is the temperature of something that remembers being molten. It is like standing near bread that has finished baking and will never cool.

What this means for your body: you will sweat. You will sweat continuously. Not the dramatic, dripping sweat of exertion—though that will come when you walk—but the slow, constant perspiration of a body trying to cool itself in an environment that will not let it. The humidity is total. The condensation forms on every surface, and your skin is a surface. Sweat will not evaporate efficiently. Your body's primary cooling mechanism will be compromised.

**How to prepare:**

Bring clothing that does not trap moisture against your skin. This sounds counterintuitive—most travelers instinctively want to cover up in unfamiliar environments—but tight, water-resistant fabrics will create a microclimate of trapped sweat and heat between the cloth and your body, and that microclimate will breed skin breakdown faster than anything the Expanse itself can do to you. Loose, moisture-wicking layers. Linen if you can source it from a universe that still produces it. Synthetic moisture-wicking fabrics if you cannot. Avoid cotton. Cotton kills in the cold; in the Green Expanse, it simply makes you miserable, holding water against your skin like a sponge, which is exactly what the root-floor does and you do not want to match the root-floor.

Change your clothes. Bring more clothing than you think you need, because everything you wear will become damp within an hour, and nothing will dry. The air is too saturated. You will need to rotate through garments, wearing each one until it becomes intolerable and then switching to something dry. The damp clothes will not dry while you wear them. They will not dry if you set them down on the root-floor—that would simply add the floor's moisture to their own. You might, if you are clever, rig a line between two pillars and hang damp clothing in the warm rising air, and it will dry somewhat. Somewhat. The Expanse does not want things to be dry. It wants everything to be part of its cycle.

Bring barrier cream. Zinc oxide. Petroleum jelly. Something to create a film on your skin in places where skin meets skin—under arms, inner thighs, beneath breasts, between toes. The constant moisture and warmth will macerate your skin within days if you do not protect it. The breakdown starts as redness, progresses to rawness, becomes open and weeping, and then the Expanse's ambient microorganisms—not the plants themselves, but the things that live in the spaces the plants create—will find you. The mites. The things smaller than mites. They tend the rot. You do not want to become rot that needs tending.

Bring electrolytes. You will lose salt through your sweat at a rate that surprises travelers from temperate universes. The heat does not feel like exertion—it feels like simply being—but your body is working constantly to cool itself, and that work costs minerals. Headaches, confusion, muscle cramps, fatigue: these are the early signs. In the Green Expanse, confusion is particularly dangerous because there is nothing to orient you, nothing to snap you out of it. You simply become more confused, more lost, more wet, more hot, and the Expanse continues exactly as it was.

## The Water: Drinkable and Inescapable

The condensation is safe to drink. This is one of the Expanse's few graces. The water that forms on the root-walls, beads on the fronds, gathers in the thin pale threads—it is mineral, clean, has been cycled through the plant systems so many times that it has been filtered beyond anything a human purification system could achieve. You can lick it from surfaces if you must. You can collect it in your hands. You will not get sick from the water itself.

But you will have trouble getting *enough* of it.

The condensation forms slowly. A bead on a frond takes minutes to grow large enough to fall. The *tunk, tunk, tunk* of the drops is steady but not rapid. If you simply stand with your mouth open, you will not hydrate. You need a collection system.

**How to prepare:**

Bring broad, smooth surfaces—sheets of flexible plastic or metal, even a simple tarp—and rig them at an angle beneath the ceiling fronds where condensation is heaviest. The water will bead on your surface instead of the fronds, run down to the lowest point, and drip into a container. This is not fast. A square meter of collection surface might yield a cup of water per hour. You will need to drink more than you normally do because of the heat. Calculate your collection needs accordingly.

Bring containers that seal. Open containers left on the root-floor will be discovered by the pale threads, which will drink from them. The Expanse takes water wherever it finds it. Your canteen, left uncapped, will slowly empty into the circulatory system of the plants. You are not the priority. You are not even a consideration. You are simply another surface where water has temporarily paused.

Bring a straw or hydration tube. This seems minor. It is not. When you are deep in the Expanse, when you have been walking for hours on the spongy root-floor and your legs are shaking and you do not want to stop because stopping means sitting on the damp ground and you do not want to sit on the damp ground, you will want to drink without removing your pack, without unscrewing a cap, without the small cascade of water that spills and is immediately absorbed by the floor. A tube lets you drink while moving. In the Green Expanse, moving is better than stopping. Stopping is when the heat settles into you like a weight.

## The Food: There Is None

This is where preparation matters most. The Expanse will give you water and air and warmth. It will not give you food.

The mites are too small to eat. Collecting them would require scraping the root-floor, which would disturb the threads, which would disturb the circulatory system, which might—might—trigger a response. Probably not. The Expanse does not seem to respond to anything. But you do not know what ten thousand years of unbroken equilibrium looks like when disrupted. Neither does anyone else. Do not be the experiment.

The pale caterpillars are technically edible. They are soft-bodied, slow, protein-dense. But there are perhaps a few dozen visible at any time across a space that takes a day to walk across. You would need to watch the ceiling for hours to spot one, climb a pillar to reach it—and the pillars are furred with the drinking-threads, which you would crush and damage—and consume something that the Expanse has produced for its own purposes. More importantly: you do not know what eating the light-glands does to a human body. The caterpillars consume them. You do not know what compounds accumulate in their tissue. You do not know if they are toxic in the slow, invisible way that things in the Expanse are slow and invisible.

Do not eat the plants. This should not need saying, but travelers always try. The plants are not food. They are substrate, structure, architecture, circulation. They are made of cellulose and lignin and compounds that ten million years of lightless evolution have produced for purposes you cannot guess. They will not nourish you. They may poison you. They will certainly not taste like anything you recognize as food—they will taste like the Expanse tastes: mineral and green and old.

**How to prepare:**

Bring everything you will eat. Every calorie. Every gram. Calculate your needs based on the longest you could possibly be there, and then add a week's worth. The Expanse does not change, but you might. You might get turned around. You might walk in what you believe is the direction of your exit and find that the pillars and fronds have rearranged themselves—not because they moved, but because you misremembered, because everything looks the same, because the Expanse has no landmarks, only plant.

Bring dense, dry food. The moisture will find anything not sealed against it. Energy bars, jerky, hard biscuits—things that will survive being damp on the outside even if the inside stays protected. Vacuum-sealed portions. Do not bring fresh fruit; it will become wet and then wetter and then part of the floor. Do not bring bread; it will mold within a day. Do not bring anything that needs cooking; there is nothing to burn, nothing to make fire from, and the smoke would have nowhere to go and the heat would not be needed and you would be performing a ritual from a world that does not exist here.

Eat efficiently. The Expanse does not care about your meals. There is no morning, no evening, no natural stopping point. You will eat when your body tells you to, which will be often, because you are burning calories just existing in the heat. Do not let hunger sneak up on you. Hunger in the Green Expanse is not the sharp pang of a missed lunch—it is a slow fog that rolls in, a vagueness, a difficulty concentrating, and then suddenly you are confused about where you are and which direction you were walking and whether you have been walking for an hour or six. Eat before you are hungry. This is the rule.

## The Ground: Walking on a Palm

The root-floor is yielding. It compresses under your weight and slowly rebounds when you lift your foot. It is like walking on a thick mattress, or on packed moss, or on the palm of a hand—which is the comparison the description offers and which is the most accurate, because the floor is alive, is composed of living and recently-living matter, is warm beneath your feet, is drinking the water that falls on it and the water that drips from your clothing and the water that you cannot help but leave behind.

This is exhausting in a way that walking on solid ground is not. Every step sinks slightly. Every step requires your leg muscles to push you up and out as well as forward. After an hour, your calves will burn. After two, your hips will ache from the subtle instability, the constant micro-adjustments. After four, you will feel as though you have been climbing stairs, though you have been walking on what appears to be flat ground.

**How to prepare:**

Bring boots with rigid soles. Flexible soles—sneakers, soft hiking shoes—will allow the floor to dictate your foot position, and the floor is uneven at the small scale, lumpy with root and stem, pocked with the places where water has been absorbed. A rigid sole distributes your weight and keeps your foot in a neutral position. This matters more than you think. Twisted ankles, strained arches, plantar fasciitis—these are the injuries of the root-floor, and they are the injuries that will end your expedition, because the Expanse does not have a way out for someone who cannot walk.

Break your boots in before you arrive. This is standard advice for any wilderness travel, but in the Green Expanse it is urgent: a blister will not dry. It will stay wet. It will macerate. It will become an open wound in an environment that is warm and wet and full of ambient life that processes organic matter. Treat your blisters immediately and aggressively. Bring moleskin, adhesive bandages, antiseptic. Do not walk through the pain. Stop and address it. You are not in a place that rewards endurance for its own sake.

Bring a walking stick. Or two. Not because the terrain is treacherous—it isn't, not in the way that mountains or rivers are treacherous—but because the sponginess will unbalance you over time, and a point of contact with the floor that isn't your foot will save your legs and your spine. The stick will also help you probe the floor ahead of you. The root-mass is centuries deep in places. It is also thin in places, over hollows where the root-pillars have arched and the mat has not yet filled the gap. You will not fall through—probably—but stepping into a depression up to your knee in an environment where you cannot see the ground through the threads and fuzz would be startling, disorienting, potentially injurious.

Do not, under any circumstances, use one of the built structures—the rod, the shards—as a walking stick or support. They are not yours. They are not the Expanse's. They belong to a story that has no teller, and you do not know what role they play in the equilibrium that has obtained since before you arrived.

## Navigation: The Problem of Sameness

The Expanse does not want you to be lost. It does not want anything. But it is built—grown—to make you lost.

There are no landmarks. The pillars are similar. The fronds are similar. The light is the same in every direction. The avenues and clearings that the pillars create feel distinct when you are in them and indistinguishable when you try to remember them. You will think: *I will remember this pillar, the one that curves slightly to the left, the one with the thick root-mass at its base.* You will not remember it. They all curve slightly. They all have thick root-mass at their bases. Your brain, evolved to remember distinct objects in distinct landscapes, will file them all under the same category: *pillar*. And when you try to retrace your steps, you will find pillars, and they will be the same pillars, and you will not know if they are the same *same* pillars.

**How to prepare:**

Bring a compass. It may not work. You do not know the magnetic properties of the universe you are entering. But if it works, it will give you a direction that is not *plant*, and a direction that is not *plant* is the most valuable thing you can have.

Bring string. A spool of bright, unnatural-colored thread—orange or pink or electric blue, something that does not exist in the Expanse's palette of amber-white and green and pale root-gray. Unwind it as you walk. Tie it to pillars at intervals. This is your lifeline. When you need to return, you follow the string. When the string ends, you stop. Do not go further than your string can reach unless you are certain—*certain*—of your direction and your will to continue.

The string will not last forever. The moisture will weaken it. The threads on the pillars might grow over it. The Expanse will slowly, patiently, without intent, consume your trail. Check your string when you return along it. If it has been swallowed by the floor or the threads, you have been given information: you have been here long enough for the Expanse to notice your passage, in its small, vegetative way.

Bring a notebook only if it is waterproof. Otherwise, bring waterproof markers and write on the pillars themselves—small symbols, arrows, numbers. The pale threads will grow over them eventually. But eventually is not immediately. Your marks will last long enough to navigate by, if you are quick.

Do not trust your memory. The Expanse is an environment designed—evolved—to defeat human spatial memory. Everything is the same because sameness is efficient. The plants do not need to distinguish one pillar from another. You do. Create your own distinctions and do not trust the ones you think you will remember naturally. You will not remember them naturally. You will remember *plant*, and *plant*, and *plant*.

## Psychological Preparation: The Equilibrium Problem

This is the preparation that matters most and that no amount of gear can solve.

The Green Expanse Beneath does not change. The light does not vary. The heat does not vary. The dampness does not vary. There are no seasons, no weather, no day or night, no cycles except the great cycle of water rising and falling and rising again, which operates on a timescale too slow to perceive. You will stand in the same amber-white light for your entire visit. You will breathe the same warm air. You will hear the same *tunk, tunk, tunk* of condensation falling.

Your brain is not built for this.

Human consciousness requires variation. It requires contrast—light and dark, hot and cold, loud and quiet, stimulation and rest. Without contrast, the mind begins to generate its own. You may see things that are not there. You may hear sounds that the Expanse is not making. You may feel watched, or accompanied, or pursued, though nothing in the Expanse is watching or accompanying or pursuing you. You may feel an urgent sense of meaning, as though the Expanse is trying to tell communicate something to you, as though the pillars are arranged in a pattern, as though the built objects are a message you are almost understanding. This is not the Expanse communicating. This is your pattern-recognition software running with no input, generating false positives.

You may, conversely, feel nothing. The sameness can produce a flattening of affect, a dullness, a sense that nothing matters because nothing changes. This is more dangerous than hallucination. Hallucinations are at least interesting. Apathy will make you stop walking. Will make you sit down on the warm, damp root-floor. Will make you decide that you do not need to eat, that you can rest for a moment, that the exit can wait. The Expanse will not argue with you. The Expanse will simply continue, and you will sit, and the threads will grow around you—not attacking, not consuming, just growing, because that is what they do—and the mites will tend you, because that is what they do, and you will become part of the floor, slowly, over centuries, a millimeter at a time.

**How to prepare:**

Set limits before you enter. Decide: I will stay for no more than X hours. Write it down. Tell someone. Make it real. The Expanse has no time. You must bring your own.

Set checkpoints. Decide: I will walk for one hour, then rest for ten minutes, then walk for one hour. Structure your time deliberately. The Expanse will not structure it for you. There is no sunset to signal the end of the day. There is no hunger that comes at mealtime. There is only the present, which is the same as the present before it and the present to come.

Talk to yourself. Narrate what you are doing. *"I am walking northeast. I have been walking for approximately two hours. I have consumed one liter of water and three food bars. The next pillar I will mark with the number seven. I am fine. I know where I am."* Say it out loud. Your voice will be something that is not the *tunk* of condensation or the creak of root. Your voice will be proof that you are real and separate from the system around you.

Do not seek meaning in the built objects. The bowl, the rod, the shards—they are not for you. They are not signs or messages or tests. They are artifacts of a presence that was here before the Expanse was the Expanse, and they will be here after, and they have nothing to tell you that you can use. Touching the rod—the warm, smooth, gray rod that the plants leave a clearance of darkness around—will feel significant. Your hand will want it to be significant. It is not significant. It is simply warm, because something inside it holds heat, and that is all. Step away. Do not linger.

Watch the pale caterpillars if you see them. They are the only other beings in the Expanse that move at a pace you can perceive. They are company, of a sort. But do not identify with them. They are not your kin. They are slow eaters of light, and they will continue eating light long after you have gone.

## What Not to Bring

Do not bring fire. There is nothing to burn that will sustain a flame, and the smoke would have nowhere to go, and the heat is not needed, and you would be introducing something into the Expanse that has not been there before. and you do not know what that does.

Do not bring weapons. There is nothing to fight. The mites cannot be fought. The caterpillars cannot be fought. The heat cannot be fought. The damp cannot be fought. A knife is useful for cutting string and opening food packets. That is what a knife is for here.

Do not bring electronics unless they are waterproofed beyond military specification. The humidity will find its way into everything. Your phone will die. Your GPS will die. Your flashlight will die, and you do not need it anyway, because the light is the same everywhere,always, and it is amber-white and warm and it comes from the fronds, and you cannot make it brighter or dimmer or different.

Do not bring expectations. The Expanse will not reveal its secrets because it does not have secrets. It has processes. It has cycles. It has a present that is identical to its past. You will not discover why the built objects were made. You will not discover what the Expanse is growing toward. You will not discover anything,because discovery requires change,and the Expanse does not change.

## The Exit: Leaving Before You Arrive

Know how you will leave before you enter. This is the most important preparation.not how you will walk, not what you will see, not what you will feel—but how you will turn around and retrace your steps and find the door or the portal or the seam in the world that you came through, and step back through it into a universe that has weather and sky and other people and the sharp relief of *not being inside something alive*.

The Green Expanse Beneath is tolerable. It is not hostile. It will not kill you. It will not even inconvenience you,particularly. It will simply be what it is,completely, and you will be what you are—small, temporary, irrelevant—and the Expanse will not notice your departure any more than it noticed your arrival.

This is the hardest thing to prepare for: the knowledge that you do not matter. That your presence is a thin, temporary interruption in a process that was functioning before you and will function after you. That the water that falls on you and the heat that rises through you and the light that shines on you is simply passing through, on its way to somewhere else, which is the same place it has always been going.

Prepare for that. Prepare for the experience of being unnecessary. And then go anyway, because you are a traveler, and that is what travelers do—we go, into places that do not need us,and we see what it is like to be there.

It is like this: warm, and bright, and damp, and green, and quiet, and complete.

And then you leave.
