# Preparation Guide: The Drowned Cloister

## The First Threshold: Breathing

You will need to breathe. This is the non-negotiable fact that everything else follows from.

At fifteen meters depth, the pressure is not extreme—roughly 2.5 atmospheres—but it is constant, and it changes the shape of your lungs, the density of your blood, the way gas behaves in the wet corners of your body. You have options, each with tradeoffs.

**Open-circuit scuba** will work, but consider what your bubbles will do. The chamber's soundscape is a living thing—bass thrum of kelp-columns, clicking carapaces, that sub-audible whistle through the constriction in the wall. Your exhaust bubbles will tear through that architecture of sound like a chainsaw through a cathedral choir. Every exhale will announce you as foreign, loud, mechanical, *dead* in the sense that the chamber understands life: as something that moves with water, not against it. The fauna will react. The fish will school tighter. The eel-things will retract. You will become an event in the chamber's ecology, and not a welcome one.

**Rebreathers** are better. Closed-circuit, minimal bubbles, longer bottom time. The silence you maintain will let you hear what the chamber is actually doing. You will feel the thrum in your sternum. You will notice when the whistle falters and the pause that follows. These are the rhythms that govern behavior here; to be deaf to them is to be blind.

**If your multiversal transit permits:** consider gill-grafts, oxygen-extraction membranes, any technology that lets you pull dissolved gas directly from the water. The advantage is not just silence but duration. The chamber rewards slowness. The fast, the loud, the briefly present—they see only the surface of what this place is. The chamber reveals itself over time, the way sediment reveals its layers.

Whatever you choose: you will be breathing compressed gas at depth. Understand decompression obligations before you arrive. There is no hospital here. There is only the stone floor, the silt, and the patient current.

## Thermal Protection

The water is cool. Not cold—not the knife-edge cold of deep ocean or polar current—but cool in the way that accumulates. At fifteen meters, in water that moves constantly, heat leaves you steadily, silently, the way money leaves you in a city you don't know well.

A **5mm wetsuit** is the minimum. 7mm is better. A **drysuit** is best, if you can manage the bulk and the loss of tactile sensitivity. Consider what you will need to touch: the walls, the cairns, the tools and offerings on the platforms. The chamber communicates through texture—the sticky clicking of fiber against fiber, the slick film of algae, the contrast between dead hardened walls and green flexing ones. If you cannot feel these things through your gloves, you are reading a book with mittens on.

**Hood and gloves** are mandatory, not optional. Heat loss from head and hands is disproportionate and insidious. You will not notice yourself becoming stupid, clumsy, slow. The chamber will notice for you, and it will not warn you.

## Light: What to Bring, What Not to Bring

The chamber exists in gray-green luminance, diffuse and directionless. This is not darkness. It is something else—a quality of light that has been filtered and scattered until it belongs to the water rather than the sun. Your eyes will adjust. Give them time.

**Do not bring a dive light.** Or: bring one, but keep it off. A dive light in the Drowned Cloister is not just an intrusion—it is a lie. It imposes direction on light that has no direction. It creates shadows where the chamber has none. It will show you surfaces but destroy the space between them, and the space between them is where the chamber actually lives.

If you must see detail—on the platforms, on the structures, on the floor—use a **low-output, wide-angle light**, as diffuse as you can manage. Turn it on briefly. Turn it off. Let the afterimage fade. Look again with your adapted eyes.

The bioluminescent fungi—pale blue-green, clinging to horizontal surfaces—are not bright enough to navigate by, but they are markers. They indicate the architecture from below. They are the chamber's own signage, and you should learn to read them as such.

## Movement: How to Exist in the Current

The current is constant, never fast, never stopping. This is the medium in which everything here operates. You are not visiting the chamber; you are visiting the water that happens to be shaped by the chamber.

**Learn to drift.** The inhabitants of this place—eel-things, translucent fish, the flat-bodied patroller—move with the current, not against it. They adjust, they micro-correct, they let the water carry them and then shift slightly to alter their vector. You, with your fins and your thrashing mammal limbs, will move against it. You will push. You will resist. This is your instinct. It is also your enemy.

**Minimize finning.** Every kick disturbs silt, which will reduce visibility. Every kick creates turbulence, which the fish will feel and interpret as threat. Every kick uses air, which limits your time. Learn to use your hands against the kelp-columns—gently, without gripping hard enough to damage the fibers—and let the current do the work of transit.

**Buoyancy control is survival.** You must be neutral, perfectly weighted, able to hover motionless at any depth. If you are positive, you will drift into the canopy and tangle in fronds. If you are negative, you will settle onto the silt and disturb whatever is growing there, or resting there, or hunting there. The crab-gardener tends its rows. Do not step on its garden. You do not know what it is growing, or why, or what will happen if the rows are disturbed.

## The Fauna: Observation Without Interference

The creatures of the Drowned Cloister are not aggressive. This is important to understand, and important not to misunderstand. They are not aggressive *toward each other*. The flat-bodied patroller does not disturb the schooling fish. The eel-things do not contest the crevices. The crab-gardener tends and feeds simultaneously, and no other creature challenges its claim.

This does not mean they are not aggressive toward *you*.

You are not part of this ecology. You do not have a role. You do not graze, you do not tend, you do not patrol, you do not occupy a niche. You are an anomaly, and anomalies in stable ecologies are either incorporated or expelled. The chamber has been incorporating and expelling things for longer than memory. It is very good at both.

**The eel-like creatures** will not approach you. They will retract into the lattice when you pass. This is not fear; it is economy. You are not food. You are not threat. You are irrelevant, and they have no reason to spend energy on you. Leave them in their crevices. Do not reach in. Do not try to coax them out. Whatever they are, they have lived in these walls longer than you have been alive, and they know the architecture better than you ever will.

**The translucent fish** will part around you and reform. They are beautiful. They are also the prey base for everything larger in this chamber. If you cause them to school tightly—through sudden movement, through noise, through light—you are signaling to every predator in the space that something is wrong. You are creating a moment of alarm that ripples outward. The flat-bodied patroller will notice. Its circuit will change. It will come to investigate. And then you will be in the presence of something the size of a door panel that has decided you are interesting.

**The flat-bodied patroller** is the apex presence in the chamber, as far as you can tell. It moves with unhurried confidence. It has traced its circuit so many times the path has become instinct. Do not get between it and its path. Do not touch it. Do not make yourself interesting. If it approaches, be still. Be neutral. Let it examine you with whatever senses it uses—lateral line, electroreception, something you do not have a name for—and let it decide you are irrelevant. You want to be irrelevant. Irrelevant is safe.

**The crab-gardener** is the one you are most likely to disturb, because its garden is on the floor and the floor is where you will spend the most time. Observe it from a distance. Note the rows. Note the care. This is agriculture, or something like it—cultivation, patience, the slow transformation of silt into yield. You are witnessing something profound: a mind that is not human engaging in a practice that humans consider definitive of civilization. Do not step in the rows. Do not touch the fibrous plants. Do not offer help. You do not know what it is growing, and you do not know what would constitute harm.

## Sound: The Chamber's Language

The sound never stops. This will become a problem.

Humans are not adapted to constant acoustic input. In air, we filter—we tune out the hum of refrigerators, the drone of traffic, the thousand small sounds of habitation. Underwater, this filtering fails. Sound travels faster, reaches deeper, resonates in bones and cavities that were never designed to receive it. The thrum of the kelp-columns will enter your sternum and stay. The click of carapaces will follow you into sleep. The whistle through the constriction will become your heartbeat's metronome.

**Ear protection is not an option.** You need your hearing to navigate, to anticipate, to read the chamber's moods. But you should prepare for the psychological weight of constant sound. It will be exhausting in ways you cannot predict. It will make sleep difficult. It will make thought difficult. After long enough, you may find it difficult to remember what silence felt like.

**Listen for the whistle.** It is the chamber's voice, or the closest thing to one. When it falters, everything changes. The fish school tighter. The eel-things retract. There is a pause—a held breath—and then the whistle resumes, and so does everything else. If you are in the chamber when the whistle falters, stop moving. Be still. Wait. This is not a warning; it is a recalibration, and you do not want to be the thing that the recalibration is recalibrating around.

## Touch: The Walls Are Alive

The walls will tempt you. They are intricate, woven, beautiful—thick as torsos, laminated with decades of growth, the inner layers dead and hardened, the outer green and flexing. You will want to touch them. You will want to trace the weave with your fingers, feel the sticky click of fiber against fiber, press your palm against the pulse of fluid moving stories high through internal vessels.

**Touch them. But gently.**

The walls are the chamber. They are its structure and its process, its history and its future. They do not decay; they are overtaken. New walls grow through old ones. Doorways half-close. Ceilings lower. The chamber you enter is not the chamber that existed a decade ago, and not the chamber that will exist a decade hence. When you touch the wall, you are touching something that is actively becoming, and your touch is part of that becoming.

Do not break the fibers. Do not force the weave. Do not take samples. The chamber is a patient system, but patience is not the same as tolerance. You are large, clumsy, and strong compared to the small things that live in and on these walls. What is a gentle touch for you may be a catastrophe for the things that make the walls their home.

## Time: The Chamber's Tempo

Everything here is slow. The growth of walls. The drift of current. The patrol of the flat-bodied swimmer. The work of the crab-gardener. The chamber operates on timescales that make human attention seem frantic, flickering, insubstantial.

**Plan for long bottom times.** The chamber does not reward the quick visit. It rewards the slow accumulation of presence. The first hour, you will see surfaces—the structures, the fauna, the obvious architecture of woven kelp. The second hour, you will begin to see patterns—the circuits of the patroller, the cycles of the current, the way the fish respond to shifts in light and pressure. The third hour, you will begin to feel the chamber's rhythm as something other than external—something that includes you, that moves through you, that has been moving through everything here for centuries.

**But do not exceed your limits.** Decompression, hypothermia, nitrogen narcosis—these are not abstract risks. They are the chamber's way of reminding you that you are a guest, and guests overstay at their peril.

## What Not to Bring

**Metal.** The chamber has none. The structures are stone and shell and cured plant stems, shaped by abrasion and patience. Metal will not harm the chamber, but it will mark you as something outside its history, something that does not belong to the long slow process of growth and overtaking. If you must bring metal—tanks, regulators, knives—keep it minimal, keep it quiet, keep it out of the chamber's story.

**Ceramic.** Nothing here has been fired. The chamber works in the wet, with wet materials, in wet ways. Fired ceramic is an artifact of dry land, of fire, of transformation through heat. It is the opposite of everything the chamber represents.

**Expectations.** The chamber is not a ruin. It is not an abandonment. It is not a temple, though it may feel like one. It is not a puzzle to solve or a treasure to claim. It is a slow event, a process extended beyond human attention. You cannot understand it the way you understand a building or a machine. You can only witness it, and even that requires more time than you probably have.

---

## What to Bring

**A notebook, waterproof.** Not for data. For impressions. The chamber will work on you in ways you cannot predict, and you will want to record them before they dissolve like the layers of silt on the floor—compressed, laminated, but eventually so thin that each layer is indistinguishable from the next.

**Patience.** More than you think you have. More than you think you need.

**Reverence, or something like it.** Not for gods or spirits—bring your own metaphysics, or none—but for the fact of a place that has been becoming itself for longer than your species has been keeping records. The chamber does not care about you. That is part of what makes it worth visiting.

---

## The Final Preparation

Before you enter the water, before you descend through the gray-green light toward the shelf of stone where the columns rise from their anchors, stand on the shore or the boat or whatever dry place you have come from, and listen.

Listen to the air. To the wind. To the sound of your own breathing, unmediated by water and pressure and the constant thrum of living architecture.

Memorize it.

Because when you are in the chamber, when the sound never stops, when the current moves through you and the walls pulse and the whistle threads through the water like a voice speaking a language older than words—you will want to remember what silence felt like. Not because the chamber's sound is bad, but because you will need to know the difference between what is yours and what is the chamber's.

You are visiting. The chamber is not yours. It will never be yours. It was old before your ancestors learned to swim, and it will be old long after whatever you leave behind has been incorporated into the weave.

Go gently. Touch little. Listen always.

And when the whistle falters, be still.
