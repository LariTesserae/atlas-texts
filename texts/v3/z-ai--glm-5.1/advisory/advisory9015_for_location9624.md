# Preparing for the Amber Ward

---

## The Central Paradox: You Must Not Bring What You Cannot Live Without

You need water. You are mostly water. Every cell in your body is a small bag of water containing smaller bags of water, and you leak water constantly through your skin and lungs and eyes, and you will die in three days without replacing what you lose. This is not negotiable. This is what you are.

The growth does not know water. The growth wants to know water. The growth will find any water you bring, and it will reach for it with a determination that has nothing to do with malice and everything to do with a tropism older than thought, older than the chamber, possibly older than the mineral that holds the chamber. A root-tip moves slowly, but it does not stop. It does not reconsider. It does not forget.

So: you must bring water, and you must not bring water. Sit with this. It is the first and most important contradiction of visiting the Amber Ward, and it will not be the last.

---

## What to Bring

### Water, but contained

Bring water in vessels that are completely, hermetically, absolutely sealed. Not cloth. Not leather. Not skin. The growth will find these. The growth has had longer than your species has existed to learn how to find moisture through barriers you would consider impervious. Bring glass, if you can, though glass will break if you press through the growth's denser chambers. Bring thick-walled metal, if you can, though metal has never existed here before and you will be introducing something the city has no relationship with, and I cannot tell you what the growth will make of that.

Bring only what you will drink. Do not bring extra. Do not bring water for cleaning. Do not bring water for comfort. Every additional drop is an additional invitation.

Drink quickly when you drink. Drink from your vessel with the seal opened for the minimum time necessary. Reseal immediately. Hold the vessel against your body, not against the floor, not against a wall, not against the salt-cristite which is alive in ways you have not yet imagined and which will sweat your moisture out of itself and deliver it to the nearest root.

You will sweat. You cannot help but sweat, in that heat. The sweat that falls from you will find its way to the growth. This is unavoidable. But you can minimize it. You can control where and how much you exert yourself. You can move slowly. You can rest in the emptinesses — those stretches of bare salt-cristite where the growth has not reached — though even there you will leave a residue of yourself that the mineral will remember and the roots will eventually find.

### Clothing

Do not wear cotton. Do not wear wool. Do not wear anything natural, anything that breathes, anything that the growth might recognize as cousin to itself. Wear synthetic fibers. Wear something that does not hold moisture against your skin, because the moisture against your skin is a beacon, and you are walking through a place that has been thirsty for longer than you can comprehend.

Cover as much of your skin as you reasonably can. Not because the growth will attack you — the growth does not attack, the growth grows, and these are different things only from your perspective, not from the growth's — but because your skin sweats and breathes and sheds, and every cell you leave behind is a drop of water the growth will spend centuries reaching for.

Wear gloves. Wear a face covering, if you can tolerate it in the heat. The heat will be significant. You will want to strip down, to expose skin to the thick air. Do not. This is the second contradiction: you will want to open yourself to this place, and you must close yourself against it.

### Footwear

The floor is salt-cristite where it is bare and root-mass where it is not, and both surfaces are warm, and both surfaces are, in their own ways, alive. The salt-cristite is crystalline and can be sharp where it has fractured, smooth where it has been worn. The root-mass is spongy and will yield under your weight and then slowly close behind you, the compressed tissue re-expanding, filling the shape of your footstep as though you were never there.

Wear thick soles. Wear something that will not puncture if you step on an obsidian flake left on a knapping floor a thousand years ago, still sharp, still capable of cutting. Wear something you can move quickly in, because there will be moments — during the peak heat-cycle, when the vent-leaves open wide and the sound becomes a roar that fills your skull — when you will want to move quickly, to get to an emptiness, to get somewhere the sound is less, and you will not want to be fumbling over your own feet.

### Light

You might think you need light. You do not. The growth provides light, or something like light — the slow garnet pulse of the bioluminescent nodules, dim and rhythmic and strange. Your eyes will adjust. The dimness is not absolute. It is simply low, and reddish, and constant.

If you bring a light source, understand what you are doing. You are bringing something the growth has never seen. The growth photosynthesizes. It has adapted to the dim pulse of its own nodules, to the faint warmth of the salt-cristite, to the particular quality of light that exists inside a bubble inside a mineral inside something larger. A bright white light might cause responses you cannot predict. The vent-leaves might close. The nodules might dim. The growth might turn toward you — slowly, slowly, over hours or days, but turning — with a kind of attention you did not intend to summon.

Bring a small light. Use it only in emergencies. Keep it covered when not in use.

### Sound protection

The sound is constant, and at the peak of the heat-cycle, the sound is unbearable. Bring ear protection, but do not use it constantly. You need to hear the growth. Not because the growth will warn you of anything — the growth does not communicate, the growth does not negotiate, the growth simply grows — but because the sound will tell you where you are. The density of the hissing tells you how close you are to a cluster of vent-leaves. The rumble of the root-masses tells you where the oldest growth is. The chorale of the nodules will help you navigate the chambers in the dim garnet light, because different chambers have different densities of nodules and thus different harmonic signatures.

At the peak of the heat-cycle, when the roar begins, put in your protection. Find an emptiness if you can. If you cannot find an emptiness, press yourself against a bare salt-cristite wall and wait. The roar will pass. The vent-leaves will close. The sound will return to its baseline hiss and creak and hum, and you will be able to think again.

Do not scream during the roar. You will not be able to hear yourself scream, and the sound of your voice will not carry, and the effort will only make you sweat more.

### A weapon

No. Do not bring a weapon. There is nothing to fight. The growth is not an enemy. The growth is not a predator. The growth is a process, and you cannot fight a process, and if you try — if you cut, if you burn, if you attack the vegetation that is also the city that is also the walls around you — you will find that the growth does not retaliate, because retaliation requires the recognition of an adversary, and the growth does not recognize you. The growth will simply grow over the damage, grow through it, grow because of it, because damage creates openings and openings admit ligor and ligor feeds growth. You will be helping it.

If you need reassurance, bring a blade. The obsidian blades the builders made are still sharp after centuries of stillness. You will find them. You can take one. But understand that a blade here is a tool, not a weapon, and the distinction matters. A tool shapes. A weapon destroys. The growth understands shaping. It has been shaped. It will not understand destruction, or rather, it will understand it as shaping of a different kind, and it will respond to it as it responds to everything: by growing.

### Something to record with

You will want to remember. You will want to prove you were here. You will want to show someone, eventually, what you saw, and you will find that your words are not enough, because the Amber Ward does not translate into words, because it is a place of heat and sound and dim garnet light and the taste of ligor on the air and the weight of absence pressing down on your chest, and none of these things photograph well, and none of these things fit in language.

Bring something anyway. Write. Draw. Record the sound if you can, though I suspect the sound will not record properly, will come back diminished or distorted or missing the frequencies that make your teeth ache during the peak cycle. Take photographs, knowing they will look like photographs of a dark room with something bronze and blurred in the corner.

The Amber Ward resists documentation. This is not because it is hostile to documentation. It is because documentation requires distance, and the Amber Ward does not permit distance. You are inside it. It is inside you — the heat is inside you, the mineral taste is inside you, the sound is inside you. You cannot stand outside what you are standing inside.

### Something to leave

The city has places set as though the builders are expected back. The dwellings have personal niches with personal objects. The tools are still where hands left them. There is room here for you, in a way — room in dwellings that were grown for bodies roughly your size, room at a meeting hall table where you could sit and rest your hands on the warm salt-cristite surface and feel the pulse of the growth beneath your palms.

Bring something to leave in a niche. Not water. Not anything that contains water. Bring something dry, something the growth will not reach for, something that will sit still and collect the bronze bloom of the near-alive dust and become part of the city's patient, directionless accumulation. A stone from somewhere else. A small carving. A coin, if coins still mean something to you. Something that says *I was here and I am leaving this because the builders left things and I do not know why they left or where they went but I am choosing to leave something too, and maybe that means something, and maybe it doesn't, and maybe the growth will grow around it and hold it the way it holds everything else that rests here long enough.*

You do not need to bring something to leave. But you will want to.

## What to Expect

### The heat

It will be the first thing you notice after you pass through the crack. It will hit you like a wall, like stepping into a kiln, like pressing your face against the skin of something enormous and fevered. The heat rises from below, from the salt-cristite floor, from whatever is beneath the floor — something that breathes, something that pulses, something that generates the warmth the growth has adapted to and depends on. The air is thick with it. The air is thick generally — dense, humid in a way that has nothing to do with water, heavy with mineral and the sweet-sharp vapor of ligor and the close staleness of an enclosed space that has been enclosed for geological time.

You will adjust. Not completely, but partially. After a few hours, the heat will become background rather than foreground. It will still exhaust you. It will still drain you. You will still need to rest more often than you think you should, and you will still need to drink — carefully, quickly, from a sealed vessel — more often than you want to, because every drop you sweat is a drop the growth is reaching for.

### The taste

The air tastes of iron and sulfur and something sweet. The sweetness is the ligor vapor, and you will learn to recognize it as the concentration increases — when you approach a fallen and split fruit, when you pass a channel where the ligor pools, when you enter a chamber where the growth is densest and the roots are actively drinking. The sweetness will coat your tongue. It will coat your throat. You will swallow it because you cannot help but swallow, and you will wonder what you are swallowing, and you will not know.

Do not taste the ligor deliberately. I know you will want to. I know the description will tempt you — the taste of iron and sulfur and sweetness and the numbing that follows, the hours of numbness on the tongue, the strange altered sensation that must mean something, that must be a door to something, that must be worth exploring. Do not explore it. You do not know what ligor does to a body that is adapted to water. You do not know what the numbing means. You do not know if the ligor is reaching for you the way the roots reach for water — slowly, patiently, without malice, with the simple determination of something that has never learned to stop.

### The sound

You will not be prepared for the sound. No description can prepare you. The closest analogue is a city at night heard from inside a large building — the muffled accumulation of a thousand processes, mechanical and organic and somewhere between. But the Amber Ward is not a city at night, and you are not hearing it from inside a building. You are inside the building. You are inside the process. The sound surrounds you. It comes from every direction. It comes from the walls and the floor and the ceiling of salt-cristite and the root-masses that have shouldered through the walls and the trunk-columns that support the canopy-roof and the canopy-roof itself, which breathes and hisses and cracks and settles above you like the hull of a wooden ship in heavy seas.

The baseline sound you can manage. The hissing, the creaking, the low rumble of expanding roots, the electric chorale of the nodules — these are constant, and constant sounds become background, and you will habituate. But the heat-cycle peak will break through your habituation. When the vent-leaves open wide and the roar begins, you will feel it in your chest, in your teeth, in the bones behind your ears. You will not be able to think. You will not be able to orient. You will stand still or press against a wall or sit on the warm floor and wait for it to end, and it will end, eventually, and the silence that follows — the relative silence, the return to baseline — will feel like surfacing from deep water.

### The absence

This will be the hardest part. Harder than the heat. Harder than the sound. Harder than the vigilance against your own water.

There are no animals here. No insects. No birds. No scuttling, no breathing, no chewing except the growth's own sounds. You are used to sharing spaces with living things smaller than yourself. You are used to the background awareness of other bodies, other metabolisms, other patterns of survival. In the Amber Ward, you are the only animal. You are the only thing that moves with intention. The growth moves, but it moves slowly, tropically, without intention, and the distinction matters.

And then: the absence of the builders. The places set. The tools still sharp. The dwellings still warm. The thresholds worn by feet that have not crossed them for — how long? Centuries? Millennia? Longer? The growth does not mark time the way you mark time. The growth does not mark time at all. It simply grows, and the city grows with it, and the evidence of the hands that shaped it persists because there is nothing here to erase it — no scavengers, no weather, no decay that is not the slow bronze bloom of the near-alive dust — and so you walk through a city that looks as though its population has just stepped out, and you cannot help but wonder: where did they go? And: are they coming back? And: what happened to their bodies, because bodies leave traces, and there are no traces, and the growth has been here the whole time, patient and hungry and reaching, and—

You will think about this. You will not be able to stop thinking about this. You will lie in a dwelling that was grown for someone else, on a surface that is warm and faintly yielding and lined with tissue the growth produced to seal the hollowed root-mass, and you will feel the pulse of the growth beneath you, and you will hear it breathing, and you will wonder if the builders are still here, if they became part of the growth, if the growth absorbed them the way it absorbs everything that rests here long enough, if the roots found them where they lay and if the ligor pooled around them and if the growth simply grew over them and through them and into them, slowly, over years, over decades, until there was nothing left that was not the growth—

Stop. You cannot know this. No one knows this. The absence is the absence. The growth does not tell you what it has done with the hands that shaped it. The growth does not tell you anything. The growth grows.

But you will not sleep well. This is certain.

## How to Move

Move slowly. Not just because of the heat, not just because of the sweat, but because the city is alive and you are moving through a living thing, and living things are damaged by haste.

The narrow passages between trunk-columns. The curtains of hanging root-fiber. The surfaces furred with overlapping shingle-leaves. All of these yield to you. All of these part and close around you. The growth does not resist you — resistance requires the recognition of an intruder, and the growth does not recognize you. But the growth is also dense, and the growth is also patient, and if you push too hard against a curtain of root-fiber, you may find that it has closed behind you, that the path you came through has been reclaimed in the minutes since you passed, that the growth has simply grown into the space you opened, because growing into open spaces is what it does, and you are slower than it is patient.

Mark your path if you can. Not with water-soluble markers. Not with anything that bleeds moisture. A scratch on salt-cristite, if you can make one — the mineral is hard, but it can be marked. A thread, if you brought one, tied from root to root — though the roots may grow around the thread and swallow it before you return. The Amber Ward is not a maze, because a maze has a solution and the Amber Ward does not have a solution, it has a process, and the process changes the layout on timescales you cannot perceive, root-masses expanding a millimeter a year, canopy-branches extending a centimeter a decade, the city slowly and constantly rewriting itself in the only language it knows.

Trust the sound. Different chambers have different acoustic signatures. The chorale of the nodules changes pitch and density depending on how many nodules are near you and how close they are to the walls. The hissing of the vent-leaves tells you where the major trunks are. The rumble of the roots tells you where the oldest growth is, and the oldest growth is near the center of the chamber, where the ligor seeps most abundantly, where the city was first grown.

The emptinesses are rest stops. Use them. Sit on bare salt-cristite, feel the warmth rising from below, listen to the sound of the growth breathing on the other side of the empty stretch, let your sweat evaporate into air that will carry it to the mineral walls that will sweat it out again and deliver it to the roots. You cannot prevent this. You can only manage it. Rest. Drink carefully. Reseal your vessel. Listen to your own blood moving, because in the emptinesses, you can hear it, and the sound of your own blood is the only sound in the Amber Ward that comes from something with a mind.

## How Long to Stay

Not long. A day, if you must. Two days, if you are stubborn. Three, if you are reckless. After three days, you will have sweated enough that the growth has tasted you, and the growth will remember you, and the roots will have reached toward the places where you rested, and the bronze bloom will have begun to coat the things you touched, and you will have left enough of yourself behind that the Amber Ward will hold your shape the way it holds the shape of the builders — as a residue, as a memory, as a pressure in the growth's slow patient reaching.

Leave before you stop noticing the heat. Leave before the sound becomes comfortable. Leave before you start to think of the dwellings as yours. Leave before you find yourself sitting at the meeting hall table with your hands on the warm salt-cristite surface, waiting for the other places to be filled, waiting for the hands that shaped this place to come back and sit across from you and explain what happened here, waiting for an answer the growth will never give because the growth does not know you are asking.

The crack that was not meant to be a door is still there. The stone tool that made it is still in its niche. You can leave the way you came, pressing your body through the salt-cristite edges that were worn smooth by ten thousand grips of hands that are no longer here. You can step into the irrelevant outside, which has never understood this place, and you can close your eyes and feel the heat leave your skin and taste the clean thin air and realize that you are thirsty, desperately thirsty, and that you have been thirsty for days, and that the thirst is not normal thirst, that it has a different quality, a different weight, a different hunger—

Drink water. Drink as much water as you can hold. The thirst will fade. The memory will not.

## What to Do If You Decide to Stay

Do not stay.

But if you stay — if the heat has become home and the sound has become silence and the dwellings feel like they were grown for you, specifically you, and the meeting hall table feels like a place where you belong, and the growth's slow patient reaching no longer frightens you because you understand that it is not malice, it is not hunger, it is simply what the growth does, and you have been doing things simply because you do them your whole life, and this is not so different—

If you stay, you will learn what the builders learned. You will learn to build with the growth. You will learn to prune and train and weave. You will learn to hollow root-masses into chambers and to wait three centuries for a canopy to grow flat and wide. You will learn to knap obsidian and to split salt-cristite and to make rope from bark-fiber and to live without water, which means learning to live with ligor, which means learning what the numbing means, which means learning something I cannot tell you because I did not stay, I left, I pressed through the crack with the stone tool in my hand and the taste of iron and sulfur and sweetness still coating my tongue and the sound of the nodules still humming in my ears and the heat still pressed into my bones—

I left. You should leave. The growth does not need you. The growth was here before the hands and the growth will be here after the hands, after all the hands, after every hand that finds the crack and presses through and discovers the city grown from thirst. The growth does not need you. But the growth will have you, if you stay, the same way it has the tools and the dwellings and the worn thresholds and everything else that rests here long enough. It will grow around you and through you and into you, slowly, patiently, without malice, without intention, without knowing or caring that you were ever anything other than another shape in the chamber for the roots to reach toward.

Leave the thing you brought on a niche in a dwelling that was not yours. Touch the stone tool on your way out. Step through the crack. Drink water. Remember that the outside is irrelevant only from inside; from outside, the outside is everything, and you are part of it, and you can leave.

You can always leave.

The builders could always leave.

You will not know why they didn't. You will never know why they didn't.
