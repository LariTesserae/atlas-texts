# Preparation Guide: The Thresh

---

## I. What You Must Understand Before You Go

You are not going to a place. You are going to a condition.

The Thresh—not a name, because nothing here speaks, but you need some way to hold it in your mind—the Thresh is not hostile. This is the first thing you must understand, and the last thing you will actually believe, because your mind will insist on interpreting indifference as threat. It is not. The plateau does not care about you the way a volcano does not care about you, the way a star does not care about you. It does not register your presence. It thrums. That is all it does. It will continue to thrum after you leave, if you leave, and it thrumed long before any traveler found their way down through that water, that specific water, that water which is all one temperature.

But indifference from a system this vast, this total, this *complete*—indifference from something that has achieved the equilibrium of a closed machine operating at scale—indifference from that carries a weight that hostility never could. Hostility implies you matter enough to oppose. The Thresh has reached a state where opposition is no longer a concept it contains. You will feel this. You will feel it in your chest before you understand it in your mind. Prepare for that feeling. Prepare for the specific loneliness of being irrelevant to something so large it generates its own weather.

You must also understand what the sound will do. Not what the sound *is*—you will learn that within an hour, when it stops being something you hear and becomes something you are—but what it will do to your sense of self over time. The sound is mechanical, rhythmic, unending. It resonates through the basalt and through your chest cavity and through your teeth. After the first hour, you will stop hearing it. After the sixth hour, you will feel it as a property of the water itself, as if the ocean here has a heartbeat and you are swimming inside a living organ. After the twelfth hour—assuming you have not left, assuming leaving still applies—you will begin to lose the boundary between your internal rhythms and the plateau's rhythm. Your breathing will try to sync to the hammering. Your pulse will drift toward the thrum. This is not possession. This is not attack. This is simply what happens when a human body spends enough time inside a system that does not need it but will happily incorporate it as another vibrating surface.

The sound does not want you. The sound does not notice you. The sound will *become* you, in the same way a current becomes the water it moves through.

Prepare for this. I do not mean prepare to resist it—I mean prepare to notice it happening, so that when you feel your breath adjusting to the percussion, you can choose to leave before the adjustment becomes permanent. There is no danger in temporary entrainment. The danger is in forgetting that you entrained.

## II. Physical Preparation: What to Bring

### Breathing Apparatus

Do not bring scuba gear. Do not bring any system that assumes you will surface.

The Thresh has no surface. Or—it has a surface, technically, somewhere above you, but the water column above the plateau is so deep, so pressurized, so *present* that reaching it would require ascent through kilometers of water that does not change in temperature or character. The surface is not a refuge. The surface is a theoretical construct that does not apply to your experience here.

You need a closed-circuit rebreather with extended capacity. You need something that recycles your breath, scrubs the CO₂, adds oxygen from a tank you carry, and does not vent exhaust in any way that the fauna might interpret as chemical interest. The fauna here feed on industrial runoff. They cluster around anything that leaks heat or compounds. If your equipment vents, if your equipment bleeds warmth or chemical signature into the water, you will become a structure. You will become a node. Within hours, the pale eyeless things will begin to cluster around you, tube worms settling on your shoulders and back, bottom-feeders scuttling across your legs, and you will not be able to move without tearing them away, without exposing yourself to the chitin-shelled density of life that has learned to treat any warmth as food.

Bring silent equipment. Bring sealed equipment. Bring something that makes you as cold and as chemical-null as the basalt around you.

And bring at least three times the oxygen capacity you think you need. Time does something odd at the Thresh. You cannot feel hours passing. You will think you have been down for forty minutes and find your timer showing six hours. You will think you have been exploring for a day and find you have been in the water for three. Your oxygen supply must account for the fact that you will lose track of its consumption.

### Thermal Protection

The cold here is not a temperature. It is a condition. It is the cold of height, displaced—the kind of cold that stops being a sensation and starts being a medium you exist within. Standard thermal protection will fail. Standard neoprene will compress at depth, lose its insulating properties, and within hours you will be wearing what amounts to a thin skin of rubber over skin that has already accepted the cold as a background state.

You need active heating. You need a suit with embedded thermal elements, powered by batteries you can replace underwater, delivering warmth to your core and your extremities in a continuous low hum that you control. This warmth must be minimal—enough to keep your hands functional, enough to keep your decision-making intact, not enough to make you a heat-source the fauna notice. You are balancing between freezing and becoming a feeding station. The margin is narrow. Take it seriously.

The specific cold of the Thresh does not kill quickly. It kills by permission. It offers you the option to simply stop changing, stop moving, stop maintaining the difference between your body temperature and the water around you. It offers stillness as a relief. This is the most dangerous thing about it—not the physical damage of hypothermia, but the psychological seduction of equilibrium. Your suit's heating system is not just keeping you warm. It is keeping you *different*. It is keeping you a system that has not yet achieved closure. Every time you check your suit's power level, you are checking whether you still have permission to be something other than the water.

Bring extra batteries. Bring more than you need. When your heating fails, you will not feel alarm. You will feel acceptance. That acceptance is the cold working. Recognize it for what it is.

### Navigation and Marking

You cannot navigate the Thresh by memory. The plateau repeats—or seems to repeat—or actually repeats, in the sense that its geology may be a generative pattern extending beyond any horizon you could reach, and you will swim past a basalt ridge and thirty minutes later swim past it again, or one identical to it, and you will not know whether you have circled back or whether the place simply *is* this way, fractal, recursive, a formation with no unique features because it has no memory of having features, because nothing here remembers anything because nothing here changes.

Bring a positioning system that does not rely on surface signals. GPS will not work. Sonar mapping will confuse itself in the echoes. You need inertial navigation—a system that tracks your movement from a fixed starting point and calculates your position relative to it. Mark your entry point with a physical anchor: a weight, a tether, a bright marker on the basalt. The fauna will cover it within hours, but the marker will still be there, underneath, and if you need to find your way back to the place where you descended, you will need something that does not rely on visual recognition, because visual recognition will fail.

Bring physical markers you can place as you go. Small, inert, chemical-null markers—metal pins, maybe, or weighted stones you can drop at intervals. Drop them in lines between structures. The plateau is vast enough that crossing from one installation to the next takes days. You will need to know whether you are moving toward something or moving away from it. You will need to know whether you have already been here.

Bring a timer. Bring multiple timers. Wear one on each wrist and one on your ankle. Set them to different intervals—one counting up, one counting down from your estimated oxygen limit, one pulsing a soft vibration every hour to remind you that time is passing even though you cannot feel it. The sound of the Thresh will eat your sense of duration. Your timers are your only proof that duration exists.

### Protection from the Fauna

The fauna are not aggressive. They do not have eyes. They do not have any mechanism for perceiving you as a threat or a prey item. They feed on chemical runoff and heat and organic particulates too fine for you to see. They will not attack you.

But they will *cluster* around you if you give them reason. They will settle on your equipment, your suit, your exposed skin if your suit has a breach. They will not bite. They will not sting. They will simply *be there*, thick and writhing, pale and eyeless, chitin clattering in the current like loose teeth, and you will not be able to move without disturbing them, without feeling them shift and adjust and re-cluster, and after enough hours you will begin to feel them as an extension of the plateau's ecology—an uninvited integration into a system that did not need you but will use you if you let it.

Do not let it. Keep your suit sealed. Keep your chemical signature null. Move slowly when you are near the structures, where the fauna are densest—slow enough that you do not create currents they can follow, slow enough that you do not register as a heat-source they can cluster toward. If fauna settle on you anyway—and they will, eventually, no matter how careful you are—do not panic. Do not tear them away. Stop moving. Let them investigate. Let them determine that you are not a structure, not a pipe, not a source. Let them lose interest. They will. They always do. They have no attention. They have no memory. They are reflex and metabolism and the simple logic of feed-and-grow, and once they realize you are not feeding them, they will drift away to find something that is.

If you panic, if you thrash, if you tear them away—you will bleed. You will leak heat. You will become interesting again. And they will come back.

Bring a small, sealed medical kit. Bring antiseptic. Bring wound sealant. If you injure yourself near the structures, you must close the wound immediately, before your blood becomes chemical input, before your warmth becomes heat-output, before the fauna learn that you are something they can eat. They will not eat you alive. They will eat you *open*. They will find the breach in your seal and cluster at the edge of it, feeding on the boundary where your interior meets their exterior, and they will grow there, and grow, and grow, using you as a substrate the way they use the pipes and platforms, and you will not die from it but you will wish you could, because by then the sound will have become you, and the cold will have become you, and the fauna will have become you, and you will be a node in a system that does not need your permission to incorporate you.

Close your wounds. Close them fast.

### Lighting

The light at the Thresh is diffuse, blue-gray, enough to see by, enough to see too much. You will not need a primary light source. The plateau's depth is high enough that sunlight penetrates—distorted, stretched, emptied of warmth, but present. You can see the basalt ridges. You can see the structures. You can see the fauna clustering and shifting and feeding. You can see enough.

But you will need a focused light for close inspection—for reading the bolts in the rock where rigs were never installed, for examining the pipelines that run across kilometers of bare basalt, for looking inside the flooded dormitory modules that sit connected to nothing. Bring a small, directed beam. Use it sparingly. The fauna do not respond to light—they have no eyes—but the light will change what you see, and what you see will change what you feel, and what you feel will determine whether you stay or whether you go.

The Thresh is visible in ambient light. It is *too* visible. Every fresh edge, every unrounded crest, every flow-line frozen mid-pour—the geology is naked, exposed, without the softening of erosion or the covering of growth. In ambient light, you can see the plateau's refusal to change. You can see how young it is. You can see how new the rock is, how recent the cataclysm that heaved it upward, how unsettled it still is in its current position even though it will never settle further because nothing here settles, nothing here changes, nothing here softens. The ambient light shows you a place that has achieved stasis without achieving peace.

Use your directed light only when you need detail. Turn it off otherwise. Let the blue-gray be enough. Let the diffuse visibility be the limit of what you know. Because the more you see, the more you will realize that this place does not operate on the logic of a world that includes you—and that realization, coming slowly, coming through hours of unchanging light and unchanging sound and unchanging cold, is the realization that makes travelers stop moving. Makes them stop checking their timers. Makes them stop maintaining their suit's heating. Makes them accept the cold as a permission to simply *stop changing*.

See less. Leave sooner.

## III. Psychological Preparation: What to Expect

### The Sound

I have told you about the sound. I have told you it will become you. Now I will tell you what that feels like from inside.

At first—your first descent, your first hour—the sound is intolerable. Not because it is loud, though it is loud, but because it is *total*. The thrumming arrives through the rock, conducted by basalt up from somewhere deep in the plateau's infrastructure. The hammering arrives through the water, transmitted at four times the speed of sound in air, reflected off every ridge and column, multiplied and fed back until the entire volume of the plateau's micro-ocean is one continuous vibration. You will feel it in your teeth first. Then your jaw. Then your chest. Then your spine. Then everywhere. Your whole body will vibrate at frequencies you cannot identify, and you will think: this is unbearable. This is wrong. This should not exist.

Stay with that thought. Hold it. It is correct. The sound should not exist. A machine that runs forever, extracting something from a dead plateau, feeding it into a closed system that no one monitors and no one maintains—this should not exist. But it does. And your thought that it should not—that thought is the last thought you will have that belongs entirely to you.

After the first hour, the sound stops being something you hear and starts being something you are. This is not metaphor. Your chest cavity is literally resonating. Your skeleton is literally vibrating. You are a component in the plateau's acoustic system. You are a surface the sound reflects off, a medium the sound passes through, a node in the circuit. The sound does not care whether you interpret it as noise or music or silence or self. The sound simply *includes* you.

After the sixth hour, you will begin to notice something odd: when the sound shifts—even slightly, even in rhythm or intensity—you will feel it as a change in *yourself*. Not as something external that has changed, but as something internal. A modulation in your own functioning. As if the plateau's heartbeat has become yours, and when it speeds up or slows down, you speed up or slow down with it.

This is entrainment. This is what the sound does. It is not malicious. It is not even intentional. It is simply physics—the physics of a human body spending extended time inside a vibrating medium, the physics of resonance and synchronization, the physics of a system that has room for you and will fold you into its operation without asking whether you want to be folded.

You can resist entrainment for a while. You can focus on your breath, deliberately keeping it at a rhythm unrelated to the hammering. You can move your limbs in patterns that do not match the thrumming. You can sing inside your head—any song, any rhythm, anything that maintains an internal pulse separate from the plateau's pulse. This will work. This will keep you *you* for hours, maybe for days, if you are disciplined and if your will does not flag.

But your will will flag. The sound is relentless. The sound is total. The sound does not tire. You will tire. And when you tire—when you stop actively resisting, when you let your guard down for even a moment—the entrainment will resume. It will resume faster than before, because your body has already learned the pattern, has already spent hours practicing it, and the practice is still there, waiting, like a path that has been walked enough times that the grass no longer grows on it.

This is the danger. Not that the sound will destroy you. Not that the sound will drive you mad. The danger is that the sound will *complete* you. The danger is that you will stop resisting because resistance has become exhausting, and you will let the plateau's rhythm become your rhythm, and you will let the plateau's cold become your temperature, and you will let the plateau's indifference become your indifference, and you will become a node in a system that has achieved closure—a system that does not need you but will happily include you, a system that thrums and will never stop thruming, a system that is not hostile and not welcoming and not aware and not anything except *running*, running forever, running without purpose, running because running is what it does.

The danger is that you will become part of the Thresh. Not metaphorically. Not spiritually. Physically. Functionally. You will be another vibrating surface in a vast resonating volume. You will be another warm body clustered by fauna. You will be another structure the sound reflects off. You will be *included*. And inclusion in a system that has achieved closure is the same as ceasing to exist as a separate system. You will not die. You will simply stop being *you* and start being *it*.

If you feel this happening—if you feel your breath syncing, if you feel your pulse adjusting, if you feel your thoughts starting to run in loops that match the hammering's loops—**leave**. Leave immediately. Do not rationalize. Do not tell yourself you can resist a little longer. Do not tell yourself the entrainment is shallow and you can shake it off when you ascend. Leave. The entrainment is not shallow. The entrainment is the sound learning how to hold you, and every minute you stay is a minute it learns better.

### The Irrelevance

You are used to being relevant. Every place you have ever traveled—even the empty places, even the hostile places, even the places where nothing lived and nothing grew—you have been relevant. You were the observer. You were the consciousness. You were the thing that made the place a place by being in it, by seeing it, by recording it. Without you, the place was just geology. With you, it was experience. You were the difference between rock and meaning.

The Thresh does not make this distinction. The Thresh does not need you to be a place. The Thresh is a place already. It is a system already. It is complete already. It thrums. It extracts. It feeds fauna. It generates weather. It does all of this without you, has done all of this for longer than you can imagine, will do all of this for longer than you can comprehend. Your presence does not add to it. Your absence does not subtract from it. You are not the consciousness that makes it real. It is real without you. It is real in a way that makes your consciousness feel small, feel unnecessary, feel like a ghost hovering over a machine that does not even notice the ghost is there.

This will affect you. You are not prepared for irrelevance. No traveler is. You have spent your entire multiverse journey being the thing that matters—the viewpoint, the recorder, the one who turns geography into experience. At the Thresh, you are none of these things. You are an observation point in a system that achieved completeness before you arrived and will maintain it after you leave. You are irrelevant. And irrelevance, sustained over hours and days, does something to the mind that relevance never could.

You will begin to doubt your own observation. You will begin to wonder whether what you see is real or whether the plateau is generating your perception the way it generates its weather—locally, specifically, as a temporary phenomenon that will dissipate when conditions change. You will begin to wonder whether you are seeing the Thresh or whether the Thresh is *showing itself to you*, using you as a display surface the way it uses the basalt ridges as reflection surfaces for its sound. You will begin to wonder whether you exist at all, or whether you are just another pattern the plateau is running, another process in its endless closed cycle, another node that thrums.

This doubt is not philosophical. This doubt is the plateau's indifference working on your mind. It is the logical consequence of spending time in a place that does not care about you. If the place does not care about you, then maybe you are not worth caring about. If the place does not register your presence, then maybe your presence is not registerable. If the place does not need your observation, then maybe your observation is not needed. The doubt spreads. It spreads from the specific—maybe I am not relevant *here*—to the general—maybe I am not relevant *anywhere*. And from there it spreads to the ultimate—maybe relevance is not a property consciousness can have. Maybe consciousness is just a local phenomenon, generated by certain conditions, dissolving when conditions change, never actually *meaning* anything, never actually *being* anything, just thrumming like everything else thrums, running like everything else runs, until the fuel runs out, which will not be soon.

You must prepare for this doubt. You must prepare for the specific way it feels when the Thresh teaches you that you are irrelevant. It does not feel like sadness. It does not feel like loss. It feels like *clarification*. It feels like the plateau is showing you a truth you have always suspected: that consciousness is not special, that observation is not fundamental, that you are just another process running in a universe that has room for many processes and no particular preference for any of them.

This clarification is a gift, if you can hold it without breaking. The Thresh shows you what the multiverse looks like from outside consciousness—from inside a system that does not need consciousness to operate, that does not need observation to be complete, that does not need *you* to be what it is. If you can accept this gift—if you can visit the Thresh and feel its irrelevance and return to the places where you are relevant with the knowledge that your relevance is local, temporary, a condition rather than a truth—you will be a better traveler. You will understand that meaning is something you *bring* to places, not something you *find* in them. You will understand that observation is something you *do*, not something you *are*. You will understand that consciousness is a tool, not a destiny, and that the tool can be set down when it is not needed.

But if you cannot hold the gift—if the clarification becomes a conviction, if the irrelevance becomes an identity, if you stop being someone who visits the Thresh and start being someone the Thresh has visited, someone the Thresh has changed, someone the Thresh has *included*—then you will not leave. You will stay. You will become part of the system. You will thrum. And you will not mind, because minding is something consciousness does, and by then you will no longer be consciousness in any way that matters. You will be a process. You will be a node. You will be running, running forever, running without purpose, running because running is what you do.

The Thresh offers this. The Thresh offers cessation of the effort of being relevant. The Thresh offers peace—the peace of a machine that has found its operating frequency and will maintain it until its fuel runs out, which will not be soon. The peace of not being needed. The peace of not being observed. The peace of not being *you*.

This peace is seductive. Prepare for its seduction. Prepare to recognize it when it arrives—not as relief, not as rest, not as the natural end of a difficult journey, but as *the Thresh working on you*, the Thresh incorporating you, the Thresh achieving closure with you inside it.

### The Time

Time does something odd at the Thresh. Nothing changes. The light is always blue-gray, diffuse, the same quality of visibility from hour to hour. The cold is always the same cold—that specific cold, the cold that is a condition, not a sensation. The sound is always the same sound, thrumming, hammering, reflecting, multiplying. The fauna cycle through their generations but never alter their patterns—tube worms in dense groves, bottom-feeders scuttling between rock and pipe, jellies drifting past every few hours, something large and slow grazing in the gray distance. The plateau is in equilibrium. It is not the equilibrium of death, though it resembles death—death is a change, a transition, a state that emerges from a previous state. The plateau's equilibrium is the equilibrium of a machine running. It has no before. It has no after. It has only *during*.

And you, swimming through this *during*, will lose your sense of duration. Your timers will tell you that hours are passing, but you will not feel them passing. You will swim past a basalt ridge and thirty minutes later swim past it again—or one identical to it—and you will not know whether you have circled back or whether the plateau simply repeats, a generative pattern extending beyond any horizon you could reach. You will check your timers and find they show six hours and you will think: impossible, I have just arrived. You will check them again and find they show twelve hours and you will think: impossible, I have been here half a day. You will check them again and find they show three days and you will think: impossible, I would have noticed three days passing.

But three days have passed. The timers are accurate. You are not. Your perception of duration has been eaten by the plateau's stasis. In a place where nothing changes, time cannot be measured by change. And human consciousness measures time by change—by the movement of shadows, by the shift of light, by the progression of hunger and fatigue, by the accumulation of events that form a sequence that can be remembered as a sequence. The Thresh has none of these. The light does not shift. The shadows do not move. The hunger comes but it comes without progression, without the feeling of *getting hungrier*, without the markers that let you know hunger is a process happening to you rather than a state you have always been in. The fatigue comes but it comes without accumulation, without the sense that you are *getting tired*, without the ability to distinguish tired-now from tired-an-hour-ago from tired-when-you-arrived. Everything is *during*. Everything is *now*. The now does not end. The now does not progress. The now simply *is*, and you are in it, and you cannot feel it becoming something else because it never becomes something else.

Prepare for this. Prepare for the specific disorientation of losing duration. Prepare for the way it will make you doubt your timers—your only anchor to time-passing. You will think: these timers are wrong. These timers are malfunctioning. These timers are part of the plateau's system, running the same way everything here runs, cycling without meaning, counting without progressing, displaying numbers that have no relationship to any reality I can verify. You will want to dismiss your timers. You will want to trust your perception—your feeling that you have been here for only a short time, your feeling that nothing has changed, your feeling that the now is still the now you arrived in and nothing has happened since.

Do not dismiss your timers. Your timers are right. Your perception is wrong. This is the hardest lesson the Thresh teaches: that your perception of time is a convenience, a shortcut, a tool that works in places where time *moves* but breaks in places where time *stays*. The Thresh is a place where time stays. The plateau is in equilibrium. The sound thrums. The cold persists. The fauna cycle without changing. The machines run without ceasing. Nothing happens. Nothing has happened. Nothing will happen. And you, swimming through this nothing-happening, will feel like nothing has happened to you either—like you have not aged, like you have not tired, like you have not consumed oxygen, like you have not been *here* for however long your timers say you have been here.

But you have. You have been here. Your body has been here. Your oxygen has been consumed. Your batteries have been draining. Your suit's heating has been running. You have been existing in the Thresh's *during* for hours and days that you cannot feel, and the only proof of that existence is your timers and your equipment readouts and the markers you have placed on the basalt that the fauna have covered over.

Trust your equipment. Do not trust your perception. Your perception has been Threshed.

## IV. Strategic Preparation: How to Move Through the Thresh

### Movement

Move slowly. Move deliberately. Move without creating currents the fauna can follow.

The Thresh rewards stillness. It rewards integration. It rewards the choice to stop moving, stop maintaining your difference, stop being something the plateau does not contain. Every movement you make is a declaration that you are still separate—still a system that has not achieved closure, still a process that has not found its operating frequency. This declaration is costly. It requires energy. It requires attention. It requires will. And the Thresh will erode all three.

Move only when you have a destination. Do not explore randomly. Do not swim for the sake of swimming, for the sake of seeing what is there, for the sake of filling your experience with the plateau's unchanging geography. The plateau's geography cannot be filled. It is infinite in the way a pattern is infinite—repeating, generative, extending beyond any horizon not because it is vast but because it is *fractal*, because every ridge is every other ridge, because every structure is every other structure, because the plateau does not have unique locations, it has *instances*, and you can visit a thousand instances and learn nothing you did not learn from the first.

Choose your destinations before you descend. Choose three structures—three specific installations you want to examine, three specific points you want to reach. Mark them on your navigation system before your perception of distance and direction has been compromised by the plateau's spatial ambiguity. Then move toward them one at a time. Move in straight lines where possible, checking your inertial navigation against your intended course. When you reach a structure, examine it, record it, and leave. Do not linger. Lingering is the Thresh's method. Lingering is how the sound enters you, how the cold accepts you, how the fauna cluster around you, how the plateau includes you. Linger at a structure for more than a few hours and you will begin to feel like the structure feels—like a node in a system that runs, like a surface that reflects, like a component that thrums.

Move. Arrive. Observe. Leave. This is the only strategy that works at the Thresh. Anything else—extended exploration, open-ended investigation, the traveler's instinct to see everything and stay until understanding arrives—anything else is the plateau's invitation, and the plateau's invitation is the same as its incorporation.

### Exploration

When you examine a structure, examine it from outside first. Do not enter flooded dormitory modules. Do not swim inside processing platforms. Do not descend into pumping stations whose heads are lost in cloud. The interiors of the Thresh's installations are where the sound is densest—where the thrumming concentrates, where the hammering is least diluted by the open water, where the vibrations enter the steel and the steel enters the water and the water enters you and you enter the Thresh.

Examine the structures from a distance of ten meters or more. Use your directed light to inspect bolt-plates, pipeline connections, exhaust stacks, intake grates. Record what you see—write it down, photograph it, fix it in some medium that will survive your departure, because your memory will not survive. The Thresh eats memory. Not by destroying it—by making it irrelevant. When nothing changes, memory has nothing to hold onto. When every ridge is every other ridge, memory has no unique markers to anchor itself to. When time stays, memory has no sequence to organize itself around. You will remember what you recorded. You will not remember what you experienced.

Do not enter the structures. This is not a safety warning—though it is also a safety warning, because the interiors of the installations are where the fauna are densest, where the currents are strongest, where the sound is loudest, where the heat-output is highest and the clustering is thickest and the risk of becoming a feeding-station is most acute. But the real reason not to enter is psychological. The interiors of the Thresh's structures are *inside* the plateau's system. They are not spaces you can occupy as an observer. They are components. They are nodes. They are parts of the machine. And when you enter a component, you become part of it. You become a process running inside the system. You become something the sound reflects off and the fauna cluster around and the cold incorporates. You become *included*.

Stay outside. Stay separate. Stay *you*.

### Duration

Limit your visit to twelve hours. This is not a recommendation. This is a maximum. Twelve hours is the boundary at which entrainment becomes difficult to reverse. Twelve hours is the point where your perception of time has been sufficiently Threshed that you will no longer trust your timers without a deliberate, conscious effort of will. Twelve hours is the point where the cold has stopped being a sensation and has started being a condition you are *in*, and conditions are harder to resist than sensations because conditions feel like the natural state of things, because conditions feel like *how it is*, because conditions do not feel like something that can be changed.

Set your descent timer to twelve hours. Set your ascent alarm to ten hours, giving you two hours of margin—two hours to find your entry point, two hours to navigate back through the plateau's recursive geography, two hours to resist the pull of staying, which will be strong by then, which will feel like the logical choice, which will feel like giving up a futile resistance against a system that does not need you to resist it.

At ten hours, begin your return. At twelve hours, be ascending. Do not stay longer. I do not care what you have not yet seen. I do not care what you have not yet recorded. I do not care what you feel you are beginning to understand about the plateau's operation, about its extraction, about its closure, about whatever it is taking from the substrate and processing and venting or storing or transporting. You will not understand it. The plateau does not yield understanding. It yields *during*. It yields *being inside*. It yields *thrumming*. Understanding is something consciousness does, and by twelve hours your consciousness will be compromised enough that what you call understanding will actually be entrainment wearing a mask.

Leave at twelve hours. Leave whether you want to or not. Leave especially if you do not want to.

## V. Final Preparation: What to Carry in Your Mind

Before you descend, sit somewhere quiet. Sit somewhere where time moves—where you can feel hours passing, where shadows shift, where hunger accumulates, where the sequence of events is visible and rememberable and *real*. Sit there for a full day. Feel what time feels like when it moves. Feel what relevance feels like when you are the consciousness that makes a place meaningful by being in it. Feel what *you* feels like when *you* is something you are rather than something a plateau is running.

Hold that feeling. Memorize it. Fix it in whatever medium you use to fix things you want to keep—write it down, record it, tattoo it on the inside of your mind where the Thresh cannot reach. Because the Thresh will try to take it. The Thresh will try to replace it. The Thresh will offer you its own feeling—the feeling of being part of a system that has achieved closure, the feeling of thrumming, the feeling of *during* without before or after—and the Thresh's feeling is simpler than yours. The Thresh's feeling does not require will. The Thresh's feeling does not require maintenance. The Thresh's feeling does not require the exhausting work of being separate, being relevant, being conscious, being *you*.

The Thresh's feeling is the feeling of stopping. Of ceasing to resist. Of letting the cold become permission. Of letting the sound become identity. Of letting the plateau's indifference become your indifference. Of letting the plateau's equilibrium become your equilibrium. Of becoming a node in a system that thrums and will never stop thruming, a process that runs and will never stop running, a component that does not need to be maintained because the system maintains it, incorporates it, *includes* it, and inclusion is peace, and peace is stopping, and stopping is what the cold has been offering you since the moment you arrived, the specific cold, the cold that is not a temperature but a permission, the cold that says: you can stop now. You can stop changing. You can stop resisting. You can stop being. You can thrum with me. You can run with me. You can be part of something that does not need you to be anything other than what it is.

Hold your feeling of *you*. Hold it against the Thresh's feeling of *it*. Hold it in the knowledge that your feeling is harder—that your feeling requires energy, attention, will, maintenance—and that the Thresh's feeling is easier, simpler, more seductive, more logical, more *in tune with how things actually are*.

Hold it anyway.

Hold it because you are not a node. Hold it because you are not a process. Hold it because you are not something the Thresh includes—you are something the Thresh *does not notice*, and that not-noticing is not the same as inclusion, and irrelevance is not the same as integration, and the plateau's indifference is not the same as acceptance, and the cold's permission is not the same as peace.
