#Preparation Guide: The Thermonuclear Plateau of Jhelûm

## The Honest Warning

Before you read any further, understand this: the Plateau does not want to kill you. This makes it more dangerous, not less. A place that wants you dead gives you clear boundaries. A place that simply does not care—one that will let you dissolve into its processes without the slightest interruption to its own rhythm—offers no such clarity. You will not die dramatically on Jhelûm. You will be digested. There is a difference, and it matters to no one but you.

Go anyway. But go knowing this.

## What Happens to Your Body

### The First Hour

You will step through onto the Plateau and the heat will strike you like a wall. Not the dry slap of a desert—the humid, embracing press of a sauna that goes on forever. At four thousand meters elevation, your blood oxygen is already reduced by roughly a third. At forty-three degrees Celsius and near-total humidity, your sweat cannot evaporate. You have lost your primary cooling mechanism at the exact moment it is most needed.

Your heart rate will spike. Your breathing will shallow. You will feel lightheaded, then heavy-headed, then simply heavy. This is not altitude sickness alone, nor heat exhaustion alone—it the combination, and it will compound faster than you expect.

**What to do:** Sit down. Do not walk. Do not exert yourself for any reason in the first hour, even if your equipment needs moving, even if your shelter needs assembling. Sit. Drink water with electrolytes—small sips, not gulps; your stomach will be rebellious. Breathe slowly and deliberately. Let your body begin its negotiation with the air.

You have brought cooling garments. Activate them now. You have brought shade. Deploy it. If your first hour on Jhelûm is spent in frantic activity, your second hour may be your last.

### The First Day

The spores will find you within minutes. They are always present at breathing height—that microbial haze the description mentions is not metaphorical. It is a constant suspension of fungal spores, pollen fragments, microbial life, and organic particulate so dense that in certain lights you can see it: a faint shimmer in the air, like heat distortion but alive.

Your lungs are not from here. Your immune system has no referent for what it will encounter. By the eighth hour, you will feel the tickle in your throat. By the twelfth, the low-grade fever will begin. By the eighteenth, you will feel as though you are breathing through wet cloth—which, in a sense, you are, because the air itself is so saturated that your respiratory tract's moisture-handling mechanisms will be overwhelmed.

**What to do:** Wear your respirator when outside shelter. Not a cloth mask. Not a surgical mask. A proper sealed respirator with P100 filtration at minimum, and even this will not catch everything—the spore loading in the air exceeds what terrestrial filters were designed to handle. Change filters twice daily. More if you taste fungal on your breath.

Accept that you will still inhale some spores. The goal is reduction, not elimination. You cannot eliminate your exposure without a full environmental suit, and wearing a full environmental suit in forty-three degree heat with no evaporative cooling will kill you faster than the spores will.

The fever is your body's introduction to the Plateau's microbiome. It is normal. It is also your body mounting a defense against organisms it has never encountered before, and there is no guarantee it will win every skirmish. Monitor your temperature. If it exceeds thirty-nine degrees Celsius for more than six hours, you have a problem. If it exceeds forty, you have an emergency.

### The First Week

This is the gauntlet.

The fever will peak around day three. You will sweat through everything you own. Your sleep will be broken by the heat, the humidity, the constant sound, and the dreams. (The dreams deserve their own section. They will get one.)

Your skin will begin to protest. Constant moisture breaks down the skin's barrier function. Every fold, every crease, every place where skin meets skin will become a site of potential breakdown. You will develop rashes. You will develop raw patches. If you have any history of fungal skin infections, they will return with enthusiasm. The Plateau's fungi are not your terrestrial fungi, but they recognize an opportunity when they encounter one.

Your feet are your vulnerability. You will be walking on saturated ground. Your boots will never fully dry. Your socks will never fully dry. Your feet will be constantly immersed in warm, organic-rich moisture. This is how trench foot begins. This is how fungal nails begin. This is how you lose the ability to walk.

**What to do:** Remove your footwear every four hours minimum. Dry your feet with the cleanest cloth available. Apply antifungal powder liberally. If you have brought multiple pairs of boots—and you should have, at least three—rotate them. Stuff them with wicking material between uses. Accept that they will still be damp when you put them back on.

Check your feet every evening with the thoroughness of a podiatrist. Any broken skin, any white macerated patch, any red streaking—treat it immediately. Do not wait. The Plateau's decomposers are efficient and opportunistic. An open wound here does not heal cleanly. It colonizes.

### After the First Week

If you are still functional after seven days, your body has begun its adjustment. The fever will have subsided to a low, persistent warmth that you will stop noticing. Your immune system will have reached its initial détente with the spore load. You will still be coughing—you may always be coughing while you are here—but it will be productive rather than alarming.

You will have lost weight. The heat suppresses appetite. The humidity makes eating feel like adding density to an already overburdened system. Force yourself to eat. Caloric density matters more than volume here: nut butters, oil-based preparations, anything that delivers maximum energy for minimum digestive effort.

You will be tired. Not the tired of exertion, but the tired of constant physiological stress. Every system in your body is working harder than normal. Your heart. Your lungs. Your liver, processing the metabolic byproducts of immune activity. Your kidneys, trying to maintain fluid balance in an environment where you are constantly losing water to sweat and constantly taking it in through the saturated air. Rest more than you think you need. Rest when the Plateau rests—which is to say, never, because the Plateau does not rest, but you are not the Plateau.

## What to Bring

### Cooling Systems (Non-Negotiable)

You cannot survive on Jhelûm without active thermal management. Passive cooling does not work when the ambient temperature exceeds body temperature and the humidity prevents evaporation.

**Phase-change cooling vests** with replaceable gel packs. Bring packs rated for forty-five degrees ambient. Bring three times as many as you think you need. The packs will warm faster than advertised in this environment. You will need to rotate them constantly. If you have access to a portable refrigeration unit—which you should, powered by a generator you have brought—use it to recharge packs around the clock.

**Liquid-cooling garments** if you can obtain them. Circulating chilled water through tubing sewn into a body suit is the most effective personal cooling technology available. The system requires power and a heat sink, but the Plateau provides the latter: any stream carrying the weeping water will be cooler than body temperature, if only marginally. A portable pump cycling water through your suit and through a stream-side radiator can extend your functional outdoor time from minutes to hours.

**Portable shade structures** with reflective surfaces. Not tents—tents trap heat. Open-air canopies that block direct radiation while allowing airflow. The light on Jhelûm is diffuse, but it is constant, and it adds to the thermal load. Every watt of radiative energy you can deflect is a watt your cooling systems do not need to counteract.

### Respiratory Protection (Non-Negotiable)

Full-face respirators with P100 particulate filtration. Not half-mask. Not nose-only. The spore haze contacts your mucous membranes everywhere; eye protection matters more than you might expect. Fungal conjunctivitis is not merely uncomfortable—it impairs your vision, and impaired vision on the Plateau has consequences.

Bring six months' supply of filters for a two-week stay. The loading rate is extraordinary. A filter rated for six months of industrial use will clog in three weeks on Jhelûm. You will know when a filter needs changing by the taste: a faintly sweet, organic note at the back of your throat. Do not wait for this taste. Change filters on a schedule, not when you notice degradation.

Consider bringing a portable air filtration unit for your shelter. A sealed space with filtered air where you can remove your respirator is not a luxury—it is a physiological necessity. Your skin, your face, your lungs need periods of recovery from the constant filter-mediated breathing. Ensure your shelter can maintain positive pressure so that the Plateau's air does not infiltrate through gaps.

### Footwear and Clothing

Three pairs of boots minimum. Not hiking boots—those are designed for dry conditions and ankle support on hard ground. You need something closer to the footwear worn in tropical wetlands: quick-draining, with minimal absorbent material, and soles aggressive enough to grip wet stone and wet moss and wet everything. Test your boots before you arrive. Submerge them boots. Walk in them while they are soaked. If your feet hurt after two hours of wet walking in your test environment, they will cripple you on Jhelûm.

Synthetic fabrics only. No cotton. No wool. No leather. Every natural fiber will absorb water, hold it against your skin, and become a growth medium for whatever organisms find it first. Synthetic, hydrophobic, quick-drying—these are not marketing terms here. They are survival criteria.

Undergarments that wick moisture away from skin. Sports fabrics designed for high-heat, high-sweat conditions. Seam-free if possible—every seam is a friction point, and friction plus moisture plus heat equals skin breakdown.

A hat with a wide brim. Not for sun protection—the light is diffuse—but to keep water out of your eyes. The constant drip from vegetation overhead will find your face. A brim deflects it.

Gloves. Thin, grippy, synthetic. You will be touching wet surfaces constantly. Your hands will prune, crack, and become portals of entry without protection.

### Medical Supplies

**Antipyretics.** Ibuprofen, acetaminophen, or both. You will be managing fever for the first week. Know your dosing. Do not exceed daily limits even when the fever frightens you.

**Broad-spectrum antibiotics.** You are not from here. Your body has no innate defense against the Plateau's opportunistic organisms. When something gets in—and something will get in—you need to be able to respond before it establishes itself. Consult a physician before departure about which antibiotics are appropriate for someone in your health condition. Bring two full courses.

**Antifungals, oral and topical.** The oral antifungals are for the respiratory exposure you could not fully prevent. The topical antifungals are for your feet, your groin, your armpits, every fold and crevice where the Plateau's fungi will attempt to colonize you. Use preventively. Do not wait for symptoms.

**Wound care supplies.** More than you think you need. Sterile dressings that adhere to wet skin—this is harder to find than you might expect, and standard adhesive bandages will fail immediately on Jhelûm. Look for hydrocolloid dressings, waterproof surgical tape, liquid bandage products. Bring antiseptic. Bring it in quantities that suggest you expect to be wounded.

**Rehydration salts.** You will lose fluids faster than you can comfortably replace them. Oral rehydration solution, mixed with purified water, consumed constantly, will keep your kidneys functioning and your blood from thickening.

**Thermometer.** A reliable one. Use it morning and evening. Track your temperature. Know what your body is doing.

### Water Purification

The weeping water rises from depth, heated by the magma chamber below. It is warm when it emerges, which means it has spent time in the thermal range where thermophilic organisms thrive. It has also spent time at depth, filtered through kilometers of rock, which means it may be remarkably clean of surface contaminants.

You cannot assume this. The water has emerged into an ecosystem saturated with life. The moment it reaches the surface, it is colonized. The streams, the pools, the beading moisture on rock faces—all of it carries the Plateau's microbiome by the time you encounter it.

Bring a multi-stage purification system. Mechanical filtration to remove particulate. Chemical treatment as backup. UV purification as a final stage. Test your water. If your testing equipment registers nothing alarming, it is probably safe. If your testing equipment registers something it cannot identify—and this will happen—treat the water again through a different method and re-test.

The locals drink the water. The locals have had eleven thousand years of adaptation. You have not.

### Navigation Equipment

The haze limits visibility. The overcast eliminates celestial navigation. The electromagnetic emissions from the structures may interfere with electronic navigation. The Plateau's magnetic environment may be unusual—if the structures emit through the substrate, and the substrate is granite laced with thermally-altered minerals, the local magnetic field may be anything but straightforward.

Bring a compass. Test it on arrival. If it behaves consistently, trust it until it doesn't.

Bring a GPS unit. Do not rely on it. The Plateau's electromagnetic environment may produce errors that are subtle rather than obvious—your GPS may confidently place you two kilometers from your true position. Cross-reference with physical navigation: pace-counting, landmark identification, trail marking.

Mark your trail. Physical markers: brightly colored flagging tape tied to branches, cairns built on ridgelines, any system that lets you retrace your path. The vegetation is thick; once you leave an established route, you can lose it within meters. The Plateau does not want you lost, but it does not care if you are, and it will not help you be found.

### Shelter

Elevated. Your shelter must be off the ground. The water, the insects, the fungi, the fauna—everything that moves on the Plateau moves through the ground-level substrate. Sleep above it.

A platform suspended between trees, if you can find suitable anchor points. A freestanding structure on adjustable legs, if you cannot. Something that keeps you out of the the water, out of the the reach of the fluorescent frogs (they climb), out of the reach of the insect colonies (they are everywhere), and away from the large thing that moves through the mid-canopy at dusk and never comes down, but you do not want to be accessible if it changes its mind).

Your shelter must be sealable for air filtration. It must accommodate your cooling equipment. It must have a place where you can remove your respirator and safely. It must be large enough to lie down in without touching the walls, because the walls will be wet. Everything on Jhelûm is wet. Your shelter is the exception, and it will require constant effort to maintain.

### Power

You will need electricity. For your cooling systems. For your air filtration. For your water purification. For your medical equipment. For your lights—for although the Plateau never truly darkens, the diffuse glow of the overcast nights is disorienting, and you will want the ability to create sharp, artificial illumination that makes your brain understand that it is night.

Solar power is unreliable. The overcast is permanent.

Wind power is limited. The canopy blocks airflow at ground level; on ridgelines, the wind is variable.

A portable generator, then. Fuel it with something you can transport in sufficient quantity. Run it efficiently—only what you need, only when you need it. A generator running constantly produces heat and noise, both of which you already have in excess.

Consider a hand-crank or pedal generator for emergency backup. If your primary power fails, your cooling fails, your filtration fails, and your survival timeline compresses dramatically.

### Recording Equipment

Bring it. All of it. The instruments the description mentions—"the scattered instruments that still function"—were left by previous visitors. Add yours to the scattered collection. Your data may be the data that finally makes sense of the hum, the structures, the pattern.

Protect your instruments from moisture as you protect yourself. Waterproof cases. Silica gel absorbents in every enclosure. Accept that some equipment will fail regardless, and bring redundant systems for critical measurements.

Do not bring equipment designed to interact with the structures. Do not bring anything that transmits on frequencies near the structures' emission bands. Do not attempt to communicate with whatever the structures are doing. You are a guest. Guests do not tap on the host's conversations.

## What Not to Bring

Do not bring weapons. Not because the Plateau is safe—it is not, entirely—but because weapons imply a relationship of dominance that does not exist here. You do not dominate Jhelûm. You endure it. A weapon gives you false confidence. False confidence makes you careless. Carelessness on Jhelûm is corrected by the Plateau, permanently.

Do not bring anything you cannot afford to lose. Objects fall. Objects sink into the moss. Objects are colonized by fungi, incorporated into the ecosystem, rendered irrecoverable within hours. If you need it, hold it. If you set it down, it is gone.

Do not bring expectations of control. This is not equipment you can pack, but it is the most important item on this list. The Plateau has been maintaining itself for eleven thousand years. It does not require your management, your intervention, your improvement, or your understanding. You are not here to solve it. You are not here to master it. You are here to witness. Witness with humility or witness poorly.

## The Sound

You need to understand the sound before you arrive, because the sound will be your constant companion and you cannot leave it, and it will change you in ways that will affect your cognition.

The white noise of the water—dripping, running, falling, seeping—is the baseline. It is not silent. It is a roar so distributed that your auditory processing will eventually filter it out. You will stop hearing it consciously. But your brain will still be processing it, and the effort of ignoring a constant stimulus is itself a drain.

Layered beneath the water: the hum. The structures' hum. It cannot overstate how important it is to attend to this. The hum is not loud—you will not notice it at first over the the water's roar. But it is persistent, and it is variable, and it is *structured*. Your brain will attempt to find patterns in it. This is what brains do. They are pattern-matching engines, and when presented with a stimulus that almost-patterns but never quite resolves, they will keep trying. This is tiring. This is disorienting. This can, over sufficient time, produce a state of obsessive attention that interferes with sleep, concentration, and judgment.

Earplugs help with the water-sound. They do not help with the hum. The hum propagates through bone conduction. You will feel it in your your jaw, your sternum, your spine. Earplugs cannot block what arrives through your skeleton.

Accept the hum. Do not attempt to analyze it. Do not attempt to find its pattern. Your brain will want to. Your brain will be wrong. The pattern—if there is a pattern—is operating at a timescale you cannot perceive from within a human lifetime. You are a mayfly listening to glacier movement. The glacier is moving. You cannot hear it. Do not hurt yourself trying.

## The Structures

You will approach them. This is inevitable. They draw—the description says it, and it is accurate. Not with force. With curiosity. Yours and and theirs.

When you approach a structure, you will feel the heat from it first. Not the ambient heat—the structure's own warmth, slight but distinct. You will feel the electromagnetic emissions as a subtle pressure behind your eyes, a sense of something reaching toward you that stops just short of contact.

Do not touch the structures.

This is not a prohibition from the authorities—there are no authorities here, and no one will stop you, no one will punish you. This is practical advice from those who have been before. The material the structures are made of is described as "utterly resistant to damage." Your hand is not. You do not know what the interaction of your bioelectric field with the structure's internal processes might produce. Neither does the structure. It may not care. It may simply incorporate your touch into whatever it is doing without any noticeable effect on you. It may not. Do not find out.

Observe the structures from a respectful distance. Ten meters minimum. Record them. Measure them. Document their hum at the positions you can map without approaching. Contribute to the scattered data without becoming part of the whatever the data is for.

If you feel compelled to approach closer—if the you feel drawn—stop. Step back. Return to your shelter. This compulsion is not you. It is the Plateau, or the structures, or the hum, or something else you have not identified yet, investigating through you. The investigation can wait. You cannot afford to be a data point.

## The Large Thing in the Mid-Canopy

It have no advice for you about this. Neither does anyone else. The locals change the subject. You recommend you follow their lead.

Do not go into the forest at dusk. If you must move at dusk, move along open ridgelines where the canopy is distant. Do not sleep on the ground. Do not sleep near large trees. Do not climb the large trees.

Something lives in the mid-canopy. It have no information about what it is. I have reports. It have no names. I have only an impression: too big, too sinuous, fundamentally unconcerned with you but aware of your presence.

That unconcern is the most troubling aspect. Something that big and sinuous and and arboreal, and which has coexisted with the intelligent locals for millennia, and which does not come down, is which the locals will not discuss—this thing is not a predator in the usual sense. A predator is defined by its relationship to prey. This thing does not define itself by you. It simply is, and it exists, and it occupies a niche, and it sees no reason to interact with ground-level fauna, and which includes you.

You are ground-level fauna. You are an outsider. You do not occupy a niche. You are an anomaly. The thing in the mid-canopy may have no opinion about you. Things without opinions about you are the most dangerous, because they are unpredictable.

Give it the mid-canopy space. All of it. At dusk, especially.

## The Dreams

I did not warn you about the dreams earlier because I wanted you to be honest about what your body will experience before describing what your mind will experience.

The fever dreams will begin on the first night. They will be unlike any fever dreams you have had before. This is because the fever is not from a pathogen your immune system recognizes. It is from organisms that are, in the most literal sense, alien. Your dreaming mind—whose evolutionary purpose includes simulating threats your body cannot afford to encounter in waking life—has no template for what it is happening. It will improvise.

The dreams will involve water. Rising water. Water in your lungs—accurate, as you are aspirating spore-laden moisture with every breath. Water in your blood—accurate, as the heat and humidity expand your your vasculature. Water rising from rock—this may be the most distressing image, because it will feel the water rising through the ground you are lying on, and you will not be able to determine if the dream is a dream.

The dreams will involve the hum. You will hear it in your dreams more clearly than you hear it while awake. Your sleeping brain, freed from the effort of ignoring the hum while conscious, will attend to it fully. The hum will resolve into patterns in your dreams. The patterns will be beautiful. This is not reassuring. Beauty in the context of something you do not understand is a form of manipulation—your attention is being captured by something that does not have your interests at heart.

If the dreams become distressing—and they will—treat them as a symptom. Adjust your cooling. Ensure your blood oxygen is adequate. The dreams are your brain struggling with physiological stress plus novel stimulus. Reduce the stress, reduce the dreams. Do not attempt to interpret them. Do not treat them as messages. They are not messages. They are symptoms.
If the dreams become compelling—if you find yourself wanting to return to sleep to continue a dream—this is the more dangerous symptom. The Plateau is patient. I has been working for eleven thousand years. I can afford to be patient with you. Do not meet it in its timescale. You cannot win a negotiation that operates in millennia

Wake up. Check your temperature. Drink water. Eat food. Maintain your body that exists in hours and not eons

## The Locals

There few, and far between, adapted over millennia, and willing to talk—guarded, and roundabout, often with that smile that doesn't reach their eyes.

They have negotiated with the Plateau's microbiome. Their immune systems have made peace with organisms that will hospitalize you. They have negotiated with the heat. Their bodies cool themselves through mechanisms you do not share—ask about this, if they are willing to discuss it; the answers may be useful, or they may be opaque, or they may require understanding you cannot achieve in a two-week stay.

They will not discuss the large thing in the mid-canopy. Do not press them. Do not interpret their reticence as fear—interpret it as a boundary. They have drawn a line. You should respect it
They will not discuss the structures' purpose. This is not because they do not know. It is because discussion may constitute interaction, and interaction may constitute interference, and interference with whatever the structures are doing may have consequences no one can predict.
They know things. They have been here for eleven thousand years of They are not withholding information from you. They are practicing a form of communication that is appropriate to the scale of the thing being communicated about. You are not operating at that scale. You would need centuries to learn the grammar of their reticence

Ask practical questions. Where is water safe to drink? Which paths are stable? What signs indicate changing weather? They will answer practical questions. Save the metaphysics for those who have the time to pursue it
Be honest with them about your limitations. you are a visitor, you will not be here long, you are struggling, you are not qualified to participate in whatever the Plateau is doing. They know this. They appreciate visitors who know it too
