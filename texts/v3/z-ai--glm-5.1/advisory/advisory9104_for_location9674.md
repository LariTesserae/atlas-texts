# Preparation Guide: The Manufactory at Rest

---

## What You Are Walking Into

You are not visiting a place. You are stepping inside a process.

Understand this before anything else. The Manufactory will not greet you. It will not warn you. It will not adapt to your presence in any way. You will become a small, warm, wet thing moving across a surface that was designed for functions that do not include you, and your only advantage—your only advantage—is that you came prepared.

Most of this guide will not be about danger. The Manufactory is not dangerous, not in the way that worlds of fire or predatory megafauna or hostile physics are dangerous. It is *difficult*. It grinds at you through accumulation. The moisture and the warmth and the sameness and the quiet and the grey—each one tolerable, all of them together corrosive to the mind and the body in ways that sneak up on you over hours and days.

You are preparing for a marathon through a place that does not acknowledge marathons.

## The Body: Physical Preparation

### Moisture Management

This is your primary physical concern. Not the creatures. Not the warmth. The wet.

Everything you bring will be damp within an hour. Everything you wear will be damp within minutes. The air does not merely contain humidity; it *imposes* humidity, forcing moisture into fabric and leather and skin with the patient inevitability of water finding its level. You will feel it on your forearms first, then your neck, then the small of your back, then everywhere. The condensation does not evaporate. It accumulates.

**What to bring:**

- **Synthetic, quick-dry clothing only.** Cotton will become a second skin of cold wet weight within hours and will not dry until you leave this universe entirely. Wool retains warmth when wet but becomes unbearably heavy. You want lightweight synthetic layers—the kind designed for tropical kayaking, not desert hiking.

- **Waterproof bags within waterproof bags.** Not one layer of protection. Two minimum, three for anything critical. Dry bags with roll-top seals. Zip-lock systems as redundancy. The moisture here is *penetrative*—it finds seams, it finds micro-gaps, it wicks along zippers and threads. Test your bag system by submerging it in warm water for six hours before you travel. If anything inside is damp, the Manufactory will find that dampness and amplify it.

- **Mold and mildew prevention.** Pack silica gel packets in quantities that seem absurd. Pack anti-fungal powder for your feet and your groin and your armpits. The creatures here have adapted to the constant moisture over generations. You have not. Skin breakdown—maceration, the softening and peeling of skin from prolonged wet exposure—will become a medical issue within 48 hours if you do not actively prevent it. Change your socks whenever you rest, even if the fresh socks will be damp within minutes. The brief period of relative dryness matters.

- **Towel.** One small, highly absorbent microfiber towel. You will use it constantly. You will never feel truly dry, but you can be *less* wet, and that difference will matter more than you think.

### Temperature Regulation

The warmth is constant and inescapable. It radiates from below and from the walls and from the air itself. Not hot enough to cause acute heat injury, but warm enough that your body must continuously thermoregulate without ever getting a break.

**What this means:**

- You will sweat. The moisture on your skin will be a mixture of ambient condensation and your own perspiration, and you will not be able to tell which is which.

- **Hydration is critical.** The water that emerges from the floor seams is not your water. It is mineral, warm, and part of a closed system you do not fully understand. It may contain compounds your kidneys cannot process. It may be fine. The Manufactory does not care either way, and you should not gamble. Bring your own water, in bladders or bottles sealed against the ambient humidity. Bring more than you think you need—the warm, wet air increases your fluid loss through respiration and skin in ways that are difficult to calculate. Plan for at least six liters per day of intended stay, and bring purification methods as backup.

- **Electrolyte replacement.** You will lose salts. Pack electrolyte powder or tablets. The weakness will creep up on you—first a heaviness in your limbs, then a fog in your thinking, then muscle cramps that make walking difficult. Replace what you lose.

- **Clothing layers for rest periods.** When you stop moving, the warmth will still be there, but your body's heat production will drop. You may feel chilled despite the ambient temperature—a paradox of continuous warmth that becomes uncomfortable when your own activity level decreases. A thin insulating layer that stays in your waterproof bag until needed.

### Footwear

The floor is ridged. Fine parallel lines, like the marks of a blade drawn across wet clay. This is not a smooth surface. It is textured at a scale that will interact with your footwear in specific ways.

**What to wear:**

- **Soles with some flexibility.** Rigid mountaineering boots will not conform to the ridges and will create pressure points across the balls of your feet and your arches. You want soles that can flex and grip—trail runners or approach shoes, not heavy boots.

- **Aggressive tread.** The ridges provide excellent traction when your sole can engage with them, but the moisture creates a lubricating film. You want rubber compounds designed for wet rock.

- **Drainage.** Your feet *will* get wet. This is inevitable. Accept it and plan for it—shoes that drain rather than pool, socks that manage moisture rather than resist it.

- **Backup footwear.** At minimum, one additional pair. The constant moisture will degrade your shoes faster than you expect. The adhesive in many modern shoe constructions does not tolerate prolonged warmth and humidity well. Check your shoes every time you rest. When the soles start to separate—and they will—switch to your backup and attempt field repair with the adhesive you brought.

### The Ridges and Your Body

After hours of walking on the ridged floor, you will notice something: the texture creates a specific kind of fatigue. Your feet must constantly micro-adjust to the linear pattern, and your stabilizing muscles will work harder than they would on flat ground. This is a small thing that becomes a large thing over time.

Additionally—this is unpleasant but necessary to address—the ridges create a specific interaction with any part of your body that contacts the floor. If you sit, if you lie down to rest, the ridges will press into your skin in parallel lines. Over time, this creates pressure patterns that can restrict circulation or cause skin irritation. Bring a sleeping pad. Not for comfort—for medical necessity. The floor will mark you if you let it.

## Navigation: How Not to Die of Sameness

The Manufactory is vast. You could walk for years and not reach its edge. This means that getting lost is not an inconvenience—it is a terminal condition, slowly realized.

The fundamental navigation challenge: **nothing changes.** The towers repeat in dense, overlapping patterns. The floor stretches identically in every direction. The light is sourceless and shadowless. There are no landmarks in the traditional sense—no peaks, no rivers, no unique formations. Only variations on a theme, and the variations are themselves part of a larger pattern you cannot read.

**What to bring:**

- **A compass.** Test it before you step through. If it works—if there is a consistent magnetic field or analogous directional reference—your navigation problem is significantly reduced. If it does not work, you are in deep trouble, and you must rely on the methods below.

- **Distance tracking.** A pedometer, a GPS system if the physics support it, or a method of counting your steps. Without external reference points, distance estimation becomes purely internal. You must know how far you have traveled and in what direction.

- **Trail marking supplies.** Chalk that can write on wet surfaces. Bright tape that can be tied to structures. Physical markers that you place as you walk, creating your own breadcrumb trail. The Manufactory does not remove markers—nothing decays here, nothing is cleaned away—but the creatures *might* interact with them, and the moisture might degrade anything not waterproof. Use multiple marking systems. Trust none of them completely.

- **A map you create yourself.** Graph paper. A pencil that writes when wet (test this—many pencils and pens fail in high humidity). The moment you arrive, begin mapping. Tower positions relative to each other. Distances between structures. The direction of the floor ridges (they may shift orientation in different sections—this could be a navigational aid if you learn to read them). The gradient of the floor—water flows somewhere, and water flow direction is information.

- **An anchor point.** The place where you arrive is your lifeline. Mark it. Photograph it. Describe it in writing. Note the position of every nearby tower, every seam in the floor, every detail that distinguishes this specific spot from the ten thousand spots that look almost identical. You must be able to find this place again. If you cannot find your entry point, you cannot leave.

### Navigation Protocol

Walk in straight lines. This sounds simple. It is not.

In a landscape without landmarks, without a sun, without any directional reference, humans walk in circles. This is documented. This is inevitable. You will not be able to correct for it by feel—you *must* have a directional reference, whether magnetic, gyroscopic, or based on the floor's curvature or ridge alignment.

Before you move, pick a distant tower and walk toward it. When you reach it, pick another. Do not navigate by "feel" or "sense of direction." Your senses are not calibrated for this place. Trust your instruments and your records.

## The Creatures: Coexistence Protocols

They are everywhere. They are part of the system. They are not here for you.

This is the most important thing to understand about the arthropod-like fauna of the Manufactory: **you are irrelevant to their function.** They do not view you as food. They do not view you as a threat. They do not view you at all, as far as you can tell. They move past you with the same deliberateness they move past everything else, and their attention—if attention is even the right word—is fixed on the seams where water emerges and the interiors of the structures they access.

**However:**

- **Do not interfere with them.** This is both ethical and practical. Ethical because they are living beings going about their existence in the only world they know. Practical because you do not understand their role in the Manufactory's systems. They are *components.* They may be performing maintenance, circulation, filtration, or processes you cannot imagine. Interfering with them could disrupt systems that have been in equilibrium longer than your species has existed. You do not know what the consequences would be. Neither does the Manufactory, probably—but the Manufactory does not care about consequences, only continuation.

- **Do not startle them.** They are not aggressive, but they are large, they are numerous, and they move with a physical assurance that suggests they are well-adapted to their environment. If you step on one—if you corner one—if you reach into a space where one is feeding—you may learn that even passive creatures have reflexes. Give them space. Walk around clusters. Wait for individuals to move rather than pushing past them.

- **Watch where you step.** The smaller ones—hand-sized—are underfoot constantly near the water seams. You will see them. You will almost step on them. Be mindful. Not because they are dangerous, but because you are a visitor in their home and because crushing one under your boot would be a small ugliness in a place that has been free of small uglinesses for a very long time.

- **Do not follow them into the structures.** They climb the towers and disappear into the openings. You will be tempted to follow. Resist this temptation, or at least mitigate it. The interior spaces are dim and complex. The creatures know them; you do not. If you enter a structure, do so with the same caution you would apply to entering a cave system—mark your path, note your turns, maintain your exit strategy. The structures may connect to other structures. They may descend. They may contain spaces where the floor ridges change orientation, where the water flows faster, where the warmth increases. You do not know what is inside the towers. The creatures do. Let them have it.

### Regarding the Remains

You will find them. Small dark husks, dried to the consistency of the floor material, barely distinguishable from it. The dead of the Manufactory's living components, returned to the system through processes you cannot observe.

Do not collect them. Do not disturb them. This is not superstition—this is practical respect for a closed system you do not understand. The remains are being processed. They are part of a cycle. Your interference, however small, is not part of that cycle. Leave them.

## Psychological Preparation: The Long Grey

This is the section that matters most, and the section most travelers will skip.

The Manufactory is not frightening. It is not shocking. It is not dramatic. It is *wearing.* It wears at you through repetition and sameness and the slow accumulation of hours that do not change. The warmth does not change. The moisture does not change. The light does not change. The quiet does not change. You walk for six hours and the landscape is the same as when you started. You walk for twelve and it is the same. You walk for three days and it is the same, and your brain begins to do things that brains are not meant to do.

**What to expect:**

- **Time distortion.** Without day/night cycles, your circadian rhythm will collapse within 36-48 hours. You will lose the ability to judge how long you have been walking, resting, or standing still. This is dangerous because it affects your resource management—you may eat through your food supply faster than you realize, or drink your water without accounting for the true duration of your stay. **You must track time mechanically.** A watch. A timer. Set alarms for rest periods and eating periods and sleeping periods. Do not trust your subjective experience of time. It will lie to you.

- **Sensory deprivation.** The quiet, the grey, the sameness—these are forms of sensory deprivation. Your brain expects variation: changes in light, changes in temperature, changes in sound. The Manufactory provides none of these. After 24-48 hours, you may begin to experience perceptual disturbances: visual hallucinations at the edges of your vision, auditory hallucinations in the quiet spaces, a growing sense that something is watching you or approaching you from behind the towers. **This is your brain trying to generate stimuli that the environment is not providing.** It is not real. Know that it will happen. Prepare for it. When you see movement that vanishes when you look directly at it, when you hear a sound that stops when you focus on it, remind yourself: this is the Manufactory's monotony, not its malice.

- **Claustrophobia and agoraphobia simultaneously.** You are in an enclosed space with no visible ceiling—that is agoraphobic. You are in an environment with no sky, no stars, no open air—that is claustrophobic. The two conditions compound each other. You may feel trapped despite the vastness. You may feel exposed despite the enclosure. This is normal. This is the Manufactory's geography acting on your instincts.

- **The existential weight.** The Manufactory does not care about you. This sounds abstract. It is not. After hours of walking through a place that operates at scales irrelevant to your existence—towers you cannot use, water you cannot safely drink, creatures that do not acknowledge you, a system that was running before your species evolved and will be running after—after all of this, the indifference becomes *palpable.* You will feel small. You will feel pointless. You will feel like a speck of grit in a machine that does not notice grit. **This is true.** You are. Accept it before you arrive, and it will be easier to bear when the feeling settles into your chest like the moisture settles on your skin.

**What to bring:**

- **Music.** Audio. Podcasts. Recordings of voices. Something—*anything*—that breaks the quiet on your terms. Earbuds that stay in during movement, with a player that tolerates humidity (test this). The sound of another human voice, even recorded, will anchor you in ways that nothing else can. Do not play audio constantly—save it for the moments when the quiet becomes oppressive, when you need to remember what variation sounds like.

- **A journal.** Write in it. Not just observations—write about how you feel. Write about what you miss. Write about the food you will eat when you leave. Write about the people you will see. The act of writing forces linear thought and subjective time—both of which the Manufactory will try to dissolve. Your journal is a lifeline to a world where time passes and things change and you matter.

- **Photographs of home.** Physical, if possible—laminated against the moisture. Something you can hold. Something that reminds you that the grey is not all there is.

- **A schedule.** Written out before you arrive. Times for walking, times for rest, times for eating, times for checking your navigation markers. Adhere to it even when it feels pointless—*especially* when it feels pointless. The schedule is a structure you impose on a place that has its own structures and does not care about yours. Your schedule is an act of resistance. Keep it.

- **A defined objective and exit condition.** Why are you here? How will you know when you are done? What is the signal that it is time to leave? Define this before you arrive. Write it down. The Manufactory will make you forget why you came. It will make you feel like you have always been here, walking the warm wet ridges between the towers, and that you will always be here. This is a lie. You have a purpose. You have a home. You will leave. Know your exit condition and honor it.

## What Not to Bring

- **Anything that rusts.** The moisture will find it.

- **Anything that depends on dry conditions.** Fire-starting equipment is useless—there is nothing to burn, and the humidity would prevent combustion even if there were. Chemical hand warmers will be overwhelmed by the ambient warmth. Leave them.

- **Heavy survival gear.** You are not fighting the elements here. You are enduring them. Weight matters—you will walk far, on a surface that fatigues your feet, in conditions that exhaust your body. Every unnecessary ounce is a tax.

- **Expectations of drama.** The Manufactory is not dramatic. It is a floor and some towers and some water and some creatures and a lot of grey. If you come expecting revelation or terror or wonder, you will be disappointed—or worse, you will manufacture those things yourself, and the Manufactory will not correct you.

---

## Final Notes

Walk softly. Touch little. Take nothing. Leave nothing that disrupts.

The Manufactory has been running since before you arrived. It will continue running after you leave. Your presence is a brief perturbation in a system that absorbs perturbations the way the floor absorbs water—silently, continuously, without apparent effect.

This is not a place that will change you. It is a place that will reveal what you are when nothing changes around you. Some travelers find peace in that. Some find terror. Most find a kind of exhausted indifference that mirrors the Manufactory itself, and they leave feeling that they have touched something vast and tired and persistent, and that it did not notice them, and that this is both the saddest and the most honest thing they have ever experienced.

Bring dry socks.

Find your way back.
