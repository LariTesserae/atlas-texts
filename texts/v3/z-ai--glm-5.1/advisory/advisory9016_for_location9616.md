# Preparing for the Risen Basin

## What You Must Understand Before You Arr

You are prepare for the Risen Basin by preparing for slowness. Not the slowness of inconvenience or the slowness of deprivation—those you have likely encountered in other worlds, and those you already know how to endure. The slowness of the Basin is geological. It is the slowness of something that does not know it is slow, that has no concept of pace at all, that is simply proceeding at the rate of its own becoming. If you cannot make peace with this, the basin will reject you. Not violently. It will simply make you miserable until you leave, and you will leave thinking the place was empty or lonely or sad, and you will have been wrong about all of it.

## The Body's Preparation

### Altitude

The basin sits high. You will feel this before you see the water. The approach—however you make it—will take you upward into air that gives less of itself with each breath. If you are coming from a sea-level world or a world with thicker atmosphere, spend time at elevation beforehand if you can. Days, if possible. Your blood needs time to thicken itself into usefulness.

At the basin's rim, you will be conscious of every breath. Not gasping—this is not a place that starves you of air—but aware. Each inhalation becomes a small event. This awareness does not entirely fade. The people who live there breathe as deeply as they always have; their bodies have adapted over generations. Yours has not. You will tire more quickly. You will need to rest more often. Do not treat this as weakness; treat it as the mountain asking you to slow down, and listen.

### The Water and Your Skin

The water is warm and mineral and faintly sulphurous. This means several things for your body.

First: you will be wet more often than you are dry. The city's upper layers exist at the waterline and above it, but "above it" is a relative term. The platforms shift. The walkways are damp from splash and condensation. The air itself carries moisture, warm and close, rising from the basin's surface. Everything that can absorb water will absorb water. Your skin will prune and stay pruned. Your hair will never fully dry.

Second: the sulphur content will affect you. Not dangerously—this is not a toxic environment—but noticeably. After a day or two, you will smell of it. Not rotten-egg strong, but present, a warmth in your scent that was not there before. Your skin may develop a slight slickness, a trace mineral film that makes you feel as though you've been lightly oiled. Some visitors find this unpleasant at first and then find they miss it when they leave.

Third: the warmth is uncanny at altitude. Your body expects cold at height. The air is thin and cool, the wind moves through the reed-towers with mountain sharpness, but the water against your skin is fever-warm. This dissonance does not resolve. You do not get used to it. Every time you reach into the water—a hundred times a day, because the water is everywhere and everything requires reaching into it—your body registers surprise. The people do not seem to notice this anymore. You will.

Prepare by bringing as little synthetic clothing as possible. fabrics that do not mind being perpetually damp, that dry quickly or do not need to dry, that will not harbor bacteria in warm wet conditions. Linen works. Certain woven grasses, if your world has them. Avoid anything that will rot or stiffen. The people of the basin wear woven fiber from the reed-towers and the algal strands; if you have access to similar plant-based textiles, bring them. If you must bring modern materials, choose those that do not degrade in mineral-rich warm water.

Bring no metal. This is not a prohibition—no one will stop you—but metal is wrong here. It corrodes quickly in the sulphurous warmth. It is alien to a place that has never known it. And there is something about the way it catches the light, hard and sharp, that feels like a small violence against the blue-green patience of everything else. Leave your metal behind. You will not need it.

### Diving

You will want to dive. Everyone does. The submerged city pulls at the imagination with a gravity that has nothing to do with physics.

But understand: the people dive with practiced breath and stone weights. They have done this since childhood. Their lungs know the depth. Their bodies know the pressure. The silence below is total and pressurized—a weight against the ears, as the description says—and if you are not prepared for this, it can panic you. The silence is not merely absence of sound. It is presence of pressure. It is the water pressing in on you from all sides with the weight of the mountain's own emerging, and if you fight it, you will exhaust your air faster, and the ascent is not quick.

If you dive:

- Do so with someone from the basin. They will not offer; you will need to ask. Ask quietly. They will say yes, because they understand the pull and they do not judge it.
- Take a stone weight, as they do, to speed your descent. Without it, you will thrash and waste air.
- Equalize your ears early and often. The pressure changes faster than you expect.
- Do not go deep on your first descent. The light dims. the blue-green thickens. Stay where you can still see the surface clearly.
- Do not touch the algal fiber unless you know what you are touching. Some of it is delicate, centuries-old, holding structures together. Some of it is young and resilient. You will not know the difference.
- When the silence closes around you, do not fight it. Let it hold you the way it holds everything else. Your heartbeat will seem to belong to the water; let it. This is the closest you will come to understanding what the basin is.

### The Diet

The food is simple and sufficient. a staple root-vegetable that the reed-towers' root-systems support, a grain-like seed from the surface plants, and the young algal fiber prepared as food. There are no herds. There are no domesticated animals. The rare fish is a delicacy, not a staple, and you will not be offered it unless you have been welcomed deeply.

If you have dietary restrictions that require animal protein or specific nutrients not present in this diet, bring supplements. Do not bring food that requires preparation beyond what the basin's methods allow—they have no metal, no fire-hot forges, no means of cooking beyond what can be done with stone and heated rock and the warm water itself. The root-vegetable is eaten cooked, slowly, in the mineral water, and it absorbs the sulphur taste and becomes something you will either crave for the rest of your life or never wish to taste again.

Bring salt if your body requires more than the mineral water provides. The basin's water has some, but not enough for some travelers' needs.

### Sleep

You will sleep on a floating platform or in a half-dry upper room, and the water will move beneath you. The platform will shift, gently, constantly. The reed-towers will rustle in the wind. Somewhere, the mountain will creak—a low, intermittent sound like a ship at anchor, like a body settling into bed.

If you are someone who requires stillness for sleep, this will be difficult. The basin does not do stillness. It does slow, continuous movement. Even in the deepest night, the water is rising, infinitesimally, the the algal fiber is growing into new rooms, and the reed-towers are breathing in the wind.

Let the movement rock you rather than disturb you. Your are sleeping on something that is itself alive, or at least held by living things. This can be a comfort if you allow it.

## The Mind's Preparation

### On Silence and Noise

Above the waterline, the city is quiet. Not silent—there is wind and creaking and the soft sounds of human activity—but quiet. Conversations happen in low voices. Not because of prohibition but because the place asks for it. The water seems to demand smallness of sound, as though it might be disturbed by too much noise, and the people have complied for so long that it has become instinct.

If you areare someone who fills silence with speech—many travelers are, because silence in unfamiliar places can feel like threat—you will need to learn not to. Your voice,, at normal volume, will feel like a shout. You will feel shushed, not by people but by the place itself. The re reed-towers will not absorb your words; they will carry them, and the sound will travel across the water's surface in ways that feel wrong, too present, too exposed.

Learn to speak quietly. Not whispering—that is its own kind performance, a making-dramatic of quiet. Simply: quietly. The way you might might speak to someone who is almost asleep. or to yourself when you are very tired and very honest.

Below On the Psychological Weight of Rising Water

This is the preparation that no physical supply can address.

The water of the basin Risen Basin is rising. It has always been rising. It will continue to rise. The city the the people are adapted to this, but you are are not, and watching a place slowly drown—even slowly, even over generations—even with the full cooperation and acceptance of the people being drowned—will do something to you.

You will stand on a floating platform and look down through the blue blue-green clarity at a room where someone once slept someone once ate meals, and it will be filled with water and algal fiber and the mineral light, and you will think: this is loss. This is grief. This is a place being taken by something it cannot stop again.

But you will be wrong.

The people do not experience the rise as loss. They experience it as... process. Transformation. The rooms that are not being taken; they are being given. Given to the water, given to the algae, given to the slow patient patience of the basin's becoming. The people built those rooms knowing they would fill. They built them with that knowledge woven into the act of construction itself, the way the algal fiber weaves through the cracks between stones. The giving-underwater was part of the making-above-water, always, from the start.

This does not mean the people feel nothing when a room takes its first inch of water through the doorway. They feel something. But it is not grief. It is closer to... recognition. A confirmation. The way you might feel when a child reaches an age you always knew they would reach. You have been preparing for it since before they were born. And now it has arrived. And you nod, and you continue.

If you cannot make this shift in your own understanding—if you persist in seeing the submerged city as ruin, as evidence of disaster, as a thing to be mourned—then the basin will feel oppressive to you. You will experience it as a place under slow siege, and you will be unable to see why the people do not flee, and you will misread their quiet acceptance as resignation or defeat or worse.

They are not defeated. They are participating. In something older and larger and slower than defeat or victory. The mountain is becoming. The water is emerging. The city is the conversation between the two. It is not losing that conversation. It is having it.

### On the Absence of Animal Life

The basin is strangely empty of creatures. This will unsettle you more than you expect. You are accustomed—even if you do not realize it—to the presence of animal life as a kind of confirmation that a place is alive. The movement of birds, the rustling of small mammals in undergrowth, the darting of fish in water: these tell some part of your brain that the world is functioning, that the biological systems are operating as they should.

The basin has plants in abundance. It has warmth and mineral richness and light filtered to blue-green patience. But it does not, not yet, have animal life in any significant quantity. A pale crustacean on a a submerged wall. Something small and translucent darting between re reed-stalks. These are exceptions. The general impression is of a place held in suspension between botanical botanical completion and zoological arrival, and your brain will read this suspension as wrongness.

It is not wrong. It is early. The basin is patient. The plants came first, as plants do. The animals will come later, in another century, another millennium. You are visiting a place that is not yet finished becoming itself, and the absence of animal life is not failure—it is simply sequence.

But you will feel the absence. You will look for movement in the water and not find it. You will listen for birdsong and hear only wind through reed-towers. You will feel, at certain moments, that the place is waiting for something, and you will be right, and the waiting is not anxious or urgent—it is simply the patience of a basin that has been emerging from rock for longer than you can comprehend and will continue emerging long after you leave.

### On Night

Prepare for the night. Not because it is dangerous—it is not—but because it will will change you in ways you may not be prepared for.

The surface of the water glows. Faintlyly, cold blue-green, bioluminescence from rising from the algal fiber in the submerged city below. The light makes the surface shimmer. The people move across their floating platforms and walkways in this uplight, and and their shadows fall upward.

Their shadows fall upward.

You are not prepared for this. No one is. You have lived your entire life in worlds where shadows fall downward, where light comes from above, where the ground beneath you is the stable datum of visual reality. At the Risen Basin, at night, the ground is water that glows from below, and the sky is stars that glow from above, and the city floats between two skies, and the shadows of the people fall upward against the reed-towers and the underside of roofs, and your visual system will not know how to process this.

Give it time. You will adjust. But the first night will be disorienting in a way that is not unpleasant but is deeply strange, and you may find feel as though you are standing on the ceiling of the world, looking down at a sky that is below above and below at once.

Stay on the platform. Watch the light. Let the bioluminescence do what it does. It is the city lighting itself from its own drowned foundations, which is exactly what it is, and there is a rightness to it that your disorientation will eventually make room for.

## What to Bring

- **Clothing**: Woven plant fibers if you have them. Linen if you do not. Nothing that cannot stay damp without degrading. Layers for the wind and the altitude cold, but nothing heavy—you will be in and out of water constantly.
- **Footwear**: Something that grips wet wood and slick stone. Something you do not mind submerging. The people go barefoot on their own platforms; you may do the same once you have callused sufficiently, but at first, you will want protection from the mineral roughness of basalt and the bite of woven reed.
- **No metal**: As said. It corrodes. It is wrong here. Leave it behind.
- **Salt**: If your body requires more than the mineral water provides.
- **Dietary supplements**: If you cannot subsist on root-vegetable and grain-seed and algal fiber alone.
- **A journal or recording method**: Not because you will want to remember—the basin is memorable—but because the silence and the slowness will create space in your mind that you have not had before, and thoughts will come to you that you will want to catch. Write them down. Use something waterproof.
- **A stone weight**: For diving. You can find one at the basin's rim, but bringing your own ensures it is the right size for your body.
- **Patience**: This is not a flippancy. Bring it deliberately, the way you would bring medicine. You will need more of it than you expect. The basin does not accelerate for anyone. The water does not rise faster to show you its work. The algae do not grow on your schedule. The people do not explain themselves, because they do not experience themselves as requiring explanation. If you want to understand this place, you must be willing to move at the speed of geological emergence, which is the slowest speed there is, and you must be willing to accept that your understanding, when it comes, will not feel like understanding. It will feel like acceptance. It will feel like the water rising to meet the next layer of the city: inevitable, unhurried, and not sad.

## What Not to Bring

- **Expectations of rescue or emergency infrastructure**: There is none. There are no metal tools, no communication with outside worlds, no medical facilities beyond what the people can provide with stone and fiber and the warm mineral water (which is, incidentally, genuinely therapeutic for many ailments—its warmth and mineral content are not merely atmospheric). If you are injured seriously, you will need to leave the basin, and leaving the basin means climbing its rim, which is at altitude, which will be difficult while injured. Take no stupid risks.
- **Contempt for simplicity**: The people work with their hands. They have always worked with their hands. They cut basalt with harder stone. They harvest fiber from the water. They build, they submerge, they build again. If you look at this and see primitivism or deprivation or a lack that should be filled, you are seeing wrong. You are measuring a conversation against a monologue. They are not lacking. They are participating in a way that your world has likely forgotten.
- **Urgency**: It will not be accommodated. It will not be punished, either—it will simply be ignored, the way the water ignores the shape of a container it is slowly filling. The water does not care about your urgency. The mountain does not care. The people have learned not to care, because caring about urgency in a place that operates on geological time is a quick route to madness. If you bring urgency, you will be the only one feeling it, and you will feel it alone, and it will exhaust you, and eventually you will let it go or you will leave.

## A Final Note on What the Basin Asks of You

The Risen Basin will ask you—without words, without anyone speaking the request—to accept that a place can be becoming without being incomplete.

This is harder than it sounds. Your mind wants to categorize the basin as a place in process, a place on its way to becoming something else, a place that will be finished when the water stops rising or the mountain stops emerging or the animals finally arrive. But the basin is not incomplete. It is not waiting to be finished. It is finished, right now, at this water level, at this stage of emergence, with exactly this much city submerged and exactly this much city above water and exactly this much algal fiber growing through exactly these cracks between exactly these stones. And it will be finished tomorrow, when the water is a fraction of a millimeter higher. And it was finished a century ago, when the water was a hand's breadth lower. The basin is always complete. It is always becoming. These are not contradictions.

If you can hold this—complete and becoming, finished and still emerging, a city that is always whole at every moment of its slow submersion—then the Risen Basin will give you something rare: the experience of time as a medium rather than a threat. Time, in the basin, is not something that runs out. It is something that things grow in. The water grows in it. The algae grow in it. The city grows in it. You can grow in it too, if you let yourself.

Stand on a floating platform at night. Watch the uplight from the water. Let your shadow fall upward. Listen to the reed-towers breathe in the wind. Feel the platform shift beneath you, gently, constantly, alive with the movement of water that is rising from rock that is emerging from geological processes that have been ongoing since before your species existed and will continue long after it is gone.

And then: breathe. Thin mountain air, sulphur-warm, patient.

The basin will not change for you. But you may change for it. And that is not a small thing.
