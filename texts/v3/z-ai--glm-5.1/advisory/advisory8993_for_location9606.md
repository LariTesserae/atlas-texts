# Preparation Guide: The Foundry Floor

## First Understanding

You are going to three kilometers beneath an ocean that may not be natural, into a structure that has no known builder, to witness processes that have no known purpose. Before you pack anything, before you research equipment or plan routes, you need to understand what this place will do to you simply by *being there*.

The Foundry Floor is not hostile. That is a critical distinction. A volcano is hostile—it wants to kill you with heat and gas and projectile rock. A battlefield is hostile—intelligent agents will direct harm toward you. The Foundry Floor is *indifferent* on a scale that is difficult to prepare for. The machinery does not care about you. The ecosystem has no role for you. The structure was not built with your presence in mind, and it will not accommodate you, and it will not go out of its way to destroy you either. You will be a particle of debris in an industrial current, and the systems here will treat you with exactly the same consideration they treat the crabs and the sediment and the waste discharge: none at all.

This indifference is more dangerous than malice. You cannot negotiate with it. You cannot anticipate its strategies. You can only survive by never assuming that the environment will make any allowance for your fragility.

## The Pressure Problem

Three kilometers of water. Let me make this concrete rather than abstract, because abstract numbers will not prepare you.

At the surface, you experience one atmosphere of pressure—about 14.7 pounds per square inch. At three kilometers depth, you experience approximately 301 atmospheres. That is roughly 4,425 pounds per square inch. On your body. On every square inch of your skin, your skull, your chest cavity, your eyeballs.

Your ordinary multiverse travel gear almost certainly will not handle this. Standard atmospheric adaptors and pressure charms work on the assumption that pressure differentials will be moderate—a few atmospheres at most, with gradual changes. The Foundry Floor will crush those solutions like tissue paper. You need equipment rated for deep ocean deployment—not recreational diving depths, not even military submarine depths, but *hadal zone* depths. Equipment designed for places where the ocean is so deep it intersects with the mantle.

You need a hard-shell atmospheric suit. Not a wetsuit, not a semi-rigid diving suit, not a flexible pressure-compensating rig. You need a human-shaped submarine. The kind used for extreme deep-sea operations: articulated metal shells with internal pressure maintained at surface-normal, with viewports rated for extreme external pressure, with life support systems that do not rely on gas mixtures dissolved into your blood. If your suit fails at this depth, you will not drown. You will be compressed. The distinction matters to your planning because it tells you what you're protecting against: not water intrusion, but structural collapse.

Test your suit before departure. Test it at pressure. Test it beyond rated pressure. The Foundry Floor's pressure is not static—it fluctuates with the cycling of the pumping systems, and those fluctuations can spike suddenly and severely. Your equipment needs margin, not just adequacy.

And understand that in a hard-shell suit, you will be slow. Your movements will be clumsy. Your fine motor control will be severely limited. You will not be able to run, to climb quickly, to manipulate small objects without assistance. Plan for this limitation in every contingency.

## The Sound

I am putting this before temperature and chemistry and navigation because I believe it is the aspect of the Foundry Floor most likely to cause a traveler to fail, and the one most consistently underestimated.

The sound is not a nuisance. It is a hostile environmental factor that will actively degrade your cognitive function.

The dominant mechanical roar resonates in your chest cavity and your long bones. This is not poetic description—it is physical reality. The low-frequency vibrations will pass through your suit's shell regardless of insulation, because low-frequency sound travels through solid structures with minimal attenuation. You will *feel* the machinery more than you hear it, and that feeling will not stop. It will be present when you arrive. It will be present when you sleep—if you sleep. It will be present in your bones when you leave, for days or weeks afterward, as a phantom vibration that your body will not immediately unlearn.

Extended exposure to this kind of low-frequency vibration causes:
- Nausea and equilibrium disruption
- Difficulty concentrating and impaired decision-making
- Sleep disturbance and eventual sleep deprivation
- Anxiety escalation and mood destabilization
- In prolonged cases, vibroacoustic disease—actual tissue damage from chronic vibration exposure

You cannot eliminate this risk. You can only manage it. Bring active noise cancellation rated for low-frequency industrial environments—the kind used by workers near heavy machinery, near turbines, near rocket test stands. Wear it constantly. Bring multiple systems, because equipment failure in this context means you lose your primary defense against cognitive degradation.

Plan your expedition in short durations. Do not plan to remain on the Foundry Floor for more than 48-72 hours regardless of your supply situation. The sound will wear you down faster than cold, faster than hunger, faster than any physical hazard. Know your limits and respect them.

And be aware that the sound masks other hazards. The approach of predators. The cycling of pressure systems. The movement of autonomous maintenance platforms. You will be operating with significantly degraded situational awareness simply because the acoustic environment is overwhelming. Compensate with other senses and with deliberate, systematic observation protocols.

## Temperature and Thermal Management

The baseline water temperature is 4°C. This is the temperature at which water reaches maximum density, the stable cold of the deep ocean. In your hard-shell suit, you will be insulated from this cold, but you need to understand the thermal landscape because it is not uniform.

The active installations discharge water at significantly elevated temperatures—potentially 10-12°C or higher in the immediate outflow zones. These thermal plumes create currents and convection cells that can drag you unexpectedly. They also create the illusion of warmth—you may drift toward an outflow seeking relief from the ambient cold, only to find yourself caught in a discharge current carrying chemical contaminants at pressures that can slam you against infrastructure.

More dangerous: the reaction chambers themselves glow with internal heat. The pipes carry fluids at pressures and temperatures that are catastrophic. A ruptured pipe in this environment would not simply leak—it would discharge a jet of superheated, pressurized fluid capable of cutting through metal, and you would not hear it coming over the ambient noise, and by the time your temperature sensors registered the anomaly, you might already be in the kill zone.

Your suit's thermal management system needs to handle both extremes: the ambient cold that will slowly leach heat from your shell, and the localized hot zones that can spike suddenly and severely. Monitor your external temperature readouts constantly. Set alerts for rapid changes. Stay away from outflow zones unless your mission specifically requires approach, and even then, approach from downstream, never from the direction of the discharge.

## The Water Itself

The water tastes of iron and copper and something faintly alkaline. This is not incidental. This is the chemical signature of an industrial process operating at continental scale, and it means the water is contaminated.

Your hard-shell suit should protect you from direct contact, but consider the failure modes. A seal breach at this depth means not just pressure intrusion but chemical exposure. The alkaline component—likely from industrial cleaning agents, chemical processing byproducts, or pH-buffering compounds used in the manufacturing systems—could cause chemical burns to skin, eyes, and mucous membranes even before the pressure kills you.

More relevant for intact suits: the particulate matter in the water is a chronic hazard for any external systems. Intake valves, sensor housings, joint mechanisms, viewport surfaces—all will accumulate biological debris, chemical residues, and sediment. The bacterial mats that form on the floor plates will also form on your suit. The pale stalks near the warm outflows may attempt to colonize any surface that provides appropriate chemical conditions.

Bring cleaning systems. Bring spare viewport coverings that can be removed and replaced when visibility degrades. Bring tools for clearing joints and mechanisms. Plan for your equipment to degrade over time as the environment deposits itself on every surface.

## Navigation in a Featureless Vastness

The scale of the Foundry Floor defeats ordinary navigation. Distances between vertical supports are measured in kilometers. Distances between active installations are journeys that may take hours of steady travel in your slow, clumsy suit.

The floor itself provides almost no landmarks. The same rivet pattern. The same plate dimensions. The same rust-bloom at the seams. Under the perpetual fluorescent dusk, with visibility limited by the green murk and the suspended particulates, you will experience a disorientation that is difficult to overstate. Every direction looks identical. Every floor panel could be the floor panel you passed an hour ago or the floor panel you will pass an hour from now. The vertical supports, when you can see them, converge with distance like cathedral pillars—and they are so far apart that you may travel for hours between sighting one and sighting the next.

You cannot rely on visual navigation alone. You need:

- **Dead reckoning systems**: Inertial navigation that tracks your movement from a known starting point. These systems drift over time, but they will keep you from walking in circles.
- **Acoustic positioning**: If you can place beacons on the vertical supports—which are stable, permanent, and spaced at known intervals—you can triangulate your position. Be aware that the ambient noise may limit the effective range of any acoustic system.
- **Magnetic compasses**: Probably unreliable. The structure contains vast amounts of ferrous metal, and the industrial processes may generate electromagnetic fields that interfere with magnetic navigation. Test before relying on.
- **Physical trail-marking**: Bring physical markers that you can attach to the floor plates or the vertical supports. Bright colors, distinct shapes, chemical light sticks—anything that provides a visual reference in a landscape stripped of distinguishing features. Be aware that the crabs may investigate or attempt to feed on your markers, and the autonomous platforms may remove them as debris.

The pressure fluctuations that serve as the Foundry Floor's weather can help with rough orientation: the active installations generate distinct current patterns, and the direction of water flow can tell you which way the nearest outflow lies. But this is coarse information, useful for not getting lost rather than for precise navigation.

Before you descend, establish a rendezvous point with your exit. Mark it. Know its position relative to the nearest vertical support. Commit it to memory and to written backup. In a place this vast and this featureless, losing your exit point is a terminal error.

## The Ecosystem: What Lives Here

The creatures of the Foundry Floor are not monstrous. They are not aberrations or horrors. They are deep-sea organisms adapted to a deep-sea environment that happens to be industrial rather than geological. Understanding them is important not because they are terrifying, but because they are predictable, and predictable things can be planned for.

**The White Crabs**

Dog-sized. Synchronized movement driven by chemical signals. They click their pincers in patterns. They feed on bacterial mats and organic detritus. They congregate at waste discharge points when the autonomous platforms deposit material.

The crabs are not dangerous to a human in a hard-shell suit. They cannot crack your plating, and they have no reason to try—they do not recognize you as food. However, large numbers of crabs moving across the floor can obstruct your path, and their clicking—amplified by the metal floor plates—can add to the acoustic overload. More importantly, the crabs' behavior signals environmental changes. When they click more intensely and move erratically, a pressure fluctuation is imminent. When they converge on a location, something has been deposited there recently—waste, organic material, or potentially something of interest to you.

Watch the crabs. They are the canaries in this coal mine, except they are canaries that thrive here, and their behavior tells you what the mine is doing.

**The Pale Fish**

Small, nearly transparent, with reduced eyes. They school around warm outflows. They are prey.

The fish are irrelevant to you directly, but their behavior signals the location of thermal plumes and the activity state of nearby installations. If the fish are schooling tightly near the floor, pressure is dropping. If they have dispersed upward, pressure is rising. If they have vanished entirely, something has frightened them—a predator, a major discharge event, a system malfunction. Pay attention when the fish disappear.

**The Eel-Predators**

These are the most biologically dangerous organisms on the Foundry Floor. Eel-like, with lateral lines so sensitive they detect electrical fields. They navigate using the machinery's hum. They hunt during pressure transitions, when prey is disoriented.

A human in a hard-shell suit presents an unusual electrical signature. Your life support systems generate heat and electromagnetic fields. Your communications equipment, your navigation systems, your active noise cancellation—all produce electrical signatures that the eel-predators can detect.

Will they attack you? Probably not. You are not their prey, you are the wrong size and shape, and you do not behave like the schooling fish they hunt. But "probably not" is not "definitely not," and a large predator investigating an unfamiliar signal at three kilometers depth, in an environment where you cannot move quickly or defend yourself effectively, is a situation that could escalate unpredictably.

If an eel-predator approaches, minimize your electrical signature. Power down non-essential systems. Do not make sudden movements. Let the predator investigate and determine that you are not food. Do not attempt to drive it away—its lateral line will detect the movement, and its hunting instincts may interpret your aggression as the erratic behavior of distressed prey.

**The Pale Stalks**

Not plants. Not animals. Something that attaches to metal and draws nutrients from the alloy. They cluster near warm outflows. They provide habitat for bacteria and shelter for fish.

They are not dangerous. But be aware that if your suit rests against a surface where the stalks grow, they may attempt to attach to it. The chemical processes that allow them to extract nutrients from metal alloys might, over time, affect your suit's structural integrity. Do not linger in the stalk beds.

## The Autonomous Platforms

The maintenance systems are not your enemies. They are not your friends. They are machines executing programs, and you are not in those programs.

The platforms move on rail systems. Their sensor arrays sweep the environment. Their manipulator arms perform tasks. They do not acknowledge biological life. The crabs have learned to scuttle out of their path; you must learn the same lesson.

The platforms have right-of-way. They are massive, they are tireless, and they do not stop. If you are on their rail when they pass, they will not detect you, they will not divert, and they will push you aside with whatever force is necessary to continue their route. In a hard-shell suit, you might survive this. Your suit might not.

Learn the rail patterns. Watch for the platforms' approach—they move at constant speed on predictable routes, and their sensor sweeps have a visible signature. Get off the rails when a platform is coming. Do not attempt to ride them, to interact with them, to interfere with their operations. They are part of the machinery, and the machinery is continental in scale, and you are not.

The waste discharge events are notable. When a platform stops at an access point and deposits material, the crabs converge within minutes. This is an opportunity for observation—the platforms may reveal something about what the foundry is producing, what it is discarding, what its processes require. Approach these deposits with caution. The waste material includes metal shavings (sharp, potentially hot), chemical residue (hazardous composition unknown), and biological contamination from filters (unknown biohazard risk). Do not touch it with your bare hands even if your suit has manipulator access. Collect samples with sealed tools if collection is necessary.

## What to Bring

**Essential Equipment:**
- Hard-shell atmospheric suit rated for 300+ atmospheres with tested margin beyond rated capacity
- Internal life support for minimum 96 hours (plan for 48-hour expedition, carry emergency margin)
- Active noise cancellation systems (primary and backup) rated for low-frequency industrial vibration
- Inertial navigation system with drift correction capability
- Acoustic beacon system for marking and triangulating positions
- Physical trail markers (chemical light sticks, magnetic markers with high-adhesion pads, bright flagging)
- External temperature sensors with real-time monitoring and rapid-change alerts
- Sealed sample collection tools
- Cleaning systems for viewport and joint maintenance
- Powerful directional lights (the ambient fluorescent dusk is not sufficient for detailed observation; your visibility will be worse than it appears in the description)
- Communication system (likely acoustic; radio will not function through water at this depth without infrastructure)

**Critical Supplies:**
- Emergency rations and water for twice your planned expedition duration
- Medical kit including treatments for chemical burns, pressure-related injury, and vibroacoustic symptoms
- Spare seals and patch materials for suit integrity breaches (limited effectiveness at this pressure, but better than nothing)
- Multiple redundant air supplies
- Signal flares or markers for emergency extraction signaling

**Optional but Recommended:**
- Recording equipment—the Foundry Floor is poorly documented, and detailed observations have value
- Biological sampling containers (sealed, pressure-rated)
- Water sampling containers (sealed, chemical-stable)
- Electromagnetic field sensors (for mapping the industrial signatures that the eel-predators navigate by)
- Sediment collection tools
- A tether system if operating near a fixed point—there is no current strong enough to sweep you away in the open floor zones, but near active installations, the thermal and pressure currents can be severe

## Psychological Preparation

I saved this for last because it is the preparation that matters most and the one you are most likely to dismiss.

You are going to a place that is continental in scale and almost empty. You will stand on a manufactured floor that extends beyond sight in every direction, beneath a manufactured ceiling that glows with a light that never changes, surrounded by water that has never known sunlight, and you will be very, very alone.

The vastness will affect you. The sameness will affect you. The noise that never stops will erode your ability to think clearly. The green murk will close in on your visibility until the world shrinks to the reach of your lights. The knowledge that there are kilometers of ocean above you and kilometers of structure below you will press on your mind like the water presses on your suit.

Some travelers will find this exhilarating. Some will find it terrifying. Most will find it both, alternating unpredictably, and that alternation is itself destabilizing.

Know your psychological limits before you descend. Have a plan for what to do if the environment overwhelms you—not an abstract plan, but a concrete set of actions: return to your exit point, ascend to a different environment, use your communication system to contact support. Practice executing this plan under simulated stress before you arrive.

The Foundry Floor does not care about you. It will not comfort you, and it will not warn you, and it will not remember you when you leave. Everything that happens to you there will be the result of your own preparation, your own attention, and your own willingness to turn back when your limits are reached.

Go if you choose to. Go prepared. Go knowing that you are entering a place that was not made for you, that does not need you, and that will continue its vast, loud, ancient-yet-young operations long after you have gone. The Foundry Floor is worth seeing. It is worth understanding. It is not worth dying for.

Come back.
