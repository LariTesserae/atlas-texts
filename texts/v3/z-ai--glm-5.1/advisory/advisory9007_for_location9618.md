# Preparation Guide: The Howling Plateau

## Before You Step Through

### Accept What You Cannot Prepare For

The first thing to understand is that no preparation will make the sound tolerable. You can mitigate it. You can protect yourself from it. You cannot make it something your mind accepts as background. The howling of the Plateau is not like wind on any other world you have visited. It is not weather. It is a geological fact as constant as gravity, and your brain—trained by a lifetime of weather that comes and goes—will keep waiting for it to stop. It will not stop. You will lie in your shelter at night and wait for the pause that does not come, and the waiting will wear you down more than the sound itself.

Prepare mentally for this. Speak with travelers who have been to places of perpetual stimulus. Understand that you will go through phases: first shock, then resistance, then exhaustion, then a strange kind of surrender that is not the same as peace. The scattered people who live here have not adapted to the sound in the sense of no longer noticing it. They have adapted in the sense of no longer expecting it to change. There is a difference, and you must find it quickly or the Plateau will break something in you that takes a long time to mend.

The surrender I am describing is not defeat. It is the recognition that you have entered a place that was ancient before your species existed, that will be ancient after, and that the sound is the voice of that antiquity made manifest. You do not overcome the Howling Plateau. You survive it. You pass through. And if you are very fortunate and very attentive, you learn something about what endurance truly means.

### Hearing Protection—Your Single Most Critical Investment

Do not arrive without multiple layers of hearing protection. Do not rely on a single method. The sound of the Plateau attacks from multiple vectors—air conduction through your ear canals, bone conduction through your skull, and a strange resonant quality that seems to bypass both and vibrate directly in your chest cavity and sinuses. You need defense against all three.

**Primary ear protection:** Custom-molded industrial earplugs rated for continuous exposure above 100 decibels. Not foam disposables—these will loosen with the constant wind pressure and the sweat of travel, and a momentary gap in protection on the Plateau can leave your ears ringing for days. Get the kind that seal against the concha of your ear with a slight negative pressure. Test them before you arrive. Test them in high-wind conditions if possible. Ensure you can insert them quickly and reliably with numb fingers.

**Secondary ear protection:** Over-ear defenders, the kind used by flight deck crews on aircraft carriers. These serve double duty: they provide a second layer of acoustic isolation, and they protect the outer ear from wind chill and the abrasive effect of constant particulate-laden air. The wind carries fine dust—microscopic flakes of the-body-that-remained, polished by eons of erosion—and this dust will abrade any exposed skin given enough time. Your ears are particularly vulnerable.

**Tertiary consideration—bone conduction:** This is the hardest to address. A tight-fitting hood or head covering that presses against the mastoid bone behind each ear can dampen some conduction, but it will not eliminate it. Some travelers report that a soft cervical collar, the kind used for neck injuries, helps by damping vibrations in the cervical spine that otherwise transmit to the skull. I cannot confirm this from personal experience, but the logic is sound. Pack one. It adds negligible weight.

**The warning about over-protection:** You must be able to remove some layer of protection in safe moments—inside the hummock-shelters, pressed against the green—because total auditory isolation on the Plateau is its own hazard. You need to hear the-sound-that-hunts when it runs. You need to hear the shift in wind tone that signals a direction change. You need to hear the people when they speak to you, however rarely. Design your hearing protection in layers so that you can modulate your exposure rather than toggling between all and nothing.

And know this: even with all of these measures, you will feel the sound. In your teeth. In your sternum. In the fillings of your teeth if you have them. In the fluid of your eyes, slightly, so that the world seems to shimmer at certain frequencies. This is not damage. This is the Plateau. Accept it.

## Clothing and Skin Protection

### The Wind Is an Abrasive Force

The air on the Plateau is not merely moving—it is carrying. Billions of years of erosion have produced a fine particulate that the wind suspends and drives across the stone at velocities that can strip paint from metal. Your skin is softer than paint. You will need to cover every square centimeter of exposed skin at all times when outside the hummock-shelters.

**Base layer:** A moisture-wicking synthetic or merino wool layer that fits close to the skin. You will sweat on the Plateau—the stone is warm, the sun is intense, the wind is constant—and sweat that sits against the skin will chafe raw within hours under the friction of outer layers. The base layer must manage moisture actively.

**Insulating layer:** The Plateau is warm during the day, often shockingly so, but the temperature drops when the sun does. The stone holds its heat but the air does not. You may encounter a thirty-degree differential between midday sun and midnight air. A lightweight, compressible insulating layer that you can carry in a pack and deploy when the sun drops is essential. Do not assume the warmth of the stone will be enough. The wind strips heat from your body faster than the stone can return it.

**Wind shell:** This is your armor. It must be windproof—completely, absolutely windproof—and it must be abrasion-resistant. The best material I have seen used on the Plateau is a tightly woven ballistic nylon with a DWR coating. It will degrade over time under the particulate assault, but it will last long enough for a typical stay. Reinforce the forearms, the front of the thighs, and the shoulders—these face the wind most directly. The shell should have a fitted hood that can be drawn tight around the face, leaving only a narrow opening for goggles.

**Goggles, not glasses:** The wind will drive dust into your eyes with every gust. Safety glasses are insufficient. You need sealed goggles with anti-fog treatment and interchangeable lenses—dark for the glaring stone, clear for dusk and dawn when you will be checking for condensation and reading the terrain. Bring at least two pairs. The particulate will scratch the lenses over time, and obscured vision on the Plateau is a fast path to a wrong step into a fracture you did not see.

**Gloves:** Your hands will be in constant contact with the stone—the-body-that-remained—and with the vegetation. The stone is polished but can have micro-fractures that will slice skin. The vegetation is thick-stemmed and fibrous, capable of causing friction burns and splinters. Wear gloves with a cut-resistant palm and a flexible enough fit that you can still manipulate your water-collection gear and your shelter-building tools. Bring three pairs. They will fail faster than any other piece of clothing.

**Footwear:** This is, after hearing protection, your most critical equipment. The stone is warm—sometimes hot enough to be uncomfortable through thin soles—and the friction of walking on its polished surface will destroy inadequate footwear rapidly. You need boots with thick, heat-resistant soles that insulate you from the stone's warmth while providing grip on a surface that is closer to glass than to rock. The soles should be Vibram or equivalent, with an aggressive tread pattern to catch the micro-texture of the-body-that-remained. The uppers must be abrasion-resistant and high enough to keep dust and debris out. Gaiters are advisable—the fine particulate will find its way into your boots otherwise, and the chafing will be exquisite.

Break your boots in before you arrive. Do not arrive with new footwear. The Plateau is not a place where blisters heal quickly or cleanly.

**Face covering:** A balaclava or similar face covering that can filter the dust you breathe while protecting the skin of your face. The dust has a metallic taste—iron, perhaps, from the ancient stone—and it will coat your mouth and throat if you breathe it unprotected for long. A simple cloth mask is insufficient; you need something that seals against the face and filters particulate down to at least N95 equivalence. The scattered people have developed thicker tissue in their respiratory tracts over generations; you do not have this advantage.

## Water: The Central Problem

### You Cannot Carry Enough

This is the calculation that defines every journey on the Plateau: water weighs, and the distances are vast. The green pockets—where water can sometimes be found—are separated by kilometers of bare stone. You cannot carry more than a few days' supply, and the water sources are not reliable. The rain falls without warning. The dimples drain without warning. A water source that existed last week may be a dry depression this week, its hummock already beginning its slow migration toward the next nearest pool.

**Arrive with maximum capacity.** Your water storage should be flexible—collapsible containers that take up minimal space when empty but can expand to hold several days' supply when you find water. Platypus-style soft bottles, not rigid canteens. Carry more capacity than you think you need. On the Plateau, excess water capacity is never wasted; it is insurance against a dimple that has drained overnight.

**Learn to read the dimples before you arrive.** Study every account, every map, every description of the named water-features. *The Wound Where Water Remembers.* *The Throat.* *The Place That Swallows Rain and Gives Back Nothing.* These names contain information—the first implies memory, persistence, a place where water lingers longer; the second implies a depth, a narrowness, perhaps a reliability; the third is a warning. A place that takes water and returns nothing is a place where the stone has fractured and the water drains deep. Do not plan your route around this one. Plan around the first two.

**Dawn condensation is your lifeline.** Every morning, when the warm stone meets the cold night air, a film of moisture appears across the surface of the-body-that-remained. This moisture is present for perhaps thirty minutes before the wind or the sun or the stone itself claims it. You must be awake and ready to collect it at first light. Bring absorbent cloths—microfiber, chamois, anything that can be pressed against the stone and then wrung into a container. The water will be warm and mineral-heavy and it will taste of iron and time, but it is water, and it will keep you alive.

Do not sleep through condensation. Set an alarm if you must. Train yourself to wake in the dark. The scattered people do not need to be told this; their bodies know the rhythm. Yours does not. You must compensate with discipline.

**Water purification is complex here.** The rain and condensation are relatively clean—the stone filters what it absorbs—but the pooled water in the dimples may contain organic matter from the hummocks, particulate from the wind, and microbial life that your immune system has never encountered. Bring a multi-stage filtration system: a coarse filter to remove particulate, a fine filter to remove organisms, and a chemical or UV treatment as a final stage. Do not skip any stage.
