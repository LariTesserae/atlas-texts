# Preparation Guide: The Nursery

---

## I. UNDERSTANDING WHAT YOU ARE ENTERING

Before you pack a single item, you need to understand something: you are not visiting a place. You are entering a process. The Nursery is not a location that exists in a stable state and happens to contain an unusual ecosystem. It is an *event* — a continuous, violent, accelerating act of creation that has not yet learned to slow down. Everything that follows in this guide proceeds from this recognition. Your preparations are not for surviving a hostile environment. They are for surviving an environment that does not yet know it is an environment, that is still deciding what it wants to be, and that will happily incorporate you into that decision.

You will be offered absorption. Everything in The Nursery wants to make you part of itself. Not maliciously. Not with intent. With *appetite*. The distinction matters, but it will not save you if you let it happen.

---

## II. THE SOUND: YOUR FIRST AND FINAL CONCERN

The sound is not a feature of The Nursery. It *is* The Nursery, made audible. Understand this immediately: the biological cacophony you will encounter is not like a loud forest back home. It is a constitutive condition of the place itself, as fundamental as gravity or air. It does not stop. It does not diminish. It does not have quiet intervals where you can rest your ears and reset your sense of what silence feels like. It is a wall, a climate, a medium.

**What the sound will do to you:**

Within the first hours, you will find it overwhelming. This is expected. Your brain will attempt to process it as noise — as something to be filtered, ignored, pushed to the background of awareness. This will fail. The sound has too many components operating at too many frequencies. The drone of insects will settle into your jaw and teeth as a vibration. The calls — the dropped-glass birds, the drumming frogs, the barking and clicking and shrieking things — will layer over that drone in patterns that your pattern-recognition faculties will desperately try to parse. Your brain will work overtime trying to find meaning in the wall of sound because that is what brains do: they seek signal. They cannot help it.

By the end of your first day, the sound will be living inside your skull independently of your ears. You will close your eyes and it will be there. You will try to focus on anything else and it will be there. This is not damage. This is adaptation. Your brain is reorganizing itself around the new sensory reality. This is also the beginning of danger.

By the second day, you will have begun to forget silence. Not merely stopped experiencing it — you will find that you cannot *recall* it. The memory of quiet will feel abstract, theoretical, like trying to remember a color you dreamed once. This is a warning sign. Note it.

By the third day — if you have stayed this long, which you should not — you will begin to parse the sound. You will start to separate the strands. You will notice that the wall of noise has structure: layers, rhythms, call-and-response patterns, intervals that repeat. You will begin to identify which sounds are territorial declarations, which are mating signals, which are warnings. You will feel a satisfaction in this understanding that should alarm you deeply.

Because the sound includes the calls of the tool-users. And when you can distinguish their calls from the rest of the chorus — when you can hear the *language buried in the noise* — you have begun to join the communication. And when you join the communication, you join the system. And the system consumes.

**Preparation for the sound:**

Bring ear protection, but understand its limitations. Foam earplugs will reduce the decibel level reaching your eardrums, which will help prevent mechanical hearing damage. They will not help you navigate. In The Nursery, you navigate by sound. Remove your earplugs and you are assaulted; keep them in and you are deaf in a place where deafness is disorientation, where disorientation is danger.

The solution is not ideal but it is functional: bring earplugs rated for high-frequency reduction with moderate low-frequency passthrough. You want to dampen the shrieking, the clicking, the glass-shard bird calls, while still being able to hear the low-frequency indicators that matter — the grinding of the river, the crash of a falling tree, the deliberate footfalls of something large moving through the undergrowth at night. Custom-molded musician's earplugs with interchangeable filters are worth the investment. Bring multiple sets. You will lose them. The moisture and the constant presence of small crawling life means anything you wear will be subject to intrusion and displacement. Check your ears frequently. Remove anything that has crawled in.

There is no preparation for the psychological effect of the sound. You can only set limits. Decide before you arrive how long you will stay. Make this decision rationally, in silence, in a place where you can still remember what quiet feels like. Write it down. The sound will make you want to stay. Not because it is pleasant — it is not pleasant — but because the brain, having reorganized itself around the noise, will resist the effort of reorganizing itself back. Leaving will feel like losing a sense you've developed. This feeling is a lie. Do not trust it. Leave when your predetermined time is up.

Maximum recommended stay: **thirty-six hours**. This gives you time to experience The Nursery, to move through it, to see what there is to see, while keeping you below the threshold where the sound begins to parse itself into meaning. Do not stay two full days. Under no circumstances stay three.

## III. NAVIGATING WITHOUT SIGHT

Your eyes will not help you. This is not a metaphor. The darkness beneath the canopy is substantial — it has *material quality*. You move through it the way you would move through water. Light does not penetrate in quantities sufficient for human vision. The shafts that do break through are pale columns, architectural features of the dark space, not sources of illumination. You will stand in one and be able to see your own hands and approximately one meter of ground around you. The rest is darkness.

Your eyes will not adjust because there is nothing to adjust *to*. Human night vision requires at least some ambient light. The canopy has locked together into a single roof. What exists below operates on a different sense-realm entirely.

**What this means in practice:**

You will navigate by sound. You must learn to do this before you arrive. There is no substitute for preparation here — you cannot improvise echolocation under duress in an alien acoustic environment. Practice in the dark at home. Walk through your living space with your eyes closed. Learn the sounds of your footsteps on different surfaces, the way sound reflects off walls and furniture, the way open spaces sound different from enclosed ones. Then practice in natural environments — forests, canyons, riverbanks — where the acoustic landscape is more complex and less familiar.

In The Nursery specifically, you will learn to navigate by these sound indicators:

- **The river**: A low grinding that changes pitch as the water shifts course. This is your most reliable orienting sound. It will move. The landscape has not settled into its drainage patterns. But the sound of moving water is distinct from everything else, and you can use it as a reference point even as it shifts. Track it. Know where it is relative to you at all times.

- **The canopy**: Above, the density of sound is different — more bird calls, more insect drone, more movement. Below, the sound is closer, more intimate, the rustling and clicking of the floor-level life. You can feel the boundary between understory and canopy in the vertical distribution of sound. Use this to maintain your sense of up and down, which the darkness will otherwise steal from you.

- **The lichen-lines**: The bioluminescent growth that traces the ground in lines. These are faint — visible only as the weakest possible glow — but they produce a very slight crackling or hissing sound where they grow, a sound unlike anything else in The Nursery. If you learn to hear this, you can follow the lines. Whether following them is wise is a different question. The tool-users follow them. Or make them. The lines may lead to them.

- **The silence of a falling tree**: When a stalk-tree falls, there is a crack, a groan, a crash, and then — briefly, for perhaps three seconds — a localized silence as the surrounding creatures evacuate the collapsing structure. Learn to recognize this sequence. It tells you two things: where a tree has just fallen (avoid that area; the evacuation means displaced creatures in chaotic movement) and that the sound-wall will reconstitute itself almost immediately. Do not mistake the three-second silence for an opportunity to rest. It is not a pause in the assault. It is a reorganization.

**Artificial light:**

You will be tempted to bring it. Bring it, but use it sparingly.

A headlamp with a red-light setting is your best option. Red light preserves what little night adaptation your eyes can achieve and is less disruptive to the creatures around you. White light will draw them — not all of them, but enough. Insects first, swarming toward the brightness. Then the things that eat insects. Then the things that eat those things. White light in The Nursery is a dinner bell.

Use your light in short bursts: check your footing before a step, examine a surface before you touch it, confirm what you've heard with a moment of sight. Then turn it off. Do not leave it on. Do not use it to navigate continuously. You will develop a dependency on it that will leave you helpless when you must move without it — and there will be moments when you must move without it.

Bring at least three independent light sources. The moisture will kill electronics. Assume that any light you bring will fail at some point and bring backups for your backups. Keep them in waterproof bags inside waterproof containers inside your pack. Check them each time you use them. Replace batteries even when they seem fresh. The dampness shortens battery life unpredictably.

## IV. THE MOISTURE: A COMPREHENSIVE THREAT

Every surface in The Nursery is wet. Not damp. Wet. This includes surfaces you would not expect to be wet — the undersides of leaves, the interiors of fallen logs, the sheltered hollows of rock formations. Condensation forms continuously as the warm air meets any surface that is even slightly cooler. This includes your skin. You will stand still and feel water forming on your arms, your face, your neck. Your clothing will become saturated and stay saturated. Your gear will become saturated. Everything you touch will transfer water to you and receive water from you simultaneously.

**What the moisture will do to your equipment:**

Fabric degrades. Thread weakens. Seams fail. Leather rots — do not bring leather if you can avoid it. Metal corrodes — even stainless steel, even anodized aluminum, even titanium. The corrosion is accelerated by whatever organic compounds exist in the condensation; the water that forms on surfaces in The Nursery is not pure. It carries traces of the biological processes around it — spores, enzymes, waste products, the dissolved byproducts of the accelerated decomposition happening everywhere. Your gear is being digested, slowly, by the air itself.

Electronics are particularly vulnerable. Assume that any electronic device you bring will fail. Water-resistant is not waterproof. Waterproof is not Nursery-proof. The moisture here finds entry points that would never be relevant in normal conditions — it seeps through speaker grilles, charging ports, the microscopic gaps between screen and housing. Your phone will die. Your GPS will die. Your camera will die. If you need electronic equipment, house it in the most robust waterproof case available, open it only when absolutely necessary, and accept that it is on borrowed time from the moment you enter the canopy.

**What the moisture will do to your body:**

Your skin will macerate. Prolonged exposure to moisture — continuous, inescapable moisture — breaks down the skin's barrier function. The outer layer softens, loses integrity, becomes vulnerable to infection. In The Nursery, this means vulnerable to *immediate and aggressive* infection. Every surface is teeming with microorganisms. Every pool, every wet leaf, every drop of condensation carries spores and bacteria and fungi that are adapted to this environment's fecundity and will find your softened skin an ideal substrate.

Bring antifungal cream and apply it preventatively — to your feet especially, but also to your groin, your armpits, any fold or crease where moisture collects. Bring antibiotic ointment and use it on any break in your skin immediately, no matter how small. A scratch from a branch, an insect bite you've scratched open, a blister — in The Nursery, these are medical emergencies. Treat them as such.

Wrap your feet. This is not optional. You will be walking in saturated footwear on saturated ground. Your feet will be constantly wet. Blisters will form. The skin will soften. The risk of immersion foot — trench foot — is extreme. Bring multiple pairs of moisture-wicking socks. Change them whenever you rest, even if the clean pair will be wet within minutes. The brief period of reduced moisture matters. Dry your feet by the bioluminescent fungi if you must; the faint warmth some of them produce is minimal but better than nothing. Massage your feet when you rest to maintain circulation. Remove your boots and let your feet air out whenever you have a safe stopping point, which will be rare, which means you must create those stopping points deliberately.

**Breathing the moisture:**

The air is thick. It is warm and wet and it fills your lungs with every breath as if you are inhaling a warm sponge. People with respiratory conditions — asthma, bronchitis, any compromise of lung function — should not enter The Nursery. This is not caution; it is prescription. The humidity alone can trigger attacks. The spore count is astronomical. The air is not merely humid; it is *populated*. You are breathing in living material with every breath. Most of it is harmless. Some of it is not. You have no way to distinguish the two.

Bring a respirator. Not a dust mask — a proper respirator with HEPA filters rated for biological particulates. Wear it whenever you can tolerate it. You will not be able to tolerate it always; the additional resistance to breathing in an already-oppressive atmosphere will be its own form of distress. But wear it when you can. Your lungs are not adapted to this air. The tool-users' lungs are. You are not them.

## V. PHYSICAL PROTECTION FROM FAUNA

You are never not in the presence of animals in The Nursery. This is not an exaggeration or a poetic characterization. The density of living bodies per cubic meter is obscene. Things move in every direction at once — above you, beside you, below you. The bark of the tree you lean against is moving with beetles and ants and small flat lizards. Something drops onto your shoulder. Something lifts off from the ground near your foot. The air itself is a medium through which life moves.

**What this means:**

You will be crawled upon, landed upon, brushed against, and bitten. This is not a possibility. It is a certainty. The only variable is degree.

**Clothing:**

Full body coverage. No exposed skin below the neck. Long sleeves, tucked into gloves. Long pants, tucked into boots. A hat or hood. A neck gaiter or scarf that can be pulled up over your mouth and nose. The fabric should be tightly woven — the insects here are small and persistent and will find gaps in loose weaves. Consider treating outer layers with permethrin before arrival, but understand that its effectiveness in this environment is uncertain; the moisture may dilute it faster than expected, and the insect density may overwhelm its protective capacity regardless.

Gloves are essential. Not thin tactical gloves — real gloves with enough material to buffer a bite. The beetles and ants that flow across every surface have mandibles and stingers adapted to this environment. Most of them cannot penetrate thick material. Some of them can. Check your gloves frequently for damage. Replace them when they degrade, which they will, quickly.

Your boots are your most important piece of equipment. They must be tall — at least ankle-height, preferably higher. The floor of The Nursery is not a floor; it is a compost of fallen vegetation, root-tangle, mud, pools, and the constant movement of ground-dwelling creatures. A low shoe exposes your feet to everything crawling at ground level. A tall boot provides a barrier. Gaiters over the boots provide an additional barrier. Seal the gap between boot and pant with tape or straps. Things will still get in. They always get in. The question is how many and how fast you can remove them.

**The things that drop on you:**

Accept that you will be landed upon. Practice the motion of brushing something off your shoulder without flinching, without losing your balance, without panicking. Flinching in The Nursery has consequences. The terrain is sharp and unforgiving. A stumble can send you into a ravine or against a stone face or into a pool whose depth and contents you cannot assess. Move deliberately. Always. Even when something is crawling on your neck. Even when something has landed on your face. Brush it off calmly. Then keep moving.

**The larger animals:**

At night — which is to say, during the slightly-less-dark period when the larger animals emerge — you will hear them. Crashing in the undergrowth. Pairs of eyes at various heights reflecting the bioluminescent glow. You cannot see them clearly. You cannot know what they are. Some may be herbivores. Some may be predators. Some may be the tool-users.

Your strategy for large animal encounters is avoidance, and your tool for avoidance is sound. The Nursery is already deafeningly loud. Your presence adds to it. But you can make yourself sound *wrong* — loud, sharp, discordant, unlike anything else in the soundscape. Carry a noise-making device. An airhorn is effective but will draw attention from every direction. A metal whistle is more directional and more controlled. Use short blasts if you hear something approaching that you cannot see. Most creatures here have learned what to avoid. The sound of something unfamiliar and loud may trigger their avoidance instincts.

It may not. Some creatures here are apex. They have not learned to avoid anything because nothing has threatened them. If you encounter something that does not retreat from your noise, you retreat. Slowly. Deliberately. Without turning your back if you can help it. Without running, ever. Running in The Nursery is how you fall into ravines, collide with stalk-trees, stumble into pools, and end up on the ground, which is where you are most vulnerable.

**Do not sleep on the ground.**

This may be the most important instruction in this guide. The ground is where the highest density of life exists. It is where the pools form, where the larvae develop, where the small darting fish have no business being but are anyway. It is where the crawling things are thickest and where the things that eat the crawling things come to hunt. It is where the fungi grow brightest, which means it is where the moisture is most persistent and the decay most active. It is where you will be most quickly colonized.

If you must rest — and you will need to rest; the sensory assault and the physical difficulty of movement are exhausting — find an elevated position. A ledge on a ravine wall. A thick branch of a fallen stalk-tree that has not yet been fully colonized. Any surface that is above the ground-level density. Test it before you commit your weight. The wood here decomposes with impossible speed; a branch that looked solid an hour ago may have been softened by the fungi and moisture to the point of collapse.

Rest in short intervals. Do not sleep deeply. You cannot afford unconsciousness in a place where you are being constantly assessed. Nap with your back against a surface, your pack in front of you, your light in your hand. Set no alarms — you will not hear them over the wall of sound, and the sound itself will wake you if something approaches, because the sound *changes* when something large and deliberate moves near you. Learn to recognize that change.

## VI. THE TOOL-USERS: RESPECT AND DISTANCE

You will see them, eventually. Or you will see something — the darkness makes certainty impossible. Low shapes moving with deliberation through the bioluminescent glow, following the lichen-lines, modulating the sound around them with calls that mean something to others of their kind.

They are not animals. They are not human. They may have been human once, a long time ago, before the canopy closed and the sound became the world. They have adapted — eyes that don't need light, ears that parse the wall of sound, a body built for the dark and the density and the damp. They use stone tools. They follow paths they may have made. They know when the river will shift.

**How to regard them:**

As inhabitants. As the closest thing The Nursery has to a native people. You are in their home. They have been here longer than the landscape itself — which is impossible, which is the third wrong thing, which is a wrongness that they have incorporated into their existence so thoroughly that it is no longer wrong to them.

**Your behavior around them:**

Observe from distance. Do not approach. Do not attempt contact. Do not leave offerings or gifts — you do not know their customs, their taboos, their responses. You do not know what they consider a threat. You do not know what they consider prey. You do not know if they differentiate between the two categories the way you do.

If you find yourself near them — if the lichen-line you've been following leads you to a place where the sound reorganizes itself into the patterns that indicate their presence — stop. Be still. Let them pass. They move with purpose; they are going somewhere, and where they are going is not where you are going. They will likely not notice you. Their attention is tuned to their own concerns, their own communication, their own adaptation to this place. You are not part of their world. You are an anomaly — a sound that doesn't fit, a movement that doesn't match the patterns they parse from the noise.

But if they notice you — if the sound changes in a way that indicates they are aware of you, if their calls shift, if the organized patterns of their language redirect toward your position — do not run. Running triggers pursuit instincts in predators. You do not know if they are predators. You do not know if they have pursuit instincts. You know almost nothing about them. Running is still a bad idea because running in The Nursery is always a bad idea, for reasons of terrain alone.

Instead: back away slowly. Make yourself small. Do not make eye contact — you cannot see their eyes anyway, and they cannot see yours. Do not make noise beyond what you are already making by existing. Do not try to communicate. Do not reach for your light. Do not reach for a weapon. Do not reach for anything. Let them see — or hear, or sense, through whatever adapted faculties they possess — that you are leaving their space. That you are not challenging. That you are not staying.

They will let you leave. Probably. The Nursery has not made them aggressive toward anomalies; it has made them efficient within their own system, and you are not part of that system. You are not prey they hunt or competitors they displace or resources they consume. You are nothing to them, or you are something so outside their categories that they have no response prepared for you.

Rely on this. Do not test it.

## VII. THE RIVER: A NECESSARY DANGER

You will need the river. It is your most reliable navigational reference, the orienting sound that lets you maintain a sense of where you are in the dark. It is also unstable, unpredictable, and dangerous.

The young landscape hasn't settled into its drainage patterns. The river shifts course often — undercutting banks in days, creating new channels overnight. What was a dry path yesterday may be a running channel today. What was a shallow crossing this morning may be a chest-deep flow by afternoon. You cannot trust any observation about the river that is more than a few hours old.

**Approaching the river:**

Listen before you move. The river's sound will tell you its approximate location and approximate force. A high, fast grinding means a strong current in flood — likely after rainfall above the canopy, which you will not be able to observe but which you may hear as a change in the drip-pattern from above. A low, steady grinding means normal flow — though "normal" is a meaningless concept for a river that has not yet decided where it belongs.

Do not attempt to cross the river unless you must. The current is strong and the water is opaque with sediment and organic material. You cannot see what is in it. The small darting fish you find in the pools have come from somewhere; some of them may be in the river. Other things may be in the river. Things that find pools too confining. Things that follow the current to wherever it goes next.

If you must cross — if the river has shifted to block your only viable path, if the sound of something approaching from behind you leaves no alternative — find the widest point you can locate. Narrow points are faster and deeper. Wide points are slower and shallower. This is basic hydrology, but in The Nursery, basic hydrology is complicated by the fact that the riverbed itself is changing as you watch. The bank you step on may not be a bank. The bottom you step on may not be solid. Probe with a stick before committing weight. Move sideways to the current so that if you lose your footing, you are pushed toward the nearer bank rather than carried downstream.

Remove your pack before crossing or secure it so that it will not drag you down if you fall. You need to be able to shed weight instantly if the current takes you. The river in The Nursery is not a river at home. It has not been tamed by centuries of settling into a stable channel. It is still learning what it can do, and it is discovering that it can do a great deal.

## VIII. THE BIOLOGICAL SPEED: UNDERSTANDING THE FEVER

The speed of growth in The Nursery is not natural. This is the first wrong thing you will notice, after you've been there long enough to notice anything beyond the overwhelming assault of sound and dark and wet. The vegetation has colonized the sharp geography with a desperation that feels like panic. Trees that should have taken generations to reach canopy-height have done it in one. The fallen tree that you heard crash to the forest floor will be colonized within a day, will be part of the topography within a week, will be gone within a month — eaten, grown through, absorbed back into the standing biomass.

Something is driving this. Something at the root of it — in the soil, in the stone, in the water that collects in the sharp young hollows. Something that is not quite nutrient and not quite energy and not quite information. A fourth thing. You cannot prepare for the fourth thing because you do not know what it is. You cannot protect against it because you do not know how it operates. You can only limit your exposure.

**Practical precautions:**

Do not drink the water from the pools or the river without filtration and purification. This should go without saying, but the urgency of the heat and the wet and the exhaustion may make any water seem appealing. Resist. The water in The Nursery is not just water. It is the medium of the fever itself. It carries whatever is driving the acceleration. What it will do inside your body is unknown. Do not find out.

Bring your own water. More than you think you need. The moisture in the air means you will sweat profusely; the density of the environment means you will exert yourself more than expected; the duration of your stay may be longer than planned if you become disoriented or injured. Carry at least four liters per person per planned day, plus an additional four liters as reserve. Use a hydration bladder with a drinking tube so you can drink while moving without stopping to unpack. The tube will grow mold. Clean it with purification tablets at each rest stop.

Do not eat anything in The Nursery. Not the fruiting bodies of the fungi, not the seed-pods the tool-users crack open, not the fish in the pools, not the insects that crawl across your hand. The acceleration that drives The Nursery's growth may well drive whatever consumes its products. You do not know the effect of ingesting biomass that has been generated at this rate, under these conditions, by this unknown driver. The tool-users eat here. You are not the tool-users.

Bring dense, calorie-rich food that requires no preparation — energy bars, dried meat, nuts, hard cheese. Keep it sealed in waterproof containers. Open it only briefly, eat quickly, reseal immediately. The moisture and the crawling life will find any opening. A meal left exposed for more than a minute will be colonized.

## IX. WHAT TO BRING: A SUMMARY LIST

**Essential:**

- Custom-molded earplugs with high-frequency reduction and low-frequency passthrough (minimum 3 sets)
- Full-coverage clothing: tightly woven, permethrin-treated, long sleeves and pants
- Tall waterproof boots with ankle support and aggressive tread
- Gaiters to seal boot-pant gap
- Heavy gloves (3+ pairs; they will degrade)
- Hat or hood
- Neck gaiter
- Headlamp with red-light setting (3+ independent light sources total)
- Extra batteries (2x what you think you need)
- HEPA respirator with biological particulate filters (extra filters)
- Waterproof cases for all electronics (multiple layers of protection)
- Hydration bladder with tube (4+ liters capacity)
- Water purification tablets or filter
- Dense calorie food in waterproof containers (3+ days worth even for a 1-day stay)
- Antifungal cream
- Antibiotic ointment
- Waterproof medical tape
- Blister treatment
- Anti-diarrhea medication
- Pain relievers
- Metal whistle
- Sturdy walking stick (for probing, balance, and as last-resort defense)
- Waterproof pack with multiple internal dry-bags
- Notebook with waterproof pages and writing instrument (for recording observations, navigation notes, and — critically — your predetermined departure time, written while you can still remember what silence feels like)

**Recommended:**

- Lightweight tarp or emergency bivvy for elevated rest
- Camera with maximum waterproofing (accept that it will die; bring disposables as backup)
- Portable power bank in waterproof case
- GPS device with pre-loaded maps (for use only above canopy; below canopy, satellite signal is nonexistent)
- Signal mirror (for use if you emerge and need to attract attention)
- Compact first-aid kit with sutures and wound closure strips
- Antihistamines (for allergic reactions to unknown insect bites and spores)
- Tweezers (for removing crawling things from ears, nose, other body openings)
- Backup footwear

**Do not bring:**

- Leather goods (they rot)
- Cotton (it absorbs and retains moisture catastrophically)
- Unnecessary electronics (they will fail)
- Weapons designed for use against humans (the threats here are not human and will not respond as humans do)
- Anything you cannot afford to lose (The Nursery keeps what it receives)
- Anything you would be reluctant to leave behind if leaving required abandoning your pack

## X. MENTAL PREPARATION: THE THRESHOLD OF UNDERSTANDING

You are not physically prepared for The Nursery. No one is. The human body did not evolve for environments like this, and no amount of equipment will fully bridge the gap between your physiology and the conditions you will encounter. You will be wet, loud, slow, blind, and confused in a place where everything else is adapted, sleek, fast, aware, and certain. You will be the only thing in The Nursery that does not belong. Everything else — the ferns, the stalk-trees, the insects, the fungi, the tool-users — is part of the system. You are outside it. You are an anomaly.

This is your advantage. It is also your danger.

Your advantage: the system has not prepared a response for you. You do not fit into the territorial declarations, the mating signals, the warnings. You are a gap in the communication. Things will assess you, but they will not know what to make of you. This buys you time. This creates a small space of uncertainty in the constant transaction of predator and prey and competitor that surrounds you. Use this space. Move through it. Do not linger in it; uncertainty resolves into categorization eventually, and you may not like the category you are assigned to.

Your danger: the system wants to incorporate you. Not deliberately. Not with malice. The same indiscriminate hunger that drives the speed of growth, that absorbs fallen trees in a month, that pushes the river to find its course and the vegetation to colonize every surface — that same hunger operates on you. The sound wants to become your sound. The moisture wants to become your moisture. The spores want to colonize your lungs. The crawling things want to find homes in your gear and your clothing and your skin. And at the edge of the dark, the tool-users are watching you with whatever they watch with, deciding — or not deciding, simply existing in a way that may or may not include you in its consequences.

The mental preparation is simple to describe and nearly impossible to execute:

You must remain *other*.

The Nursery will offer you absorption. It will offer you the peace of becoming part of the sound, the warmth, the dark. It will offer you the relief of no longer being the only thing that doesn't fit. After hours of fighting the noise and the wet and the blindness and the constant crawling presence, the prospect of simply... letting go... of allowing the sound to reorganize your brain, of allowing the moisture to soften your edges, of allowing the dark to take your sight — this prospect will seem not just appealing but *logical*. The system is so much larger than you. So much more efficient. So much more *certain of what it is*. You are nothing here. Why not become something here?

This is the threshold. This is the moment where the traveler becomes the resident. Where the visitor becomes the native. Where you stop hearing the sound and start joining it.

You must not cross this threshold.

Write down, before you arrive, what silence feels like. Not a description of silence — a description of what silence *means* to you, what it is for, why it matters. Write it in your own voice, as clearly and specifically as you can. This is your anchor. When the sound begins to parse itself into meaning, when the patterns start to make sense, when the prospect of joining the communication begins to feel like understanding rather than consumption — read what you wrote. Remember who you were when you wrote it. Remember that this person still exists
