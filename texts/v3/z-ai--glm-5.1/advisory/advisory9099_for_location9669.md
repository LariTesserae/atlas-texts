# Preparation for the Throat

## What You Are Walking Into

You are not an explorer. You are not a scientist. You are not a hero. You are a soft, wet, light-dependent organism about to enter a sealed cathedral of consumption that has been closed longer than your species has had a word for "closed." Understand this first. The Throat does not care about you. The creatures will not attack you, but they will not help you, and the difference between those two things is thinner than the cracks in the floor. You are weather. You are a pressure event. You are a warm body that will leave and a cool space that will close. If you die here, you will be dissolved—enzymatically, over long time—into a powder that feeds the next generation. There is no dignity in this. There is only economy.

Prepare accordingly.

---

## BEFORE YOU GO: The Decision

**Do not go alone.** This is not a recommendation about safety, though it is also that. This is a recommendation about sanity. The Throat is a place where perception degrades, where the mind reaches for input and finds only heat and vibration and the susurration of millions of bodies adjusting in sequence. A companion gives you reference. They give you a pulse that is not yours, a breath that is not the chamber's, a voice that exists in the present tense rather than the geological one. If you go alone, you will eventually lose the distinction between your own trembling and the trembling of the place. You will become part of the rhythm. This is not poetic. This is what happens.

That said: **do not bring more than two companions.** Three bodies in the Throat produce three times the displacement, three times the heat-stress, three times the panic when the floor shifts beneath you. More than three and you become a migration. You become an event. The creatures will accommodate you, but the chamber itself—the shape of it, the way it breathes—was not built for crowds. It was built for one. Whoever carved those grooves did so alone. You should understand why before you follow them.

**Choose companions who do not panic.** Not people who *say* they do not panic. People who have been in the dark and the heat and the close and have come out with their names still their own. People who can tolerate the sensation of living things moving beneath their feet without screaming. People who can feel something brush their ankle in absolute blackness and breathe through it. You will test this before you go. You will find out what happens when they cannot see. You will find out what happens when something touches them that they did not invite.

If they break, do not bring them. The Throat will break them worse.

## THE BODY: Physical Preparation

### Heat

The heat arrives before you do. It rises through the cracks. It rises through the floor. It rises through your boots and your clothes and your skin and into your blood, and it does not leave. This is not desert heat. This is not tropical heat. This is kiln heat. Bakery heat. The heat of a place where water has not existed for so long that the stone has forgotten it. Your sinuses will close. Your lips will split. Your eyes will dry and then ache and then feel like fruit left in an oven, and if you stay long enough, your corneas will begin to dessicate.

**Hydration is not prevention; it is delay.** You cannot carry enough water to counteract the Throat. What you can do is arrive over-hydrated, drink constantly while you are there, and leave before the water runs out. Calculate your water by time, not by thirst. You will not feel thirsty; the heat is so dry that thirst becomes irrelevant—you simply stop functioning, like a machine whose coolant has boiled away. Drink every fifteen minutes whether you want to or not. Drink warm water; cold water will shock your system and cause cramping in a place where cramping means falling, and falling means pressing your hands into the floor to catch yourself, and pressing your hands into the floor means pressing your hands into *them*.

**Saline solution.** Pack it in squeeze bottles. Your eyes will need it. Your sinuses will need it. The dryness is not subtle—it is aggressive, it is corrosive, it will make your nasal passages swell shut within the first hour if you do not maintain them. Breathe through your nose if you can; the air is hot enough that mouth-breathing will cook your throat. But your nose may fail you. Prepare for both.

**Electrolyte replacement.** You will sweat. You will not realize how much you are sweating because the air is so dry that the sweat evaporates before it forms, pulling moisture from your skin so efficiently that you may not feel wet at all—just suddenly cold when you leave, suddenly depleted, suddenly aware that you have lost a liter of water you never felt leave. Pack salt tablets. Pack potassium. Pack magnesium. The creatures have evolved for this environment over millions of years; you have not. Chemistry is the only thing that stands between you and collapse.

**Your skin will need protection, but not the kind you think.** Do not wear thick clothing. The heat will cook you inside it. Do not wear minimal clothing; the stone is rough, the floor is alive and moving, and when you fall—and you will fall—exposed skin will abrade against stone and chitin and the shed exoskeletons packed into the grooves. Wear thin, breathable layers that you can add or remove as the heat shifts. Long sleeves. Long pants. Treat the fabric with moisture-wicking compounds if you have access to them. The fabric will not stay dry. Nothing stays dry here. But it can slow the loss.

**Your hands and feet are your primary interface with the Throat.** Gloves—thin ones, tactile ones, the kind that let you feel texture through the fabric. You will need to navigate by touch. You will need to read the grooves on the wall with your fingertips. You will need to know whether what you are stepping on is stone or body or something in between. Thick gloves will kill this perception. No gloves will expose your skin to the chemical residue in the air and the enzymatic dust on the floor. Find the balance. Practice with the gloves on before you go. Practice distinguishing textures in the dark. Practice identifying what is alive and what is not by pressure alone.

Boots must be sturdy but flexible. The floor is not flat. It buckles and cracks and blisters, and in some places it is not the floor at all—it is a crust of living bodies over deeper bodies, and if you step wrong, if you step *into* them, you will sink to the ankle in a mass of pale chitinous things that will accommodate you without complaint and will close around your foot when you lift it, and the sensation of ten thousand legs adjusting against your skin through your boot will be—

Practice for this.

There is no practice for this.

Understand that it will happen and plan to continue walking anyway.

### The Dark

No light reaches the Throat. None. The entrance is a chimney two hundred meters tall that narrows to the width of a ribcage, and even if you could climb it—and you will need to—the sun above is described as "a dim starvation." Light here is not absent; it is *irrelevant*. It belongs to another paradigm of existence.

**Do not rely on artificial light.** This is the hardest instruction to follow, because you are a creature of sight, and the dark will feel like an attack. You will want a flashlight. You will want a lantern. You will want a flare, a glowstick, a match, anything that proves the world still has edges.

Do not use them.

Here is why: light will not reveal the Throat to you. Light will reveal *part* of the Throat to you, and the part it reveals will be worse than the whole. You will see the floor—that is, you will see them, the millions of them, the pale grey bodies packed tight, the legs and feeding-appendages and the places where individuals are indistinguishable from the mass, and you will see them moving, accommodating, shifting beneath your weight like grain, and you will freeze. You will panic. You will forget that they are not attacking you, that they have never attacked anyone, that you are weather and obstacle and nothing more. You will see the mass and your primate brain will scream *INFESTATION* and you will try to run, and running in the Throat means falling, and falling means pressing your hands into them, and pressing your hands into them means feeling them accommodate around your fingers, and—

Use light only at the walls. Use light only to read the grooves. Use light only in short, directed bursts, and even then, understand that what you see may be worse than what you imagine. The imagination has limits. The Throat does not.

**Navigate by touch and sound.** This is what the person who carved the grooves did. They were here in the dark for a long time—long enough to carve meters of patterns into stone, long enough for the grammar of their marks to change, long enough to need proof that their hands still worked. They learned the Throat by body. You must do the same.

Walk slowly. Map the floor with your feet before you commit your weight. The ridges and depressions are not random; they follow the pressure patterns of the heat, the places where the stone has buckled and cracked. Learn to read them. The blisters are stable—they will hold your weight—but the areas around the cracks are softer, both from the heat and from the density of creatures clustered at the warmth. Step on a blister and you are on stone. Step near a crack and you are on them. The difference is audible if you listen: stone is silent; the floor of bodies gives a faint crunch, a subtle compression, like walking on dry leaves or old snow. This sound will become familiar. It will become the sound of *correct*. It will become the sound of *here*.

**Bring a cane or probe.** Not a light. A tool for touching the world at a distance. Sweep it ahead of you. Feel the topography. Feel where the walls are, where the ceiling drops, where the floor changes texture. The creatures will not be disturbed by it; they will accommodate it as they accommodate everything. You will learn to feel the difference between the smooth blisters and the clustered masses, between the shallow grooves and the deep ones, between the wall that is merely warm and the wall that is hot enough to burn.

Mark your path. The grooves on the wall are someone else's map; you will not be able to read them, not in the dark, not in the time you have. But you can make your own. Chalk is useless—the heat will dry it to powder before it marks. Scratches are possible but slow. The best method is physical: small objects placed at intervals—stones, pieces of gear, anything you can feel with your foot or probe. Leave them at turns. Leave them at the places where the ceiling changes height. Leave them at the entrance. You will need to find your way out, and out is through a chimney two hundred meters tall that narrows to the width of a ribcage, and you will be hot and dehydrated and possibly hallucinating, and the dark will be total, and you will need every landmark you can find.

### The Air

The air tastes of sulfur and iron and a third note—something sweet and chemical that coats the back of your tongue. This is not a metaphor. The air is actively depositing something on your mucous membranes. You will taste it for days after you leave. You will wake up tasting it. You will brush your teeth and it will still be there, faint and sweet and wrong.

**Respiratory protection is mandatory, but imperfect.** A full-face respirator with particulate filters and chemical cartridges will remove the worst of the sulfur and the iron, but the third compound—the sweet one, the one you cannot identify—may not be filtered by anything you can carry. The concentration in the air is low; the chamber is large, and the heat rises, and the worst of the chemical output comes from the cracks, not the open air. But you will be near the cracks. You will be where the creatures are densest, where the heat is most intense, where the chemical deposition is thickest. Limit your time in these areas. Breathe shallowly. Do not exert yourself; heavy breathing pulls more of the compound deeper into your lungs.

**What is the compound?** You will not know. No one above has cataloged it. It may be a byproduct of the creatures' enzymatic dissolution of their dead. It may be a mineral precipitate from the superheated gas. It may be something else entirely—something that only exists where the heat comes from below and the pressure comes from all directions at once, something that the mountain produces and the creatures breathe and the person who carved the grooves inhaled for however long they were here.

You will not know what it does to you. Not immediately. This is the risk. You accept it or you do not go.

**Carry an emergency air supply.** A small tank, a regulator, enough for thirty minutes of breathing. This is not for the Throat itself; the air is breathable, if unpleasant. This is for the chimney. If you panic in the chimney—if you climb two hundred meters in the dark through a passage that narrows to the width of your ribcage and you feel the walls warm against your shoulders and you cannot turn around and you cannot see and the air is hot and close and you cannot breathe—having thirty minutes of air that is not the Throat's air may be the difference between moving and freezing. It may be the difference between climbing and dying.

## THE MIND: Psychological Preparation

### The Floor

You will walk on living things. You will feel them move beneath you. You will feel them accommodate—shifting, pressing, filling the space where your foot was, closing around your weight like a hand closing around a stone. They will not hurt you. This does not matter.

Your primate brain does not distinguish between "not dangerous" and "not horrifying." It processes the sensation of millions of legs against your boot sole as a threat. It processes the movement as an attack. It will scream at you to run, to jump, to get *off*, to find solid ground, and there is no solid ground, there is only them, and the stone beneath them, and the heat beneath the stone.

**Prepare by exposure.** If you have access to environments with high densities of small organisms—caves with bats, mines with insects, anywhere you can experience the sensation of being surrounded by life that does not care about you—spend time there. Learn the difference between "danger" and "density." Learn to feel the movement and breathe through it. Learn to let your foot come down on something that yields and keep walking.

You will not be fully prepared. The scale is different. The Throat has millions, not thousands, and they are everywhere—floor, walls, ceiling in places, packed into the grooves, clustered at the cracks, layered beneath you so that when you step you are stepping on three or four or ten depths of them, and the ones at the bottom are compressed into something like parchment, something like slate, and the ones at the top are moving against your foot, and the ones in the middle are molting, shedding their exoskeletons in the heat, and you will feel all of this as a complex texture of give and shift and faint compression—

Breathe. Keep walking. You are weather.

### The Dark

You will not see. You will touch and hear and feel, but you will not see, and the dark will not be an absence—it will be a presence. It will press against your eyes. It will fill the space where sight should be. And your mind, deprived of input, will begin to *provide*.

**Expect hallucinations.** Not dramatic ones—not monsters in the dark, not faces in the walls. Subtle ones. The sensation of movement when there is none. The feeling of being watched. The sound of breathing that is not yours and not the chamber's and not the creatures'. The sense of a shape in the space ahead of you, a presence, a person—perhaps the person who carved the grooves, perhaps someone else, perhaps no one at all.

These are not real. They are also not imaginary. They are your brain's attempt to model an environment it cannot perceive, and the model will be wrong, and you will know it is wrong, and you will still feel it, and the feeling will not stop.

**Test your reality constantly.** Touch the wall. Touch the floor. Touch your companion if you have one. Say your name. Say where you are. Say what you are doing. Keep a running narration in your head or out loud—whatever keeps you anchored to the present, to the physical, to the true. The Throat is a place that wants to swallow you into its rhythm, and the rhythm is not yours, and the rhythm is old, and the rhythm is patient.

### The Grooves

Someone was here before you. Someone was here in the dark and the heat for long enough to carve meters of patterns into stone. With their hands. Or with a tool that has since become indistinguishable from the stone. They carved and they carved and the grammar of their marks changed—they repeated and faltered and repeated differently—and then they stopped.

You will want to know why they stopped.

You will imagine reasons. Some of them will be wrong. Some of them will be so wrong that they will follow you out of the Throat and into the daylight and into your sleep. Some of them will be the kind of wrong that changes how you think about being alone, about being in the dark, about being a person who needs to prove their hands still work.

**Do not try to read the grooves.** They are not for you. They are a record of something—perhaps a map, perhaps a history, perhaps the scratchings of a mind that had been in the dark too long—but whatever they record, it was recorded by someone who was not you, in a language you do not speak, in a context you cannot reconstruct. Touch them. Feel them. Understand that someone made them. But do not try to understand *what* they made. You will project. You will fill in the gaps with your own fears. You will see meaning that is not there, or miss meaning that is, and either way you will leave with a weight you did not carry in.

### The Rhythm

The chamber breathes. The creatures shift in sequence, and the walls contract slightly, and the heat rises and falls, and the whole place has a pulse that is not yours. You will feel it. You will sync with it—your heartbeat will try to match it, your breathing will slow to its pace, and for moments at a time you will be part of the Throat instead of a visitor to it.

This is not dangerous. It is also not safe.

The rhythm is patient. The rhythm has been here longer than you. The rhythm does not care if you match it, but matching it is easy, and not matching it requires constant effort, and constant effort requires energy, and energy requires water and food that you do not have enough of, and eventually you will be too tired to resist.

When you feel yourself syncing—when your pulse slows and your breathing deepens and the dark stops feeling like absence and starts feeling like home—

That is when you leave.

## THE EXIT: Leaving the Throat

The way out is the way in: the chimney, two hundred meters of vertical or near-vertical climbing through a passage that narrows to the width of your ribcage. You will be exhausted. You will be dehydrated. You will be hot in a way that has become part of you, and the cold air above will feel like violence.

**Climbing the chimney is the most dangerous part of the Throat.** The walls are the same dark stone—warm, smooth in places, grooved in others. The creatures line the walls here too, less densely than in the chamber but present, and they will be under your hands as you climb, and you will feel them press against your palms as you grip, and you will need to grip anyway.

**Use a rope.** Fix it at the top before you descend. This is not optional. Climbing two hundred meters in the dark, in the heat, through a narrowing passage, with no visual reference, is not something you do without a safety line. If you fall in the chimney, you will fall into the chamber, and the fall will not kill you, but the position you land in and the creatures you land on and the panic that follows may.

**The narrowing is the worst part.** You will reach a point where the chimney is the width of your ribcage—literally, the width of your body—and you will have to press through. Sideways. In the dark. With the stone warm against your chest and your back. With the creatures against the walls pressing slightly as you pass. With the heat rising from below and the cooler air from above meeting at your skin.

If you are claustrophobic, this will be your test. If you are not claustrophobic, this may make you so.

**Breathe.** This is the only instruction that matters. Keep breathing. Keep moving. Do not stop. If you stop in the narrowing, you will have time to think about where you are, and thinking about where you are is the enemy of moving through it.

## AFTER: What You Carry Out

The heat will stay with you for hours. Your skin will be flushed. Your blood will feel thick with it. The cold air above will feel like an attack, like a betrayal, like a thing that is being done to you deliberately, and some small part of you—a part you will not want to acknowledge—will want to go back.

The taste will stay longer. Sulfur and iron and the sweet chemical note, coating your tongue, your throat, the inside of your nose. You will brush your teeth and it will fade but not leave. You will taste it in your sleep. You will taste it when you eat, a faint wrongness beneath the familiar flavors.

The sound will stay longest. The susurration. The vibration. The rhythm that was not your pulse but became it for a while. You will hear it in quiet rooms. You will feel it in the silence before sleep. You will not be able to unhear it because it was never truly a sound—it was a pressure, a presence, a way that the world moved when you were in it, and your body learned it the way it learned your own heartbeat, and you do not unlearn your heartbeat.

**Some travelers do not recover from the Throat.** Not physically—physically, you will heal, if you hydrated and if you left in time and if the compound in the air did not do something that you will not discover for years. Psychologically. Some people come out and cannot shake the rhythm. They seek out warm places. They seek out dark places. They seek out the feeling of being surrounded by life that does not care about them, and the alternatives—daylight, cold, open space—feel like exile.

If you feel this, you are not broken. You are remembering. The Throat is a place that wants to swallow you, and it is patient, and it has been swallowing things since before the mountains rose, and some part of every living thing recognizes the feeling of being *consumed by a place* as a kind of home.

Resist this.

Go outside. Stand in the cold. Let the wind hit your face. Remind yourself that the world has edges and you are one of them.

## THE LIST: What to Bring

- **Water**: More than you think. Warm, if possible—cold water will shock your system in the heat. Calculate by time, not thirst. Drink every fifteen minutes.
- **Electrolyte tablets or solution**: Salt, potassium, magnesium. Your body will lose these faster than you can perceive.
- **Saline solution in squeeze bottles**: For eyes and sinuses. Use preventively, not reactively.
- **Full-face respirator with chemical and particulate filters**: Will not catch everything but will catch enough. Test the seal in hot conditions before you go.
- **Emergency air supply**: Small tank, thirty minutes. For the chimney, for panic, for the moment when you cannot breathe the Throat's air any longer.
- **Thin breathable layers**: Long sleeves, long pants. Not thick. Not waterproof. You need to lose heat, not retain it.
- **Tactile gloves**: Thin enough to feel texture, thick enough to protect from abrasion and chemical residue.
- **Sturdy flexible boots**: Good ankle support, good grip, flexible enough to feel the floor beneath you.
- **Cane or probe**: 1.5 meters, lightweight, with a textured tip. For sweeping the floor and walls in the dark.
- **Rope**: 250 meters of climbing rope. Fixed at the chimney entrance before descent. Non-negotiable.
- **Climbing harness and hardware**: For the ascent. Test it in the dark before you need it.
- **Path markers**: Small, durable objects—carabiners, metal tags, anything with a distinct shape you can feel with your boot or hand. Place at turns and landmarks.
- **Watch or timer with tactile or audible alerts**: You cannot see a watch face. You need to know how long you have been in the heat. Set alarms for water breaks, for maximum time limits, for the moment when you must turn back regardless of what you have found.
- **Medical kit**: Focused on heat injury and lacerations. Bandages, burn gel, antiseptic. You will fall. You will scrape your hands and knees on stone and chitin. Treat wounds immediately; infection in a place this hot will be fast and aggressive.
- **Communication device**: If your multiverse has devices that work without light or conventional signals, bring one. If not, establish verbal check-ins with your companion at regular intervals. If your companion does not answer, go to them. Do not assume the dark ate them. Assume they need help.

## FINAL INSTRUCTION

The Throat does not want you. It does not want anything. It has been swallowing and breathing and consuming itself since before the mountains rose, and it will continue after you leave, and the creatures will close the space where you stood, and the heat will rise, and the grooves in the wall will fill and empty of bodies as they always have.

You are a crumb in the gullet of something ancient. This is not a metaphor. This is geology.

Go. See. Touch the walls. Feel the marks. Understand that someone was here before you, in the dark, in the heat, alone, and they needed to prove their hands still worked.

Then leave.

Leave before the rhythm becomes yours. Leave before the heat becomes home. Leave before the dark stops feeling like absence and starts feeling like the only thing that makes sense.

The Throat will swallow. You do not have to be swallowed with it.
