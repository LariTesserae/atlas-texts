# Preparation Guide: The Conurbation

## What You Are Walking Into

You are not visiting a city. You are inserting yourself into a living process that has been running longer than human civilization has existed. The Conurbation will not accommodate you. It will not notice you. It will not change its rhythms by even a fraction of a degree to make your existence there more tolerable. You will be a foreign organism in an ecosystem that has achieved balance without any reference to your kind, and if you are not adequately prepared, you will be processed—consumed by the biofilm grazers, filtered by the water-dwellers, digested by the scavengers, and incorporated into the mineral cycles within days. This is not a threat. It is simply how the system works. Everything that enters the Conurbation is eventually broken down and redistributed. Your goal is to delay that outcome long enough to experience the place and leave on your own terms.

What follows is not comprehensive. Nothing could be comprehensive. The Conurbation is continental in scale and has been evolving for time periods that make a mockery of preparation checklists. But you can increase your chances of survival, and more importantly, you can increase your chances of actually *perceiving* where you are rather than simply enduring it until you cannot anymore.

## THE SOUND: Protecting Your Body and Your Mind

The sound is the first thing, and it never stops being the thing. You need to understand this not as an inconvenience but as a chronic physiological assault. Continuous exposure to noise at the levels the Conurbation produces does not merely cause hearing loss—it reshapes your stress responses, degrades your sleep architecture, alters your cortisol cycles, and, over sufficient time, produces measurable changes in cognitive function. And the Conurbation has *sufficient time*. A week of unmitigated exposure will damage you. A month will change you. Longer than that and you may not recognize what you've become.

**Ear protection is non-negotiable, but standard earplugs are insufficient.** The sound of the Conurbation is not purely acoustic. You feel it in your teeth, in your bones, in the vibration of your skull. The low-frequency components—the tectonic groan of the settling structure, the bass thrum of the transit nodes, the subsonic pulse of the water systems—pass through foam and silicone as if they were not there. You need layered protection: high-quality industrial earplugs rated for continuous use, over which you wear active noise-cancelling headphones designed for industrial environments. Even this will not eliminate the sound. It will reduce it from *damaging* to *relentless*. That is the best you can hope for.

**You must create your own silence.** Because the Conurbation will not provide it, you need to bring it with you. This means constructing or acquiring a personal shelter that can achieve some degree of acoustic isolation—not perfect silence, but enough reduction that your nervous system can experience something approximating rest. The materials for this should be dense and layered: mass-loaded vinyl, acoustic foam, viscoelastic damping compounds. You will be building a cocoon inside the city, a small space where the roar is reduced to a murmur. This shelter must be sealable against moisture and against the smaller fauna, because the same gaps that let sound in will let life in, and the life that enters your shelter while you sleep will not be friendly.

**Schedule acoustic rest.** Your ears and your brain need periods of reduced stimulation. Plan your time in the Conurbation around regular retreats to your shelter. Four hours of relative quiet for every twelve hours of exposure is a minimum. If you notice tinnitus, vertigo, or a persistent sense of pressure in your ears, you have already gone too long without rest and must withdraw immediately.

**The sound will affect your psychology in ways you cannot predict.** The Conurbation's acoustic environment is not random noise. It has rhythms, patterns, cycles. The emergence of the cobalt weavers produces a specific sound signature. The predator blooms produce another. The water systems cycle in ways that are almost tidal. Over time, your brain will attempt to parse these patterns, to find meaning in them, and this attempt will be exhausting and possibly destabilizing. You may begin to hear voices in the grinding, music in the hissing, messages in the rhythm of the infrastructure. This is not the city speaking to you. This is your pattern-recognition machinery misfiring under conditions it was never designed to handle. When this happens, retreat to your shelter and rest. Do not follow the sounds. Do not try to find their source.

## THE MOISTURE: Keeping Yourself and Your Gear Functional

The humidity in the Conurbation is not merely high. It is a physical presence, a medium through which you move, a condition that will affect everything you carry and everything you wear. The air is saturated. Condensation forms on every surface, including your skin, and it does not evaporate because there is nowhere for the moisture to go. In the lower levels, standing water is a constant. In the mid-levels, every surface is slick. In the upper levels, the condensation is at least accompanied by more airflow, but it never stops.

**Your primary enemy is not drowning. It is degradation.** Everything you bring will be wet within hours of arrival and will remain wet for the duration of your stay. This means:

*Clothing must be synthetic and quick-drying.* Cotton and natural fibers will absorb water and never release it, becoming heavy, chafing, and eventually compromised. You want materials that wick moisture away from your skin and do not retain it in their weave. However, there is a tension here: the biofilm that forms on every wet surface in the Conurbation will also form on your clothing, and synthetic fibers provide an excellent substrate for the simpler organisms that constitute the base of the city's food web. You will need to clean your clothing regularly—not for aesthetic reasons, but to prevent it from becoming a living surface that attracts grazers and, consequently, the predators that follow them.

*All gear must be waterproofed, and all waterproofing must be maintained.* This is not a one-time preparation. The moisture of the Conurbation will find every seal, every seam, every microscopic gap in your protections. You must inspect and re-seal your equipment daily. Anything that cannot tolerate indefinite immersion in warm water will fail. This includes most electronics, many medications, paper, leather, untreated wood, and anything with adhesive components that are not specifically rated for continuous humidity exposure.

*Bring multiple changes of clothing and a way to dry them.* You will not achieve truly dry clothing in the Conurbation's ambient conditions, but you can achieve functional dryness by using a portable heat source inside your sealed shelter. This is one of the many reasons your shelter must be both waterproof and sealable: you need a space where you can create conditions different from the ambient environment, even if only temporarily.

**Protect your feet.** The combination of constant moisture and constant walking—which you will be doing, because the Conurbation's transit systems are not designed for human use—creates ideal conditions for trench foot, fungal infections, and skin breakdown. Your boots must be waterproof, breathable, and structured to allow you to walk on wet, slick surfaces without slipping. The floors of the Conurbation are not designed for human feet. They are designed for the locomotion of the fauna—chitinous pads, segmented limbs, gripping mechanisms that human anatomy cannot replicate. You will be walking on surfaces that are irregular, often vertical, always wet, and occasionally moving. Your footwear is your primary interface with this environment. Do not skimp on it.

## THE HEAT: Managing Thermal Stress

The Conurbation is always warm. Not hot—warm. Skin-temperature warm. The kind of warm that feels pleasant for the first hour and oppressive by the fourth and inescapable by the twelfth. Your body dissipates heat through evaporation of sweat, but evaporation requires a moisture gradient between your skin and the air, and the Conurbation's saturated atmosphere eliminates that gradient. You will sweat. The sweat will not evaporate. You will remain wet with your own moisture in addition to the ambient moisture, and your core temperature will slowly rise.

**You cannot acclimatize to this.** Acclimatization to heat requires the body to increase its sweat rate and decrease its electrolyte loss, but both of these adaptations assume that sweat can evaporate. In the Conurbation, it cannot. You are in a continuous warm immersion, and your body's primary cooling mechanism is disabled. You must manage your temperature actively rather than relying on physiological adaptation.

*Move slowly.* Every action generates heat. Every step, every climb, every moment of exertion adds to your thermal load. Plan your movements carefully and execute them deliberately. The Conurbation is not a place for haste. Haste produces heat, heat produces exhaustion, exhaustion produces mistakes, and mistakes in this environment are rapidly fatal.

*Carry water and drink constantly.* You are losing fluid to sweat even when you cannot feel yourself sweating because you are already wet with ambient moisture. Dehydration will creep up on you. Drink before you are thirsty. Drink more than you think you need. Your urine should be nearly clear at all times; if it is not, you are already dehydrated and must rest and rehydrate immediately.

*Know the signs of heat exhaustion and heat stroke.* Dizziness, nausea, headache, confusion, cessation of sweating (which you may not notice because of the ambient moisture), rapid heartbeat, flushed skin. If you experience any of these, you must cool yourself immediately. This means retreating to your shelter, removing clothing, and using whatever methods you have available to bring your core temperature down. Carry cooling towels, portable fans, even ice packs if you can manage the logistics. Your shelter should be equipped with a cooling system—this is not a luxury, it is a survival requirement.

*Plan your exploration around the thermal environment.* The lower levels are warmer because the heat rises from the exothermic processes below and the air circulation is poorest there. The mid-levels are warm but have more airflow. The upper levels have the most airflow and the greatest potential for cooling, but they are also more exposed to the weather systems that the Conurbation generates. Schedule your most demanding activities for the periods when you will be in the most favorable thermal conditions, and always have a retreat path to your shelter.

## THE FAUNA: Coexisting Without Being Consumed

You will be surrounded by more living organisms than you have ever been surrounded by in your life. Most of them will ignore you. Some of them will be dangerous. A few of them will be actively threatening. Understanding the difference is essential to your survival.

**The grazers are not a threat to you directly, but their presence indicates danger.** Where grazers gather, predators follow. The soft, slow-moving herds that feed on the biofilm are the primary food source for the segmented predators that see in infrared and move like tearing silk. If you find yourself in an area thick with grazers, leave. You are in a hunting ground. The predators that hunt grazers are fast enough to outrun you, see well enough to track you by your body heat, and are accustomed to taking prey that is larger and more robust than you are. You cannot fight them. You can only avoid them.

**The cobalt weavers and other builder species are not hostile, but they are indifferent to your presence in ways that can be lethal.** They build. They repair. They maintain. They do not care whether you are standing in the space they are about to fill with silicate compound, or leaning on the wall they are about to reconstruct, or sleeping in the corridor they have decided to reroute. Their construction schedules are not published. Their territorial boundaries shift. A passage that was clear when you entered it may be closed when you try to leave. You must always be aware of your surroundings and always have multiple exit routes planned. If you see weavers beginning to gather and work in your vicinity, watch them carefully. They are not warning you, but their behavior will tell you what is about to happen to the space you are occupying.

**Do not touch the fauna.** This should be obvious, but the temptation will be real. The Conurbation's animal life is abundant, visible, and often strange in ways that inspire curiosity. The weavers are beautiful. The filter-feeders in the water systems are mesmerizing. The air-column dwellers are ethereal. Do not touch them. You do not know their defensive mechanisms. You do not know their immune systems. You do not know what compounds they secrete or what parasites they carry or how they will react to the novel chemistry of a human body. The Conurbation's ecosystem has been evolving without any reference to Homo sapiens. Your biochemistry is as foreign to it as its biochemistry is to you. Cross-contamination will not go well for either of you, but it will go especially poorly for you.

**The air-column fauna are a persistent low-level hazard.** The small darting shapes and larger drifting forms that fill the warm updrafts between the towers will not deliberately attack you, but they are numerous enough that collisions are inevitable, and many of them have sharp surfaces or defensive secretions or stinging mechanisms evolved for aerial combat with each other. A fine mesh face net and eye protection will save you from the worst of these encounters. Do not leave your skin exposed in areas where the air-column fauna are dense. The particulate matter they generate—shed chitin, waste products, bodily fluids from their constant aerial battles—will coat your skin and clothing and become substrate for biofilm if not cleaned regularly.

**The micro-fauna are perhaps the greatest long-term threat.** The filter-feeders and grain-sized organisms that shimmer in the air and coat every wet surface are too small to see individually but too numerous to avoid. You will inhale them. You will ingest them. They will colonize every moist surface of your body—your skin, your eyes, your respiratory tract, your digestive system. Most of them will probably find your biochemistry inhospitable and die. Some of them may not. Some of them may find your warm, wet, nutrient-rich tissues to be an ideal growth medium. You must maintain aggressive hygiene: clean your body daily with antimicrobial agents, treat every scratch and abrasion immediately, and monitor yourself for signs of infection or colonization. Bring broad-spectrum antimicrobials, but understand that they are a blunt instrument against organisms that may not share any evolutionary history with the pathogens they were designed to treat.

**Sleep in a sealed shelter.** I have said this before, but it bears repeating with specific reference to the fauna. The predators that hunt the grazers are the most visible threat, but they are not the only one. The burrowers can tunnel through stone. The biofilm organisms will colonize any surface that remains wet and warm, including you. The air-column dwellers will land on anything that provides a momentary perch. Your shelter must be sealed against all of these—not hermetically, because you need to breathe, but with filters fine enough to exclude the micro-fauna and barriers strong enough to resist the attention of anything larger. Design your shelter as if you were designing a spacecraft for a hostile planet, because that is functionally what you are doing.

## NAVIGATION: Finding Your Way in a City That Rewrites Itself

The Conurbation is not static. Corridors close. Walls are built. Passages are rerouted. A route that was clear yesterday may be impassable today because the weavers have decided to reconstruct it, or the burrowers have compromised its structural integrity, or a population of grazers has moved through and attracted predators that make the area too dangerous to traverse. You cannot rely on memory alone. You cannot rely on landmarks, because the landmarks change. You cannot rely on maps, because maps become obsolete faster than you can update them.

**You must develop a wayfinding practice that accounts for continuous change.** This means:

*Mark your path.* Carry a marking system that is visible, durable, and distinguishable from the marks that the Conurbation's own processes produce. Chalk will wash away in the moisture. Paint will be colonized by biofilm within hours. Physical markers—brightly colored tags, specific arrangements of objects—work better, but they may be moved or incorporated by the fauna. The weavers, in particular, have been observed incorporating foreign objects into their constructions. If you mark your path with something that looks like a building material, the weavers may build it into a wall and eliminate your trail. Use markers that are clearly artificial and clearly non-structural.

*Carry redundant navigation tools.* A compass may or may not function reliably depending on the mineral composition of the surrounding structure. GPS will not work; the Conurbation's atmospheric boundary layer and the density of the built environment will block satellite signals. Inertial navigation systems can track your movement relative to a starting point, but they accumulate error over time and must be periodically corrected. The most reliable navigation method may be the oldest: dead reckoning combined with careful observation of the city's own orienting features. The light comes from above. The water flows down. The weavers work outward from established structures. The thermal gradients have consistent patterns. Learn to read the city's own signals, and you will always have some sense of where you are.

*Never travel without a return plan.* Before you move into any area, identify at least two ways to get back to your shelter. If you cannot identify two ways, do not enter the area. The Conurbation will close routes on you. This is not a possibility; it is a certainty. When it happens, you will need an alternative. If you have no alternative, you will be forced to improvise in an environment that is actively hostile to improvisation, and the odds of a successful improvised escape from a living city that is indifferent to your survival are not good.

*Learn the schedules of the fauna.* Not because they publish timetables, but because their behaviors have rhythms. The weavers emerge at specific points in the light cycle. The grazers move in patterns that are influenced by the water flow and the biofilm growth cycles. The predators follow the grazers on a delay that is relatively predictable once you understand the dynamics. The filter-feeders in the water systems pulse in ways that correspond to the flow rates, which are in turn governed by the city's own circadian-equivalent processes. These rhythms are not your rhythms, but they are rhythms, and they can be learned, and learning them will help you predict when and where the city will be more or less passable.

## FOOD AND WATER: Sustaining Yourself in an Alien Metabolism

**Do not eat anything you find in the Conurbation.** This is absolute. The food web here is based on biofilm, mineral compounds, and the recycling of organic matter through a chain of organisms that share no evolutionary history with anything you have ever eaten. The grazers feed on biofilm that metabolizes mineral compounds in the condensation. The predators eat the grazers. The scavengers eat everything that falls. At no point in this cycle does anything occur that your digestive system is equipped to process. The proteins are wrong. The lipids are wrong. The carbohydrates are probably not carbohydrates as you understand them. Even if a substance appears familiar—a gelatinous mass that looks like fruit, a secretion that resembles honey, a tissue that has the texture of meat—it is not what it appears to be, and consuming it will at best pass through you undigested and at worst poison you or introduce alien organisms into your gastrointestinal tract.

**Bring all the food you will need.** Calculate your caloric requirements for the duration of your stay, add a buffer of at least fifty percent for the increased demands of thermal stress and constant physical exertion, and pack accordingly. Choose foods that are shelf-stable, calorie-dense, and tolerant of the ambient conditions. The Conurbation's moisture will compromise any food packaging that is not completely waterproof. Vacuum-sealed pouches, sealed containers, dehydrated meals that can be reconstituted with clean water—all of these are viable options, but only if their seals remain intact. Inspect your food stores daily for signs of moisture intrusion or biofilm colonization. If you see either, discard the affected food immediately. The cost of replacing it is less than the cost of the infection or poisoning that will result from consuming contaminated supplies.

**Water treatment is a critical survival skill.** The Conurbation has water everywhere, and much of it has been filtered through the bodies of the filter-feeding fauna and the infrastructure's own purification systems, but it is not safe for human consumption. It contains micro-organisms, mineral compounds, and biological byproducts that your body cannot process. You must treat all water before drinking it. Boiling is the most reliable method, but it requires fuel and time and produces heat that you must then manage. Chemical treatment with iodine or chlorine may be effective against some organisms but not others, and the mineral content of the water may neutralize the treatment before it can work. Mechanical filtration through a high-quality portable water filter with an absolute pore size of 0.2 microns or smaller will remove most biological contaminants but will not address dissolved minerals or chemical compounds. The safest approach is to combine methods: filter first, then treat chemically, then boil if possible. Even then, you cannot be certain that the water is safe. The organisms in the Conurbation's water systems evolved in conditions that are fundamentally different from anything your water treatment systems were designed to address. Your best option is to bring as much of your own water as you can carry and to treat local water only as a supplement, never as your primary supply.

## PSYCHOLOGICAL PREPARATION: Enduring the Experience

The physical challenges of the Conurbation are severe, but they are predictable. You can prepare for them with equipment and planning and discipline. The psychological challenges are harder to anticipate and harder to mitigate, because they emerge from the sustained experience of being in a place that is fundamentally incompatible with human expectations of how the world works.

**You will be alone in a way you have never been alone.** The Conurbation is full of life, but none of that life is human, and none of it will communicate with you in any way you recognize. The fauna are not pets. They are not companions. They are not even rivals. They are elements of a system that operates on scales and timelines that make your individual existence irrelevant. You will be surrounded by activity that is clearly purposeful, clearly organized, clearly intelligent in some distributed and alien fashion, and you will be entirely excluded from it. This is a form of isolation that is more profound than physical solitude. It is the isolation of being a foreign object in a functioning system—present but not participant, visible but not relevant, alive but not connected.

**The absence of plants will affect you more than you expect.** You may not think of yourself as someone who needs green things. You may believe that you are comfortable in built environments, that you do not require natural settings to maintain your psychological equilibrium. The Conurbation will test this belief. The color green does not exist here. Photosynthesis does not happen here. The visual environment is composed of grays and blues and browns and the iridescent flash of chitin, and it is beautiful in its way, but it is a beauty that operates on frequencies your emotional hardware was not calibrated to receive. After a week, you will begin to crave greenness with an intensity that surprises you. After two weeks, you may find yourself staring at any surface that even hints at a verdant hue, manufacturing the color where it does not exist. This is not madness. It is your nervous system protesting the absence of a signal it has relied on since your species first evolved. Bring something green. A photograph, a small plant in a sealed container, a piece of fabric dyed the color of leaves. It will seem absurd. It will not be absurd. It will be a lifeline to a world that recognizes you.

**The sound will erode you.** I have described the physical effects of continuous noise exposure. The psychological effects are equally severe. Sleep disruption, irritability, difficulty concentrating, emotional volatility, social withdrawal (which is particularly dangerous given your isolation), and eventually a state of chronic low-level dissociation in which you feel separated from your own thoughts and actions by a wall of sound. Your shelter is your defense against this, but your shelter can only reduce the noise, not eliminate it, and you cannot spend your entire visit in a sealed box. Plan for the psychological toll of the acoustic environment. Bring audiobooks, music, anything that allows you to impose your own auditory patterns on the roar. The noise-cancelling headphones that protect your hearing can also serve as a tool for psychological preservation if you use them to create moments of chosen sound rather than merely reduced sound.

**The scale will humble you.** The Conurbation is vast beyond comprehension. It has its own weather. It has its own geological processes. It has been running for time periods that render your individual lifespan statistically insignificant. You will feel small. Not small in the way that standing before a mountain makes you feel small, but small in the way that a single cell in a body feels small—functional, perhaps, in the context of a larger process, but individually meaningless. This can be profoundly disturbing or profoundly liberating, depending on your disposition. I recommend preparing for the former and being open to the latter.

**You will not understand what you are seeing.** The Conurbation is the product of an intelligence—or a distributed set of intelligences, or an emergent property of a self-organizing system, or something for which we have no adequate term—that operates on principles your cognitive architecture was not designed to parse. You will see patterns everywhere. You will sense meaning in the movements of the weavers, the flows of the water, the cycles of the fauna. Some of that meaning will be real. Most of it will be projection. The difficulty is that you will not be able to tell the difference. You will be a visitor in a foreign language, trying to read the grammar of a world that does not acknowledge your presence. Accept this. Do not force interpretation. Do not impose narrative. Observe. Record. Experience. The Conurbation does not need to be understood. It needs to be witnessed, and you are there to witness it.

## WHAT TO BRING: A Partial List

- Industrial earplugs (rated NRR 33 or higher), multiple sets
- Active noise-cancelling headphones with industrial rating
- Materials for constructing a personal shelter: mass-loaded vinyl, acoustic foam, waterproof sealants, structural supports
- Waterproof, breathable clothing in multiple layers, all synthetic
- Waterproof boots with non-slip soles, designed for wet irregular surfaces, at least two pairs
- Face net (fine mesh) and sealed eye protection
- Broad-spectrum antimicrobial agents, topical and systemic
- Water purification system (mechanical filter + chemical treatment + capability to boil)
- All food required for duration of stay, plus 50% buffer, all vacuum-sealed or waterproof-containerized
- Portable cooling system for shelter
- Navigation tools: compass, inertial tracking device, marking materials
- Light sources (the mid and lower levels are dim, and your headlamp will fail if not waterproof)
- Medical kit focused on: wound care, burn treatment, infection management, heat illness, allergic reaction
- Something green
- Recording equipment (waterproof) for documentation
- Communication equipment (may not function, but worth carrying)
- Emergency shelter—a lightweight, sealable backup in case your primary shelter is compromised
- A clear understanding of your own limits, and a plan for leaving when you reach them

## A FINAL NOTE

The Conurbation is not evil. It is not hostile. It is not a trap or a threat or a punishment. It is a system—a vast, ancient, self-sustaining system that has achieved a kind of equilibrium that your species has never accomplished and may never accomplish. It is beautiful in ways that will take you weeks to perceive. It is terrifying in ways that will never fully diminish. It is worth experiencing, or I would not have written this guide. But it must be experienced on its own terms, and its terms are not yours.

Go prepared. Go humble. Go with the understanding that you are entering something that does not care whether you survive, and that this indifference is not cruelty but simply the nature of a world that has learned to exist without you.

And when you leave—if you leave—you will carry the sound with you. Not as a memory, but as a presence, a low hum in the background of your consciousness that never quite fades, a reminder that somewhere, in a basin on a world that is not your own, the Conurbation is still building, still breathing, still roaring, still alive.
