# Preparation Guide: The Ascendant

---

## Before You Even Consider Going

Ask yourself why.

This is not a place that rewards tourism. It doesn't offer vistas in any traditional sense—the light is too flat, the horizons too strange, the distances too heavy. It doesn't offer discovery, not really; the structures will not decode themselves for you, and the amber film will not perform on command. It doesn't offer challenge—the land will not fight you, will not test you, will simply endure while you expire. It doesn't offer transformation, except the transformation of becoming a dessicated thing that stopped moving in a place that never started.

If you're going because you've never been, that's not enough. That impulse will carry you through the first hour and then abandon you, and you'll be standing in the same spot three hours later with cracked lips and no memory of deciding to stop walking.

If you're going because you want to understand the structures, accept now that you probably won't. The logic they operate under was not built for your mind. You can document, catalog, measure. You cannot *comprehend*, not in the way you want to. The best you can hope for is a deepened appreciation for how much you don't know, and you can get that anywhere for cheaper.

If you're going because the description called to you—because something about a place that is still becoming resonated with something in you that is still becoming—then you're the kind of person who will go regardless of what I write here, and I respect that, and I want you to survive it.

## Water: Your Single Non-Negotiable Priority

You will lose moisture faster than you believe possible.

The description says your lips crack within the first hour. That's not hyperbole. That's not a literary flourish. That is a measured observation of what this place does to biological tissue. The Ascendant pulls water from you the way a kiln pulls moisture from clay—steadily, completely, without malice, without pause. Every breath costs you. Every moment your skin is exposed to the air costs you. The process is so consistent and so impersonal that you won't notice it happening until you reach for your canteen and find it lighter than expected, and then you'll do the math and realize you've been drinking at twice your normal rate and you're *still* behind.

**Bring more water than you think you need. Then triple it.**

I mean this literally. Calculate your expected water consumption for the duration of your stay based on extreme-heat desert protocols. Then multiply by three. The Ascendant is not a desert—deserts have cold nights, deserts have some atmospheric moisture, deserts give you a reprieve. The Ascendant offers no reprieve. The warmth is constant. The dryness is constant. Your body will be fighting a losing battle against evaporation from the moment you arrive until the moment you leave, and the only variable you control is how much water you give it to fight with.

**Water storage considerations:**

Your containers matter. The heat here is radiant and persistent, rising from below, and anything in direct contact with the stone will warm rapidly. Standard plastic canteens will leach taste and eventually deform. Metal containers will heat to uncomfortable temperatures and make your water warm, which sounds like a minor inconvenience but becomes psychologically significant when you're desperate for relief and every sip is tepid. Double-walled vacuum flasks are ideal—they'll keep your water cool and prevent the external temperature from driving you to drink more than you need. Mark the outside with measurement lines so you can track consumption. You will lose track of time in the Ascendant. You will lose track of everything. Your water level should not be one of the things you lose track of.

**Electrolytes:**

You're not just losing water. You're losing salts. The thin air will make you breathe harder, even at rest. The heat will make you sweat, even if you don't notice it because the air is stripping the moisture away before it can pool on your skin. You need electrolyte replacement—tablets, powder, whatever system you prefer—but you need it. Pure water won't sustain you. Your muscles will cramp, your thinking will fog, and in a place where the ground shifts and the structures make no sense and the light never changes, foggy thinking is a death sentence.

**Water discipline:**

Establish a drinking schedule before you arrive. Not "drink when thirsty"—by the time you're thirsty in the Ascendant, you're already behind. Set a timer on whatever device you're using (more on devices later) and drink at fixed intervals. Small sips, frequently. Gulping wastes water through inefficient absorption and increases urine output. You want a steady input matching your steady loss.

Know your exit route. Know how long it takes to get back to wherever you came from. And know that your water budget must include the departure. The worst thing you can do is drink your reserves exploring and then face the transit back with nothing. The Ascendant will not provide. The Ascendant will not notice your absence.

## Respiratory Preparation: The Thin Provisional Air

The atmosphere here is barely adequate. It is breathable, yes—but only provisionally, only in the way that a fraying rope is load-bearing until it isn't. The elevation is extreme. The air pressure is low. And the composition is wrong in ways your lungs will notice before your conscious mind does.

**Altitude sickness is a real and present danger.**

You are several kilometers above whatever this world considers sea level. If you're arriving from a location near your own world's sea level, the change will be violent. Acclimatization is not optional. If possible, stage your approach—spend time at intermediate elevations before arriving. If staging isn't possible, at minimum arrive with a supply of acetazolamide or your world's equivalent, and understand that the first 12-24 hours will involve headache, nausea, and shortness of breath even with medication.

Your thinking will be slower at altitude. Your reactions will be delayed. In a place where the ground shifts and fissures open and the geothermal pulse changes the landscape beneath your feet, slowed thinking is dangerous. Plan for it. Build margins into every decision. If a route looks marginal, assume you'll perform worse than you expect and choose the safer option.

**The sulfurous gas from the vents:**

During the geothermal exhale, the vents open and gas escapes. It's described as mostly colorless and mostly odorless—mostly. The "mostly" should concern you. Sulfur dioxide at low concentrations irritates the eyes, throat, and lungs. At higher concentrations, it causes bronchoconstriction, chemical burns to mucous membranes, and pulmonary edema. The Ascendant's vents are not predictable. You won't know the concentration until you're in it.

Bring a respirator. Not a cheap particulate mask—a real respirator with cartridges rated for acid gases and organic vapors. Keep it accessible, not buried in your pack. When the ground exhales and the haze thickens and you taste something metallic and sharp at the back of your throat, you will not want to be digging through your gear. You will want to pull the mask over your face immediately, and you will want it to fit properly, and you will want to have tested it before you arrived so you know how to breathe through it without panicking.

**Supplemental oxygen:**

Consider it. Seriously consider it. A small canister with a regulator, not for constant use but for emergencies—when the gas is thick, when the altitude sickness hits hard, when you need to think clearly and the air isn't enough. It's weight. It's bulk. It's also the difference between a bad moment and a fatal one.

## Protection From the Ground Itself

The warmth comes from below.

This is not a metaphor. The stone is hot. Not uniformly hot—there are gradients, cooler zones between the vents, areas where the fresh stone has had time to cool before the next pulse. But the vents themselves will blister skin on contact, and even the "cool" stone is warm enough that you'll feel it through your boots after a few minutes of standing still.

**Footwear is critical.**

You need boots with thick, insulating soles. Not minimalist trail runners. Not thin leather hikers. You need something between you and a surface that is actively being heated by mantle pressures. Vibram soles, thermal barriers, whatever your world offers in terms of heat-resistant footwear for foundry workers or volcanologists. The soles should be thick enough that you can stand on warm stone for extended periods without discomfort, because you *will* stand for extended periods. The Ascendant encourages stillness, and the stillness will cook your feet if you let it.

The boots also need to handle sharp, fractured basalt. The polygon columns have edges. The fresh stone at fissures has edges that haven't been rounded by erosion. The structures are made of stacked and fitted stone with gaps and irregularities. You need ankle support, puncture resistance, and grip on surfaces that may be smooth and vitreous or rough and newly fractured, sometimes within the same step.

Break them in before you arrive. Do not bring new boots to the Ascendant. Blisters on top of desiccation on top of altitude sickness is a combination that will end your expedition.

**Clothing:**

This requires careful balance. The air is hot. The ground is hot. You will be hot. But you also need to protect your skin from the desiccating air and from the stone itself.

Cover your skin. All of it. Long sleeves, long pants, gloves, a head covering. The fabric should be lightweight and breathable—something that blocks the radiant heat while allowing what sweat you produce to evaporate. Moisture-wicking synthetics or lightweight merino wool, depending on your preference and availability. Denim is too heavy and will trap heat. Cotton will soak through and stay wet. You want technical fabrics designed for hot-climate expeditions.

The gloves serve double duty: they protect your hands from the hot stone if you need to steady yourself, and they protect your hands from the sharp edges of the basalt columns. Lightweight climbing gloves or mechanics gloves with touchscreen-compatible fingertips if you're using electronic devices.

A neck gaiter or similar covering for your face—not a respirator, but a fabric layer that can slow moisture loss from your breath and protect your lips and cheeks from the dry air. You will still need lip balm. Bring more than you think you need. SPF doesn't matter here—there's no meaningful UV—but moisturization matters more than you can imagine.

**Eye protection:**

The light is dim but the haze carries particulates—sulfur, silicate dust, mineral fragments too small to see but large enough to irritate. Wraparound goggles or tightly fitting sunglasses with side shields. Not for shade—the light levels are low enough that shade is counterproductive—but for physical protection. You do not want to be dealing with irritated eyes in a place where the visual landscape is already confusing and the landmarks shift.

## Navigation: The Land That Forgets Its Shape

You cannot navigate the Ascendant the way you navigate normal terrain.

Landmarks shift. The ground rises. Ridges subside. Fissures open and close with the geothermal pulse. The structure you noted on your outbound leg may be partially submerged or fully revealed by the time you return. The cairn you built an hour ago may now sit in a shallow depression, or may have been swallowed entirely by the welling of fresh stone at a nearby crack.

**Do not rely on visual landmarks.**

This is hard. This goes against every navigation instinct you've developed. In normal terrain, you look for the peak, the tree, the rock formation, the distinctive silhouette. In the Ascendant, there are no distinctive silhouettes—or rather, there are, but they're temporary. The land is still being made. It hasn't committed to any of its features yet.

**Bring a compass.**

The description doesn't mention magnetic anomalies, but it also doesn't mention that they're absent. Test your compass when you arrive. If it works, use it. If it doesn't, you need another plan.

**Electronic navigation:**

A GPS device or equivalent—something that calculates position from satellite signals or dimensional locators or whatever your technology offers—is more reliable than visual navigation here, but it comes with caveats. The amber haze and the mineral density of the atmosphere may interfere with signal reception. The lack of mapped terrain means your device can tell you where you are but not what's between you and where you want to go. And electronic devices have batteries, and batteries behave unpredictably in extreme heat.

Bring redundant navigation tools. A GPS. A compass. A physical map you've started drawing yourself from the moment you arrive, with the understanding that it will be wrong within hours. And most importantly: the discipline to track your movements in real time, counting steps, noting directions, maintaining a running sense of where you are relative to your entry point.

**The most important navigation tool is your exit.**

Mark your entry point. Mark it physically, with something the land cannot absorb—a metal stake driven into a crack, a bright object placed on a stable platform, something that won't be confused with the local stone and won't be swallowed by the next pulse of upwelling rock. Check on it regularly. The Ascendant will try to take it from you, not through malice but through the simple process of becoming. Your entry point is your lifeline. Guard it.

## Time and Its Absence

There is no day. There is no night. There is only the perpetual amber twilight, the unchanging warmth, the geological breath that might mark hours or might mark something else entirely.

You will lose track of time. This is not a possibility; it is a certainty. Your circadian rhythms will uncouple from reality within the first few hours. You will not know whether you've been walking for thirty minutes or three hours. You will not know whether you've been standing still for five minutes or fifty. The description mentions finding yourself in the same spot for longer than intended—that is not a character flaw. That is the Ascendant working on you the way it works on everything: slowly, persistently, without announcement.

**Wear a watch. A physical one, with hands if possible, something you can read at a glance without activating a screen.** Set it to your home time zone and do not change it. This is your anchor to a reality where time passes in recognizable increments. Check it obsessively. Write down the time when you start an activity and the time when you stop. Note the duration. Without these records, your stay in the Ascendant will become a single undifferentiated mass of warmth and dimness, and you will not be able to accurately report what you experienced or how long you were there.

**Set alarms.** Drinking alarms. Movement alarms. Check-in alarms. Every 30 minutes, drink water. Every 2 hours, eat something. Every 4 hours, assess your status: how much water remains, how far you've traveled, what your exit timeline looks like. The alarms should be tactile—a vibration on your wrist, a physical interruption—not auditory, because the auditory landscape of the Ascendant is already strange and you don't want to confuse an alarm with the groan of the stone or the hiss of the vents.

**Sleep:**

You will need to sleep, eventually. The Ascendant will not provide a comfortable place to do so. The ground is hot, hard, and uneven. There is no shade. There is no softness. There is no darkness to cue your body that it's time to rest.

Bring a sleep pad with insulation—not for cold, but for heat. You want something between you and the radiant warmth of the stone, something that will let your body cool slightly and enter sleep. A bivvy bag or lightweight shelter can provide darkness and a sense of enclosure; even if the temperature doesn't require it, the psychological comfort of walls—even fabric walls—should not be underestimated in a place so relentlessly open.

Set a sleep alarm. Sleep for a defined period—6 hours, 8 hours, whatever your body requires—and then wake up and move. The Ascendant does not reward lingering. The structures will still be there. The film will still be spreading. The land will still be rising. You can return tomorrow. You cannot return if you lie down and never get up.

## Food: The Problem of Eating in a Place Without Appetite

The heat suppresses appetite. The thin air makes eating feel like work. The dryness makes every bite stick in your throat. And the psychological weight of the place—the sense that you are temporary, that nothing here was made for you, that your presence is an interruption in a process that will continue long after you leave—makes the act of eating feel almost absurd.

Eat anyway.

You are burning calories. The altitude alone increases your caloric needs by 10-20%, even at rest. Walking across the fractured basalt, maintaining balance on shifting surfaces, coping with the heat and the dryness and the disorientation—all of this costs energy. You may not feel hungry. Eat anyway.

Bring food that requires minimal preparation and minimal water to consume. Energy bars, jerky, trail mix, whatever dense calorie sources your world offers. Avoid anything that makes you thirsty—salty snacks, dry crackers, dehydrated meals that require water to reconstitute. You cannot afford to spend your water on food preparation.

Eat on a schedule, tied to your alarms. Treat it as a medical requirement, not a pleasure. The Ascendant does not offer pleasures of the conventional kind.

## Psychological Preparation: The Weight That Isn't Metaphorical

This is the section that matters most, and the section you're most likely to skip.

The Ascendant will get inside your head. Not through any supernatural mechanism—the weirdness parameter is low, the place follows rules, nothing here is trying to harm you. But the accumulated weight of the environment—the heat from below, the dimness from above, the silence broken only by geological processes, the constant slow shift of the land, the structures that make no sense, the film that pulses with something that might be life, the knowledge that you are the only thing here that expires—this weight will press on you.

**The lassitude is real.**

The description mentions standing in the same spot for longer than intended. This is the Ascendant's most dangerous quality. Not the dehydration, which you can prepare for. Not the altitude, which you can medicate. Not the unstable ground, which you can navigate around. The lassitude. The sense that movement is optional. That standing still is easier. That the warmth is sufficient and the air is sufficient and the view is sufficient and there's no pressing reason to take the next step.

This is not laziness. This is not weakness. This is an emergent property of an environment that does not change on a human timescale, that does not demand response, that does not reward urgency. The Ascendant is patient in a way that biological organisms cannot be, and that patience is infectious. You will find yourself slowing. You will find yourself pausing. You will find yourself watching a fissure seal or a structure emerge and thinking *I'll just wait and see what happens next*, and then more time will pass than you expected, and your water will be lower than you calculated, and the way back will be longer than you remember.

**Countermeasures:**

- **Have a purpose.** Not a vague intention to "explore." A specific goal: document three structures, observe one full geothermal cycle, map the boundary of the amber film in a given area. When you achieve the goal, leave. Do not add goals. Do not tell yourself "just one more." The Ascendant has infinite "just one more." You do not have infinite water.

- **Have a partner if possible.** Not because the Ascendant is dangerous in a way that requires rescue—though it is—but because another person is an anchor to a timescale that matters. A partner will notice you standing still. A partner will check the time. A partner will say "we should go back," and even if you don't want to hear it, you will hear it, and it might be enough.

- **If you cannot have a partner, have a protocol.** Written down. Carried with you. That says: *If I have been stationary for more than fifteen minutes, I must move. If my water is below X amount, I must begin my return. If I cannot see my entry marker, I must retrace my steps until I can.* Follow the protocol even when it feels unnecessary. Especially when it feels unnecessary. The Ascendant makes unnecessary feel like the natural order of things.

- **Talk to yourself.** Out loud. Narrate what you're doing, what you're seeing, what you're planning. Your voice will sound wrong here—too wet, too organic, too temporary—but that wrongness is a reminder that you are something the Ascendant is not. You are brief and biological and you need things. Say it. Hear it. Believe it.

- **Do not try to understand the structures.** I mean this sincerely. Document them. Measure them. Photograph them if your equipment works in this light. But do not stare at them trying to decode their logic. The logic is not for you. The attempt will pull you into a frame of reference where human timescales and human purposes don't apply, and that frame of reference is dangerously comfortable. The structures will still be meaningless when you leave. Your job is to leave.

## What Not to Bring

- **Tents or heavy shelters.** The warmth makes them unnecessary for temperature regulation, and there's nothing to attach them to—no soil, no level ground, no protection from the geothermal vents that might open beneath you. A lightweight bivvy for sleep is sufficient.

- **Weapons.** There is nothing to fight. The fauna is absent. The structures are not hostile. The film will not attack you. A weapon is weight you could spend on water, and it will make you feel prepared in ways that are false.

- **Excessive technology.** Batteries fail in heat. Screens are hard to read in the amber dimness. Complex devices with moving parts will get clogged with silicate dust. Bring what you need and nothing more: a light source (headlamp with red mode to preserve your night vision, such as it is), a camera if you want one, navigation tools, communication equipment if it works here. Leave the rest.

- **Expectations.** The Ascendant will not reveal its secrets to you. It will not give you a narrative arc. It will not provide closure. You will arrive, you will be warm and dry and confused, you will see things you cannot explain, and you will leave. That is the experience. Anything more is a story you tell yourself, and the Ascendant is not in the business of validating stories.

## On the Structures and the Film

I want to say something about the things that live—or might live, or might be living, or might be something for which the concept of living is irrelevant—in this place.

**Do not touch the film.**

This should be obvious. The film exists at temperatures that blister skin. But beyond the physical danger: you do not know what the film is. You do not know whether your touch will contaminate it, damage it, or trigger a response you cannot anticipate. The structures are positioned at the boundary of the film's territory. Whatever made the structures is aware of the film. Whatever made the structures may still be present, in a form you cannot perceive, operating on a timescale you cannot sense. You are a visitor. Act like one.

**Do not damage or alter the structures.**

They are not yours. They were not made for you. They may be markers, instruments, messages, or something with no analogue in your experience. Knocking over a standing stone to see what's underneath is not discovery; it is vandalism. Adding your own stones to a cairn is not participation; it is contamination. Observe. Document. Withdraw.

**If a structure is emerging from fresh stone, do not attempt to help it or hinder it.**

The land is still being made. The structures are still being made, or revealed, or both. The process is not for you to accelerate or obstruct. Watch, if you want. Note the speed, the angle, the relationship to nearby fissures. Then move on.

## The Final Preparation: Knowing When to Leave

The Ascendant will not tell you when to leave. It has no opinion on your presence. It will not warn you when your water is low, when your thinking is foggy, when you've been standing still too long. It will simply continue—rising, cracking, exhaling, inhaling, producing new stone and new fissures and new surfaces for the amber film to colonize—and if you stay too long, it will continue without you, and your body will desiccate in the warmth, and eventually the stone will rise around you and you will become part of the geology, a mineral shape in a mineral place, and the structures will be built around you the way they're built around everything else.

Leave before you think you need to.

Leave when you still have a third of your water. Leave when your legs still feel strong. Leave when the structures still seem interesting rather than inevitable. Leave when the warmth still feels like a novelty rather than a condition.

The Ascendant will be here when you return—if you return. It will still be rising. It will still be becoming. It will not have finished, because it will never finish, because the mantle pressures have no intention of stopping, and the land will go on being born long after every visitor has come and gone and been forgotten.

That is the final preparation: understanding that your departure is not a loss. It is not a failure to stay. It is the correct response to a place that does not need you and cannot keep you and will not mourn you.

Prepare thoroughly. Visit carefully. Leave intact.

The plateau breathes without you. It breathed before you arrived. It will breathe after you go.

Let it.
