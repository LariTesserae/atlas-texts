# Preparation Guide: The Meridian Plateau

## I. What You Must Understand Before You Arrive

You cannot climb to the Meridian Plateau. You cannot walk there, or fly there, or approach it by any trajectory through normal space. The description says this plainly, and you must believe it: *there is no approach; there is only arrival*. This means that however you transition between worlds—whether by doorway, by ritual, by technological intervention, by the simple and terrible act of stepping sideways through the skin of reality—you will not see the Plateau until you are already standing on it. You will not have the comfort of watching it grow closer. You will not have time to adjust.

This matters more than you think it does.

Most locations, even the strangest ones, offer a transition zone—a liminal stretch where your mind can begin to map the new rules. A corridor that gets colder. A sky that changes color by degrees. A forest where the trees become steadily more wrong. The Meridian Plateau denies you this. You will arrive, and you will be there, and the thin white sky will already be above you, and the bone-colored ground will already be beneath your feet, and your body will already be struggling with an atmosphere that does not want to sustain you.

You must be ready *before you step*. Not psychologically—though that too—but logistically. Physically. Materially. Whatever you need to survive those first disoriented minutes must be on your person at the moment of transition, because there is nothing on the Plateau that will help you, and there is no one to retrieve you if you fail.

## II. Respiratory Preparation

This is your first and most pressing concern, and I want to be very clear about why.

The description tells you the atmosphere is thin. It tells you the sky has "barely enough [atmosphere] to hold the color blue." It does not tell you the exact pressure, and it does not tell you the composition, and these are two very different problems that require two very different preparations.

**The pressure problem:** At the elevation where the sky appears washed-out white rather than blue, you are dealing with atmospheric pressure that is likely below 300 millibars—possibly significantly below. For reference, humans begin to lose effective consciousness at around 300 millibars without supplemental oxygen. At 200 millibars, you have minutes. At the pressures where the sky itself stops scattering blue light effectively, you may be dealing with conditions closer to the surface of Mars than to any mountaintop on Earth. Your lungs are not designed to extract oxygen from air this thin. Your alveoli are not designed to maintain gas exchange when the pressure differential barely exists.

**The composition problem:** The description mentions that the air tastes different on the other side of the arch structure—that "something has been added to it that you cannot name." This is a single data point about a single location on a vast plateau, but it implies something critical: the atmosphere may not be uniform, and it may not be composed of anything your biology recognizes. The minerals here have "reformed without water" in their crystal lattices. The life here does not use chlorophyll. The fundamental chemistry of this place has moved past the chemistry that produced you. There is no reason to assume its air is made of the nitrogen-oxygen mix your body requires, and there is every reason to assume it isn't.

**What to bring:**

* **A closed-circuit breathing apparatus.** Not an oxygen mask. Not a nasal cannula. A full closed-circuit system that supplies breathable gas from a tank you carry and recycles your exhaled air, scrubbing carbon dioxide and returning oxygen. You want this for two reasons: first, because it makes you independent of local atmospheric pressure—you are breathing your own environment, carried on your back—and second, because it prevents you from exhaling moisture and carbon dioxide into an ecosystem that may have forgotten what those things are.

* **Minimum 72 hours of breathable gas supply.** The description mentions walking for days between structures. It mentions watching a creature for an hour as it crosses to the horizon. The scale of this place is immense, and your ability to traverse it is limited by your supplies. Carry more air than you think you need. Carry twice what you think you need.

* **A backup rebreather system.** If your primary unit fails, you will not have time to reach safety. There is no safety to reach. Your backup should be a separate, independent system—not a redundant component in the same housing.

* **Supplemental oxygen for emergency exertion.** Even with a closed-circuit system, you may need to move fast—away from a rising ridge, toward a structure you've spotted, back to a landmark you left. Emergency oxygen allows you to push your body beyond the limits of your baseline supply. Use it sparingly. It is your lifeline.

**What to do:**

* **Breathe slowly.** Your instinct in a thin, strange atmosphere will be to breathe faster, to gasp, to pull air in desperate gulps. Your rebreather handles this, but your stress response does not know that. Train yourself before arrival to maintain slow, deliberate breathing under duress. Practice in small, confined spaces. Practice while frightened. Practice until the rhythm is automatic, because the Plateau will frighten you, and fear makes you breathe wrong, and breathing wrong on the Plateau means breathing wrong inside a closed system that you cannot replenish.

* **Do not remove your mask for any reason.** Not to speak. Not to eat. Not to check if the air "smells different." The arch structure changes the composition of what passes through it; you identified this from the description. Other structures may do the same. You will not always know when you have entered a zone of altered atmosphere. Keep the mask on. Always.

## III. Ocular and Dermal Protection

The sun on the Meridian Plateau arrives "as if through vacuum, sharp and unadorned." The thin atmosphere does not scatter or absorb meaningful amounts of ultraviolet radiation. What reaches your skin and eyes is nearly the full output of whatever star illuminates this world, filtered by almost nothing.

**What this means:**

You will burn. Not slowly, not the way you burn on a beach—you will burn the way you would burn at high altitude, fast and deep and potentially disabling. UV radiation at these levels damages skin cells faster than they can repair, causes corneal inflammation (snow blindness), and accelerates the breakdown of connective tissue. You are not adapted to this. Nothing about your body is prepared for unfiltered stellar radiation in a near-vacuum atmosphere.

**What to bring:**

* **Full-coverage UV-blocking clothing.** Not sunscreen—sunscreen requires reapplication, rubs off, sweats off, and cannot protect you adequately at these radiation levels. You need fabric that blocks UV entirely: tightly woven synthetic or specially treated natural fibers that cover every inch of skin from your neck down. Think of a beekeeper's suit, but lighter, because you also need to manage heat.

* **A full-face shield or goggles with UV filtration.** Your eyes are the most vulnerable target. Even indirect UV, scattered off the bone-white ground, will accumulate damage. Your face shield must block 100% of UVA and UVB, must seal against your face (because the thin atmosphere will allow radiation to reach any exposed skin around the edges), and must not fog—because if your face shield fogs and you remove it to clear it, you have exposed your eyes.

* **Head and neck protection.** The back of your neck, your ears, your scalp—all of these will burn. A hood is better than a hat. A seal against your rebreather mask is better than a hood that gaps.

* **Thermal regulation layers.** The description says the air is cool but the ground is warm. This means you will be standing in a temperature gradient: cold air, warm feet, unfiltered sun on your head. Your body will struggle to regulate. Bring layers that you can add or remove without exposing skin. Bring insulation for your core and ventilation for your extremities. Do not assume that "cool air" means "cold conditions"—the radiant heat from the sun, combined with the warmth rising from the ground, may create conditions where you are simultaneously sunburned and hypothermic.

**What to do:**

* **Check your coverage constantly.** A gap of an inch at your wrist, a lift of your hood in the wind, a tear in your fabric—any of these will result in severe burns within minutes. Carry repair tape. Carry spare gloves. Carry a backup face shield.

* **Monitor your skin even through your clothing.** UV can penetrate some fabrics over time. If you feel heat building in a specific area, add a layer or relocate to shadow.

## IV. Hydration and Nutrition

There is no water on the Meridian Plateau. This bears repeating, and then repeating again, because you have never been anywhere that has truly forgotten water. Deserts remember water. Deserts wait for water. The Plateau has *moved past* water. Its minerals would dissolve if exposed to humidity. Its crystal lattices have reformed in water's absence. This is not a dry place; it is a place where dryness has become a fundamental property of matter, and your body—which is sixty percent water—is an intrusion of a chemistry that this landscape has transcended.

**What to bring:**

* **Water.** More water than you have ever carried for any journey. Calculate your expected duration on the Plateau and bring enough water for twice that duration, minimum. You will be breathing through a closed-circuit system that recycles your exhaled moisture, which helps—but you will still sweat (despite the cool air, the sun and ground-warmth will heat you), and your body will still require water for metabolic processes, and there is absolutely no resupply available. None. Not from the plateau, not from structures, not from anything you encounter. If you run out of water, you die.

* **High-density caloric food.** You will be walking for days across a flat, featureless plain. The walking itself is not strenuous—the ground is "so uniformly smooth" that it presents no obstacles—but the distance is vast, the atmosphere is thin, and your body will be working hard simply to maintain itself. Bring food that packs maximum calories per gram: fats, oils, dense protein bars. Do not bring food that is mostly water or bulk. Every gram of weight you carry must earn its place.

* **Electrolyte supplements.** You will sweat. You will breathe hard. You will lose sodium, potassium, magnesium. Your rebreather will not replenish these. Pack electrolyte tablets or powders that you can add to your water supply.

**What not to bring:**

* **Anything moist.** No wet wipes. No perishable food. No liquids in containers that might rupture. If you spill water on the ground—any water, even a drop—the description tells you the minerals "would dissolve if exposed to humidity, would shatter if rain fell on them." You do not know what this means for the structural integrity of the surface you are standing on. You do not know whether a spill could create a sinkhole, a soft spot, a depression that destabilizes the ground beneath you. Treat your water as you would treat a volatile chemical: contain it, seal it, never let it touch the ground.

* **Any biological matter that could contaminate the environment.** The lichen on the Plateau feeds on "the slow decay of the surface itself, extracting trace energies from the mineral lattice." It has evolved in total isolation from the chemistry of life as you know it. Your skin cells, your hair, the bacteria in your gut, the flora on your breath—all of these are foreign organisms carrying foreign chemistry. If they establish themselves on the Plateau—if they find a niche, however small—they could disrupt an ecosystem that has been stable for a billion years. This is not hypothetical. It is not alarmist. It is the minimum ethical consideration for visiting a place this old, this fragile, this complete in its alienness.

## V. Navigation and Orientation

The Plateau extends "in every direction" with horizons that "feel impossibly distant because the air is too thin to occlude them." There are no landmarks in the conventional sense—no mountains, no rivers, no vegetation that grows tall enough to serve as a reference point. The structures are scattered "with no visible logic" across distances that take days to cross. The ground is "almost perfectly flat," curving with the planet's skin.

**What this means:**

You will get lost. The certainty of this is almost absolute. On a flat, featureless plain under a washed-out sky, with no dominant features and no visible geometry to the arrangement of structures, your ability to maintain a direction is dependent entirely on your instruments. Your sense of direction, your intuition about where you have been and where you are going—these are evolved responses to environments with slopes, with water, with sunlight that creates recognizable shadow patterns. None of those cues exist here. The light is "unforgiving" and casts "absolute" shadows, but the shadows are bisecting and clean—they do not create the dappled, interpretable patterns your brain uses for orientation.

Furthermore: the ground itself moves. The surface *reconfigures*. Ridges rise and settle. Depressions form and fill. If you leave a mark on the surface—a footprint, a scratch, a cairn—the Plateau may erase it. The description says the surface returns to flatness after movement; it does not say how quickly, and it does not say whether it erases evidence of your passage. You must assume that it does.

**What to bring:**

* **A reliable compass or inertial navigation system.** Not a magnetic compass—you do not know if this planet has a magnetic field, or if it does, whether the field aligns with any useful reference direction. You need a system that tracks your movement relative to your starting point: an inertial measurement unit, a dead reckoning system, or (if your multiverse transit method allows) a GPS equivalent that works across realities. Test it before you arrive. Trust it completely.

* **A way to mark your path.** This is complicated by the Plateau's apparent ability to reconfigure itself, and by the ethical constraint against leaving foreign material. You need markers that are inert, that will not contaminate the environment, and that—if the Plateau erases them—will not have caused damage in their brief existence. Small cairns made from the Plateau's own mineral surface might work; they would be native material, and their disruption of the surface would be minimal. But they might also be reabsorbed by the Plateau's movements within hours. Consider this a secondary method only.

* **A recording device.** Not just for documentation—though that matters—but for orientation. If you can photograph the horizon from multiple points and compare the images, you may be able to identify structures at extreme distances, track their apparent movement relative to your own, and triangulate your position. The thin atmosphere and excellent visibility work in your favor here; you can see very far, and the structures are dark against the pale ground. But you must be able to record what you see, because memory alone will not serve you in a landscape this uniform.

* **A distance tracker.** Pedometer, odometer, laser rangefinder—something that tells you how far you have walked. On the Plateau, distance is the only measurement that matters. Everything is flat. Everything is pale. Everything is far. Your sense of how far you have traveled will be distorted by the monotony of the landscape and the clarity of the atmosphere; objects that appear close may be hours away. Trust your instruments, not your eyes.

**What to do:**

* **Arrive with a plan.** You will not be able to improvise navigation on the Plateau. Before you transition, decide what you are looking for, what direction you will walk, how far you will go before turning back, and what your constraints are (air supply, water supply, time). Write this plan down. Carry it with you. Follow it.

* **Return to your arrival point regularly.** If you are fortunate enough to arrive at a location that you can recognize—near a structure, near a ridge, near anything—mark it mentally and physically. Make it your base. Return to it before your supplies are half-depleted. Do not push onward hoping that something interesting lies just beyond the next horizon. On the Plateau, the next horizon is always the same, and it is always far.

## VI. Psychological Preparation

This may be the most important section of this guide, and the one you are most likely to dismiss.

The Meridian Plateau is not dangerous in the way that violent places are dangerous. Nothing will attack you. Nothing will chase you. The creature you may see—the low, broad shape moving with "a deliberation that suggests it does not need to hurry"—will not interact with you. The structures will not harm you unless you do something foolish with them. The ground will not swallow you; it will simply rise and settle like the breathing of something vast and patient.

But the Plateau will *affect* you. You must prepare for this.

**The silence:** You have never experienced silence like this. You may believe you have—you may have stood in deep wilderness, in snowfall, in an anechoic chamber—but those silences were full of potential. They were silences that *could* be broken, that waited to be broken, that were defined by their capacity for sound. The Plateau's silence "is empty of potential." It is not waiting for anything. It is complete. It is the silence of a process that has been running for a billion years and does not require input from you.

This silence will do things to your mind. You will begin to hear things that are not there—your own pulse, transmitted through bone; the rasp of your breath inside your mask; the subtle whine of your equipment. You will strain to hear the pulse in the ground, the rhythm "felt more than heard," and you will begin to attribute patterns to it that may not exist. You will feel the silence pressing against you, and you will want to fill it with sound—your voice, your footsteps, the crackle of your radio. Resist this impulse. The silence is not an enemy. It is not even a condition. It is simply what this place is, and fighting it will exhaust you.

**The scale:** The Plateau is vast beyond your ability to comprehend. You can see horizons that would be hidden on any other world, and what you see is *nothing*—bone-colored flatness, pale sky, the shimmer of a boundary that keeps rewriting itself. You will walk for hours and see no change. You will walk for days and arrive at a structure that is four meters high and two meters wide in a landscape that extends beyond your ability to measure. The contrast between the Plateau's immensity and the smallness of its features will erode your sense of progress. You will feel that you are walking in place, that the horizon is receding as fast as you approach it, that nothing you do will bring you closer to understanding this place.

This feeling is not entirely wrong. You cannot comprehend the Plateau on its own terms. You cannot "solve" it or "map" it or "conquer" it. You can only be present for it, witness it, and leave. Your preparation must include accepting this. You are not coming here to master something. You are coming here to stand near something that has been running since before your species existed and will still be running long after you are gone.

**The isolation:** You should not visit the Plateau alone, but you probably will. Multiverse travelers are, by nature and by circumstance, often solitary. If you can bring a companion, do so. If you cannot, prepare yourself for the reality that you will be the only human mind within reach, and that the nearest mind may be the slow, patient creature traversing the surface on cilia too fine to see—a mind that operates on a timescale you cannot share, that perceives a world you cannot inhabit, that has nothing to say to you and nothing to learn from you.

**The uncanny:** The Plateau is not alien in the way that alien worlds usually are. It is not strange in a way that excites or terrifies. It is strange in a way that *wears*. The bone-colored ground, the pale sky, the structures that are clearly technology but clearly not technology you can understand, the pulse in the ground that sounds like breathing—these will accumulate in your mind like sediment. You will not be frightened on the first day. You may not be frightened on the second. But by the fourth day, by the fifth, by the day when you watch a ridge rise and settle and realize that you are standing on a machine that has been running for a billion years and you cannot even identify what it is manufacturing—you will feel something that is not fear, exactly, but something adjacent to it. A recognition that you are irrelevant. A recognition that the Plateau does not care whether you understand it, whether you survive it, whether you ever leave it.

This recognition can be liberating or devastating. Prepare for both possibilities.

**What to bring:**

* **Music, audiobooks, or recorded voices.** Not to play constantly—you need to hear the silence, to feel the pulse—but for moments when the isolation becomes unbearable. A few minutes of human speech in your ear can re-anchor you to your own mind.

* **A journal and writing implement.** Write every day. Write what you see, what you feel, what you think you understand and what you know you don't. Writing forces your mind to organize itself, to impose narrative on experience, to create structure in a place that offers none.

* **An anchor object.** Something from your home world, something personal, something that reminds you of who you are and where you come from. A photograph, a stone, a piece of clothing, a song you can hum. When the Plateau's indifference starts to erode your sense of self, this object will be a lifeline.

* **A timekeeping device.** Not to measure the duration of your visit—you should have that already—but to remind you that time is passing. The Plateau has no day-night cycle mentioned in the description. The sun traverses the sky "with an unforgiving clarity," but you do not know how long a day is here, or whether days exist at all. Your own sense of time will distort. A watch, a timer, a simple hourglass—something that marks hours and minutes in units you understand—will help you maintain your grip on the temporal framework your mind requires.

## VII. Interacting with the Structures

The structures on the Meridian Plateau are ancient technology. They are "older than the erosion that flattened this plateau." They have been "functioning—continuously, silently, without evident maintenance—since before your species' oldest fossils were laid down." They are doing something. You do not know what. You cannot know what. The description offers analogies—heat manipulation, light storage, air filtration, particulate generation—but it explicitly states that your physics can describe these mechanisms "only by analogy."

**What this means:**

You cannot understand these structures. You cannot reverse-engineer them. You cannot predict their behavior. You cannot know whether your presence near them affects their operation, whether your body heat or your breath or the electromagnetic field of your equipment interferes with processes that have run undisturbed for epochs beyond counting.

**What to do:**

* **Observe from a distance first.** Before you approach any structure, spend time—hours, not minutes—watching it from as far away as your optics allow. Note its behavior. Note whether it changes when you move toward it. Note the ground around it—is it flat, ridged, depressed? Does the pulse in the ground change frequency near the structure? Does the air taste or feel different in its vicinity? (You cannot remove your mask to check, but you may be able to detect temperature changes, pressure changes, or subtle shifts in the way light behaves near the structure.)

* **Do not touch.** The description mentions touching the structures—the cold column, the glowing dome—but this is a report of what happened, not a recommendation. The column pulls heat from its surroundings so intensely that frost forms on its surface. What does it do to the heat in a human hand? What does it do to the thermal regulation of a human body pressed against it? You do not know. The dome stores light and re-emits it. The arch changes the composition of the air that passes through it. These are not inert objects. They are active processes, and you are a fragile collection of chemical reactions that evolved in an entirely different context.

* **Do not linger near the arch.** The arch "changes the composition of the air" on one side of it. You are wearing a closed-circuit breathing system, so you will not be breathing the local atmosphere—but what about the atmosphere against your skin? What about the atmosphere inside your suit, if it is not perfectly sealed? What about the trace gases that may cling to your clothing and be released later? The arch is a membrane, and you do not know what it is filtering, what it is adding, or what those substances do to human tissue. Give it a wide berth.

* **Do not interfere with the plate structure.** The plate emits mineral dust "so fine it hangs in the thin air for seconds before settling back down." This dust is "not the uniform bone-powder of the surface"—it is something made inside the plate, something the plate is producing or processing or excreting. Do not breathe this dust. Do not let it contact your skin. Do not collect it without proper containment. It is a product of a technology you cannot understand, and its interaction with your biology is unpredictable.

* **Do not camp near any structure.** The description notes that when a ridge formed near the cold column, "the column's temperature dropped sharply." The structures and the ground movements are connected—part of the same process, the same "operation." When the ground moves near a structure, the structure's behavior changes. If you are sleeping near a structure when it changes behavior—when it pulls more heat from the environment, when it emits more light, when it alters the air around it—you may not have time to react.

## VIII. Understanding the Plateau as a Process

This is the core insight of the description, and the one you must hold in your mind at all times: "The Meridian Plateau is not a place. It is an operation."

You are not visiting a location. You are stepping into a machine that has been running for a billion years. The ground beneath your feet is part of the machine. The air around you is part of the machine. The structures are components, or perhaps outputs, or perhaps byproducts. The lichen, the slow creatures, the mineral stacks—they may be part of the machine, or they may be opportunists living within its processes, or they may be something else entirely that your categories cannot describe.

This means:

* **The ground will move.** Not unpredictably—the description notes that the movements are "regular in their irregularity"—but on timescales that may be too slow for you to notice in the moment. Watch the horizon. Watch for ridges forming near you. If you feel the pulse in the ground change frequency—faster, slower, more intense—be aware that the Plateau may be entering a more active phase.

* **Your presence may be noticed.** Not by a mind, not by an intelligence—you have no evidence that the Plateau is conscious in any way you would recognize—but by the process itself. You are warm. You are moist. You are a source of heat and humidity and carbon dioxide in a place that has been anhydrous for longer than your species has existed. The cold column pulls heat from its surroundings. What does it do when a source of concentrated heat walks past it? The lichen feeds on trace energies in the mineral lattice. What does it do when a new source of energy—your body heat, your movement, your metabolic processes—passes over it? You may be data. You may be fuel. You may be irrelevant. You do not know.

* **The operation may have an end state.** Or it may not. The description says you "cannot tell if you are seeing the end of it or the middle or a stage that has no name." The Plateau has been running for a billion years, and it is still running, and you are standing in the middle of it. You may be seeing the final stages of a process that will complete in your lifetime. You may be seeing a single iteration of a cycle that has no end. You may be seeing a snapshot of something that will still be running when the star that illuminates it burns out. You cannot know, and the not-knowing is part of the experience.

## IX. What to Do If Something Goes Wrong

**If your breathing apparatus fails:** You have minutes. The atmosphere is too thin to sustain you, and possibly composed of gases your body cannot process. There is no way to repair a broken rebreather in the field. This is why you carry a backup. If both fail, you will die. There is no rescue. There is no one to call. Accept this before you arrive.

**If you become disoriented and lost:** Stop moving. Sit down. Check your navigation equipment. If your equipment has failed, try to recall your last known direction of travel. Look for structures on the horizon—dark shapes against the pale sky. Walk toward the nearest one only if you have sufficient supplies to reach it and return. If you cannot identify any landmark, stay where you are and wait. Do not walk in a random direction. The Plateau is vast, and you will only get more lost.

**If you are injured:** The ground is flat and smooth, so tripping and falling hazards are minimal—but the bone-mineral surface is hard, and a fall could cause fractures or lacerations. Carry a basic first aid kit. Know how to splint a fracture, close a wound, and manage shock. If you are seriously injured and cannot walk, you will not survive. There is no rescue.

**If the ground begins to move beneath you:** Walk calmly to stable ground. The description suggests that ridges rise and settle over hours, and depressions form and fill over days. You will likely have time to move. But do not run—running in a pressure suit with limited visibility and unstable footing is a recipe for a fall. Walk. Watch the ground ahead of you. Move toward flatness, away from the disturbance.

**If you experience psychological distress:** This is not a hypothetical. This is a near-certainty. The isolation, the scale, the strangeness, the silence—they will affect you. When you feel panic rising, when the indifference of the Plateau becomes overwhelming, when you feel that you are disappearing into the vastness—stop. Sit down. Breathe. Touch your anchor object. Write in your journal. Listen to a few minutes of recorded music or speech. Remind yourself that you chose to come here, that you are still yourself, that you will leave.

## X. What to Do If You Cannot Leave

The description does not tell you how to leave the Meridian Plateau. It does not tell you how you arrived. It says only that "there is no approach; there is only arrival," which implies that departure may be similarly binary—you will leave, or you will not, and the method may be as incomprehensible as the arrival.

If you cannot leave—if your method of transition fails, if you are stranded, if you are stuck on the Plateau with dwindling supplies and no way back—then you must make a choice. You can walk until your air runs out. You can sit where you are and wait. Or you can approach the operation itself—the pulse in the ground, the moving surface, the ancient technology—and see if it responds to you.

This is not a recommendation. It is a description of a last resort. You may be irrelevant to the Plateau, or you may be a variable it has not encountered before. You may be absorbed, or transformed, or ignored, or destroyed. You do not know. You cannot know. But if you are going to die on the Plateau regardless—if your supplies are gone and your time is up—then you may decide that engaging with the operation is preferable to passive extinction.

If you make this choice, do it with full awareness of what you are doing. You are not "making contact" with an alien civilization. You are not "exploring ancient technology." You are stepping into a process that has been running for a billion years and introducing yourself as a new element. The process may incorporate you. It may destroy you. It may ignore you. It may do something for which no word exists in any language you speak.

Whatever it does, it will not be about you. The Plateau is not waiting for you. It is not expecting you. It is running its program, and you are standing in the middle of it, and you always will be, because the program is always coming, and it never arrives, and it never ends.

## XI. Final Preparations

Before you step, before you arrive, before the thin white sky opens above you and the bone-colored ground extends beneath you, take a moment. Sit with the reality of where you are going. Not the description of it—you have read that, and you understand it intellectually—but the *reality* of it. The silence that has no potential. The scale that has no reference. The process that has no end.

You are going to a place that does not need you. That has never needed anything. That has been completing itself for longer than your planet has had oceans, and will still be completing itself after your sun is a white dwarf and your species is a layer of curious sediment in some future geologist's core sample.

Go prepared. Go humble. Go knowing that you will not understand what you see, and that this is not a failure—it is the only honest response to something that has been running for a billion years.

And when you arrive, and the sky thins above you, and the silence settles around you like something that has been waiting for you even though it hasn't—remember why you came. Not to conquer. Not to understand. To witness. To stand in the presence of something that makes your brief life seem like a flicker of static in a signal that has been transmitting since before the concept of signals existed.

To be there. To be present. To be, for a moment, part of an operation that will continue long after you have left, long after you have died, long after everything you have ever known has been ground to bone-colored powder on a surface that has forgotten water, and light, and time, and everything except the slow pulse of a program that is still running, and running, and running.

Go. And come back, if you can.
