# Preparation Guide: The Terrarium at the Roof of the World

---

## I. Mental Preparation: What You Are Walking Into

Before you pack a single item, you need to understand what this place is, because it will restructure your sense of scale and purpose within hours of arrival, and that restructuring will hurt if you aren't ready for it.

You are not visiting a location. You are stepping inside a device. A living, breathing, working device the size of a small park, built by intelligences that treat water and heat and chlorophyll as engineering materials. You will be inside it the way a bacterium is inside a body—present in the system, subject to its processes, not consulted about its purpose.

This is the critical psychological preparation: *you may be inside its care, or you may simply be inside its operational radius.* It may be maintaining you as part of its function, or it may be ignoring you entirely. You will not be able to tell. You must be at peace with that uncertainty before you arrive, because the uncertainty will not resolve. The structures will not explain themselves. The system will not acknowledge you. You will be an anomaly in a process that predates your arrival and will continue after your departure, and the only meaningful question is whether you can observe it without breaking it—or without it breaking you.

Practice this: sit in a room with a machine you don't understand—an industrial transformer, a server rack, a dialysis machine—and simply exist near it. Do not touch it. Do not try to reverse-engineer it. Just sit with the fact of its operation. Notice how your mind generates narratives about its purpose. Notice how those narratives are almost certainly wrong. Notice how the machine does not care about your narratives.

The Terrarium will be like that, except the machine is also a garden, and also a weather system, and also possibly alive.

## II. Physical Preparation: The Body You Are Bringing

### Altitude

You will be above the cloud line. This means altitude sickness is not a possibility; it is a certainty unless you prepare. Acclimatize for at least three days at altitude before attempting the transit. Bring acetazolamide. Know the symptoms of cerebral edema—the confusion, the ataxia—and establish a hard rule with yourself: if you cannot walk a straight line heel-to-toe, you leave immediately. The Terrarium will still be operating when you return. Your brain will not regenerate if you don't.

The irony is that the altitude is not the most hostile feature of this place. It is merely the most conventional.

### Your Feet

The rock is new. This is not a metaphor. The stone has not been weathered, not been softened, not been forgiven by time. Its edges hold their original angles—sharp as broken glass, the description says, and it means it exactly. A fall on this rock will not scrape you. It will slice you. It will open your skin in lines as clean as surgical incisions.

Bring boots with the following specifications:
- Full-grain leather, minimum 2.4mm thickness
- Vibram or equivalent lug sole with aggressive tread—the stone is wet, always wet, and the moisture does not behave as you expect it to
- High ankle support—the footing is uneven and the surfaces are unreliable
- No mesh panels, no ventilation ports—the water here will find any opening

Break these boots in completely before arrival. Do not bring new footwear to a place where every step is a negotiation with a surface that wants to cut you.

Gaiters. Wear gaiters. The water beads on stone and seeps from cracks; it will find its way into your boots from above if you let it, and once your socks are saturated in that mineral-tingling water, you will not be comfortable again until you leave.

### Your Hands

You will want to touch the structures. This is inevitable and not entirely unwise—tactile information matters. But the rock between the structures will cut your hands if you use them for balance, which you will, because the footing is treacherous.

Bring gloves that allow dexterity but protect against edges. Climbing gloves with polyurethane palms, perhaps, or thin cut-resistant work gloves. You need to be able to feel the rhythm of the structures through the material—do not insulate yourself so thoroughly that you lose that access.

But also: bring bandages. Bring skin adhesive. Bring the understanding that you will likely bleed at some point, and that your blood, falling on this young stone, will become part of the water cycle within minutes. Whether that matters is a question you should sit with before you arrive.

### Your Skin

The water here leaves a residue that tingles for hours. This is a substance you do not understand, produced by geology you cannot categorize, and you are going to get it on you. There is no avoiding it—the condensation is so pervasive that it is more like a condition of the air than a liquid.

Cover what you can. Long sleeves, long pants, moisture-wicking base layers under water-resistant shells. But know that the humidity will defeat any sealed system eventually. The water here is patient. It will find you.

Bring a neutral-pH skin cleanser. Wash the residue off at every opportunity. Pay attention to any changes in sensation—if the tingling intensifies, spreads, or deepens, leave. The structures process water through their systems and excrete something different than what enters them. You do not know what the air above the thickets contains. You do not know what the shimmer is. Your skin is a membrane, and membranes are where exchange happens.

### Your Lungs

This requires its own section because it is the preparation most travelers will neglect.

The air above the thickets shimmers with something. It might be humidity. It might be a product of the metabolism between structures and plants. It might be the refined atmosphere the description mentions—that "clarity or sharpness" that suggests the air itself has been processed.

Do not assume this air is safe to breathe simply because the plants thrive on it. Plants are not you. Chlorophyll optimization is not human optimization.

Bring a respirator. A full-face respirator with P100 particulate filters and organic vapor cartridges, because you do not know whether the hazard is particulate, chemical, or something else entirely. Wear it whenever you are near the thickets, which is whenever you are anywhere, because the thickets are the dominant feature.

But also—and this is the difficult part—you will need to take it off sometimes. You will need to taste the air directly, to feel that sharpness and clarity on your tongue, because your body is a sensing instrument and the filtered data will be incomplete. When you do this, do it briefly. Do it upwind of the thickets if such a thing exists here. Pay attention to what your lungs do in the minutes and hours after exposure.

If you feel clarity, good. If you feel *too much* clarity—the kind that makes the world seem simpler than it should be—leave.

### Your Eyes

The light here is diffuse and gray. There is no direct sun above the cloud line because the clouds condense out of the air itself. This means your pupils will be dilated more than usual, which means any sudden brightness will be painful.

More concerning: the structures have geometries that are "almost-right" and surfaces with depth that shouldn't exist. Looking at them directly may cause eye strain, headaches, or a particular kind of perceptual fatigue that comes from trying to resolve visual information that doesn't conform to expected patterns.

Bring polarized sunglasses. Use them when studying the structures. The polarization may help your visual cortex process what it's seeing, or it may reveal aspects of the shimmer above the thickets that are otherwise invisible.

If you develop a headache that worsens when you look at the structures and improves when you look away, stop looking at the structures. This seems obvious. It will not be obvious when you are there, because the structures are fascinating in a way that borders on compulsion. You will want to look. You will want to understand. The headache is your visual cortex telling you that understanding is not available through that channel.

## III. Supplies: What to Bring

### Water

This is the most critical logistical concern. You cannot drink the water here. It tastes mineral and strange and leaves a tingling residue on your lips. The description says this; believe it.

The structures process the mineral-laden seepage and output something clear and faintly luminescent and exactly 33 degrees Celsius. The plants drink this output and thrive. You may be tempted to drink it too. The plants are not you. The structures have optimized the water for whatever they are growing, and you are not what they are growing.

Bring your own water. Calculate your needs for the planned duration of your stay, then double it. The humidity will make you sweat continuously; the altitude will increase your respiratory water loss; the warm stone beneath your feet will raise your core temperature incrementally. You will need more water than you think.

Pack it in multiple containers. If one fails—if it gets punctured by sharp stone, if the mineral water eats through a seal—you need redundancy.

### Food

Bring food that does not require cooking. The heat from the stone is not reliable for food preparation, and you should not be starting fires or using stoves in a device you do not understand. Would you light a candle inside a running engine? The Terrarium is operating. Do not introduce combustion byproducts into its system.

Calorie-dense, moisture-rich foods. The humidity will make dry foods soggy and unappetizing. You will not want to eat much—the altitude and the strangeness will suppress your appetite—but you must eat anyway. Set reminders if necessary.

Do not eat anything that grows here. This should not need to be said, but it does: the plants have been optimized by the structures for purposes you do not understand. Their chlorophyll has been modified. Their leaves are translucent near the sources. They are products of a process, and you are not the intended consumer.

### Shelter

You will need to sleep, and you will need to sleep off the ground if possible. The stone is body temperature and continuously wet; sleeping on it means sleeping in a constant seep of mineral water, which will saturate your clothing, your sleeping bag, your skin.

A cot with raised legs would be ideal—something that keeps you three or four centimeters above the water table, which is approximately three centimeters below the surface. A hammock strung between structures is inadvisable because you should not be attaching things to the structures.

Bring a waterproof bivy sack and a sleeping bag rated for warm conditions—you will not need insulation from cold, but you will need protection from moisture. The condensation here is relentless. Everything that can get wet will get wet. Your shelter is not a place to stay dry; it is a place to be slightly less wet than the alternative.

Choose a sleeping site on bare stone between thickets, not within them. You do not know what the fauna are. You do not know what the shimmer does to a sleeping body. You do not know whether the structures' operational cycle changes at night, or whether the breathing rhythm accelerates, or whether the system does something different when the diffuse light fades. Sleep where you can see the structures and they are not directly above you.

### Tools

**A hand lens or loupe (10x minimum).** The details here matter. The texture of the leaves, the grain of the stone, the residue left by the water—these are data points you will want to examine closely. Your eyes alone will not be sufficient, and you should not be pressing your face against surfaces in this place.

**A thermometer.** The description specifies that the structures' output is exactly 33 degrees Celsius. "Exactly" is a significant word. Measure it yourself. Measure the stone temperature. Measure the water temperature at different points in the cycle. If the temperatures change, that is information about the system's state.

**A notebook and waterproof writing instrument.** Do not rely on electronic devices. The humidity will compromise them, and you do not know whether the shimmer above the thickets, the refined atmosphere, the operational exhalation of the system—whatever it is—will affect electronics. Write your observations by hand. The act of writing will also slow your thinking, which is valuable here. You do not want to have quick insights about this place. You want to have careful ones.

**A camera, if you must, but treat it as supplementary.** Photographs will not capture what the structures look like from the corner of your eye. They will not capture the shimmer or the depth of the surfaces. They will capture the geometry, which is almost-right, and the almost-rightness will be flattened into definite-wrongness by the lens. Take photos, but do not trust them to show you what you saw.

**Sample containers, if your protocol allows collection.** Small glass vials with secure lids. Collect the mineral input water. Collect the luminescent output water. Collect the residue from a leaf. Collect the condensation from a weeping wall. Label everything meticulously. Do not open the containers while you are still at the Terrarium—whatever is in them is part of this system, and you do not want to be breathing concentrated versions of substances designed for a process you don't understand.

But also: consider whether you should be taking samples at all. You are inside a device. Removing components from a running device may affect its operation. A milliliter of water probably doesn't matter. Probably. Sit with the uncertainty before you take anything.

### Medical Supplies

Standard field kit plus:
- Wound closure strips and skin adhesive for the cuts you will inevitably receive
- Antihistamines in case of reaction to the mineral residue, the shimmer, the refined air, or any of the other substances you will be exposed to that have no names
- A broad-spectrum antibiotic, prescribed in advance, because any cut that gets infected in this continuously wet environment will not heal on its own
- Electrolyte supplements, because you will be sweating constantly and drinking only the water you brought
- A pulse oximeter, to monitor whether the refined air is affecting your blood oxygenation

## IV. Behavioral Protocols: How to Conduct Yourself

### Move Slowly

This is the most important behavioral directive and the one most likely to be ignored.

The Terrarium absorbs sound. Your footsteps, your breath, the rustle of your clothing—all of it feels intrusive in a place where the only natural sounds are the slow seep of water and the occasional tone from hidden fauna. This intrusiveness is not just aesthetic. It is information. The quiet here is substantial; it has weight. The rock is listening. You are making noise in a system that may be monitoring for anomalies, and you are the anomaly.

Walk softly. Move deliberately. Do not run, ever—not even if you are frightened, not even if something unexpected happens, not even if you want to leave quickly. Running means heavy footfalls, means deeper breaths, means more noise, means more disturbance. Running also means more risk of falling on stone that will slice you open.

The footing does not allow haste. This is a feature, not a bug. The Terrarium is telling you something with its footing. Listen.

### Do Not Touch the Structures Unnecessarily

You will touch them. You should touch them—the tactile information, the rhythm, the warmth—is important data. But do not touch them idly. Do not lean on them. Do not rest against them. Do not treat them as furniture in a landscape.

Each structure is a node in a functioning system. Your body is conductive, moist, warm, and full of minerals. You do not know what your touch introduces into the system. You do not know whether the faint rhythm you feel—the breathing at ninety-second intervals—is something you can disrupt with your own electrical field, your own biochemistry, your own warmth.

Touch once, with intention, with your gloved hand. Feel what there is to feel. Then step back. You are an observer, not a component. Do not become a component.

### Do Not Sleep Near the Structures

This was mentioned under shelter, but it bears repeating with reasoning: the shimmer above the thickets is a product of the system's metabolism. You do not know what it does to a human body over eight hours of unconsciousness. The plants thrive under it, but the plants are the product. You are not the product.

The fauna—if it exists—stays near the thickets. The tracks are found near the structures. The tone that might have been an animal came from the thicket nearest the largest structure. Whatever lives here has arranged itself around the structures' output. You should arrange yourself differently.

### Track Your Cognitive State

The refined atmosphere—the clarity, the sharpness—may affect your thinking. This is not a warning about toxins (though it may be that). This is a warning about perception.

When the air seems clearer than it should be, when the world seems sharper than normal, your brain will generate explanations. It will decide that it is finally seeing truly. It will decide that the clarity is truth. This is a dangerous conclusion, because clarity is not the same as accuracy. A lens can make the world sharp and still distort it.

Pay attention to whether your thoughts seem unusually certain. Pay attention to whether conclusions are arriving too easily. The Terrarium is a device that processes things—water, heat, plant matter, atmosphere. It may be processing you too. Not maliciously. Not intentionally. Simply as part of its operation, the way a filter processes air: incidentally, continuously, without opinion about what passes through.

If you notice that you are feeling *right* about things you cannot possibly know, leave.

### Respect the Water Cycle

The water enters the plateau saturated with minerals. The structures take it in and output something clear and luminescent. The plants drink the output. The plants exhale something into the air. The shimmer rises. Something happens at scales you cannot perceive.

This cycle is the Terrarium's operation. Do not interrupt it. Do not dam the seepage. Do not redirect the output channels. Do not collect water in quantities that would deprive a thicket. You are inside a working system, and the system is doing something that may be important—or may be trivial, or may be something for which importance is not a relevant category—but either way, it is not yours to disrupt.

This is basic courtesy extended to a process you do not understand. Treat it with the same respect you would treat a stranger's meditation, or a ceremony in a language you don't speak, or a patient in a hospital: you are present, you are observing, you are not the one doing the work.

## V. Duration: How Long to Stay

The description mentions observations on the second day and the third day. It mentions noticing what the structures are doing. This timeline is significant: the Terrarium reveals itself slowly, and the traveler who stays one day will leave with a fundamentally different understanding than the traveler who stays three.

Stay at least three days if you can. The first day is for orientation—the altitude, the wet, the sharp rock, the pervasive strangeness. Your body will be adjusting, your mind will be cataloging surfaces, and you will not have the capacity to notice patterns. The second day is for patterns—the distribution of thickets, the relationship between structures and plants, the shape of the system. The third day is for function—what the structures are doing, what the water becomes, what the Terrarium is for.

Do not stay longer than five days. This is not a hard limit based on known danger; it is a soft limit based on the accumulation of uncertainty. Five days of breathing refined air, of contact with mineral residues, of sleeping near a system whose purpose you cannot identify. Five days of being inside a device that may be taking care of you or may simply be operating around you. The uncertainty compounds. The clarity, if you feel it, deepens. The almost-right geometries become more familiar, and familiarity with something that is almost-right is more dangerous than strangeness.

Leave while you are still capable of being disturbed.

## VI. Departure: How to Leave

The Terrarium will not hold you. There is no barrier, no boundary mechanism, no indication that the system cares whether you stay or go. This may be the most unsettling thing about it: your presence is simply not relevant to its operation.

When you leave, check yourself:

Check your skin for residue. Wash thoroughly with your neutral-pH cleanser, multiple times, until the tingling stops or you can no longer distinguish the tingling from your imagination.

Check your cognition. Are you thinking clearly? Are your conclusions your own? Do you feel as though you have left something behind, or as though something has been added to you? The refined atmosphere may have refined you in ways you cannot detect from inside the process.

Check your samples, if you took them. Seal them. Label them with the date and location. Do not open them until you are in a controlled environment with people who can help you analyze what you've brought back.

Check your memory. Write down everything you remember, immediately, before the clarity fades or the strangeness dulls. The Terrarium will seem less significant in retrospect than it did while you were inside it. This is a trick of distance. Do not let the trick work. Write it down while it still matters.

## VII. Final Preparation: The Question You Must Answer Before You Go

The Terrarium is working. It is taking care of something. You do not know what. You may be inside its care, or you may simply be inside its operational radius.

Before you step onto that plateau, before you feel the warm stone through your boots and hear the saturation-sound of water being perpetually squeezed, you must answer this question for yourself:

*If you discover that you are inside its care—if the structures have been processing the air for you, refining the atmosphere for your benefit, optimizing the environment for your survival—will you stay?*

And the corollary:

*If you discover that you are not inside its care—that the system is doing something entirely unrelated to you, that your presence is incidental, that the clarity and sharpness are not gifts but exhaust—will you still want to have been there?*

These questions are not hypothetical. They are the emotional core of the experience. The Terrarium will not answer them for you. It will simply operate, as it has been operating, and you will be left to decide what your presence meant.

Prepare for that. Pack for that. Everything else—the boots, the water, the respirator, the bandages—is just logistics.

The real preparation is deciding what you will do when a device the size of a small park looks at you with its almost-right geometries and its slow breathing rhythm and its translucent leaves, and does not tell you whether you are its patient, its contaminant, or its invisible guest.

Go anyway. But go knowing that you may never know.
