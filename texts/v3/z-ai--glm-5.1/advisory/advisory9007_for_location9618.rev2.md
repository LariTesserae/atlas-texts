# Preparing for the Howling Plateau

## What You Must Understand Before You Go

You are not going to a place. You are going to a process. The Howling Plateau is not a location you visit—it is a condition you enter, a slow catastrophe you agree to inhabit for a time. Everything that follows from this understanding is practical. Everything that follows from the belief that you are simply "going somewhere high and windy" will hurt you.

The plateau does not care whether you survive it. This is not hostility. The plateau has been eroding mountains for two billion years. Your lifespan is less than a blink of its attention. It will wear you the same way it wears the stone—with absolute patience, without malice, without noticing you at all.

Prepare accordingly.

## The Sound: Your First and Permanent Enemy

### What to Expect

The wind will be waiting for you before you arrive. If you approach on foot from the lower elevations, you will hear it perhaps an hour before you crest the rim—a low moan that you might mistake for distant machinery, for a waterfall, for tinnitus. Within thirty minutes of reaching the plateau proper, you will understand that it is none of these things. Within two hours, you will understand that it will never stop.

The howl varies. This is almost worse than if it were constant at a single pitch. Your brain cannot habituate to a sound that shifts—sometimes a roar, sometimes a shriek, sometimes that bass moan that vibrates your sternum and blurs your vision. The standing waves in certain locations will press against your chest like a hand. You will feel the sound before you parse it as sound, and this disjunction between the tactile and the auditory will be deeply unsettling for the first several days.

Your ears will ring after the first night. This is normal in the sense that it happens to everyone. It is not normal in the sense that it is acceptable. Damage is cumulative.

### Hearing Protection: The Paradox

You need ear protection. This is non-negotiable. You also need to hear.

The scattered people who live on the plateau have solved this through generational adaptation—narrowed canals, thickened drums. You do not have two billion years to evolve. You must compensate.

**Bring multiple forms of hearing protection, each for different circumstances:**

- **High-fidelity filtered earplugs** (such as those made for musicians) for general wear. These reduce volume by 15-25 decibels while preserving frequency range. You will still hear the wind. You will still hear each other. You will not go deaf as quickly. Wear them from the moment you crest the plateau rim. Do not remove them in the open. Do not convince yourself that "it seems quieter today." It is not quieter. Your ears are adjusting, and adjustment is the first step toward damage.

- **Over-ear protection** (rated NRR 25+) for sleeping and for passage through the worst wind channels. Combine with filtered plugs for maximum protection during sustained wind events. You will look absurd. You will not care how you look after the third night.

- **A supply of standard foam earplugs** as backup. They are cheap, light, and effective for short-duration exposure when you must remove your primary protection for communication. They muffle sound badly. This is sometimes preferable to the alternative.

**But here is the paradox, and you must hold it in mind at all times:**

The plateau is not silent, and you cannot afford to be deaf to it. The-sound-that-hunts has no external ears, but you do—or you did, before you protected them. You need to hear it approaching. You need to hear the shift in wind that precedes the rain. You need to hear the crack of stone that signals a new fissure, a draining dimple, a route change. You need to hear the hummocks thrumming, because that thrumming changes pitch when something moves through the vegetation.

**Protocol:** Use filtered plugs during all open traverse. Remove protection only inside wind-shelters, pressed against the hummocks, where the sound drops below damaging levels. Train yourself to read the plateau's signals through the filtered sound—through the shifts in the wind's voice, through the vibration in your feet. The scattered people have learned to hear with their hands against the stone. You can learn this too, partially, if you try.

**What will happen to you psychologically:**

The sound will wear you down in ways you do not expect. Not just hearing damage—not just the physical toll—but the erosion of thought itself. Constant noise degrades concentration, interrupts sleep, fragments attention. You will become stupid more quickly than you become deaf. Decision-making will suffer. Emotional regulation will suffer. After four or five days, you may find yourself crying without cause, or laughing at nothing, or standing still in the open while the wind strips the moisture from your eyes, simply because you have forgotten how to move.

This is the sound doing its work on you. Recognize it. Name it. Return to shelter. Rest with your filtered plugs and your over-ear protection layered together. The plateau is not going anywhere. You may need to stop.

## The Heat: From Below and Above

### What to Expect

The plateau is warm. Not hot in the way a desert is hot—no scorching 50°C afternoons, no air that shimmers and burns—but *persistently* warm, in a way that seeps through the soles of your boots and into your feet and never quite stops. The stone holds the sun's heat from above and receives volcanic warmth from below. Walking across the-body-that-remained is like walking on a creature that breathes slowly, steadily, radiating heat with each exhale.

Normal daylight will bake you. The stone throws reflected light and heat back at you from below as the sun strikes from above. You are sandwiched. The air temperature may read as 25-30°C—manageable, even pleasant on paper—but the effective temperature on your body will be higher due to radiant heat from the ground. You will dehydrate faster than your calculations predict.

Night brings cold air descending, but the stone stays warm. This differential produces the dawn condensation, which is your best water source. It also produces a strange dissonance in your body: your upper half cold, your feet warm, your brain confused about whether to constrict or dilate your blood vessels.

### Footwear

**This is critical.** Your feet will be in constant contact with warm stone for every hour you spend outside the wind-shelters. The-body-that-remained is smooth—wind-polished almost to glass in places—and warm, and unyielding. You need soles that insulate against heat while providing traction on mineralized near-glass.

- **Thick, multi-layer soles** with a thermal barrier. Mountaineering boots with insulated midsoles are a starting point. The stone will transmit heat through thin soles within minutes; by the end of the first day, your feet will feel like they're standing on a stovetop set to low.

- **Aggressive tread.** The stone is smooth. The stone is slippery when wet (which happens briefly, unpredictably, when the rain falls and before the stone drinks it). The stone is slippery when the dawn condensation coats it. You will fall. Minimize how often and how badly.

- **Bring two pairs.** One will degrade. The stone abrades everything slowly—everything except itself. Check your soles daily. Replace before failure, not after.

### Clothing

Layer for the temperature differential. You will be too hot in the sun, too cold in the shade and at night, and the wind chill of constant howling will compound the cold dramatically.

- **Light, breathable base layer** for the daytime heat. The wind will evaporate your sweat rapidly, which is both a blessing (cooling) and a curse (stealth dehydration—you may not feel yourself sweating because it dries so fast).

- **Insulating mid-layer** you can add and remove easily. The scattered people wear nothing you would recognize as clothing—woven vine-bark, loose, layered—but they have the advantage of adaptation. You do not. Bring fleece or wool. Avoid cotton; it absorbs the dawn condensation and holds it against your skin, which can lead to hypothermia when the cold air descends and the wind chill spikes.

- **Windproof outer shell.** Not water-resistant—*windproof*. The howl drives cold air through woven fabrics like they are gauze. You need a solid barrier. Test it before you leave: stand in a wind tunnel, or the closest equivalent you can find, and verify that you cannot feel air movement through the fabric at speed.

- **Cover your skin.** The wind carries dust, mineral fragments, and the fine particles of two billion years of eroded mountain. These will abrade any exposed skin over time—windburn that progresses to rawness that progresses to infection if untreated. A face covering is essential; the scattered people would not recognize why you need one, because their skin has adapted, but yours has not. Bring breathable fabric that covers your face, neck, and hands.

### Hydration

**You will not find reliable water.** This cannot be emphasized enough.

The rain comes and goes without pattern. The dimples pool water for hours or days—then drain without warning when a new fissure opens. The dawn condensation provides a film of moisture that the vegetation drinks before the sun burns it away. The scattered people have spent generations learning which dimples hold water and for how long, and even they must relocate when the water relocates.

**Your water strategy:**

- **Carry maximum capacity.** This is non-negotiable. The plateau is vast—hundreds of kilometers of stone between water sources. You cannot predict when you will next find drinkable water. Bring every container you can carry without compromising mobility.

- **Collect dawn condensation.** This is your most reliable water source, and it requires preparation. Lay out clean fabric or absorbent material before dawn—on the stone, on the hummocks, on any surface where moisture gathers. When the condensation forms, wring the fabric into your containers. The water will be warm, mineral-heavy, slightly sweet. It is safe to drink. It will not be enough on its own.

- **Collect rain whenever it falls.** You will have perhaps ten minutes. Be ready. The rain smells of iron—it is mineral-heavy, and you will taste the stone in it. This is normal. Drink it anyway.

- **Do not trust a dimple until you have watched it hold water for more than one cycle.** The plateau drains without warning. A dimple that was full yesterday may be empty today, the water vanished into a crack that was not there before.

- **Purification tablets or filter.** The water is generally safe—the mineral content is high but not harmful in the short term—but the dimples where water pools are also where the burrowing animals emerge, where the vegetation concentrates, where biological activity is highest. Purify when you can. Risk it when you must, but know you are risking it.

**Hydration discipline:** Drink before you are thirsty. The wind strips moisture from your body so efficiently that you may not feel yourself sweating; you may not recognize thirst until you are already dehydrated. Set a schedule. Drink at intervals regardless of perceived need.

## Navigation: The Lie of Flatness

### What to Expect

The plateau appears flat. It is not flat. It undulates in long swells and dimples across hundreds of kilometers—the ghost shapes of mountains that spent two billion years being ground down. From any single vantage point, you cannot perceive these undulations. You must walk for days to feel them.

This means: distances are deceptive. A hummock that appears to be an hour's walk away may take half a day to reach because the terrain rises and falls in ways your eyes cannot parse. A dimple that holds water may be closer or farther than it appears. The stone sheen reflects and refracts light in ways that distort perception—heat shimmer, mineral glare, the strange visual noise of a surface polished to near-glass by eons of wind.

You will get lost if you navigate by sight alone.

### How to Navigate

- **The cairns.** The scattered people maintain cairns between water-dimples—stones stacked waist-high, slightly lighter in color than the surrounding stone. They are your lifeline. They require constant maintenance (the wind takes stones from them regularly), and they are sometimes difficult to distinguish from the surrounding rock, but they are the only human-made landmarks on the plateau. Learn to spot them. Follow them. Do not leave sight of one without confirming the location of the next.

- **The hummocks.** The vegetation masses are visible from greater distances than the cairns—they are dark against the light stone, and they are large. But the hummocks move. A hummock you used as a landmark yesterday may be centimeters further today, and over weeks, that drift compounds. Do not treat hummocks as fixed waypoints. Treat them as approximate guides.

- **A compass.** The plateau's volcanic underlayer may produce magnetic anomalies—test your compass before relying on it, and note any inconsistencies. If it works, use it. If it doesn't, you must rely on the cairns and the sun.

- **Celestial navigation.** The sky on the plateau is enormous—horizon to horizon, flat, with thin clouds scudding at speeds that will make you dizzy. The stars are clear at night. Learn the sky before you arrive. Know the pole. The wind may obscure your sense of direction by sheer sensory overwhelm; the stars will not lie to you.

- **Mark your own path.** Carry something you can use to leave markers—bright fabric, reflective material, anything that catches the eye against the stone. The plateau is the color of old bone and mineral glass; high-visibility markers will stand out. Leave them at intervals you can see between. Collect them on your return if possible; the scattered people will not disturb them, but the wind will.

- **Travel in the dimples' direction.** The plateau's movement tends toward the water. The cairns mark the paths between dimples. The hummocks drift toward water. The-sound-that-hunts follows the hummocks. The people follow the water. Everything that lives here navigates toward the water, and so should you. If you are lost, find a dimple. You may not be the only one there.

## The Vegetation: Your Shelter and Your Warning

### What to Expect

The hummocks are the only substantial life on the plateau—mounded masses of something between fern and vine, four meters high, thirty across, each a single organism that has been growing and dying back for perhaps thousands of years. They cluster in the lee of rises, in the dimples, in every fracture where the stone has cracked wide enough for roots. They are the only shade. The only wind-break. The only source of material for tools and shelter. The only place where the sound drops below damaging levels.

They are also alive in ways that will unsettle you. Their leaves—dark, waxy, hand-shaped, with too many fingers—clap and flutter in the wind, adding a percussive thrumming to the howl. This thrumming vibrates in your sternum. You will feel it before you hear it. This is normal. This is also useful.

The hummocks migrate. Millimeters per year—slowly, imperceptibly, but consistently, seeking water, retreating from the worst wind channels. You may not notice this movement. You will notice its effects: a hummock that was sheltering a wind-shelter may drift away over the course of weeks or months, exposing the structure to the full force of the wind. Check your shelters. Check the hummocks' positions relative to your camps.

### How to Use the Hummocks

- **Shelter in their lee.** The hummocks break the wind. In the space between a hummock and the stone, the howl drops to a roar, then a moan, then—rarely, blessedly—something almost like silence. This is where the scattered people build their wind-shelters. This is where you will sleep, eat, communicate, rest.

- **Do not damage them.** The hummocks are the only reason anything survives on the plateau. They hold the soil (thin, precious, accumulating in their root-systems over centuries). They shelter the burrowing animals. They provide material for the scattered people's tools and shelters. They moderate the wind around the dimples where water pools. They are the closest thing the plateau has to an ecosystem, and they are irreplaceable on any timescale you can comprehend.

- Take only what has already died—dead stems, fallen leaves, fibrous inner bark from fallen sections. The scattered people harvest this way. Learn from them.

- **Listen to the thrumming.** The pitch changes when something moves through the hummock. The-sound-that-hunts moves between hummocks at speed; the vegetation will announce its passage before you see it. The burrowers emerge from the roots at specific moments; the hummock's vibration changes when they are active. Learn the baseline thrum, and you will know when something has disturbed it.

- **Beware the cycles.** The hummocks grow and die back on schedules that do not correspond to any season you understand. A lush hummock may become skeletal in two weeks. A skeletal hummock may burst back into leaf just as quickly. Do not rely on a hummock for shelter material or shade without checking its current state. A dead hummock provides no wind-break. A dormant hummock may provide less than you expect.

## The Predator: The-Sound-That-Hunts

### What to Expect

You may never see it. You will see its tracks.

The-sound-that-hunts is a lean, low, four-legged predator that runs the open stone between hummocks at impossible speeds. It has no external ears. Its eyes are recessed beneath bony ridges. It runs straight into the wind—always into the wind, never curving—and no one has ever seen one sleep.

It feeds on the burrowers when it can catch them leaving their holes. It is not large—perhaps the size of a lean wolf—but it is fast, and it is adapted to the plateau in ways you are not. It does not care about you. It may not even perceive you as relevant. But it runs straight lines across the stone, and you may be standing on that line, and it will not curve around you.

### How to Survive It

- **Get off its line.** If you see it running—or if you hear the change in the hummocks' thrumming that signals rapid movement across the stone—move perpendicular to its path. It does not curve. It does not chase. It runs. If you are not on its line, it will pass you.

- **Do not run from it in a straight line.** You cannot outpace it on open stone. Your legs are not built for this terrain; its legs are. If it is approaching on your line, move sideways. Get to a hummock. The vegetation will slow it—or at least, the scattered people believe it avoids the dense growth. This may not be true. But the hummock provides cover, and the open stone does not.

- **Do not provoke it.** Do not attempt to study it, photograph it at close range, or block its path. The scattered people coexist with it by treating it as a feature of the landscape—like a wind channel, like a fissure, like the stone itself. It is. It does what it does. Move accordingly.

- **Watch for tracks.** Claw marks in the thin soil of the dimples—deep, deliberate, straight lines. If you see tracks, you know the-sound-that-hunts traverses this area. Plan your routes to avoid its running lines if possible. Sleep in hummocks, not on open stone.

- **It is not your main danger.** Understand this. The predator is dramatic and frightening, but the plateau itself—the wind, the dehydration, the disorientation, the slow attrition of your body and mind—is what will hurt you. Fear of the-sound-that-hunts should not dominate your preparation. Respect it. Account for it. Do not fixate on it.

## The Scattered People: How to Exist Among Them

### Who They Are

They are few. They live at the intersection of three dimples, where the water stays longest and the hummocks grow largest and the wind is merely very loud. They have been here for generations—long enough for their ear anatomy to change, long enough for their language to become primarily signed, long enough for their bodies to adapt to the heat and the wind and the stone.

They have no metal. They have no textiles beyond woven vine-bark. Their tools are hand-axes of flaked obsidian. Their structures are stone circles and wind-shelters that they rebuild continuously because the wind takes ten percent of what they build every year.

They are not primitive. They are optimized for an environment that would destroy you. The distinction matters.

### How to Interact

- **Learn to sign before you arrive.** The scattered people will not speak aloud to you—not because they cannot, but because speech on the plateau is a private act, conducted in the shelter of hummocks, with mouths close to ears. Their primary language is signed. If you cannot sign, you will be functionally mute for much of the day. Bring a communication system—written, if nothing else—but understand that writing requires wind protection, still hands, and surfaces that the stone does not provide easily.

- **Respect their taboos.** They do not speak of what the stone says below the wind. When asked about the underground hum, they touch their throats and shake their heads and make a gesture that may mean *we do not speak of what the stone says* or *we cannot* or *we are not the ones who listen*. Do not press them on this. Do not record the hum in their presence if you have recording equipment. Do not ask them to interpret it. This is their plateau, their adaptation, their survival. What they choose not to speak of is not your mystery to solve.

- **Offer what you can.** You are a traveler. You have access to tools, materials, and knowledge that they do not. Offer what you can without condescension. A good blade. A length of cord. A container that holds water without leaking. These are valuable here. But do not offer technology that requires maintenance they cannot provide, fuel they cannot obtain, or replacement parts they cannot fabricate. The plateau strips everything to its essence. Give them what works at that essence.

- **Do not stay too long among them.** They are adapted to this place. You are not. Their kindness in sheltering you may expose them to risk—their food and water are calculated for their numbers; your presence is a drain on resources that take centuries to accumulate. Accept their hospitality. Reciprocate. Move on when you can.

- **Learn their water-knowledge.** The scattered people know which dimples hold water and for how long. They know the signs that a dimple is about to drain. They know where the hummocks are drifting and why. This knowledge is survival, distilled over generations, and it is more valuable than anything in your pack. Ask to learn. Listen when they teach. Pay attention.

## The Stone: What You Walk On

### The-Body-That-Remained

The stone is not rock anymore. Two billion years of wind erosion have polished it into something denser, harder, more mineralized—a near-glass that catches the sun and throws it back in fractured planes. It is warm. It is smooth. It is unyielding in ways that regular stone is not.

- **You cannot break it.** Do not plan to. Your tools will not shape it. The scattered people build with it—they fit stones together without mortar, using pieces that have already fractured along natural lines—but they do not cut it. They find what the wind has already broken and use what they find. You will do the same.

- **It cuts.** Despite its smoothness, the-body-that-remained fractures along planes that can be razor-sharp. The edges where the stone has cracked are dangerous. Watch your hands. Watch your footing when crossing a fissure. A fall onto a fresh fracture edge will open you to the bone.

- **It holds heat.** The stone stays warm through the night. This is useful for sleeping—if you shelter against it, you will be warmed from below. It is dangerous during the day—if you rest against it in direct sun, you will overheat. The scattered people sleep with their backs against the stone and their faces to the open air, absorbing the warmth while the wind cools their upper bodies. Learn this. It works.

- **It shifts.** Thermal cycling—warm days, cold nights—causes the stone to expand and contract. Cracks open and close. New fissures appear. Dimples drain. The ground beneath you is not fixed. What was solid yesterday may be fractured tomorrow. Listen for the sound of cracking—like a shot, like a branch breaking—and be aware that the terrain is changing around you constantly, even if you cannot see it.

## Psychological Preparation: What the Plateau Does to the Mind

### The Erosion of Self

You are going to a place that has been eroding mountains for two billion years. You are soft. You are water and carbon and delicate infrastructure of bone. The plateau will erode you too—not physically, not at first, but mentally, in the way that constant stimulus erodes all defense.

**The sound will be the first assault.** You cannot think clearly in constant noise. Your inner monologue will fragment. You will lose the thread of thoughts mid-sentence. You will find yourself standing still, staring at nothing, because the effort of maintaining attention is exhausting. This is normal. This is the plateau doing what it does. Recognize it, and retreat to shelter when it becomes severe.

**The emptiness will be the second.** The plateau is devastatingly empty across most of its extent—just stone and wind and warmth and the howl. You may walk for days without seeing a hummock, without seeing a cairn, without seeing anything that moves except the clouds and the dust. This emptiness is not peaceful. It is the emptiness of a place that has already been worn down to its minimum, that has nothing left to offer and nothing left to hide. It will make you feel small. It will make you feel irrelevant. It will make you feel like the plateau is correct—that you are temporary, that everything you have ever cared about is temporary, that only the stone endures, and the stone is becoming less with every century.

**This is the plateau's philosophy, and it is contagious.** Guard against it. Remind yourself why you came. Carry something that connects you to elsewhere—a photograph, a letter, a small object that has no function except meaning. Touch it when the emptiness gets too large. The plateau is very old and very patient, and it is very good at convincing visitors that nothing matters. Do not believe it. You are not a mountain. You do not need to endure for two billion years. You need to endure for the length of your visit.

**The movement will be the third erosion.** Everything on the plateau is moving at a speed just below your perception. The hummocks drift. The dimples shift. The cairns degrade. The green pockets breathe in and out. When you stay long enough—weeks, perhaps—you will begin to feel it: the sense that the ground is not fixed, that nothing is fixed, that stability is an illusion produced by the limits of human attention. This is not a delusion. This is accurate perception of the plateau's nature. But it is destabilizing. You may feel as if you cannot trust anything to remain where you left it—because you cannot.

**Protocol for psychological stability:**

- **Maintain routine.** The plateau has no seasons, no regular cycles you can perceive. Impose your own. Wake at the same time. Eat at the same time. Check your equipment at the same time. A routine is a structure you build inside yourself, and it will hold when the external structures fail.

- **Keep a journal.** Write in it every day, even if what you write is only "the wind continues, the stone is warm, I am here." Writing forces linear thought. It resists the fragmentation that the sound produces. It gives you a record of your own continuity.

- **Talk to your companions.** If you are traveling alone, talk to yourself. Not aloud—the wind will take it—but in writing, in sign, in the shelter of the hummocks. Language is a tool for maintaining selfhood. Use it.

- **Sleep when you can.** The sound makes sleep difficult. The temperature differential makes it uncomfortable. The psychological weight makes it elusive. Sleep anyway. Use your hearing protection. Position yourself against the warm stone, out of the direct wind, in the lee of a hummock. Rest is not optional; it is maintenance, and you need more of it here than you do elsewhere.

- **Set a departure date.** The plateau has no seasons, no visible change, no sense of progress. You could stay forever and never feel that you have reached a natural ending point. Know when you are leaving. Count the days. The scattered people live here; you are visiting. Do not confuse the two.

## What to Pack: The Complete List

### Essential

- High-fidelity filtered earplugs (multiple pairs; they will degrade)
- Over-ear hearing protection (NRR 25+)
- Foam earplugs (backup supply, minimum 30 pairs)
- Insulated mountaineering boots with aggressive tread (2 pairs)
- Breathable base layers (moisture-wicking, not cotton)
- Insulating mid-layers (fleece or wool)
- Windproof outer shell (solid barrier, not woven)
- Face and neck covering (breathable, windproof)
- Hand protection (thin enough for dexterity, thick enough for abrasion)
- Maximum-capacity water containers
- Dawn condensation collection fabric (large, absorbent, clean)
- Water purification tablets or filter
- Compass (tested for magnetic anomalies)
- High-visibility route markers (bright fabric, reflective material)
- Obsidian hand-axes or equivalent cutting tools (the scattered people's technology works here; much of yours will not)
- Cord or rope (useful for everything the plateau tries to take apart)
- First aid supplies focused on: abrasion treatment, burn treatment, dehydration treatment, hearing damage assessment, laceration repair
- Sun protection (the plateau reflects light from below as well as above; you will be irradiated from both directions)
- A small object of personal meaning
- Journal and writing implement (wind-proof if possible; pencil may work better than pen)
- Sign language reference if you are not fluent

### Recommended

- Wide-brimmed hat (shade from above while allowing peripheral vision)
- Goggles (the wind carries fine mineral dust that will abrade your eyes)
- Trekking poles (the stone is smooth; falls are likely; your knees will thank you)
- Thermal ground pad (for sleeping; the stone is warm but unyielding, and the pressure points will bruise)
- Backup navigation system (celestial navigation reference, or a device that does not rely on infrastructure the plateau lacks)
- Emergency signal (the plateau is vast and empty; if you are lost and injured, you need a way to be found—something loud, something bright, something the wind cannot simply swallow)
- Gifts for the scattered people (tools, containers, cord—nothing that requires fuel or replacement parts they cannot source)

### Do Not Bring

- Anything that requires electricity (there is no grid, and solar panels will be abraded by wind-carried dust within weeks)
- Anything that relies on precise moving parts (the dust will infiltrate; the wind will stress; the plateau will break it)
- Anything you cannot carry for days across stone (weight is a cost; everything you bring must justify its weight in survival value)
- Expectations of comfort (you will be warm, you will be dry sometimes, you will hear silence rarely—this is as good as it gets)
- The belief that you can conquer this place (you cannot; you can only pass through it, and only if the plateau permits)

## Final Preparation: What to Tell Yourself Before You Step Onto the Stone

You are about to enter a place that was here before your species existed, before most of your planet's current geography existed, before the mountains that used to stand here were anything but potential. The plateau has been becoming itself for two billion years. It is nearly finished. It is not finished.

You are not important to this process. Your presence will not affect it. Your absence will not affect it. You are a moment of warmth and water walking across the-body-that-remained, and in a thousand years—which is nothing, which is a breath—there will be no trace that you were ever here.

This is not a reason to avoid the plateau. This is a reason to visit it with clear eyes.

Go. Stand on the stone. Feel the warmth from below and the wind from everywhere and the sound that has been howling since before the mountains had names. Listen to the hummocks thrumming. Watch the clouds scud. Find a dimple that holds water and drink what the stone gives you. Shelter with the scattered people and sign your gratitude and learn what they will teach.

Then leave.

The plateau will endure, which is the only thing it has ever done, which is the only thing it knows how to do. You will endure long enough to carry the howl inside you for the rest of your life—a sound in the back of your skull that never quite stops, that rises sometimes when the wind blows hard, that reminds you what patience looks like when it has had two billion years to practice.

You will not forget the plateau. This is the only mark it will leave on you, and you will carry it willingly.
