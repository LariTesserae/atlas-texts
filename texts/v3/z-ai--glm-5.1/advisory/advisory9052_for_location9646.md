# Preparing for The Stratum

## A Practical and Psychological Guide for the Ordinary Multiverse Traveler

---

## I. What You Are Walking Into

Before we discuss gear, before we discuss strategies, you need to understand something fundamental: The Stratum is not a place you visit. It is a place that *processes* you.

You will arrive on a platform. You will look out at the infinite industrial horizon of pipe and plate and girder, and you will feel small, and then you will feel *irrelevant*, and then—if you are lucky—you will feel something like awe. If you are not lucky, you will feel the first threads of something worse. The Stratum does not care about you. It does not notice you. It is building, always building, and you are simply material that happens to be moving through it.

This is important. This is the most important thing I can tell you. The creatures that live here—the skittering things, the perching things, the things that might be pipes and the pipes that might be things—they are not afraid of you because you are not a variable in their process. You are background noise. You are a condensation droplet that moves differently than expected. You are tolerated because you are too small and too brief to be worth removing.

You must make peace with this before you arrive. You must make peace with the fact that you are walking through something alive in a way that does not include you.

## II. The Body: Physical Preparations

### Breathing

The Stratum is high. You feel it immediately—that half-second delay between wanting air and having air, the slight effort at the top of each breath, the way your lungs reach and reach and only mostly arrive. If you have been to altitude before, you will recognize the sensation. If you have not, it will feel like the world is holding something back from you.

**Prepare by spending time at elevation before your jump.** Not a weekend—a week, minimum. Let your blood adjust. Let your red cell count rise. The Stratum will not give you time to acclimate; it simply *is* at this height, and your body must either keep up or fail slowly.

Bring supplemental oxygen. Not a tank—tanks are heavy, and the corridors of the Stratum will punish weight. Bring a concentrator, the kind mountaineers use, small and battery-operated. You will not need it constantly, but you will need it. Specifically: you will need it in the dense regions, where the passages narrow and the air seems thicker with moisture but thinner with oxygen, and you will need it when you exert yourself—which will happen more than you expect, because climbing through the Stratum's ladders and crawlways and shoulder-width passages requires effort, and effort at elevation is effort multiplied.

The air tastes like copper. This is not a problem initially. After six hours, it becomes a problem. After twelve, your mouth will feel like you've been sucking on old pennies. After twenty-four, you may develop nausea, headache, a metallic sense of wrongness that lives at the back of your throat. This is not poisoning—the copper levels are not that high—but it is *unpleasant*, and unpleasant degrades morale, and morale is the most fragile resource you have.

Bring a respirator. A simple one, with particulate filters and activated carbon. Wear it when the copper taste becomes oppressive. Take it off when you need to see clearly, because the respirator fogs, because the respirator narrows your vision, and in the Stratum, narrowed vision is danger.

### Water

Here is the nightmare: water is everywhere and none of it is for you.

Condensation beads on cold pipes. Mist drifts through corridors. Rain machines produce precipitation on forgotten schedules. Water pools around industrial units, runs through channels cut into flooring, collects in tanks that are always full or always empty depending on where you stand.

None of it is potable. None of it is *anything*. It cycles through the Stratum like a process without purpose, and it has been cycling through industrial substrate and polymer and that wrong-concrete for cycles beyond counting, and it contains—well, you don't know what it contains. That's the problem. The specification plates on the rain machines are unreadable. The channels lead to tanks that may have been sealed for centuries or may have been opened yesterday. The water has touched materials you cannot identify, has leached chemicals you have no names for, has been processed by machines whose function is opaque.

**Do not drink the water.** Do not collect the water and filter the water and think the filter is enough. A standard backpacking filter removes bacteria and protozoa. It does not remove industrial solvents. It does not remove compounds that exist in no chemical database you have access to. It does not remove whatever-it-is that makes the water cycle through the Stratum without purpose, without origin, without end.

Bring your own water. Bring more than you think you need. Calculate your water needs for the duration of your stay, then double it. The Stratum dehydrates you—the cool air, the constant brightness, the exertion of movement—faster than normal environments. You will drink more than you expect. You will sweat more than you realize, because the air wicks moisture from your skin without the humidity to tell you it's happening.

**Water weight:** One liter weighs one kilogram. If you plan to stay three days and you need four liters per day (which you will, at this elevation, with this exertion), that is twelve kilograms of water. That is twenty-four kilograms if you double it. That is too much to carry.

Compromise: bring a water purification system designed for industrial contamination, not just biological contamination. Reverse osmosis, chemical treatment, activated carbon filtration in sequence. Test the Stratum water before you drink it, and test it with more than strips—bring a small assay kit if you can source one. The water *can* probably be made potable. Probably. But "probably" is not certain, and certain is what you need when the alternative is drinking something that has been cycled through alien industrial processes for longer than the concept of "longer" has been meaningful.

### Food

There is nothing to eat in the Stratum. This is simple. This is clean. The threadlike growths are not plants. The creatures are not food. You do not know what the growths draw from the built environment, and you do not know what the creatures have accumulated in their tissues, and you do not know what happens to a human body that ingests material from a place where the boundary between organic and constructed is a suggestion rather than a law.

Bring dense, calorie-rich food. Bars, pastes, the kind of thing mountaineers carry. You will not want to eat—the copper taste suppresses appetite, the strange duration distorts meal schedules, the quiet makes the act of eating feel strangely loud—but you must. Set alarms. Eat by schedule, not by hunger. Hunger is not reliable here.

### Light and Vision

The Stratum is bright. Severely bright. The light comes from everywhere—panels in the ceiling, lamps on articulated arms, phosphorescent scaling on pipes that hurts to think about. You cannot escape this light. You cannot find shadow except where things touch, and things touch *everywhere*, so shadow is thin and numerous and not shadow at all, just a sliver of slightly-less-bright between surfaces.

**Bring tinted lenses.** Not sunglasses—sunglasses are too dark, too distorting. Bring industrial safety glasses with adjustable tinting, the kind welders use when they need to see detail without full darkening. You need to see the specification plates. You need to see the creatures before they are next to you. You need to see the geometry of the corridors, which will try to deceive you. But you also need to protect your eyes from the constant assault of brightness, which will fatigue your vision faster than anything else. The orange afterimage of girder-work that you see when you close your eyes is not just annoying—it is a warning. Your eyes are being overworked. Protect them.

Bring a flashlight anyway. Yes, the Stratum is bright. Yes, the light is structural, inescapable, everywhere. But "everywhere" in the Stratum includes places where the density overwhelms even the engineers of light—passages where cable bundles drape so thick that the ceiling panels are obscured, chambers where the construction has built over the light sources and not bothered to install new ones, crawlways where you will press your body through gaps that the light cannot follow. The flashlight is for when the Stratum's light fails to reach somewhere that you must reach.

### Clothing

Cool, not cold. The temperature is not dangerous. But cool + exertion + rest = chill, and chill in a place with no warm shelters is a slow degradation.

Dress in layers. Base layer wicking, mid layer insulating, outer layer protective. The outer layer must be *tough*—the Stratum is sharp edges and rough welds and protruding industrial fittings, and it will tear soft fabric, and torn fabric means exposed skin, and exposed skin in a place where the boundary between organic and constructed blurs is a risk you should not take. Wear something synthetic, something that resists abrasion, something that will slide against the pipe-surfaces rather than catch.

Cover your skin. All of it. Long sleeves, long pants, gloves, closed-toe boots with grip—the plates are smooth, and condensation makes them slick, and the drainage channels are trip hazards. A hood or hat for when you are in the open chambers and the light bears down from thirty meters above. A collar you can raise against the mist that drifts through certain corridors.

The boots are critical. You will be climbing. You will be stepping over conduits and ducking under cable bundles and balancing on narrow walkways with drops on either side—drops that are architectural, that were made to be this high, that will not be forgiving. Boots with ankle support. Boots with grip. Boots you have broken in before you arrive, because blisters in the Stratum will not heal well, and every step you take will be on hard surfaces that do not compress, do not give, do not care about your feet.

## III. Navigation: The Central Problem

The Stratum shifts. Not constantly—this is not a maze that rearranges itself around you like some punitive myth—but *periodically*, and unpredictably, and in ways small enough that you might not notice until you try to retrace your steps and find that the door is on the wrong side now.

### The Build

The Build is the largest source of navigation failure. When it happens—when you feel that vast slow grinding in your sternum and hear the sound of materials being shaped and placed—sections of the Stratum change. New walls. New pipes. New chambers. New obstacles. New routes. What was a thoroughfare becomes a dead end. What was a wall becomes a doorway. What was a straight corridor now has a junction that was not there before.

You cannot predict the Build. You cannot locate its source. You can only accept that it will happen, and that after it happens, your mental map is wrong.

**Do not rely on a mental map.** The Stratum is too dense, too complex, too changeable. Your memory of the space will be overwritten by the space itself.

### What Works

**Physical markers.** Chalk, tape, tags—something you can leave on surfaces that will tell you where you have been. But know this: the Stratum builds over things. The Stratum fills in gaps. The fauna deposit material at junction points. Your markers *will* be obscured, eventually. They are temporary. Use them, but do not trust them as permanent.

**Directional tools.** A compass is useless—there is no magnetic north in a place with no geology, no planet, no orientation. But a gyroscope—specifically, an inertial navigation system—can track your movement through space regardless of the Stratum's geometry. The problem: inertial nav systems drift. The Stratum's strange duration may make them drift faster. They are approximate. Use them for macro-navigation (knowing roughly where you are relative to where you started), not micro-navigation (knowing exactly where a specific doorway is).

**Documentation.** Photograph everything. Video the corridors as you move through them. Record specification plates (which may change—note what they say, note when you noted it, note when it differs). The act of documentation will not prevent the Stratum from shifting, but it will give you evidence of the shift, and evidence of shift is evidence of reality, and evidence of reality is something you will need more than you expect.

**Sound breadcrumbs.** If you have the equipment, leave small transmitters at key junctions—devices that emit a tone you can detect from a distance. The Stratum is quiet. Sound carries. If you can hear your own beacon through the prepared hush, you can follow it back. But test this carefully: the Stratum's acoustics are strange. Chambers the size of town squares may echo or swallow sound. Shoulder-width passages may conduct it or deaden it. The behavior of sound in the Stratum is not the behavior of sound in open air.

### What to Do When You Are Lost

Because you will be lost. Accept this. Plan for this. Being lost in the Stratum is not failure—it is the default condition of existing in a space that does not hold still.

When you realize you are lost:

1. **Stop moving.** Sit down. Breathe. The panic that rises when you cannot find your way is the Stratum's most effective predator. It is not chasing you. It is waiting for you to exhaust yourself.

2. **Check your markers.** Look for chalk, tape, transmitters. If you find none, you are in unfamiliar territory. This is information, not catastrophe.

3. **Check your documentation.** Review photographs. Compare what you see to what you recorded. Note discrepancies. Discrepancies are how you know the Stratum has shifted.

4. **Orient by the Build.** After a Build pulse, the fauna become active. Watch them. They know what happened. They reorient. They claim new territory. They move with purpose toward something, and that something is likely a route or a resource. Follow them cautiously. Do not approach them—just watch their direction and consider whether it aligns with where you need to go.

5. **Move upward when possible.** The Stratum is a high place, built upward. The upper levels tend to be less dense, less claustrophobic, and more likely to contain those rare observation ports that can give you a landmark. You may not have external landmarks you can use, but *seeing* the edge—seeing where the construction stops and the sky begins—can help you understand the shape of where you are.

6. **Return to water.** The channels cut into the flooring lead to tanks. Tanks are large structures. Large structures tend to be in large chambers. Large chambers tend to be navigable and tend to have multiple exits. Following the drainage channels is not efficient, but it is *reliable*, and reliable is what you need when lost.

## IV. The Fauna: Protocol for Coexistence

The creatures of the Stratum are not afraid of you. This does not mean they are harmless. This means you have not yet triggered whatever would make them harm you.

**Do not touch them.** This is simple. This is non-negotiable. You do not know what they carry. You do not know what they secrete. You do not know what happens when a human hand contacts carapace that has been shaped by the Stratum's industrial processes for iterations beyond counting. Some of them grip cables that carry currents. Some of them may *be* cables that carry currents. The boundary is unclear. Respect the boundary by staying on your side of it.

**Do not impede them.** The fauna are part of the Stratum's construction process. They deposit material at junction points. They tend filament growths. They do the fine work that fills the gaps the big Build leaves behind. When you see a skittering thing carrying a fragment of material, let it pass. When you see a perching thing probing a seam for organic growth, give it space. You are not part of their process. Do not become an obstacle to their process. The Stratum removes obstacles. It builds around them, over them, through them. You do not want to be built over.

**Do not follow them into the dense regions.** The fauna thin out in the dense regions, but they are not absent—and the ones that remain are deeper into the blurring of organic and constructed. The pipes that pulse. The cable bundles that are warm and soft and flinch from pressure. These are not pipes. These are not cables. You do not know what they are. The fauna that move through these regions move through them with a proprietary ease that comes from *being of* the Stratum, and you are not of the Stratum, and the dense regions know it.

**Observe from distance.** The fauna are your best source of information about how the Stratum works. Watch them after a Build. Watch them during their small construction. Watch how they navigate, how they orient, how they respond to the condensation and the rain machines and the periodic brightening of certain phosphorescent surfaces. They know this place. They are this place. Learn from them without interfering with them.

## V. The Dense Regions: When Architecture Becomes Organic

You will know when you are entering a dense region. The passages narrow. The cable bundles thicken overhead until they resemble the circulatory system the description mentions. The threadlike growths cluster and multiply, pale and dense, making the walls look almost organic. The air is damper. The condensation is constant. And the geometry stops making sense.

### Geometry Failure

Corridors that intersect at angles summing to more than 360 degrees. Chambers whose far walls are closer than their near walls. Passages that go down and down and arrive where they began. Walls bearing the outlines of older walls. Doorways opening onto surfaces that were never meant to be exterior walls.

This is not hallucination. This is not your mind failing under the strain. This is the Stratum building through itself, over and over, without blueprint, without coordination, without regard for the topology that human navigation depends on.

**Your response:** Slow down. Move deliberately. Test surfaces before committing weight to them—some of those older walls outlined beneath newer surfaces may not be structurally sound, and a fall in the Stratum is a fall with no bottom except the next platform, which may be dozens of meters below. Use your hands. Brace against the walls (gloved hands always). Feel your way through the geometry rather than trying to *see* your way through it, because sight will deceive you here.

Accept that the space is not Euclidean. Accept that your instinct for how distances and angles work is wrong in this region. Move by feel, by incremental progress, by patient mapping of a space that will not hold still long enough to be mapped.

### The Growth

The threadlike growths in the dense regions are warmer. This is not a temperature you want to test with bare skin. They are drawing something from the built environment—chemical exudations, industrial runoff, processes happening in the walls themselves—and what they draw may not be compatible with your biology.

**Do not brush against them if you can avoid it.** This is difficult—they cluster on every surface, and the passages are narrow. Your protective clothing is your skin here. Check it after moving through heavy growth. Look for discoloration, deterioration, softening. The growths may be consuming materials in your clothing. They may be depositing materials on it. Either is a problem.

### The Breathing Walls

In the densest regions, the passages look like the inside of a throat. The walls seem to breathe. This is not your imagination—the growths are present in such density that the surfaces flex slightly with air movement, with the hum of distant engines, with the pulse of whatever circulates through those pipes that might not be pipes.

Move through these regions quickly but not urgently. Quickly because you do not want to linger where the organic and constructed blur. Not urgently because urgency leads to mistakes—touching things you shouldn't, stepping where you shouldn't, drawing attention from things that might notice you if you move like prey.

## VI. The Quiet: Psychological Preparation

The prepared quiet is the thing that breaks people.

Not the geometry. Not the creatures. Not the Build. The quiet. The engineered hush that is not absence but presence, the structural silence that surrounds you and holds you and does not break no matter what you do.

You are accustomed to ambience. You are accustomed to the background noise of living—air moving, insects humming, distant traffic, the thousand small sounds of a world that is not paying attention to you but is also not *silent*. The Stratum has none of this. The quiet is total and deliberate and it makes your own sounds—your footsteps, your breath, the rustle of your clothing—seem enormous, intrusive, *wrong*.

**After six hours,** the quiet becomes uncomfortable. You will find yourself making noise just to break it—humming, talking to yourself, tapping your fingers on pipe-surfaces. This is fine. This is coping. Let yourself do it.

**After twelve hours,** the quiet becomes oppressive. You will feel like you are being watched, because silence in your experience means something is hiding, something is waiting, something is being quiet *on purpose*. The Stratum is quiet on purpose. But it is not hiding from you. It is not waiting for you. It simply does not need sound, and so it does not make it, and your presence in its silence is your problem, not its.

**After twenty-four hours,** the quiet becomes internal. You will start to hear things that are not there—your brain, starved of input, will generate it. Distant voices. Footsteps behind you. The specific sound of someone you love saying your name. This is not the Stratum. This is your auditory cortex desperately trying to fill a void that was never meant to be filled. Recognize it for what it is. Do not follow the sounds. Do not call back to them.

**Bring audio.** A small player, earbuds, music or podcasts or audiobooks—something with human voices. Listen to it when the quiet becomes too much. But keep one ear free. You need to hear the Stratum when it makes sounds—the drip of condensation, the approach of fauna, the deep thrum of a Build pulse. The quiet is dangerous, but so is deafness to the sounds that *do* exist.

## VII. Duration and Time Distortion

Time moves strangely in the Stratum. Duration is unreliable. A corridor that takes ten minutes to traverse one way may take three minutes on the return, and when you try it again, you may lose count entirely.

**Wear a watch.** An analog watch, the kind with hands that move at a constant rate regardless of what your brain perceives. Check it frequently. When the watch says you have been walking for five minutes but you feel like it has been twenty, trust the watch. When the watch says an hour has passed but it feels like ten minutes, trust the watch. Your time-sense is being affected by the Stratum. The watch is not.

But also: **do not become dependent on the watch.** There may be regions where the distortion is strong enough that even mechanical timekeeping becomes unreliable—not because the watch is wrong, but because the relationship between the watch's time and the time you are experiencing has broken down in ways that neither the watch nor your brain can reconcile. If you find yourself in a region where the watch seems to be lying to you, leave the region. Backtrack. Find a space where time behaves, and rest there, and let your sense of duration recalibrate.

**Set hard limits on your stay.** Before you arrive, decide how long you will stay. Write it down. Set alarms on multiple devices. When the alarms tell you it is time to leave, leave. Do not extend your stay because you feel fine. Do not extend your stay because you have not seen enough. The Stratum's time distortion is insidious precisely because it feels *normal* while it is happening. You may not realize you have been there for three days when you planned for one. Your alarms and your written schedule are your lifeline to a reality where time is reliable.

## VIII. The Build: What to Do When the Stratum Moves

You will feel it before you hear it. A vibration in your sternum. A sense of vast slow motion, tectonic and patient. The sound of materials being shaped and placed and connected.

**When the Build happens:**

1. **Stop moving.** Find the nearest stable surface—a wall, a column, a thick pipe—and press yourself against it. The Build does not target you, but the Stratum is assembling itself, and things that are in the way of assembly may find themselves on the wrong side of a new wall, or beneath a new floor, or inside a new pipe. Being next to a stable surface reduces the chance that you will be in the path of construction.

2. **Cover your head.** The Build creates. It also *re*-creates—removing old structures to replace them with new, and the removal may involve falling debris, dislodged fittings, collapsing segments. Your protective clothing will help, but your head is vulnerable. Protect it.

3. **Wait.** The Build pulse takes minutes or hours. You cannot rush it. You cannot predict its duration. You can only wait, and breathe, and listen to the vast slow breathing of a structure making itself larger.

4. **After the Build, reorient.** Everything may have changed. Look around. Identify stable landmarks—large chambers tend to survive Builds, as do major structural columns and the largest pipe runs. The small things change: corridor angles, doorway positions, the specific configuration of cable bundles. The large things tend to remain. Find the large things. Navigate by them.

5. **Watch the fauna.** They know. They always know. Watch which directions they move, which new spaces they claim, which old spaces they abandon. They are reading the Stratum in a way you cannot. Follow their lead when your own navigation fails.

## IX. What to Bring: The Complete List

**Essential:**
- Water (as much as you can carry, plus purification system for Stratum water if necessary)
- Food (dense, calorie-rich, scheduled eating)
- Oxygen concentrator (small, battery-operated)
- Respirator with particulate and activated carbon filters
- Tinted safety glasses (adjustable tinting)
- Flashlight (bright, durable, spare batteries)
- Protective clothing (layers, abrasion-resistant, full coverage)
- Sturdy boots (ankle support, grip, broken-in)
- Analog watch
- Navigation markers (chalk, tape, small transmitters)
- Documentation equipment (camera, spare batteries, notebook)
- Gyroscopic/inertial navigation system (approximate but better than nothing)
- Audio player with earbuds (for the quiet)
- Multiple alarms (set to your planned departure schedule)

**Recommended:**
- First aid kit (focus on cuts, contusions, sprains—the Stratum is sharp and uneven)
- Emergency shelter (small tarp or bivy—the Stratum has no natural shelter, and you may need to rest in exposed spaces)
- Rope (10-15 meters, for climbing, for securing yourself during Builds, for pulling yourself through difficult passages)
- Chemical test strips (for testing Stratum water before purification)
- Signal mirror (for signaling in the rare case you encounter another traveler)
- Extra batteries (for everything—there is no power in the Stratum that you can use)

**Do Not Bring:**
- Weapons (the fauna are not your enemies, and the Stratum cannot be fought)
- Large electronics (no power sources, and the electromagnetic environment is unknown)
- Perishable food (no refrigeration, no cold storage)
- Anything you cannot afford to lose (the Stratum claims things—dropped items fall through gaps, left items are built over, forgotten items are consumed by the growths)

## X. What to Tell Yourself

You are not exploring the Stratum. The word "exploration" implies that you are charting something, that you are mapping something, that you are making it known. The Stratum cannot be made known. It is too dense, too changeable, too alive in ways that exceed your categories. You are *passing through*. You are witnessing. You are being processed by a place that does not notice you and will not remember you and will continue building long after you have left.

This is not despair. This is clarity. The Stratum does not owe you comprehension. It does not owe you a map that holds still. It does not owe you water you can drink or geometry that makes sense or a duration that respects your schedule. It is what it is—industrial, accretive, endlessly becoming—and your job is to move through it without breaking and without being broken.

The quiet will try to convince you that you are alone. The geometry will try to convince you that you are lost. The creatures will try to convince you that you are not real. The Build will try to convince you that nothing is stable. All of these things are true, from a certain perspective. But so is this: you are a human being with a body and a mind and a schedule and a way out, and the Stratum is a structure with processes and parameters and a patience that does not include malice.

It does not want to hurt you. It does not want to help you. It simply wants to build. Let it build. Move through the construction. Leave before the construction moves through you.

---

## XI. The Last Thing

Before you step onto that platform, before you feel the thin air and taste the copper and see the bright unrelenting light, ask yourself:

*Why am I going there?*

If the answer is curiosity, that is enough. Curiosity is a good reason to visit the Stratum. But know that the Stratum does not satisfy curiosity—it deepens it. Every question you answer will reveal three questions you didn't know to ask. Every corridor you map will reveal a junction you didn't document. Every creature you observe will reveal a behavior you cannot explain. The Stratum is not a puzzle with a solution. It is a process with no endpoint. If you go seeking answers, you will return with more questions than you started with, and they will be stranger questions, and they will not leave you alone.

If the answer is something else—something you need to prove, something you need to escape, something you need to find—then be honest with yourself about whether the Stratum is the place to find it. The Stratum gives nothing. It only builds. If you need something that builds, you are in the right place. If you need something else, you may want to keep looking.

---

*Go prepared. Go careful. Go with a schedule you will not extend.*

*The Stratum will still be there when you leave.*

*It will always still be there.*
