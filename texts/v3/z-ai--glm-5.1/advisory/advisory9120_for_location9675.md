# Preparing for the Plateau of Congealed Dark

---

## I. The Most Important Thing You Must Understand Before You Go

You will be blind. Not temporarily. Not adaptively. Functionally, fundamentally, and continuously blind for the entire duration of your stay. Every preparation you make must flow from this single irreducible fact.

Humans are not built for absolute darkness. We are not crepuscular opportunists or deep-cave dwellers by nature. Our entire cognitive architecture—our spatial reasoning, our balance, our threat assessment, our social signaling—assumes light. The Plateau will not provide any. It has never provided any. It is optimized for conditions that exclude you by definition.

You are not visiting a dark place. You are visiting a place where your primary sense is not merely useless but *structurally inappropriate*. You are a creature built to process photons entering a world where photons are not a feature of the environment. You would have a better evolutionary footing if you were visiting a planet with no oxygen—at least your body would fail quickly and honestly. Here, your body will keep functioning while your mind slowly, relentlessly misinterprets everything.

Prepare accordingly. Not by trying to solve the darkness—you will not solve it—but by learning to function within your failure.

## II. Sensory Recalibration: Training Before Arrival

### Learn Blind Navigation Now, Not Later

Begin training months before your jump. Not reading about it. Doing it.

Start with a blindfold in your own home. Wear it for an hour. Then two. Then a full waking day. You will discover, within the first thirty minutes, how much of your spatial awareness is visual. You will reach for doorframes that are three steps further than you estimated. You will knock cups off tables you thought were elsewhere. You will become disoriented in your own kitchen.

This is the point. You need to understand, in your body, not your intellect, how much you rely on sight. Because the Plateau will not give you a kitchen you've memorized. It will give you an alien topography that shifts with the growth and fall of vegetation, the flow of water, the movement of animals, and you will have no visual baseline to correct your errors.

Progress to unfamiliar environments. Have a trusted person guide you through a forest—blindfolded—for hours. Learn the grammar of ground beneath your feet: how saturated soil feels different from dry stone, how the acoustic signature of a space changes with distance from walls, how temperature gradients indicate openings and enclosures. Your feet can read the ground with remarkable precision if you let them. You have spent your life overriding their input with visual data. Stop.

Learn to echolocate. Humans can do this—not with the precision of bats or the plateau's airborne fauna, but functionally. Make sharp clicks with your tongue and listen to the return. Practice in rooms of different sizes, then outdoors, then in dense vegetation. You will not master this before you arrive, but even rudimentary echolocation will give you information that fumbling touch alone cannot—wall distances, openings, large obstacles, the difference between a solid surface and a thicket of stalks.

### Resculpt Your Hearing

The Plateau's soundscape is overwhelming and continuous. You will arrive with ears trained for a world where sound carries specific, discrete information—speech, footsteps, the approach of a vehicle—and where silence is the baseline from which meaning emerges. The Plateau has no silence. Its baseline is a roaring, layered, unending chorus of mechanical thrum, water flow, animal calls, dripping condensation, and the groan of infrastructure.

You must learn to parse rather than filter.

Your instinct will be to shut out the noise, to try to find the meaningful signal in the overwhelming static. This is wrong. The noise *is* the information. The mechanical thrum tells you where the pipes are. The water sound tells you the topography—pools, weeping walls, flowing channels. The animal calls tell you where the large many-legged creatures are moving, where the arthropod masses congregate, where the open spaces are that the flying creatures navigate. The rain-sound tells you what weather is approaching and from where.

Train by immersing yourself in complex soundscapes before arrival. Stand in industrial zones. Stand in rainforests. Stand in crowded markets where a dozen languages are spoken at once. Do not try to isolate individual sounds. Try to hold the whole field in your awareness simultaneously, like a conductor hearing every instrument in an orchestra at once. This is the skill that will keep you alive: not hearing *through* the noise but hearing *within* it.

Practice sound localization in environments with heavy reverberation. The Plateau's hard stone surfaces and dense vegetation create a soundscape rich in echoes. A call from fifty meters away may arrive from multiple directions as it bounces off cliff faces, pipe arrays, and stalk-thickets. You need to learn to identify the first arrival—the direct path—among the reverberant field. This takes practice. Start now.

### Harden Your Touch

Your hands will become your primary exploratory organ on the Plateau. They will tell you what the stalks feel like, whether a surface is stone or corroded metal or mineral deposit, whether water is flowing or standing, whether that warm shape you've encountered is a pipe or an animal.

Condition your hands for prolonged wet work. The constant moisture will soften your skin within hours. Within days, you will be at risk of maceration—tissue breakdown from continuous exposure to wetness. This is not merely uncomfortable; it is dangerous. Compromised skin means compromised touch, and compromised touch means compromised navigation.

Before arrival, spend time with your hands immersed in warm water for extended periods. Build calluses that resist maceration. Learn to distinguish textures with wet, wrinkled fingers—you will find that your tactile discrimination changes when your skin is waterlogged, and you need to understand those changes before your life depends on the information.

Learn to read temperature gradients with your skin. The Plateau has thermal variation—warm outflows near pumping stations, cooler pools in low areas, the body-heat of animals moving past you. Your skin can detect temperature differences as small as a degree. This is information. A sudden warmth ahead might mean a machine installation, which means infrastructure, which means possible shelter or resources. A sudden coolness might mean standing water deep enough to be hazardous.

## III. Psychological Preparation: The Mind in Endless Dark

### Understand What Will Happen to Your Visual Cortex

Your brain will not accept the darkness gracefully. It will try, desperately and continuously, to supply visual data where none exists. This is not imagination. This is not hallucination in the clinical sense. This is your visual cortex—the most metabolically expensive part of your brain, the one that consumes the largest share of your cognitive resources—refusing to shut down, insisting on processing, generating patterns and geometries and movements from the noise of your own neural activity.

You will see things. Shifting geometries. Forms that dissolve when you try to focus. Movements at the edge of a visual field that doesn't exist. The text calls them "interpretations, attempts to make sense of the input that isn't there." They are more persistent than that. They are your brain's operating system running idle, generating screen-saver patterns, refusing to acknowledge that the camera is capped.

This can be deeply disturbing if you are not prepared for it. People who have spent extended time in absolute darkness—cavers, researchers in sensory deprivation—report that these phantom visuals can become elaborate, convincing, and emotionally affecting. You may see landscapes. You may see figures. You may see architecture that doesn't exist. You must train yourself, in advance, to recognize these as neural artifacts. They are not the Plateau revealing itself to you. They are your own visual processing looping on itself.

Develop a mantra or a touch-anchor—a physical action you perform, like pressing your thumb to your forefinger—that you use to ground yourself when the phantom visuals become intense. "This is my brain. This is not the world. My hands are real. The ground is real. The dark is real." Repeat it. Believe it. Do not let the visuals become your reality.

### Prepare for the Absence of Temporal Markers

The Plateau has no day-night cycle. The machines run continuously. The animals do not sleep. The water flows without pause. There is no dawn to mark the start of a period, no dusk to signal its end.

You will lose your sense of time. Not gradually—quickly. Within the first few hours, your internal clock will begin to drift. Without light cues, your circadian rhythm will desynchronize. You will not know whether you have been walking for two hours or six. You will not know if you have been resting for minutes or hours. You will wake from sleep—if you can sleep—and have no sense of how long you were unconscious or what part of a cycle you are in.

This is psychologically corrosive. Humans are temporal creatures. We structure our experience around before-and-after, around the passage of measurable time, around the rhythm of activity and rest that light provides. Without that structure, experience becomes a formless mass. Anxiety increases. Cognitive performance degrades. Decision-making suffers.

You must bring your own temporal markers. A mechanical watch with a tactile bezel—you need to feel the time, not see it. Set alarms that vibrate. Establish a rigid schedule: walk for four hours, rest for one, eat, walk again. Adhere to it regardless of how you feel. Your schedule is your lifeline to temporal coherence. Without it, you will become untethered.

Practice this before arrival. Spend three days in a darkened room with no timekeeping devices. See how quickly your sense of duration distorts. Then do it again with a schedule and a tactile watch. The difference will be significant.

### Prepare for the Sound

The noise will not stop. Not when you eat. Not when you rest. Not when you are frightened. Not when you desperately want a moment of quiet to think.

The plateau's soundscape is an unending composition of mechanical thrum, water flow, animal vocalization, and the miscellaneous sounds of a dense, active ecology. It is loud enough to cause hearing damage over extended exposure. It is complex enough to prevent you from isolizing specific threats by sound alone. It is persistent enough to wear down your cognitive reserves through sheer sensory fatigue.

You must decide, before arrival, how you will manage this. Full ear protection would reduce the sound to manageable levels—but it would also remove your primary navigational and threat-detection sense. You will be trading one danger for another.

The best approach is selective attenuation. Bring high-fidelity earplugs that reduce volume without flattening the frequency spectrum—you want to hear the full range of sounds, just at a lower amplitude. Use them continuously. Remove them only when you need maximum acoustic sensitivity for navigation or threat assessment. Consider carrying a small directional microphone connected to headphones, allowing you to amplify specific directions while reducing the overwhelming ambient field. This is technology that exists. Acquire it. Learn to use it before you arrive.

Prepare yourself psychologically for the emotional weight of constant sound. It is not neutral. It is a presence. It fills the space that silence would occupy in a normal environment, and it does not leave room for the kind of internal quiet that many people need for emotional regulation. If you are someone who recharges in silence, the Plateau will be especially difficult. Develop alternative regulation strategies—breathing techniques, physical self-soothing through touch, internal visualization (carefully, given the phantom visual issue)—before you need them.

### Prepare for the Scale

The Plateau is continental. You will not cross it. You will not map it. You will not find its edges. You will experience a fraction of its expanse, and that fraction will feel infinite.

This is difficult for humans to process. We are built to comprehend environments at the scale of our movement—a day's walk in any direction defines a territory we can hold in our minds. The Plateau exceeds this scale by orders of magnitude. You cannot form a mental model of where you are in relation to the whole, because the whole is too large to model and you cannot see landmarks to orient yourself.

Accept this before you arrive. You will not explore the Plateau. You will exist within a small, touch-defined perimeter that moves with you. Your map will be the area you can reach in a day's walk from your current position, and it will be a tactile map, stored in your hands and feet and the memory of sounds, not a visual one. You will never have the bird's-eye view. You will never see the shape of the land.

If you are someone who needs to understand the big picture, who needs context and overview, this will be claustrophobic in a way that physical claustrophobia is not. It is the claustrophobia of perpetual localness, of never being able to rise above your immediate surroundings and see where you are. Find ways to make peace with this. Your experience will be local. That is enough. It has to be enough.

## IV. Physical Preparation: The Body in a Hostile Medium

### Respiratory Considerations

The air is thin. The plateau is high—elevated enough that breathing requires intention. Combined with the saturated moisture and the mineral-chemical load, every breath you take will be work.

If you have any respiratory conditions—asthma, compromised lung function, susceptibility to altitude sickness—reconsider whether this destination is advisable. The thin, wet, iron-and-chlorophyll-laden air will challenge even healthy lungs. At minimum, consult a physician about altitude acclimatization strategies before arrival.

Consider bringing supplemental oxygen. Not a full SCUBA system—you need your hands free and your mobility unencumbered—but a small canister with a nasal cannula for use when the thin air becomes overwhelming. You will not need it constantly, but you will need it. The moments when you are exerting yourself—climbing a ridge, pushing through dense stalk-thickets, scrambling over mineral deposits—are the moments when the thin air will fail you most acutely.

The air is also warm and wet enough that breathing feels like drinking. This means you are taking in significant moisture with every breath, which sounds like it might help with hydration but actually complicates things. Your lungs are not designed to absorb water vapor at this concentration, and the mineral content—iron, calcium, sulfur compounds, the acrid component that might be coal smoke or plant exhalation—means you are inhaling a chemical soup with every cycle. Over time, this will irritate your respiratory tract.

Bring a respirator mask designed for industrial environments—something that filters particulates and chemical contaminants while still allowing you to breathe in thin, humid conditions. Test it extensively before arrival. Make sure you can echolocate while wearing it. Make sure it doesn't fog or clog in high-humidity environments. Your respiration is already compromised by altitude; do not further compromise it with inhaled contaminants.

### Thermal Management

The Plateau is warm. The water is body-temperature. The air is warm and wet. The animals are furnace-hot. The machines generate heat. The vegetation may generate heat through its metabolic processes.

You will not be cold. You will be the opposite of cold. You will be constantly, oppressively warm and wet, with condensation forming on your skin as fast as you can wipe it away, with your clothes saturated from the atmosphere within minutes of stepping onto the plateau.

This is dangerous. Humans cool themselves through evaporation of sweat. When the ambient humidity approaches saturation, sweat cannot evaporate. Your core temperature will rise. You will be at constant risk of heat exhaustion and heat stroke, and you will not have the visual warning signs you might rely on in a normal environment—you will not see the flush of your own skin, the pallor of your companions, the darkening of your urine.

Wear minimal, moisture-wicking clothing. Cover your skin not for warmth but for protection—from the adhesive nets, the mildly irritating filamentous growths, the arthropod masses, the rough surfaces you will be navigating by touch. But make it as light and breathable as possible. Consider fabrics designed for tropical environments, for deep jungle operations, for the kind of conditions where heat dissipation is the primary concern.

Monitor your hydration obsessively. You will be losing water to sweat faster than you can perceive in the saturated environment. Drink on a schedule, not when you feel thirsty. By the time you feel thirsty, you are already dehydrated, and dehydration in a hot, high-altitude environment is a cascade failure that leads to confusion, poor decision-making, and collapse.

Bring electrolyte supplements. You will be sweating out salts at a tremendous rate, and the water you collect on the plateau—mineral-heavy, warm, flowing through rusted pipes and over calcite deposits—will not provide the balance you need. Pack more than you think you need. Pack triple.

### Skin and Extremity Protection

Your skin will be under constant assault.

The moisture alone would be enough to cause problems—maceration, fungal infections, breakdown of the skin barrier. But add to this the adhesive nets that cling to your face and hands, leaving a tingling irritant. The rough, wet stone you are constantly touching for navigation. The mineral deposits with their ridged, folded surfaces. The occasional contact with animals—warm, damp, indifferent. The arthropod masses that you may accidentally step into or brush against.

Cover as much skin as practical without causing overheating. Light gloves with tactile sensitivity—perhaps fingerless with reinforced palms—will protect your hands while allowing you to feel what you're touching. A head covering or hood will keep the nets and the condensation off your face and out of your hair. Footwear must be water-permeable enough to drain while providing grip on wet stone—standard hiking boots will become waterlogged and useless within hours; consider canyoneering shoes or specialized water shoes with aggressive tread.

Bring antifungal treatments. Bring them in quantities that assume you will need them constantly. Bring skin barriers—barrier creams, zinc oxide, whatever has worked for you in wet conditions before. Apply them preventively, not reactively. Once a rash or infection establishes itself in the saturated environment, it will be very difficult to manage.

Pay special attention to your feet. They will be wet constantly. They will be your primary interface with the terrain. Blisters, maceration, fungal growth, and trench foot are not minor discomforts here—they are navigation-ending, mobility-ending failures that can strand you in a place where being stranded is fatal. Stop and attend to foot care at regular intervals, more frequently than you think necessary. Dry your feet as well as the environment allows during rest periods. Change socks if you have them. If you don't have dry socks, wring out what you're wearing and put them back on—it's better than leaving them waterlogged.

### The Electrical Hazard

There is electricity in the wet air near the larger installations. You can feel it as a tingle. The text mentions it almost in passing, but this is not a minor detail.

You are a conductive organism—water-filled, mineral-laden, standing on saturated ground in a saturated atmosphere, moving through an environment where electrical current is present in the very air you breathe. The larger installations—refineries, processors, the warehouse-sized structures with their hissing steam and groaning parts—may carry enough current in their vicinity to pose an electrocution risk, especially when you are wet, especially when you are touching metal, especially when you are standing in pools of water.

Approach the large installations with extreme caution. Test surfaces before touching them with your full hand—use the back of your knuckle, which will cause a reflexive withdrawal if current is present. Do not lean against pipes near the installations. Do not step into standing water near the installations without probing the ground with a non-conductive implement first.

Bring a voltage tester—a non-contact one that you can feel vibrate or hear click. Test the air, the water, the surfaces, before you commit to contact. Your life may depend on this.

## V. Equipment: What to Bring and How to Carry It

### The Light Question

Should you bring a light source?

The question is more complicated than it appears. The Plateau has never known light. Its ecology is built for darkness. Its animals navigate by sound and echo and senses you do not share. Its vegetation has no relationship with photons. Its machines operate without visual indicators.

A light source would give you an enormous advantage—the ability to see, even partially, even locally, even temporarily. It would transform your experience from a desperate fumble to something approaching normal navigation. It would allow you to map, to document, to move with confidence.

It would also make you an anomaly of staggering significance.

Think about what light would mean in this environment. A flashlight beam in a world of total darkness would be visible—not just to you, but to everything within range. The animals, which navigate by senses other than sight, might not respond visually, but they would respond to the disruption. The flying creatures, echolocating through the dark, might be disoriented or attracted by the change. The ecosystem, calibrated for darkness, might react in ways you cannot predict.

And the machines—operating in darkness, designed for darkness, their functions unspecified and their origins unknown—might have sensors you cannot anticipate. Light might be irrelevant to them. Light might be an alarm. Light might be a trigger.

My recommendation: bring light. Bring multiple sources. But use them sparingly, thoughtfully, and only when the alternative is danger. A momentary flash to read your instruments. A brief beam to assess a hazard. Not a constant lamp, not a sweeping searchlight, not a sustained illumination that announces your presence to an ecology that has never seen such a thing.

Use red light if possible—it disrupts night vision less and may be less noticeable to organisms not adapted to any light at all. Use the dimmest setting that serves your purpose. Use it for seconds, not minutes.

And be prepared for the emotional impact of seeing, even briefly, a fraction of the Plateau. After hours or days in absolute darkness, even a small pool of light will be overwhelming. The vegetation—rubbery, wet, not green, dense beyond your imagining. The water—black mirrors, weeping walls, mineral deposits in orange and white that your fingers have been mapping blindly. The machines—corroded, colonized, still functioning. The animals—broad, low, many-legged, warm, moving past you with indifference. You will see fragments of a world that your mind has been constructing from touch and sound alone, and the fragments will not match your construction. This will be disorienting. Be ready for it.

### Navigation Tools

A compass may or may not function on the Plateau—you do not know the magnetic conditions of this world. Do not rely on it as a primary tool.

Instead, bring an inertial navigation unit—a small device that tracks your movement through accelerometers and gyroscopes and calculates your position relative to a starting point. These exist for cave exploration, where GPS is unavailable and visual landmarks are absent. Learn to use one before arrival. Understand its drift characteristics. Know how often you need to recalibrate.

Bring a tactile mapping system. This can be as simple as a board with pins and elastic bands that you use to create a raised-line map as you explore, or as sophisticated as a 3D-printing pen that creates raised topographic lines on a flat surface. You need to externalize your mental map, because your mental map will be wrong—constantly, insidiously wrong—as the disorientation of the darkness and the similarity of the terrain cause you to miscalculate distances and directions.

Mark your path. Bring physical markers—biodegradable ones, if you have environmental concerns—that you can feel and that create a distinct sound when touched. Place them at regular intervals. Use them to create a trail you can follow back. The Plateau is vast, and without a trail, you will not retrace your steps. You will become lost within hours, and lost on the Plateau means walking in circles until your supplies run out.

### Communication

If you are traveling with others—and you should be traveling with others, for reasons I will elaborate—establish communication protocols before arrival.

Sound is the only viable channel. But the Plateau's soundscape is already overwhelming, and your voice will be competing with mechanical thrum, water roar, and animal calls. You need a system that cuts through.

Short, sharp whistle blasts with pre-agreed meanings: one blast for stop, two for attention, three for danger, a long sustained blast for rally. Whistles carry further than voices and occupy frequency ranges that are easier to distinguish from the ambient noise. Carry multiple whistles—on a lanyard around your neck, attached to your wrist, clipped to your belt. You will not be able to find a whistle you've set down in the dark.

Consider learning a simplified tactile sign language—a system of touches on a companion's arm or back that conveys basic information without sound. This is for close-range communication when the noise makes whistling impractical or when silence is strategically preferable.

If you are traveling alone: do not travel alone. But if you must, establish a check-in protocol with your point of origin—your multiverse transit hub, your base camp, whatever structure supports your travel. Set regular intervals. Miss two check-ins and assume rescue will be attempted, though what rescue means in a place where searchers cannot see is a grim question.

### Tools and Supplies

Bring a machete or similar cutting tool. The stalk-thickets are dense enough to impede progress, and you will need to cut your way through in places. The vegetation is rubbery and resilient—not woody, but tough—and a blade will save you enormous energy compared to pushing through by body weight alone. Make sure the blade has a textured, non-slip grip that you can hold with wet hands. Make sure you can feel the blade's orientation without looking—consider an asymmetric grip that tells your hand which way the edge is facing.

Bring rope. Light, strong, waterproof rope. Use it to mark paths, to lower yourself down ridges, to create hand-lines between points you need to traverse repeatedly. The wet stone is slippery, and a fall in absolute darkness is a fall into the unknown—you will not see where you are going, you will not see what you are falling toward, and you will not see how far down it is.

Bring water containers that can be filled from the Plateau's flows—and bring filtration and purification systems. The water is mineral-heavy and may contain biological contaminants from the dense ecology. Do not drink it untreated. The mineral content alone—iron, calcium, sulfur—will cause gastrointestinal distress if consumed in quantity. Filter it. Purify it. Consider bringing concentration tablets that can supplement the mineral-poor water you brought from elsewhere with the electrolytes you need, while diluting the Plateau's mineral load to tolerable levels.

Bring food that does not require heating, does not spoil in warm wet conditions, and provides dense caloric content. You will be expending enormous energy—navigating by touch, fighting through vegetation, managing the psychological stress of constant darkness, and thermoregulating in a hot, humid environment. You need more calories than you think. Plan for it.

Bring multiple redundant light sources, as discussed. Bring spare batteries for everything—more than you think you need, stored in waterproof containers.

Bring a first-aid kit designed for tropical conditions—antifungals, antibiotics, wound closure strips, burn treatments (for contact with hot pipes or electrical surfaces), and electrolyte replacements. Include a tourniquet, because the risk of laceration—from cutting vegetation, from contact with corroded metal, from falls onto unseen stone—is significant, and bleeding in a hot, wet, microbe-dense environment is a fast track to infection.

Bring a thermal blanket—the compact kind that reflects body heat. Even though the environment is warm, if you become injured or disoriented or exhausted, your ability to thermoregulate will be compromised, and hypothermia can occur even in warm conditions when the body is failing.

## VI. Movement: How to Traverse the Plateau

### The Stance of Navigation

Move slowly. Move deliberately. Move with constant tactile contact with your environment.

Keep one hand on a surface at all times—the wall of a stalk-thicket, the surface of a pipe, the stone of a ridge. This hand is your primary orientation. It tells you where the solid world is, where the boundaries are, where you are in relation to the last thing you touched.

Use your feet as sensors. Step tentatively. Test the ground before committing your weight. The saturated stone can be slippery, the ground can yield unexpectedly, and the low places where water pools can be inches deep or feet deep—you will not know until you step. A probing foot is a safe foot. A probing foot that finds water above the ankle tells you to find another path.

Keep your other hand free—for balance, for clearing nets from your face, for reading your watch, for emergency signals. Do not carry things in both hands. If you need both hands for a task—cutting through vegetation, climbing a ridge—stop moving forward, do the task, then resume your navigation stance.

### Pathfinding in the Stalk-Thickets

The cylindrical stalks—arm-diameter, several meters high, growing in dense clusters—form the primary vegetation barrier on the plateau. They are rubbery and yield when pushed, then close behind you. Moving through them is like moving through a dense crowd that constantly re-forms around you.

Find the natural channels. The many-legged animals move through these thickets with fluid confidence, which means there are paths—probably at ground level, probably following the gaps where the stalks are slightly more widely spaced. Feel for these paths with your feet before you commit to pushing through a wall of stalks. If the ground beneath you is smoother, more worn, slightly drier from the passage of bodies, you are on an animal trail. Follow it. The animals know where the paths are.

When you must push through a thicket, use your cutting tool to create a gap rather than forcing your body through. The stalks will close behind you, but a cut stalk stays down. This is slower but less exhausting. Less exhaustion means better decision-making. Better decision-making means survival.

Be aware of the shelf-growths protruding from the stalks. They are layer upon layer of mineral-catching surfaces, and they are at various heights. In the darkness, you will encounter them with your face, your chest, your shoulders. They are not dangerous, but they are disorienting—you will feel a sudden obstacle where you expected air, and the startle response in absolute darkness can send you stumbling. Keep your free hand up, sweeping ahead of your face, to catch these before your face does.

### The Nets

The filamentous growths that drape between surfaces are adhesive and mildly irritating. They are nearly invisible even in light, and in darkness they are undetectable until they are on you.

They will cling to your face, your hands, your clothing. The adhesive is not strong enough to trap you, but it is strong enough to be a constant nuisance, and the mild irritant will leave your skin tingling where it contacts. Over time, this tingling can become a burning sensation, especially on sensitive facial skin.

Push through them deliberately rather than trying to avoid them. You cannot avoid what you cannot see. Accept that you will be coated in net-filaments, and plan accordingly—use your free hand to clear your face regularly, and wear a head covering that you can wipe clean or replace periodically.

If the irritation becomes severe, wash the affected area with filtered water. Do not use the Plateau's mineral-heavy water for this—use your own supply if possible. The combination of net-irritant and mineral residue can compound into a significant dermatological problem.

### The Standing Water

Avoid standing water when possible. The low places between hummocks and ridges hold black pools—warm, mineral-saturated, and populated by things that move.

You will encounter these pools by sound first—the flat, glistening sound, like a tongue clicking against the roof of a mouth, that the description mentions. Learn this sound. It is your warning.

If you must cross standing water, probe with a stick or your cutting tool before stepping. The pools can be deeper than they sound. The bottom can be uneven, with sudden drop-offs. The residents—whatever moves in the water, whatever causes the ripples that press against your ankles—will probably not harm you. The large animals are indifferent. The smaller creatures are occupied with their own lives. But the water itself, with its mineral load and its biological activity, is a contamination risk, and immersion means soaking your clothing and gear in a warm, chemically active solution that will be very difficult to dry.

### The Ridges and High Ground

Climbing is one of the most dangerous activities on the Plateau, because it combines all the hazards—slippery wet stone, disorienting darkness, physical exertion in thin air—with the consequences of a fall you cannot see.

When you climb, maintain three points of contact at all times. Two hands and one foot, or two feet and one hand. Move one limb at a time. Test each hold before committing your weight. The stone is smooth, worn by ages of moisture, and handholds will be subtle—less like grips and more like slight depressions, ridges, variations in texture that your fingers must learn to read.

Use your rope. Anchor it to something solid— pipes are good for this if they are not too corroded, and large stalks can serve if they are rooted firmly enough. Do not trust the shelf-growths or the mineral deposits as anchors; they may look solid but can crumble under weight.

The reward for climbing a ridge is information. You cannot see the landscape, but you can hear it—the sounds reaching you from different directions, the change in the wind patterns, the temperature gradients. From a high point, the Plateau's soundscape becomes a map: the mechanical thrum to the north, the rain-hiss to the west, the boom of the distant large machines to the east. Use this information to plan your next movement. But do not linger on ridges; the exposure is greater, the wind is colder despite the ambient warmth, and a fall from height in absolute darkness is a fall you may not survive.

## VII. The Animals: Coexistence Protocols

### The Large Many-Legged Creatures

These are your most common animal encounters. They are broad, low, warm, many-legged, and indifferent to you. They move through the stalk-thickets with fluid confidence, and you will encounter them by nearly stepping on them or by feeling them push past you.

Do not startle. This is easy to say and hard to do when a warm, large, unknown body suddenly presses against your leg in absolute darkness. Your startle response will be enormous. Your adrenaline will spike. Your instinct will be to recoil, to strike out, to defend yourself against something you cannot see.

Train yourself to hold still instead. The animals are not aggressive. They are not tame. They are indifferent. You are an obstacle, a stalk to push past, a feature of the landscape. If you hold still, they will move around you or past you. If you panic and strike out, you may injure them, and an injured animal—even an indifferent one—may respond differently than an uninjured one.

Learn their hum. The low, continuous vocalization that vibrates in your hand when you touch them. This hum is a sound signature—you can use it to detect their presence before physical contact. When you hear the hum, slow down. Let them pass. Wait until the sound moves away before proceeding.
