# Preparation Guide: The Subsurface Manufactory

## Before You Go: What You Need to Understand

You are not going to a place. You are going inside a process. The Manufactory is not a location you visit and leave; it is a system you enter, and once inside, it will continue its operations with complete indifference to your presence. This sounds like it should be comforting—the absence of hostility—but it is not. Hostility would at least acknowledge you. The Manufactory will not. You will be a pocket of warm, breathing matter moving through corridors that do not care whether you move through them or die in them, and eventually, if you stay long enough, you will begin to feel that your own operations are just another set of sounds in the general thrum.

This is the real danger. Not the fauna. Not the water. Not the geometry. The danger is becoming ambient.

Prepare yourself mentally for a specific kind of isolation: the isolation of being surrounded by evidence of intention—built walls, designed systems, running machines—with no one present who intended any of it. You will feel, at times, like you have arrived late to a meeting that is still happening. You have not. The meeting was never about you, and it will continue after you leave.

## Physical Preparation: The Body in the Machine

### Clothing: The War Against Wet

You will lose the war against wet. Accept this now and plan accordingly—you are not trying to stay dry, you are trying to stay functional while wet.

**Base layer:** Merino wool or a quality synthetic that retains thermal properties when saturated. Cotton is a death sentence. You will be damp within fifteen minutes of descent, and you will remain damp until you leave. Your clothing must work *while wet*. Every garment you bring must be asked the question: "What do you do for me when you are soaked?" If the answer is "nothing" or "I make things worse," do not bring it.

**Mid layer:** Fleece or synthetic insulation. Down is useless here; when it gets wet—and it will get wet—it clumps and fails. You need something that traps heat even when the fibers are saturated. Test this before you go. Soak your mid layer in a sink, wring it out without twisting, and put it on. If you feel cold immediately, find something else.

**Outer layer:** This is where you need to make a choice. Full waterproofing (gore-tex, similar membranes) will keep external water out but will trap your own moisture against your skin. In a place this consistently wet, you will steam from the inside. The alternative is water-resistant softshell material that breathes better but will eventually allow penetration. I recommend the softshell. You will be wet either way; at least this way you will not be marinating in your own sweat. Bring a full waterproof shell for situations where you must wade or stand in pooled water, but do not wear it as your default.

**Footwear:** This is critical. You will be walking on metal grating, concrete, composite flooring, and through standing water of varying depths. You need boots that are: waterproof to at least mid-calf, aggressively soled for metal surfaces (wet grating is treacherous), and—this is the part people skip—comfortable when soaked. Your feet will be wet. The question is whether wet feet in your boots becomes a blistering situation or merely an unpleasant one. Break your boots in before arrival. Then soak them and walk in them for several hours. Address whatever problems arise. Bring multiple pairs of wool socks and change them at any opportunity—any moment where you can sit in a dry-enough area, remove your boots, wring out your socks, and put on dry ones is a small victory that compounds over time.

**Gloves:** You will be touching cold, wet metal constantly. The pipes, the walls, the railings—all of it will be coated in condensation and will leach heat from your hands with startling efficiency. Bring waterproof gloves with grip, but also bring a pair of thin liner gloves for tasks requiring dexterity. Your hands will be your most vulnerable extremity simply because you cannot put them in your pockets; you need them for balance, for touching surfaces to navigate dark stretches, for checking the temperature of pipes before you brush against them.

**Head covering:** A watch cap or similar. You lose significant heat from your head, and the cold here is the kind that accumulates rather than shocks. After two hours you will feel fine. After six you will realize you are shivering and cannot remember when it started. After twelve you will be genuinely hypothermic if you have not managed your heat. Cover your head.

### Respiratory: The Taste on Your Tongue

The guide description mentions water that tastes like metal and something chemical, something that makes your tongue want to fold in on itself. That taste is in the air as well—dissolved in the condensation, aerosolized by the dripping, present in every breath you take. You are breathing recycled moisture that has passed through pipes, over machinery, across surfaces that may have been coated in substances you cannot identify.

Bring a respirator. Not a dust mask—a proper half-face respirator with P100 filters and organic vapor cartridges. You will not want to wear it constantly; it is uncomfortable, it fogs any eyewear, and it muffles sound you need to hear. But you should wear it in enclosed spaces where the chemical smell intensifies, in any room where liquid is pooling, and in the lower levels where the description mentions "something rising from below." The Manufactory is young, geologically speaking, but it is not pristine. Systems are running. Chemicals are moving. You do not know what the pipes contain, and you should not fill your lungs with their exhaust.

If you experience a metallic taste on your tongue that persists even after leaving wet areas, leave the level you are on. That is not condensation. That is something else.

### Hearing Protection: The Sound That Has Never Stopped

This is a genuine dilemma. The sound of the Manufactory is constant, pervasive, and physically taxing. The low-frequency thrum in the deep chambers will vibrate your teeth and spine. The high whine of electrical systems will settle behind your eyes. After hours of exposure, you will develop a headache that does not respond to normal pain medication because it is not a headache—it is a physical response to pressure waves impacting your body.

You need earplugs. Bring multiple types—foam, silicone, wax—because you will lose them, they will get wet, and different types work better for different frequencies. The foam plugs attenuate high frequencies well; the wax plugs are better for the low thrum. Carry them in a waterproof case.

But here is the dilemma: you need to *hear* the Manufactory. The sound of something moving in the pipes overhead is a warning. The change in the engine rhythm indicates you are approaching a deep chamber. The wet deliberateness behind a closed door is information you need. If you deaden your hearing entirely, you remove one of your primary senses in a place where visibility is already compromised.

Use hearing protection in the deep chambers, where the sound is truly a physical hazard. Remove it in the corridors, where the sound is information. Switch frequently. Take breaks in the quietest spots you can find—near the white growths, where the fractal structure absorbs sound—and let your ears rest. Tinnitus is likely. Accept it as a cost and monitor your exposure.

### Light: The Yellow Pools

The Manufactory has its own lighting—industrial fixtures spaced too far apart, leaving stretches of shadow that seem solid. You must supplement this. But you must also understand what your light will do to the space.

**Headlamp:** Your primary light. Bright, focused, and hands-free. Bring one with multiple settings—high for navigation, low for preserving night adaptation, red for situations where you do not want to attract attention. The red light is particularly important; you do not know what the fauna respond to, and the last thing you need is to broadcast your presence in a corridor where something is moving.

**Handheld flashlight:** A backup with a wider beam, useful for checking corners, examining the white growths, and reading the painted maps. Make sure it uses different batteries than your headlamp so you are not cannibalizing one for the other.

**Chemical light sticks:** The Manufactory runs on electricity. The electrical systems whine and flicker. You do not know if your presence, your equipment, or some fluctuation in the ancient systems might cause a localized outage. Chemical light sticks do not depend on batteries, do not emit electrical signatures, and produce a dim glow that will not attract attention the way a bright LED will. Tuck them into your pack and forget about them until you need them.

**Batteries:** More than you think. Twice what you calculate. The cold and the wet will degrade battery life faster than normal. Every electronic device you bring will underperform. Plan for this.

## Navigation: The Space Between Walls

### The Fundamental Problem

The Manufactory does not map cleanly. The painted maps on the walls are incomplete—they trail off into bare wall where the cartographer gave up or ran out of space. The corridors extend past the edges of documentation. The junctions do not always make geometric sense; five corridors meeting at angles that feel wrong, two sloping up, one down, two level but heading in impossible directions.

You cannot navigate this place the way you navigate a building. A building has a logic—a main entrance, a central corridor, a comprehensible relationship between floors. The Manufactory has layers that descend further than anyone has cataloged, and the connections between those layers follow an internal logic that you do not have access to. You are navigating a body from the inside, following capillaries that branch according to the needs of the organ, not the convenience of the blood cell.

### What to Bring

**Compass:** Bring one. It may not work. The description mentions cardinal directions that feel impossible—this could indicate magnetic interference from the machinery, or it could indicate that the space itself does not conform to the geometry you expect. Test your compass at the first junction. If it spins, if it points consistently in a direction that contradicts the map, if it behaves erratically in specific areas, note these anomalies. They are data. They may also be warnings about the deep chambers, where the immense turbines could generate fields that make compasses useless or, worse, consistently wrong.

**Rope:** Not for climbing—for trailing. If you enter a corridor, tie off at the junction and bring the rope with you. The corridors are featureless. They repeat. After twenty minutes of walking a corridor that has not changed, you will begin to doubt your own movement. The rope is proof of distance. It is also a lifeline—if you need to retrace your steps in darkness, following a rope is far more reliable than following your own uncertain memory.

**Marking materials:** Chalk is useless on wet surfaces. Tape will not adhere to condensation-coated walls. What works: surveyor's flagging tape tied to pipes or fixtures, bright and visible even in dim light. What works better: small, waterproof LED markers that you can attach to surfaces with magnets or adhesive pads. Place them at junctions, at intervals in long corridors, anywhere you need to confirm you have been here before. Count them as you place them. Record the count. The Manufactory will not move your markers, but you may lose track of them in the monotony.

**Notebook:** Waterproof paper, write-with-any-condition pen. Record everything. Junction configurations. Corridor lengths (estimated by time walking). Sound patterns. Which way the water is dripping. Where the white growths are thick. Where they have been scraped away. Where you saw the fauna. Where you heard them. The Manufactory is a system, and systems have patterns. You may not see the pattern in the moment, but your notes may reveal it later, when you sit in a relatively dry spot and try to understand where you are.

**The maps on the walls:** Photograph them. Trace them into your notebook with annotations. They are not helpful in the sense of showing you where to go, but they are helpful in the sense of showing you what someone else thought was important to document. The places where the map trails off—those are the edges of someone else's knowledge. Those are the boundaries beyond which you will be moving without any prior information. Note them. Approach them with caution.

### The Rule of Descent

The Manufactory descends. This is the only direction. But "down" is not always obvious. The corridors slope gradually—sometimes so gradually that you do not notice until you realize the walk back is taking longer, requiring more effort, moving against a grade you did not consciously register. Track your elevation. If you have a device that measures barometric pressure, use it. If not, pay attention to your body: the deeper you go, the colder it gets, the wetter the air becomes, and the sound changes. The deep chambers have a different acoustic signature than the upper corridors. Learn to read these signs.

Set a turn-around depth before you descend. You do not know what is at the bottom. No one does. The guide mentions rooms in the lowest levels where water is infiltrating from below, where the seams are wider, where the slow process of reclamation has begun. These are the places where the Manufactory is becoming something other than what it was built to be. You do not want to be in those rooms when you discover what it is becoming.

## Food and Water: Sustenance in a Place Without Sustenance

### Water

Do not drink the water. This cannot be stressed enough. The condensation, the drips, the pools—do not drink any of it. The metallic taste is a warning. The chemical undertone is a warning. Your tongue wanting to fold in on itself is a warning. The Manufactory is a closed system, and the water moving through it has been circulating through pipes, over machinery, across surfaces that may have been treated, coated, or contaminated decades ago. The water is not rain. It is not groundwater. It is process water, and you do not know what process.

Bring your own water. More than you think you need. Calculate your normal intake for the duration of your stay and double it. The cold suppresses the sensation of thirst, but the dry air (despite the condensation, the air is not humid in the way that satisfies—there is a difference between wet air and hydrating air) will dehydrate you steadily. You will not notice until you develop a headache, confusion, or dark urine. Drink on a schedule, not on desire.

If you run out of water and must choose between dehydration and drinking from the Manufactory's supply, choose dehydration up to the point of genuine medical emergency. The chemical content of the water is unknown, and the effects may not be immediate. If you must drink it, filter it through whatever you have—cloth, charcoal, a proper filtration system—but understand that filtration may not remove dissolved industrial chemicals. Boil it if you can, though boiling may concentrate certain contaminants. The safest approach is to not be in a position where you must drink it. Plan your water supply to avoid this.

### Food

Bring dense, caloric food that does not require cooking and is not destroyed by moisture. The Manufactory is cold enough that your body will burn calories just maintaining temperature, and the physical exertion of walking endless corridors, wading through pooled water, and carrying your gear will compound this. Energy bars, nuts, dried meat, hard cheese (within its safe temperature range)—foods that pack calories into small, durable packages. Avoid anything that will become unappetizing or dangerous when damp. Bread will become soggy. Fresh fruit will bruise and spoil. Chocolate will bloom in the temperature shifts and become a mess.

Eat on a schedule, like you drink on a schedule. The monotony of the corridors will suppress your appetite. The cold will suppress your appetite. The sound will suppress your appetite. Eat anyway. Your body is your primary tool in this environment, and it requires fuel.

Do not eat anything you find in the Manufactory. The white growths are not food. The amber viscous liquid leaking from pipes is not food. The fauna are not food. You do not know what any of these things contain, and the Manufactory is not an ecosystem designed to support human life. It is a system designed to support itself.

## The Fauna: What Moves in the Manufactory

You will hear them before you see them. Skittering in the pipes. Wet deliberateness behind closed doors. The guide describes one encounter: a low, dark shape, roughly dog-sized, drinking from a leaking pipe, moving through a gap that was too small for it—its body negotiating the space in a way that made the observer's eyes want to slide off the moment of passage.

This is the most important detail about the fauna: they belong here in a way you do not. They know the corridors. They know the pipes. They know the gaps and the routes and the rhythms of the machinery. They have adapted to the Manufactory's systems, or they were always part of them. You are a visitor. They are residents.

### What to Do

**Do not follow.** The guide says this plainly, and it bears repeating. If a creature moves through a gap that seems too small for it, do not try to follow. Do not try to understand how it fit. Do not try to find the gap again once you have lost sight of it. The geometry of the Manufactory does not always obey your expectations, and the spaces the fauna use may not be spaces you can use—or survive.

**Do not corner them.** You do not know what they are capable of. You do not know their behavior under stress. You do not know if they are territorial, if they are solitary, if they have never encountered a human before or if they have encountered many. Give them space. Let them leave. If one is blocking your path, wait. It will move. It has somewhere to be.

**Do not eat them.** This was covered under food, but it bears repeating in context. The fauna drink from pipes that leak viscous amber liquid. They have adapted to the Manufactory's water supply, which means their flesh is likely saturated with whatever chemicals and contaminants that water contains. They are not game animals. They are not a food source. They are part of the system.

**Pay attention to what they do.** They know things you do not. If a creature is drinking from a particular pipe, that pipe contains something they find useful. If they avoid a particular corridor, that corridor contains something they find threatening. If they are moving purposefully in a direction, they are following a route that works. You are not them, and you cannot always use their routes, but you can learn from their behavior. Watch them when you can. Learn what they teach.

### What Is Maintaining the Corridors

The guide mentions the white growth being scraped away—long, parallel scratches, mechanical, regular. Something clears the passages. Something maintains the Manufactory. You have not seen what.

Neither have I. And I will not speculate about what it might be, because speculation leads to assumptions, and assumptions lead to mistakes. Prepare instead for the possibility of encountering whatever is doing the maintenance. It is mechanical—regular, parallel scratches imply a tool, not teeth or claws. It is purposeful—it clears growth that impedes access. It is working. It may not notice you, the way the rest of the Manufactory does not notice you. Or it may notice you and classify you as something that either impedes access or does not.

If you encounter something maintaining the corridors, do not impede its work. Do not stand in its path. Do not try to communicate with it. Do not try to stop it. Step aside. Let it pass. It has work to do, and you are not part of that work. Make sure you remain not part of that work.

## Psychological Preparation: The Weight of Waiting

### The Ambient Self

I said earlier that the danger is becoming ambient. Let me explain what I mean.

The Manufactory is a place of continuous operation. The machines run. The water circulates. The condensation forms and drips and forms again. The corridors extend. The systems function. None of this requires you, acknowledges you, or changes because of you. You are moving through a place that does not need you, and after enough time in that environment, you will begin to feel that you are not needed anywhere. That your own operations are as mechanical and purposeless as the engines. That you are just another sound in the thrum.

This is not true. You have a life outside the Manufactory. You have people who need you. You have purposes that exist independently of the machine. But the Manufactory will not remind you of this. It will, if anything, help you forget. The monotony, the cold, the wet, the endless corridors that do not change—these wear at your sense of self. You will find yourself standing in a junction, water beading on your skin, the sound filling your chest, and you will not remember how long you have been standing there. You will not remember which direction you were going. You will not remember why you came.

This is the Manufactory's version of a trap. Not a pit, not a dead end, not a predator—a dissolution of purpose. The way to resist it is to maintain your purpose actively. Set goals, even small ones. "Walk to the next junction." "Check the map." "Eat something." "Change my socks." Complete them. Set new ones. Keep your mind occupied with the mechanics of your own survival. Do not let yourself become a passive observer of the machine's operation. You are not here to watch. You are here to move through, and then to leave.

### The Waiting

The guide says the Manufactory seems to be waiting—not for someone, not for something, but waiting the way a machine waits when it is turned on and has no work to do. You will feel this. It is a specific quality of the air, the light, the sound. The potential is present. The systems are running. And what they will produce when the final connection is made—

You do not know.

This not-knowing will sit in your chest like the sound of the engines. It will make you want to find out. It will make you want to descend further, to find the deepest chamber, to discover what the Manufactory is building when it finishes building itself. This impulse is understandable. It is also potentially lethal. The Manufactory is not a puzzle for you to solve. It is not a mystery for you to resolve. It is a system that is operating according to logic you do not have access to, and pushing deeper into that system in search of an answer is a good way to become part of the system.

Set your boundaries before you descend. Write them down. Tell someone your plan and your expected return. Hold yourself to those boundaries even when the Manufactory seems to invite you further. The corridors accept you, the guide says. They will always accept you. They will accept you until you are too deep to return, too cold to think, too wet to care, and too far from the surface to remember why you wanted to leave.

### The Quiet Spots

There is one mercy in the Manufactory: the white growths. The fractal, cartilage-colored material that spreads in warmer junctions absorbs sound. Standing near it, you will feel the constant thrum recede slightly, the whine of the electrical systems soften, the drip of condensation become less oppressive. These are your rest points. Use them. When you find a junction where the growth is thick, sit down. Eat. Drink. Change your socks. Write in your notebook. Let your ears rest. Let your mind rest.

Do not stay too long. The growth is damp, and prolonged sitting on cold, wet surfaces will leach heat from your body faster than you realize. Twenty minutes is enough. Thirty is too long. Set a timer if you need to—the Manufactory does not measure time in a way that is useful to you, and you will lose track of it in the quiet.

## What to Leave Behind

**Excessive technology:** The Manufactory is a place of machines, but your machines may not work well here. The electromagnetic environment is unknown—those immense turbines may generate fields that disrupt or damage sensitive electronics. Bring what you need, but do not rely on anything you cannot afford to lose. A phone with downloaded maps is useless if the screen fogs and the battery dies in the cold. A GPS unit is useless if the signal cannot penetrate layers of manufactured material. Analog tools—compass, watch, notebook—have no electronic vulnerabilities. Prioritize them.

**Expectations of comfort:** You will be cold. You will be wet. You will be tired. You will be surrounded by sound that does not stop. You will not find a truly dry place to rest. You will not find a truly quiet place to sleep. Sleep in shifts if you are with others; sleep lightly if you are alone. Do not expect to sleep well. Expect to sleep enough to function, and accept that this is the best you will get.

**The need to understand:** You will not understand the Manufactory. You will not learn its purpose, its history, its ultimate product. You will not solve it. You will move through it, observe it, and leave it, and it will continue its operations without you. This is not a failure on your part. The Manufactory was not built for you to understand. It was built to run. Let it run. Do your work—your work of moving through, observing, and leaving—and let the machine do its work, whatever that may be.

## Final Notes

The Subsurface Manufactory is not hostile. It is not kind. It is not haunted, not in any way that word usually means. It is a place that is doing something, and you are a person who is passing through while it does that something. Your preparation is not about surviving an attack or escaping a trap. It is about maintaining yourself—your warmth, your hydration, your orientation, your sense of purpose—in an environment that will slowly, steadily, persistently work to erode all of those things.

The water is patient. The cold is patient. The sound is patient. They will work on you for as long as you are there. Prepare so that you can work on yourself more effectively. Bring warmth. Bring water. Bring light. Bring rope. Bring a way to mark your path. Bring a reason to leave, and hold onto it.

And when you stand in the half-light at a junction of five corridors, water beading on your skin, the sound of engines filling your chest, and you choose a direction—choose the one that leads out. The corridors will accept you no matter which way you go. Make sure you choose the one that leads back to the surface, to the sunlight, to the world where things stop and start according to rhythms you can comprehend.

The Manufactory will still be running when you leave. It will still be waiting. It will still be building itself toward something you do not know.

That is not your concern. Your concern is walking out of the only exit that matters—the one you came in through, while you still remember where it is.
