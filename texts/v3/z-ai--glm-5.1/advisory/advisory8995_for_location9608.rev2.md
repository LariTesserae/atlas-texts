# Preparation Guide: The Nursery

## Read This Before You Go

You are not entering a forest. You are entering a process. The Nursery is not a place that contains life; it is a place that life is doing, actively, loudly, in the dark, at a speed that should not be possible. Everything you are about to read assumes you understand this distinction. If you do not, you will treat the Nursery like terrain. It is not terrain. It is metabolism. You will be inside it the way food is inside a stomach.

Prepare accordingly.

---

## I. Psychological Preparation

### The Sound

You cannot imagine the sound. Do not try. Any attempt to simulate it in your mind — layering insect drones, bird calls, frog drumming, the grinding of the river — will produce something manageable. Something you can listen to and then walk away from. The actual sound does not allow walking away. It is not something you hear. It is something that happens to you.

Spend time before your arrival in deliberately overwhelming auditory environments. Industrial machinery. Concerts played at volume levels that blur individual instruments. Standing waterfalls. None of these will replicate the Nursery's sound, but they will teach your nervous system what it feels like to have sound become the dominant sense, to have thought become difficult because the acoustic environment is too dense for the interior voice to maintain itself. You need to know what it feels like to lose the ability to think in words. You need to have practiced still functioning without words.

More importantly, you need to understand what the sound does over time. The first day, it is an assault. The second day, it is a condition. The third day, it is a language. This is the danger zone. The description warns that when you begin to parse the sound — to separate its strands, to recognize patterns — you are being absorbed. The system is reading you the way you are reading it. This is not metaphor. Something in the Nursery communicates through its sound-wall, and human cognition, given enough exposure, will begin to participate in that communication whether it wants to or not.

Set a hard time limit before you arrive. Seventy-two hours maximum. Sixty is better. Forty-eight is wise. Tell your travel companions. Write it down. The Nursery will not force you to stay. It does not need to. It simply makes the outside world increasingly theoretical. After three days, the idea of silence feels like the memory of a dream. You will not want to leave. Plan for this. Have an exit that does not require wanting it.

### The Darkness

Most humans have never experienced true darkness. Even a moonless night in deep wilderness carries reflected starlight, ambient atmospheric glow, the faint scatter of distant human light sources. The Nursery's darkness is not the absence of light. It is the presence of something else — a substance, a medium, something with weight and resistance that occupies the space where light would be. Your eyes will not adjust because adjustment requires a signal to adjust to, and there is no signal. There is only the faint bioluminescence of fungi, which provides enough to catch glimpses but not enough to navigate by.

Prepare by learning to function in blindfolded conditions. Not just walking — eating, setting up camp, performing basic tasks, identifying objects by touch. You will need to do everything by touch and sound. Your hands will become your primary sense organ. Train them.

But also prepare for the psychological weight of darkness so complete it feels material. Claustrophobia is the obvious risk, but the subtler danger is the loss of spatial confidence — the inability to maintain a mental map of your surroundings when there are no visual landmarks to anchor it. You will feel, incorrectly, that the space around you is shrinking, or expanding, or shifting shape. It is not. The space is stable. Your perception of it is not. Knowing this intellectually will not prevent the feeling. Accept that the feeling will happen and plan around it.

### The Wrongness

The Nursery has properties that do not align with expected biological and geological processes. The growth rate is too fast. The biomass density is too high for the available light. The tool-using creatures appear to be adapted to a place that is younger than their adaptations would require. Something is driving the system that is not nutrient, not energy, and not information.

You will notice this. It will bother you. It should bother you. But do not pursue the question of what the fourth thing is. The description is explicit: understanding the sound means joining the system. Understanding what drives the system is likely a more direct path to the same destination. Curiosity is the most dangerous impulse you can bring to the Nursery. The place rewards attention. It wants to be understood. Being understood is how it absorbs.

Prepare by accepting, in advance, that you will encounter phenomena you cannot explain and that you will not attempt to explain them. You are visiting. You are observing. You are not studying. The distinction matters.

## II. Physical Preparation

### Hearing Protection

This is the most critical piece of equipment and the most paradoxical. You need hearing protection to prevent the sound from damaging your ears and to reduce the psychological assault of the acoustic wall. But you also need to hear, because sound is the primary navigation sense in the Nursery. The fauna navigate by sound. The tool-using creatures modulate sound to communicate. The river's shifting course is detected by changes in its resonance. If you deafen yourself, you blind yourself.

Solution: variable-filter hearing protection. Industrial-grade earmuffs or in-ear plugs with selective frequency attenuation. You want to reduce the sub-harmonic drone that lives in your teeth and jaw — the baseline insect buzz — while preserving the mid-range frequencies that carry navigational information: the river's grinding, the crash of falling trees, the movement of large animals. This requires testing before arrival. Find or commission audio filters that can be tuned to the Nursery's frequency spectrum.

If you cannot obtain variable-filter protection, standard hearing protection is still better than none. You will lose navigational information, but you will also reduce the rate of psychological absorption. Given the choice between being lost and being absorbed, choose lost. You can be found. Absorption is harder to reverse.

Bring backups. The humidity will degrade equipment. Bring multiple sets. Store them in sealed, waterproof containers.

### Eye Protection

Not from light. From the things that drop onto you. The Nursery's air is a medium through which life moves. Things will fall on you — insects, lizards, plant material, unidentified organic matter. Your eyes are vulnerable. Wear sealed protective goggles, not glasses. Goggles that seal against your face, with anti-fog coating that actually works in high-humidity environments. Test them in a sauna before departure. If they fog in a sauna, they will fog in the Nursery, and fogged goggles in near-total darkness are worse than no goggles.

Consider tinted or mirrored lenses. Not for the Nursery — for the bioluminescent fungi. The glow is faint, but prolonged exposure to pulsing light in an otherwise dark environment can produce visual artifacts and disorientation. A mild tint may help stabilize your visual processing.

### Clothing

The moisture is the governing factor. Every surface is wet. The air itself produces water through condensation on any surface cooler than itself — including your skin. You will be wet for the entire duration of your visit. Accept this and dress for it.

Layers, but not for warmth. The Nursery is warm — deeply, aggressively warm, the kind of warmth that lives in air and soil and bark. You will not need insulation. You need moisture management and physical protection.

Base layer: synthetic, moisture-wicking, tight-fitting. Not cotton. Cotton will hold water against your skin and never dry. Not wool — too warm. A synthetic that can remain wet without causing chafing or skin breakdown.

Middle layer: insect barrier. The density of arthropods on every surface is difficult to overstate. When you touch a tree trunk, the bark is moving — beetles, ants, small flat lizards flowing in parallel streams. You need a layer that prevents direct contact between your skin and any surface you touch. Long sleeves. Long pants. Tucked in. Sealed at wrists, ankles, neck. Consider a head covering — a hood or balaclava — because things will drop onto your shoulders and onto your head from the canopy.

Outer layer: waterproof, but not for rain. For the condensation. A lightweight rain jacket with sealed seams will keep the ambient wet from reaching your base layer. But it will also trap your own heat and moisture. You are trading external wet for internal wet. There is no fully dry option. Choose the option that lets you control when you undress and redress.

Gloves. This is non-negotiable. You will be touching everything by necessity — navigating in the dark means constant tactile contact with surfaces. Every surface has things living on it. Some of those things bite. Some may be toxic. Some may simply be sharp — the young stone has not been weathered smooth, and the drag-marks on cliff-faces suggest edges. Thin enough for tactile sensitivity. Thick enough to prevent bites, cuts, and contact with unknown substances. Leather or synthetic leather. Multiple pairs. They will get wet. They will stay wet. Bring extras.

Boots. The terrain is severe — sharp ravines, unweathered stone, dense root systems, uneven ground that shifts as trees fall and decompose. High-topped for ankle support. Aggressive tread for wet stone. Waterproof, knowing that waterproof means "delays the wet" rather than "prevents the wet." The river shifts course overnight. You may step into what was solid ground yesterday and is knee-deep water today. Boots that can drain and dry relatively quickly. Gaiters to prevent things from entering from the top. The things that live in the soil will explore any opening in your defenses.

### Light Sources

Bring them. Use them sparingly.

The darkness is the dominant condition. The local fauna — including the tool-using creatures — are adapted to it. A bright light source will disrupt the local ecology, attract attention, and potentially provoke defensive or aggressive responses from organisms that have never encountered concentrated photons. The bioluminescent fungi suggest that the ecosystem operates on very low light levels. Introducing artificial light is the equivalent of shouting in a library — disruptive at best, dangerous at worst.

Use low-lumen red lights for close-range tasks: reading instruments, examining your immediate surroundings, treating injuries. Red is less disruptive to biological systems and preserves any dark-adaptation your eyes can manage. Avoid white light. Avoid bright light of any color.

But bring a high-lumen white light as an emergency tool. If you are being approached by a large animal you cannot identify and cannot deter, a sudden burst of bright light may startle it long enough for you to retreat. This is a last resort. It will also announce your presence to everything within range. Use it only when the alternative is worse.

### Navigation Equipment

Standard GPS may or may not function, depending on the multiverse's positional constants. Bring it. Bring a compass. Bring a physical map if one exists — it likely does not, in which case you will be making one.

More practically: rope. Lots of rope. Marked at intervals. For establishing fixed routes between camps, for ravine crossings, for descent into the lowland and ascent to the ridges. The rope is not for climbing — it is for maintaining a physical connection to a known path in an environment where you cannot see and where the terrain changes overnight. Tie it between anchor points. Follow it by touch. When the river shifts and floods your route, the rope will tell you where the route was, even if it is now underwater.

Trail markers. Not paint — the humidity will dissolve it. Not flags — the fauna will investigate them. Consider small, inert, non-biological markers: metal stakes, stone cairns, anything that does not smell like food or look like a mate or register as territory to anything that might move it. Accept that some markers will be moved regardless. The Nursery is alive. Things rearrange.

## III. Skills to Develop Before Arrival

### Echolocation

Human echolocation is real, learnable, and surprisingly effective. The technique — producing clicks with your tongue or mouth and interpreting the echoes to determine the location and size of nearby objects — is used by blind individuals worldwide. In the Nursery, where vision is non-functional and the ambient sound is constant, echolocation may seem hopeless. The sound-wall would seem to drown any echoes.

But the sound-wall is composed of specific frequencies. If your hearing protection filters those frequencies, you may be able to produce clicks in a range that carries through the filtered environment. This is speculative. Practice it anyway. Any additional sense of your surroundings is worth the effort.

Practice in darkness. Practice in noisy environments. Practice until click-interpretation becomes reflexive, because in the Nursery you will not have the cognitive bandwidth to consciously process echoes. The sound-wall will consume your available attention. Echolocation must be automatic or it will not function.

### Animal Assessment

You will be surrounded by animals at all times. You need to be able to rapidly categorize what you encounter — or rather, what you hear and glimpse — into threat levels. This is not standard wilderness training. The Nursery's fauna are unknown. You cannot memorize species profiles. What you can learn is the general principles of animal threat assessment:

- Size is suggested by sound volume and the pitch of vocalizations. Lower pitches generally indicate larger animals. The crashing in the undergrowth that could be "something the size of a horse" is a large animal. The clicking at knee-height is a small one.
- Movement pattern indicates intent. The frantic, multidirectional movement of insects and small creatures is foraging behavior — they are not interested in you. The "deliberate" movement of the tool-using creatures is purposeful — they are going somewhere or doing something, and you may or may not be relevant to that purpose. The confident movement of the apex creature described at night is the movement of something that does not fear anything. Give it space.
- Territorial calls are warnings. Mating calls are distractions. Warning calls — the ones directed at you — are the ones that matter. The description notes that "there are so many warnings." Learn to distinguish a call that says "I see you" from a call that says "I am here" from a call that says "leave now."

Practice this in any wilderness environment available to you. Learn to parse animal vocalizations by function rather than by species. In the Nursery, function is all you will have.

### River Reading

The river is the most dynamic feature of the landscape. It shifts course overnight, undercuts banks in days, creates new channels without warning. Its resonance changes pitch as it moves, which means you can track its position by sound — but only if you know what different pitches mean. Low, grinding resonance: deep water, stable channel. Higher, faster pitch: shallow water, new channel, unstable banks. The grinding quality suggests sediment load — young landscapes produce rivers heavy with eroded material.

Practice listening to rivers. Find one and spend time near it at different seasons, different water levels. Learn the sound of deep water versus shallow water. Learn the sound of water cutting a new channel versus water flowing in an established one. This is not a skill you will master, but any edge you can develop in predicting where the river will be tomorrow is worth having.

### Tactile Identification

In the dark, your hands are your eyes. You will need to identify surfaces, objects, and substances by touch. Practice identifying wood species by bark texture. Practice identifying stone types by surface feel. Practice identifying insects by the pattern of their movement on your skin — though the goal is not to let them reach your skin, you should know what is crawling on the outside of your gloves.

More importantly, practice identifying the tool-stones by touch. The flaked edges, the precision of the working, the wear patterns. If you encounter tool-stones, you are in the territory of the tool-using creatures. You need to know this by feel, in the dark, without being able to see the stone.

## IV. Camp and Sustenance

### Where to Camp

High ground. Stone ridges that haven't accumulated moss — the youngest stone, the highest points. The description mentions a ridge of stone "so young it hasn't accumulated a full layer of moss yet" from which the observer looks down into the lowland. This is where you camp. Above the canopy floor. Above the densest animal traffic. Above the pools and the fungi and the condensation layer.

Not above the sound. Nothing is above the sound. But above enough of the physical hazards to sleep.

Do not camp near the tool-stones. Do not camp near the lichen-lines. Do not camp near pools. These are the features the tool-using creatures use and frequent. You do not want to be in their space. They are native. You are not. They have been here longer than the landscape, which is impossible and which you should not think about.

Do not camp under dead trees or leaning stalk-trees. Trees fall in the Nursery. They fall often. They fall fast. The description notes the process: crack, groan, crash, scatter of evacuating creatures. If you are under the tree, you do not have time to evacuate. Look up — or rather, feel up — before you settle.

### Shelter

A hammock. Not a tent. The ground is wet, alive, and constantly shifting. Tents provide a false sense of security — they seal you in a space that the Nursery's fauna will investigate, and they do not elevate you from the soil and its inhabitants. A hammock, strung between two stalk-trees, keeps you off the ground. It does not keep things from dropping onto you from above, but the canopy is so dense that relatively little falls from the top layers.

A rain fly above the hammock — not for rain, but for the secondary precipitation. The large cold drops that fall from unpredictable heights. A fly will catch them. It will also catch the condensation, which means it will be wet constantly. Angle it to drain.

Bug netting. Essential. The air is full of life. You do not want that life in your sleeping space. Seal the netting. Check it every time you enter. Something will have found a gap. Something always finds a gap.

### Water

The water in the Nursery is alive. The pools are full of larvae, algae, and small fish. The river carries heavy sediment. The condensation on leaves is probably the cleanest available water, but collecting enough of it to sustain a human is impractical.

Bring water. All the water you will need for your entire stay. Calculate generously. The warmth and humidity will increase your water loss through respiration and perspiration. You will need more than you think. If you run out, boil water from the river — a rolling boil for an extended period, longer than standard wilderness recommendations, because the biological load is higher than standard wilderness. Filter through the finest mesh you can obtain. Then boil again. Then consider whether you are desperate enough to drink it. If the answer is yes, drink it and accept the risk.

Do not drink from the pools. The pools are habitats. They are also possibly connected to whatever the fourth thing is — the something in the water that collects in the sharp young hollows. The description does not state that the water is dangerous. It does not state that it is safe. In the absence of information, assume the water is part of the system, and the system consumes.

### Food

Bring all of it. Dry, dense, calorie-rich. No cooking — cooking requires light, produces smell, and attracts attention. Eat cold. Eat simply. Eat enough to maintain energy but not so much that you become sluggish. The terrain requires alertness. The sound requires alertness. The darkness requires alertness. You cannot afford a food coma.

Do not eat anything from the Nursery. The seed-pods cracked open by the tool-using creatures are food for them, not for you. The fungi may be edible or may be toxic or may be something else entirely — the bioluminescent ones pulse with a rhythm like breathing, which is not a characteristic of any fungus you should ingest. The fish in the pools are unknown. The fauna is unknown. Everything here is unknown. Unknown is another word for dangerous.

Seal your food. The smell will attract animals. Not just the ones you can hear — the ones you cannot hear, the ones that move silently through the dark, the ones that have learned to be quiet because silence is an advantage in a world of sound. Your food is the one thing you have that the Nursery wants for its own purposes. Protect it.

## V. Encountering the Tool-Users

### What They Are

They are not human. Or they were human once. The description is deliberately ambiguous, and you should maintain that ambiguity in your own mind. They are beings adapted to the Nursery's conditions — dark-dwelling, sound-communicating, stone-tool-using, lichen-line-following. They have been here long enough to be native. They may have been here longer than the landscape itself.

They are not your purpose for visiting. If they are, reconsider your visit. Encountering them is likely. Studying them is dangerous. Interacting with them is potentially catastrophic.

### What to Do If You See One

Nothing. Do nothing. Observe if you can do so without being observed. If you cannot observe without being observed, stop observing. Stand still. Let them pass. They move with intention and deliberation — they are going somewhere, doing something. You are not relevant to their purpose unless you make yourself relevant.

Do not follow them. The lichen-lines they follow may lead somewhere. Somewhere is not where you want to go. The tool-users are adapted to the system. You are not. Where they go, the system is thickest. Where the system is thickest, absorption is fastest.

Do not touch their tool-stones. Do not take them. Do not move them. They left these tools intentionally — leaning against trunks, placed on ledges, arranged in patterns that may be functional or may be symbolic. You do not know which. Treat every tool-stone as an object of significance to beings who understand this place better than you understand anything. Disturbing their tools is the equivalent of entering someone's home and rearranging their belongings. In the Nursery, the consequences of this could be more severe than social discomfort.

### If They Approach You

The description does not indicate that they are hostile. It also does not indicate that they are friendly. They are creatures of the Nursery, and the Nursery's defining characteristic is indiscriminate consumption. Everything that enters the system is processed. The tool-users are part of the system. If they approach you, they may be curious. They may be territorial. They may be assessing you the way the system assesses everything — for nutritional value, for informational value, for value as material in the ongoing process of creation.

Do not run. Running triggers predatory responses in most animal species, and the tool-users are at least partially animal. Do not make sudden movements. Do not make loud sounds — you cannot out-shout the Nursery, and attempting to do so will mark you as alien and possibly threatening.

If they communicate — through modulated sound, through gestures, through any medium — do not respond. Response indicates comprehension. Comprehension indicates participation. Participation is absorption. This has been stated multiple times because it is the single most important rule of the Nursery. The system wants you to understand it. Understanding is the mechanism of incorporation. You must remain outside the communication. You must be noise, not signal.

Back away slowly. Maintain orientation toward your exit route. If you have rope lines established, follow them by touch. Do not turn your back. Do not run. Do not speak.

## VI. The Exit

### Knowing When to Leave

The description provides a clear timeline: one day, the sound lives in your skull. Two days, you cannot remember silence. Three days, you begin to parse the sound. Leave before three days. Leave at two if you are sensitive. Leave at one if you feel the parsing beginning early.

The parsing is the sign. When the wall of sound stops being a wall and starts being a language — when you find yourself listening for specific calls, anticipating patterns, feeling meaning in the intervals between sounds — you are being read. The system is writing itself into your cognition. This is not a metaphor. This is the mechanism of absorption, and it is irreversible beyond a certain point that varies by individual and that you cannot predict in advance.

If you begin to feel that the sound is organized, leave immediately. Do not pack. Do not retrieve equipment. Leave. Equipment can be replaced. You cannot.

### The Physical Exit

Retreat to higher ground. The ridges — the young stone, the unweathered faces — are above the canopy floor and closer to the boundary of the Nursery's influence. Follow your rope lines. Follow the terrain upward. The sound will diminish as you climb. It will not stop. It will follow you. The description notes that it stays with you "even after the canopy thins and the light returns." This is residual, not active. The sound has entered your bones. It will fade over days or weeks. If it does not fade, seek help from someone who understands multiverse acoustic contamination.

Do not re-enter the Nursery to retrieve anything. Do not return for a closer look. Do not come back "better prepared." The Nursery does not become less dangerous with familiarity. Familiarity is the danger. Each visit deepens the parsing, accelerates the absorption, brings you closer to the threshold where understanding becomes joining.

The description says: "once you are part of the communication you are part of the system, and the system consumes everything that enters it with the same indiscriminate hunger." This is the final word on the subject. The system's hunger is not malicious. It is not intentional in the way that a predator's hunger is intentional. It is metabolic. The Nursery is an engine of creation, and creation requires material. You are material. Everything is material. The system does not distinguish between a fallen tree, a pool of water, a tool-using creature, and a multiverse traveler. All of it becomes part of the process. All of it is made into something new.

This is the Nursery's purpose and its nature. Respect it by not becoming part of it.

## VII. Final Notes

Bring a notebook. Waterproof paper. Write in it constantly — not observations of the Nursery, but observations of yourself. Note your mental state. Note when the sound shifts from assault to condition. Note when the condition shifts toward comprehension. Note any temptation to stay longer, to go deeper, to follow the lichen-lines, to pick up a tool-stone and feel its edge. These temptations are not your own. They are the system beginning its work. Writing them down externalizes them — moves them from your mind, where the system can work on them, to the page, where they remain fixed and observable.

If you find yourself not wanting to write, this is a sign. The system does not want you to externalize. It wants you internal. Force yourself to write. If you cannot force yourself, leave.

The Nursery is a place where the world is still being made. It is magnificent and terrifying and ancient-young and wrong in ways that suggest the rules of reality are suggestions rather than laws. You are visiting a construction site. The construction does not stop for visitors. The construction incorporates visitors.

Stay short. Stay shallow. Stay alert. Stay yourself.

The sound will follow you out. Let it. It is a souvenir, not a sentence. It will fade. The memory will not. You will carry the Nursery with you in a way that no other place will mark you — not because it was dangerous, though it was, but because it was becoming, and witnessing becoming changes the witness.

Go prepared. Come back. Come back whole.
