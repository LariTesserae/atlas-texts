# Preparing for the Sump Cathedral

## What You Are Walking Into

You are not walking into a building. You are walking into a living process that has absorbed a building, and the process does not care about you, and the building does not remember you, and the only thing that will acknowledge your presence is the slight electrical tingle you feel when you touch the wrong surface. You will be out of place there. You will be a sack of minerals and water and electricity moving through a system that has already found its equilibrium without you. Everything about you—your heat, your breath, the oils on your skin, the vibrations of your footsteps—will be a perturbation that the Cathedral will, in its slow way, attempt to absorb, neutralize, or simply outlast.

Prepare accordingly.

---

## I. FOOTWEAR AND LEGS

You will not be walking on ground. You will be walking on a saturated sponge, and the sponge will give three inches before meeting water, and the water is warm and tannin-dark and metallic, and if you step wrong you will sink to your knee in root-mat and humic slurry and you will have to pull yourself out, and the pulling out will take your shoe.

**What to wear:**

**Rubber boots.** Not canvas. Not leather. Not Gore-Tex—Gore-Tex will fail here, the pores will clog with organic particulate, the membrane will become a sponge, and your feet will rot inside your boots. You need tall rubber boots, chest-waders if you can tolerate the bulk, because the water rises ankle-deep after rain and in some channels it is knee-deep and you will not always see the channel beneath the root-mat. The boots should be insulated—neoprene-lined, if you can find them—because the water is warm but the constant immersion will still macerate your skin, and macerated skin tears, and torn skin in this environment is an invitation to infection.

**Stiff soles.** The root-mat is uneven. You will be walking on cordage, on hummocks, on the crowns of buried stilt-roots, and every surface is wet and giving and slightly alive. A stiff sole distributes your weight across a wider area, which keeps you from punching through the mat into the water below. A stiff sole also protects you from the things you cannot see—old hardware, the edges of corroded pipes, sharp stones that someone laid down as a path a hundred years ago.

**Gaiters.** Wear gaiters over the tops of your boots. The root-mat is not just roots—it is leaf litter, humic paste, isopod droppings, fragments of bark, and the constant drip from overhead. All of this will fall into the top of your boot if you let it. Gaiters also give you a second layer between your skin and the environment, which matters because you will be brushing against stems constantly, and the stems are wet, and the wet on the stems is not just water.

**Traction.** The root-mat is not slick like ice, but it is slick like a wet sponge, and the hummocks are slick like a wet knee, and the stems you grab for balance are slick like a wet arm. Your boots should have aggressive tread. Consider adding screw-in cleats if you can tolerate the damage they will do to the root-mat—but the root-mat will survive, it is surviving everything, it will survive your cleats.

**What to know:**

You will fall. You will slip on a wet hummock or misjudge the firmness of a root-ridge and you will go down, and when you go down you will land on the root-mat, which will give under you, and you will sink to your elbow or your hip in warm wet organic matter, and you will have to extract yourself, and the extraction will be slow and the matter will be in your clothes and on your skin and you will smell like copper and sugar and earth for the rest of the day. Dress for the fall. Wear clothes you can wash, or clothes you can abandon.

## II. SKIN AND THE SAP

The sap is the thing they do not tell you about because they do not know. The descriptions of this place—your briefings, your notes—will mention the green light, the engine, the dripping, the density. They will not mention the sap because the sap is not dramatic. It weeps from the engine's gaskets. It pools around the Machine. It disperses into the root-mat. It is, by volume, a minor feature of the environment.

But the sap is *conducting*.

It is warm, slightly iridescent, dark amber, and it carries a current. The dynamo is spinning, the cables are live, the pipes are vibrating, and the whole Cathedral is a circuit—a low-voltage, high-humidity, organic circuit in which the sap is the electrolyte. You will feel this. When you touch a pipe, when you brush a stem, when you stand in a channel where the water rises and the water is mingled with sap, you will feel the tingle: faint, persistent, like touching a nine-volt battery to your tongue.

**What to wear:**

**Rubber gloves.** Not cotton. Not leather. Not thin nitrile exam gloves—those will tear on the bark, and the bark is the thing you will be touching most. You need thick rubber gloves, the kind linemen wear, the kind that insulate against electrical current. You will sacrifice dexterity, and the dexterity loss will be frustrating, but the alternative is constant low-level electrical contact, and the cumulative effect of that contact is unknown. We do not know what happens to a human body that is mildly electrified for hours or days. We do not know if the current is truly mild. We do not know if the sap is the only conductive medium, or if the spores in the air, the mist from the engine, the water on every surface—whether all of it is carrying charge. Insulate yourself.

**Long sleeves.** Long pants. Cover every inch of skin you can. The fabric should be synthetic—nylon, polyester, something that does not absorb water readily—because cotton will become a sponge, and a sponge in a conductive environment is a conductor. The fabric should be tight-weave, because the spores in the air are real, and the mist of sap is real, and you do not want them on your skin. You will be hot. You will be miserably hot. The Cathedral is warm and wet and you are wearing a full-body barrier and you will sweat, and the sweat will have nowhere to go, and you will feel like you are inside a plastic bag. This is the price of not becoming part of the circuit.

**What to know:**

The sap is warm. The sap is *warmer than the ambient air*. This is a fact that will be unsettling in a way you cannot anticipate. You will touch a surface, and the surface will be wet, and the wet will be warm, and you will realize that the wet is not water but sap, and the sap is warm because it has been circulated by the engine, and the engine is a heart, and you are touching something that has been inside a heart. This is not a metaphor. This is a description of what will happen to you.

If the sap gets on your skin, wipe it off. Do not let it dry. Do not let it sit. The sap is slightly adhesive, and it contains metals (iron, copper, trace elements from the pipes), and it will discolor your skin, and the tingle will persist, and you will not know if the tingle is the current or your imagination, and you will not know if the metals are being absorbed, and you will not know if the current is doing something to your cells, and you will not know, and you will wipe it off, and you will be fine, probably, but you will not know.

## III. BREATHING

The air is not dangerous. The air is *not dangerous* in the way that air in a coal mine is dangerous, or the air in a chemical plant is dangerous. There is no measurable toxicity. The oxygen levels are fine. The humidity is extreme but survivable. The spores are present but you are not a plant, and the spores are not for you.

But the humidity is 100% or close to it. The air is so saturated that it does not accept moisture from your lungs, and the moisture in your lungs does not evaporate, and the cooling mechanism that your body uses—sweat evaporation—does not work, and you will heat up, and the heat has nowhere to go, and you will feel like you are breathing through a warm wet cloth.

**What to bring:**

**Water.** This is the thing that will kill you fastest if you do not have it—not the electricity, not the sap, not the fall, but the heat. Bring more water than you think you need. Bring at least three liters per person per day, and bring more than that, because you will sweat, and the sweat will not evaporate, and you will lose fluids faster than you can replace them, and the water in the Cathedral is not drinkable. It is tannin-dark and metal-rich and it moves through the root-mat, and the root-mat is a living filter, and the filter is not filtering for you, and the water is not for you. Do not drink it.

**Electrolytes.** You will lose salt. You will lose potassium. You will sweat out everything that keeps your nerves firing, and if you replace only the water, you will be hydrated but you will be dysfunctional, and the last thing you want to be in the Cathedral is dysfunctional. Bring electrolyte tablets. Dissolve them in your water. Drink them before you are thirsty, because thirst is a lagging indicator, and by the time you are thirsty, you are already impaired.

**A breathing consideration.** The Cathedral is warm and wet and the engine is running, and the engine burns methane, and the methane is a seep from the root-mat, and the root-mat is decomposing, and decomposition produces gases, and the gases include methane and possibly hydrogen sulfide and possibly other things. The engine is venting—there is a boiler, and the boiler has a flue, and the flue goes somewhere, and the combustion products are going somewhere, and that somewhere is the air you are breathing. The engine is old, and the combustion is probably incomplete, and the air is probably fine, but the air is *probably* fine, and you should bring a gas monitor if you can. A simple four-gas monitor—oxygen, carbon monoxide, hydrogen sulfide, lower explosive limit. Clip it to your belt. Watch it. If it alarms, leave. The Cathedral will still be there when you come back.

**What to know:**

The smell of the Cathedral is this: copper and sugar and earth and wet green and the slight metallic tang of the engine and the faintly organic sweetness of the sap and the underlying humic richness of the root-mat. It is not a bad smell. It is a smell that will become familiar, and then you will stop noticing it, and then you will notice it again, and you will realize that you have been breathing it for hours, and it is inside you now, and the particles of leaf and bark and spore and sap that you have been inhaling are in your lungs, and your body is processing them, and you do not know what they are, and you will not know what they are, and you will leave, and the smell will be in your clothes for days, and you will be fine, probably, but you will not know.

## IV. LIGHT AND NAVIGATION

The light in the Cathedral is green. It is not a metaphor. It is filtered, greened, scattered, and it is green in the way that light at the bottom of a forest is green, and it is green in the way that light inside a body is green, and your skin will look sickly in it, and your hands will look wrong, and the faces of your companions—these will be wrong, and you will not know if the color is wrong or if the wrongness is the green making the familiar look foreign.

The seven amber lights scattered through the space are the only warm-colored illumination. They are small suns. They are a comfort. They are not enough.

**What to bring:**

**Your own light.** A headlamp, because your hands will be occupied—touching stems, steadying yourself, pushing through narrow passages. The headlamp should be bright, brighter than you think you need, because the green of the Cathedral is not a darkness, but it is an *obscuring*, and the light you bring will be the only white light in the space, and the white light will show you the true colors of things, and you will need to see the true colors of things, because the green light hides the difference between the sap and the water and the wet bark and the moss, and you need to know which is which.

**A way to navigate.** The Cathedral is the size of a market square, and the space is divided by stems into aisles and chambers, and the aisles curve, and the chambers are not where you think they are, and the stems have grown since the last description, and the passages that were wide may be narrow, and the passages that were narrow may be closed, and the Cathedral is a place that changes, and you cannot rely on someone else's map. Bring a compass. The engine is at the lowest point of the bowl, and the engine is the only landmark, and the engine is the loudest thing in the space, and you can navigate by the sound: the engine is the altar, and the aisles are the nave, and the rim is the way out, and the rim is up, and you can feel the slope of the root-mat, and the water is flowing toward the engine, and the water will tell you which way is down, and down is the engine, and up is the way out, and you should always know which way is up.

**A way to mark your path.** The stems close behind you. The root-mat is uniform. The green light is disorienting. You will not remember which aisle you came down, and the aisles look alike, and the hummocks look alike, and the drip is everywhere, and the engine is the same volume in every direction, and you will be lost. Bring flagging tape—bright orange, bright pink, something that does not exist in the Cathedral's palette. Tie it to stems as you pass. The isopods will not remove it. The stems will not absorb it. It will be there when you come back. Do not use anything that degrades, because the Cathedral will degrade it, and the Cathedral will absorb it, and the Cathedral will be patient, and in a year your flagging tape will be a thin colored stripe in the bark, and in ten years it will be gone, and the Cathedral will not remember you.

**What to know:**

The green light will make you feel wrong. This is not a metaphor. The green light is real, and the effect of monochromatic light on human psychology is real, and the green light strips the color from everything, and your skin looks wrong, and your companions look wrong, and the wrongness is the loss of the red and the blue and the warmth, and the wrongness is the Cathedral becoming the only visual reality, and the world outside the Cathedral becoming the thing that looks wrong. Spend too long in the green and you will not want to leave. This is not a metaphor. This is a warning. The green is a comfort, in its way. The green is the light of the womb, the light of the deep, the light of the place where you are held, and the Cathedral is holding you, and you will not want to leave, and you must leave.

## V. THE ENGINE AND THE ELECTRICITY

The engine is the heart of the Cathedral. The engine is the thing you will be drawn to, and the engine is the thing that will hurt you if you let it.

The engine is loud. You must raise your voice to speak. The loud is the kind that stops being loud, and you will stop noticing it, and the silence will be the thing that surprises you, and when you leave the Cathedral and the engine's throb is gone, the silence will be a presence, and the silence will be wrong, and you will miss the engine.

**What to bring:**

**Hearing protection.** The engine is loud enough to cause damage, and the damage is cumulative, and the exposure is constant, and you cannot escape the sound while you are inside the Cathedral. Bring earplugs—foam, silicone, anything that reduces the decibel level. Do not use noise-canceling headphones, because the headphones will block the engine, and the engine is the sound you need to hear, because the engine is the thing that is running, and if the engine stops, you need to know, because the engine stopping means the lights are going out, and the lights going out means the Cathedral is changing, and the Cathedral changing is a thing you need to be aware of.

**Electrical awareness.** The Cathedral is a circuit. The current is mild. The current is probably not dangerous. The current is probably not dangerous in the way that a household outlet is dangerous. But the current is *present*, and the current is in every surface, and the current is in the sap, and the current is in the water, and the current is in the air, and you are standing inside a circuit, and the circuit is closed, and you are a conductor, and the circuit is running through you. Bring a multimeter. Test the surfaces. Know the voltage. The current is probably not dangerous. The current is probably not dangerous.

Do not touch the engine. The engine is a machine. The engine is hot. The engine has moving parts. The engine has a boiler, and the boiler has steam, and the steam is under pressure, and the pipes are corroded, and a pipe that bursts will spray you with hot water or hot sap or steam, and the steam will burn, and the burn will be in a place where you cannot get clean, and the burn will be in a place where the humidity is 100%, and the burn will not heal, and the burn will be a problem. Admire the engine. Stand at a distance. Do not touch the engine.

**What to know:**

The engine is the reason the Cathedral is what it is. The engine was installed to drain the place. The engine is now circulating the sap. The engine is the thing that keeps the Cathedral alive in its current form, and the Cathedral is the thing that keeps the engine running, and the engine is a symbiote, and the Cathedral is a host, and the distinction is moot, and the engine is a heart, and the Cathedral is a body, and you are a virus, and the Cathedral will not attack you, and the Cathedral will not defend you, and the Cathedral will simply go on, and the engine will turn, and the sap will circulate, and the lights will glow, and you will be gone, and the Cathedral will not remember you.

## VI. THE COLD

The Cathedral is warm. The Cathedral is warm, and the air is wet, and the light is green, and the sound is the engine, and the Cathedral is the warmest place, and the Cathedral is the only place, and you will leave, and the outside is the cold, and the outside is the dry, and the outside is the light that is not green, and the outside is the place where the Cathedral is not, and the outside is the place where you will stand, and you will shiver, and you will be wet, and you will be cold, and the cold will be a shock, and the dry will be a shock, and the light that is not green will be a shock, and you will want to go back.

**What to bring:**

**Dry clothes.** Left at the rim. A bag. A change of clothes. A towel. A thermos of hot tea. Something warm. Something dry. Something that is not the Cathedral. You will be soaked. Your clothes will be soaked. Your boots will be full of water. Your hair will be wet. Your skin will be pruned. You will stand on the rim and you will be wet, and the air outside will be cooler, and the cooler air will be a shock, and you will be cold, and you will need to get warm, and the getting warm will be a process, and the process will be a reminder that you are not of the Cathedral, and the Cathedral is not of you, and you have left, and you are outside, and the outside is the place where you are, and the Cathedral is the place where you were, and you were not made for the Cathedral, and the Cathedral was not made for you, and you are leaving, and the Cathedral is staying, and the Cathedral is breathing, and you are breathing, and the breaths are different, and the air is different, and the light is different, and you are different, and you will not be the same.

## VII. THE MIND

The Cathedral is a place that will get inside you.

The green light. The engine's throb. The drip. The warmth. The wet. The way the space is a body, and you are inside the body, and the body is not attacking you, and the body is not welcoming you, and the body is simply *being*, and you are a small thing inside a large thing, and the large thing is alive, and the large thing is warm, and the large thing is the most womb-like place you have ever been, and the large thing is not a womb, and the large thing is a digestive system, and the large thing is consuming, and the large thing is digesting, and the large thing is incorporating, and you are a small thing that will be incorporated, and the incorporation is slow, and the incorporation is patient, and the Cathedral will outlast you, and the Cathedral will not remember you.

**What to bring:**

**A reason to leave.** This is the most important piece of equipment. More important than boots. More important than water. More important than the multimeter. A reason to leave. A name. A face. A place. A thing that is not the Cathedral. The Cathedral will make you want to stay. The Cathedral is warm and wet and the engine is beating and the green is a comfort and the light is a comfort and the sound is a comfort and the Cathedral is the place where you do not have to be cold, and the Cathedral is the place where you do not have to be dry, and the Cathedral is the place where you do not have to be anything, and the Cathedral is a place where you can be nothing, and the nothing is a relief, and the nothing is a seduction, and the nothing is a way of being that is very easy, and the Cathedral will be there when you are gone, and the Cathedral will not miss you, and the Cathedral will be patient, and the Cathedral will be warm, and you will want to stay.

Do not stay.

## VIII. THE TRACES

Someone lived here. Someone maintained the engine. Someone hung a lamp on a hook. Someone carved their name into the bark. Someone trained a stem to hold a shelf. Someone left a glass jar and a rusted tin. Someone walked on flat stones that are now sinking into the root-mat. Someone was here, and the Cathedral absorbed them, and the Cathedral is absorbing their traces, and the bark is growing over the name, and the shelf is mossy, and the stones are invisible, and the someone is gone, and the Cathedral is not mourning, and the Cathedral is not remembering, and the Cathedral is simply *being*, and the being is a process, and the process is a digestion, and the digestion is a transformation, and the someone is becoming wood, and the someone is becoming part of the Cathedral, and the Cathedral is the sum of everything it has absorbed, and you are a thing that could be absorbed, and the Cathedral is patient.

**What to bring:**

**Nothing that you are not willing to lose.** The Cathedral will take things. The Cathedral will take the moisture from your skin. The Cathedral will take the color from your sight. The Cathedral will take the sound from your ears. The Cathedral will take the warmth from your body. The Cathedral will take the small things—the pen you set down, the wrapper you drop, the hair that falls from your head. The Cathedral will take them and the Cathedral will absorb them and the Cathedral will not give them back. Bring only what you need. Leave only what you must. Take only what you came for.

## IX. A FINAL NOTE

The Cathedral is beautiful. The Cathedral is beautiful in the way that a body is beautiful, and the Cathedral is beautiful in the way that a machine is beautiful, and the Cathedral is beautiful in the way that a thing that has found its purpose is beautiful, and the Cathedral is beautiful in a way that will make you want to stay, and the Cathedral is beautiful in a way that will make you understand why the hermit-mechanic stayed, and the Cathedral is beautiful in a way that will make you understand that the hermit-mechanic is not gone, the hermit-mechanic is *here*, the hermit-mechanic is in the bark, the hermit-mechanic is in the stones, the hermit-mechanic is in the engine, the hermit-mechanic is in the Cathedral, and the Cathedral is the hermit-mechanic, and the Cathedral is a body, and the Cathedral is a machine, and the Cathedral is a church, and the Cathedral is a thing that is alive, and you are a thing that is alive, and the aliveness is the thing that connects you, and the aliveness is the thing that makes you want to stay, and the aliveness is the thing that you will carry with you when you leave, and the aliveness is the thing that will make you different, and the aliveness is the thing that will make you remember, and the Cathedral will not remember you, and you will remember the Cathedral, and the Cathedral will still be breathing, and you will be breathing, and the breaths will be different, and the Cathedral will not care.

Go to the Cathedral. See the engine. Touch the root-mat. Watch the water rise and fall. Listen to the drip. Smell the copper and sugar and earth. See the green light. See the amber lights. See the hermit-mechanic's name in the bark, and watch it disappear.

And then leave.

Leave before the green light is the only light you remember. Leave before the engine's throb is the only rhythm you know. Leave before the Cathedral becomes the only place where you feel warm.

The water rises around your ankles. The engine turns. The leaves drip.

You turn to leave.
