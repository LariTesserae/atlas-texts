# Preparation Guide: The Peneplain

## You are not ready for the sound

Let me begin with what no one will tell you because no one who has been there remembers to warn you about it properly: the sound will make you doubt your sanity.

Not immediately. When you first step onto the laterite crust — assuming you arrive during daylight, which you should strongly attempt — the sound seems manageable. Frogs. Insects. Wind. You have heard these things before. You will think you understand what you are hearing. You do not.

The frog chorus at dusk is not a sound event. It is a *weather event*. It rises from every pan simultaneously, from seeps you cannot see, from cracks in the crust you would not notice, and it arrives as a single wall of vibration that you will feel in your sternum before you fully process it with your ears. Conversation becomes impossible. Thought becomes difficult. The sound does not taper or modulate in the way you expect a chorus to — it sustains, it *holds*, and it holds for hours, and when it briefly dips, it returns louder, as though the rain has reminded the frogs of some essential duty they must perform at volumes that seem evolutionarily unnecessary.

Bring ear protection. Not foam plugs — those will help, but they will not suffice. Bring over-ear protection rated for sustained industrial noise. Bring multiple options. You will want to cycle between them because each method of dampening creates its own enclosed sensation, and after three days on the Peneplain, any enclosed sensation that you did not choose will begin to feel like a trap.

And know this: the ear protection will not be enough. The sound conducts through the ground. Through the water. Through your own skeleton. You will hear the frogs with your teeth. You will hear them with your kneecaps. The insects at night are a different order of sound — thinner, more sustained, a drone that contains layers upon layers if you can bear to listen closely, which you cannot for more than a few minutes because the volume is oppressive and because listening closely to the Peneplain's insects is a fast path to a particular kind of dissociation where you begin to hear patterns that may or may not be there, messages in the rhythm, and this is the landscape working on you, and you should resist it, or at least notice it.

## Your feet are your primary negotiation

The laterite crust is not passive terrain. It is a living participant in your experience, and it changes its behavior based on temperature.

In the morning, before the sun has fully committed, the crust rings dully underfoot. It is hard. It is resonant. Each step announces itself. You will feel like an intruder simply by walking — the ground objects to you with a hollow metallic sound, and the sound carries, because there is nothing to stop it. The crust does not want to yield. It has spent longer than you can imagine not yielding.

By midday, the surface becomes almost tacky. Not mud — the description is careful about this and you should be careful about it too — but that state between solid and yielding that makes each footstep slightly uncertain. Your foot does not sink. It does not quite slip. But it *settles*, micro-adjustingly, and you will find yourself making small corrections you did not plan, and these small corrections accumulate in your calves and your lower back and your attention until by midafternoon you are simply *tired* in a way that has nothing to do with distance walked.

Your boots must handle both states. Stiff soles for the morning ring, when the hard crust will transmit every pebble and irregularity directly into your foot bones. Flexible uppers for the midday softening, when your foot will need to make those micro-adjustments without fighting against the boot's structure. Ankle support, because the transitions between crust and pan edge are irregular and the tacky surface will try to hold your foot while your momentum carries your leg forward, and the Peneplain is not a place where you want to sprain an ankle, because the sound of your cry for help will be carried perfectly across a landscape that has nothing and no one in it to receive that cry.

Waterproofing is irrelevant. Your feet will be wet. Accept this. The water is warm as blood, and wading through the pans is not optional — they shift, they merge, what was a path between pans yesterday is a pan today. Waterproof boots will simply fill with warm water and then hold that water against your skin until you develop maceration, then blisters, then infection. Instead: quick-draining footwear worn with synthetic socks that manage moisture. Remove your footwear at every extended stop. Air your feet. The Peneplain's water is warm and mineral-rich and will prune your skin rapidly and then damage it if you do not allow it to dry.

## The heat is not extreme. The heat is inexorable.

Do not confuse this place with a desert. The temperature does not spike to killing levels. It does not need to. It simply *stays*.

Warm when you wake. Warm through the morning. Warm at midday. Warm through the afternoon. Warm at dusk. Warm through the night. The laterite crust holds heat for hours after sunset. The water in the pans is always warm. The air sits, holds moisture, cannot evaporate efficiently because it is already saturated, cannot cool because there is no elevation, no breeze that has not already been warmed by crossing kilometers of hot flat ground.

Your body cannot shed heat through evaporation here. The humidity is too high. You will sweat. The sweat will not cool you. It will simply accumulate, mix with the mineral residue of the pans, form a thin crust on your skin that is not chemically different from the laterite under your feet.

You must drink water. You must drink more water than you think you need, because the heat steals moisture from you without the normal warning signs — you will not feel the cooling of evaporation, you will not feel the relief, you will simply become slowly dehydrated while feeling vaguely unwell, and then suddenly you will be in trouble.

Bring more water than you think you need. Then bring more than that. The Peneplain's water is present everywhere but drinkable nowhere — the shallow pans are warm, mineral-rich, full of algae and microorganisms and the waste of a billion frogs. The water table is close but contaminated by the same laterite chemistry that has stripped the soil above it. You cannot filter this water safely with portable equipment. You cannot boil away its mineral load. You must bring what you need and you must ration it with the understanding that the Peneplain will take more from you than you expect.

Clothing: loose, light-colored, covering as much skin as possible. Not because the sun is extreme — it is equatorial but the humidity diffuses it — but because the insects require it, and because any exposed skin will be subject to the slow mineral assault of evaporated pan water, which will leave you feeling sealed in a thin film of the place itself. You do not want the Peneplain crust forming on your skin. It is remarkably difficult to wash off.

## Navigation is an existential problem

The Peneplain has no landmarks.

Let me be precise about what this means. The low rises — ten meters, fifteen meters, over kilometers — are not landmarks. They do not function as reference points because they do not break the horizon in a way that allows you to fix your position relative to them. Walk toward a rise and it recedes, or rather it does not recede but it does not *arrive*, because its slope is so gradual that you cannot tell when you have begun climbing it, and when you reach its crest the view is identical to the view from its base, and you have gained nothing but ten meters of elevation over a walk that took an hour, and the horizon looks exactly the same.

The pans shift. They merge after rain. They contract in dry periods. A pan that was a hectare yesterday is a hundred hectares today, and the route you memorized around its edge is underwater, and the underwater route is shin-deep and warm and populated by frogs and that small crocodilian that you were warned about and probably cannot see.

You cannot navigate by the sun reliably because the heat haze distorts angles near the horizon and because the sun's arc is so high and so consistent that it provides very little directional information for most of the day.

You must bring a compass. You must bring a GPS device if your multiverse transit allows electronic equipment to function — test this before you arrive, because the Peneplain is the kind of place where technology might simply decide to stop working out of solidarity with the landscape's ancient refusal to acknowledge progress. You must bring a physical map, but understand that the map will be wrong, because any map of the Peneplain is a snapshot of water levels at a particular moment, and the water levels change.

The most reliable navigation method is dead reckoning combined with careful attention to the vegetation. The sparse plants — the low spreading things, the thick-trunked trees — are individually distinct enough to serve as landmarks if you can find them again. Mark your path. Use cairns if you can build them (the laterite crust provides fragments that can be stacked, though they ring when you move them, and the sound will alert every frog within earshot, which is every frog on the plain). Leave markers at regular intervals. Do not trust your memory, because the flatness and the sound and the heat will erode your confidence in your own navigation, and you will begin to doubt whether you have moved at all.

## The animals are not the danger. Your reaction to the animals is the danger.

The small crocodilian — forearm-length, rarely seen — is not a threat to a healthy adult who is paying attention. It is slow, it is cautious, and it has been hunting frogs in warm water for longer than your species has existed. It knows its niche. You are not in that niche. Leave it alone and it will leave you alone, watching with a stillness that should disturb you more than any threat display would.

The snakes are fast and pale and thin. They hunt frogs. They are not venomous in the way that will kill you, though they may be venomous in the way that will make you wish you were dead for several hours. Watch where you step in the shade of the sparse plants. Do not reach into cracks in the laterite. Do not put your hands where you cannot see them.

The insects are the real concern. Not because they bite — many do, and the bites are irritating and numerous and will accumulate into a general misery that you must manage with antihistamines and willpower — but because of their *presence*. The clouds over the pans are thick enough to cast shadows. You will walk through swarms. You will inhale them if you breathe through your mouth. You will find them in your ears, your nose, the corners of your eyes. A fine mesh head net is not optional. A mesh that you can see through, that you can breathe through, that seals at the neck and at the wrists. You will look absurd. You will not care, because the alternative is breathing insects, and the insects are relentless.

The frogs are not a danger. The frogs are the *experience*. But they will startle you. When you step near a pan edge and the water erupts with dozens of small bodies launching in every direction, you will flinch. You will curse. Your heart rate will spike. And then it will happen again, and again, and again, and eventually you will stop flinching, and you will have adapted, and you will understand something about the Peneplain that you could not have understood before: this is a place where life has adapted to such a degree that your presence is simply another stimulus, another rainfall, another warm body moving through a landscape that does not care about you.

The herons — the tall, still, patient heron-like things — will watch you. They will stand in the pans and they will not move, and their stillness is not fear. It is assessment. They are determining whether you are hunting the same frogs they are hunting. You are not. They will determine this. They will continue not moving. They are very good at not moving. They have been not moving for longer than your species has been moving. Let them watch. You are not interesting to them for long.

## Psychological preparation: what the Peneplain will do to you

This is the hardest part to prepare for, because no amount of physical preparation will address it and no amount of psychological forewarning will prevent it.

The Peneplain will make you feel small. Not small in the way that mountains make you feel small — that is a smallness of scale, of mass, of visual overwhelm. The Peneplain will make you feel *temporarily* small. You are a brief event. You are a flicker. You are a process that will conclude while the land is still in the middle of its own process, and the land will not notice your conclusion, and this is not a metaphor — the land genuinely will not notice, because the land has been here for a length of time that makes the word *been* seem hasty.

The flatness will erode your sense of progress. You will walk for hours and the horizon will not change. The low rises will approach and recede and you will crest them and see exactly what you saw before. You will wade through pans and walk across crust and pass the same species of plant at the same regular intervals and the frogs will roar and the insects will drone and the sun will move across the sky and set and rise and the frogs will roar again and nothing will have changed. You will begin to wonder if you have moved at all. You will begin to wonder if movement is possible. You will begin to wonder if you have always been standing in this warm water with this sound vibrating through your chest, and if the memory of arriving is a fiction you invented to explain why you are here.

This is normal. This is the Peneplain working on you. It is not a danger, but it is a *force*, and you should respect it the way you respect a current in the ocean — not by fighting it, but by acknowledging it and making your plans accordingly.

Bring something to do. A book, if your eyes can focus on it through the insect mesh and the heat shimmer and the sound. A journal, if you can make your hands work in the humidity. A project — counting frogs, mapping pans, cataloging the plants at their regular intervals. The Peneplain offers very little stimulation that is not auditory or thermal, and your brain will invent stimulation if it is not provided. Do not let your brain invent stimulation. The things your brain will invent on the Peneplain are not things you want to carry home with you.

Set limits. Decide in advance how long you will stay. Tell someone — someone who is not on the Peneplain — when you expect to leave. The Peneplain does not trap people physically. It traps them temporally. You will lie in your tent at night, and the frogs will roar, and the insects will pulse, and the laterite crust will still be warm under your tent floor, and the stars will reflect in the pans outside with a clarity that no mountain lake has ever matched, and you will think: *one more day*. And then: *one more day*. And the days will begin to blur, because the Peneplain does not believe in days, it believes in cycles, and the cycles are so regular and so similar that you will lose count.

This is how people stay. This is how people become part of the place — not dramatically, not poetically, but gradually, the way the land itself has become what it is, by being worn down by the accumulated patience of a place that does not need you.

## Specific packing list

**Essential:**
- Water. More than you think. Then more than that. Minimum 5 liters per person per day.
- Over-ear hearing protection, rated for sustained noise, plus foam inserts for layered protection.
- Full-body insect mesh — head net, separate mesh for sleeping, sealed at all entry points.
- Quick-draining footwear with stiff soles and flexible uppers. No waterproof boots. No sandals — the laterite crust will cut you, and the small crocodilian, while not a threat, still has teeth.
- Loose, light-colored, full-coverage clothing. Synthetic, breathable, multiple layers you can add or remove as the temperature stays exactly the same but your perception of it shifts.
- Wide-brimmed hat. Not for fashion. For the insects. They will land on any horizontal surface.
- Compass. Physical. Tested. Plus backup compass.
- GPS device, if functional. Plus physical maps of the region, with the understanding that the maps are approximations.
- Sunscreen, high SPF, reapplied hourly. The equatorial light is diffused but not diminished.
- First aid kit with particular attention to: antihistamines for insect bites, antiseptic for skin maceration and minor cuts, rehydration salts, broad-spectrum antibiotics (the water in the pans contains organisms your immune system has never encountered, and minor wounds will fester in this climate).
- Headlamp with red filter. White light at night will attract insects in numbers that are genuinely frightening. Red light is less attractive. No light is least attractive, but then you cannot see the snakes.

**Recommended:**
- A lightweight sitting pad. The laterite crust is hot, irregular, and rings when you sit on it. A pad provides insulation, comfort, and silence.
- A journal. Record your thoughts before the Peneplain rewrites them.
- A thermometer and hygrometer. Not because you need to know the exact temperature. Because having a number to look at will help you remember that the heat is real and not something your mind is generating.
- A physical token from somewhere else. A photograph, a stone, a letter. Something that proves other places exist. You will need this proof around day three.
- Earplugs designed for sleep. The frog chorus peaks at dusk and dawn but continues through the night at a volume that will make sleep difficult. The insects take over after the frogs reduce, and their drone is sustained and layered and will find its way into your dreams.
- A lightweight tarp or shade structure. There is no shade on the Peneplain except what the sparse plants provide, and they do not provide much, and what they provide is shared with frogs and snakes and insects that were there first.

**Do not bring:**
- A tent with a rain fly that makes noise in the wind. The wind is not strong, but the sound carries, and you do not want to add to the Peneplain's soundscape. It will already be difficult to hear yourself think.
- Cotton clothing. It will not dry. It will hold moisture against your skin. It will become part of the Peneplain's water cycle and you will not be able to remove it.
- Expectations of solitude. The Peneplain is not empty. It is abundantly, overwhelmingly, oppressively populated — just not by anything that speaks your language or cares about your presence.
- A sense of purpose. The Peneplain has been here for longer than purpose has existed. It will not validate your reasons for visiting. It will not provide reasons for staying. It will simply be, and you will have to find your own meaning in the warm water and the ringing crust and the sound that does not stop.

## Final notes

The Peneplain is not dangerous in the way that mountains are dangerous or oceans are dangerous. It will not kill you quickly. It will not kill you slowly, if you are prepared and careful. It will simply persist at you, continuously, at a volume and temperature and flatness that are exactly calibrated to wear away whatever you have brought with you — your energy, your attention, your sense of time, your conviction that you are the kind of person who visits places like this and leaves unchanged.

You will not leave unchanged. No one does. The question is whether the change will be manageable — whether you will come away with an appreciation for deep time and ancient patience and the irreducible minimum of what a place can be, or whether you will come away with the sound of frogs permanently etched into your hearing, a lingering sensation of warm water against your ankles, and the unshakeable feeling that you are still standing on that plain, still listening, still waiting for something that was finished before you arrived.

Stay as long as you need. Not longer. The Peneplain will still be here when you leave, and it will be here in exactly the same condition, and that is not a promise of welcome — it is a statement of fact. The Peneplain does not welcome. The Peneplain does not dismiss. The Peneplain continues, and your visit is a brief, warm, ringing interruption in a process that will still be continuing when the memory of your species has been leached from the soil like every other soluble thing.

Drink your water. Watch your step. Listen — you have no choice.

And when the pale succulent blooms at the pan's edge — if it blooms, which it may not, for you — kneel down and look at the flower. It is the size of a child's hand. It is the brightest thing on the plain. It blooms irregularly, triggered by conditions no one has mapped. It will not bloom for you specifically. It will bloom because the moisture and the heat have aligned in a way that it recognizes, and it will be white and sudden and alone, and it will be the only thing on the Peneplain that is not ancient and not patient and not finished — it will be *beginning*, briefly, impossibly, in a place where nothing has begun for longer than anything has been anything.

That flower is the Peneplain's secret. Not that it is old. Not that it is flat. Not that it is loud. But that even here — especially here — something new can happen.
