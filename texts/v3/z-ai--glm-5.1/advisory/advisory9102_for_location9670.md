# Preparation for the Wind-Sheared Heights

## I. Understanding What You Are Walking Into

Read the description again. Now understand that the description has failed, because language always fails when describing an environment that attacks the body before the mind can process it. You will arrive and the Sound will hit you like a wall. Not sound—*pressure*. A continuous physical force entering through your sternum, vibrating your teeth until you taste metal, rattling your long bones until they feel like tuning forks struck against stone. Your first instinct will be to cover your ears. This will do nothing. The Sound is already inside you.

You will want to leave immediately. This is the correct impulse. Override it only if you have reason to stay.

The Wind-Sheared Heights are not a destination. They are an *ordeal* that you move through, and they will treat you as they treat everything else that enters them: as a temporary pressure, a passing disturbance, something that moves through and is gone. The place does not accommodate. It does not provide. It does not notice you. Every preparation you make is in service of surviving an environment that is functionally indifferent to your presence.

## II. Physical Preparation Before Arrival

### Lung Capacity and Altitude Acclimation

The elevation here is extreme. The sky thins. The sun takes on a white violence. You will be functioning at altitude with less oxygen than your body wants, and you will be doing this while under constant physical assault from wind and sound. If you have any capacity to acclimate before arrival, do so. Spend time at altitude. Let your body increase its red blood cell production. Learn what it feels like to think slowly, to have your cognition thinned along with the air.

If your multiverse transit method allows you to choose your arrival point, *do not arrive at the top*. Arrive low and climb. Give your body days to adjust. The travelers who die at altitude are the ones who go from sea level to summit in an hour. Their lungs fill with fluid. Their brains swell. They die gasping in a place where the Sound swallows even the sound of drowning.

### Physical Conditioning

You will need to be stronger than you think you need to be. Walking in constant wind is walking against a horizontal force that never relents. Every step requires more effort than the step before it. Your legs will burn. Your lower back will ache from leaning into the wind. Your core will be engaged constantly as your body tries to stay upright in a medium that wants to push you over and carry you away like dust.

Condition your legs, your core, your wind (in the pulmonary sense). You will be exerting yourself at altitude in extreme conditions. The fitness level required is not marathon-level but it is well above sedentary. You should be able to walk uphill for hours with a heavy pack. You should be able to do this while partially deafened and half-blind.

### Mental Conditioning for Sensory Assault

The Sound will not stop. It will not fade into background. It will become the background—the *substrate* of all thought—but it will not stop. You will think around it. You will accommodate it the way you accommodate a headache, and like a headache, it will slowly drain your cognitive resources. You will be less intelligent here. Your working memory will be reduced. Your emotional regulation will be strained. You will make worse decisions because a significant portion of your processing capacity is being used simply to exist in the Sound.

Practice thinking in noise. Practice thinking in discomfort. Sit in a room with loud continuous sound—a fan, white noise, industrial machinery—and try to perform complex cognitive tasks. Notice how much harder it is. Notice how tired you become. That is a fraction of what the Heights will demand.

The light is the second assault. Practice being in bright environments without relief. Wear sunglasses less. Let your eyes adjust to glare. Understand that at the Heights, even closing your eyes will not save you—the light will find the gaps around your lids and the red of your lids will glow like embers. You will need to function while visually compromised.

## III. What to Bring

### Eye Protection: The Most Critical Equipment

You need goggles, not glasses. Sunglasses will not work. The wind will tear them from your face, and the light will enter from the sides and from above and from below, reflected off the pale stone and the pale dust until you are standing inside a sphere of white violence.

Bring multiple pairs of high-altitude mountaineering goggles with the following specifications:
- **Category 4 lenses** (transmitting only 3-8% of visible light) for the brightest hours
- **Category 3 lenses** for the transitional periods when full darkness is too much but the light is still dangerous
- **Full seal** around the eyes—foam or silicone gasket, no gaps
- **Strap**, not temples—secured firmly around your head, not resting on your ears
- **Anti-fog coating** or ventilation system, because your breath and sweat will otherwise condense inside the goggles and you will be blind in a different way

Bring at least two pairs of each. The wind will destroy equipment. The grit will scratch lenses. You will drop things and they will be gone before you can reach for them, carried away by the constant air or buried in dust. Redundancy is survival.

Bring a wide-brimmed hat that ties securely under your chin. The brim will shade your goggles and reduce the total light reaching your eyes. The tie will prevent the hat from becoming a distant object tumbling across the gravel.

### Wind Protection: The Second Most Critical Equipment

The wind is not weather. It is *geology*. It is a continuous physical force that has shaped stone for longer than anything living can comprehend. It will treat your body the way it treats everything: as something to be moved, abraded, stripped, carried.

You need **windproof** clothing, not merely wind-resistant. The difference matters. Wind-resistant fabric slows the wind; windproof fabric stops it. At the Heights, the wind will find any gap, any seam, any porous surface, and it will push through and steal your heat and dry your skin and carry your warmth away into the emptiness.

Your layers should be:
1. **Base layer**: Moisture-wicking, skin-tight, covering every inch of skin possible. Merino wool or synthetic. Cotton will kill you here—it holds moisture, and the wind will freeze that moisture against your skin.
2. **Insulating layer**: Fleece or down, trapping warm air. This layer must be windproof itself or protected by the outer layer, because if wind penetrates your insulation, it will carry away the warm air and replace it with cold, and you will hypothermic in minutes despite the sun.
3. **Outer shell**: Hard-faced, windproof, abrasion-resistant. This is your armor. The wind carries grit—fine particles of eroded stone that will sandblast exposed skin and abrade fabric. Your outer shell must be tough enough to withstand this. Heavy-duty mountaineering fabrics, sailcloth, military-grade textiles. Nothing delicate. Nothing fashionable. Function only.

**Cover your skin.** All of it. The wind will chap and crack exposed skin within hours. Your lips will split. Your hands will bleed. Your face will become raw and then windburned and then, if you persist, frost-nipped in the cold hours even as the sun burns it. A balaclava or hood that seals to your goggles. Gloves that tuck under your sleeves. Socks that tuck under your pants. No gaps. The wind will find any gap and exploit it.

**Reinforce your closures.** Zippers fail. Buttons fail. Velcro clogs with dust and fails. Use overlapping closures—zippers covered by flaps, secured by snaps or buckles. Tie drawstrings tight. The wind will test every seam.

### Hearing Protection: Necessary and Inadequate

Bring earplugs. The highest-rated foam or silicone earplugs you can find. NRR 33 or equivalent. Wear them constantly.

Understand that they will not be enough. The Sound enters through your chest, through your bones, through the vibration of your skull. Earplugs will reduce the ear-canal component, and this will help—this will give you a fraction of your cognitive capacity back—but it will not stop the Sound. You will still feel it. You will still think around it. The earplugs will make the difference between "unable to function" and "barely able to function," and at the Heights, that difference is everything.

Do not use noise-canceling headphones. They require power, they are bulky, they will be destroyed by the grit, and they cannot cancel a sound that is entering your skeleton. Simple passive earplugs. Many pairs. You will lose them. You will need more.

### Footwear: What Keeps You Standing

The ground is eroded sediment, bare gravel, wind-rippled dust, and stone. The stone is polished smooth as glass on its windward faces. The gravel shifts underfoot. The dust is fine and will fill your shoes if you let it.

You need boots with:
- **Ankle support**: You will be walking on uneven ground while leaning into wind. You will twist an ankle without support, and a twisted ankle here is a death sentence because you cannot rest and you cannot be carried.
- **Stiff soles**: For walking on stone and gravel. Soft soles will transmit every point of pressure through your foot and will allow stones to bruise your soles through the shoe.
- **Deep lugs**: For grip on the polished stone surfaces. The wind-smoothed rock is genuinely slippery, like wet glass, and a fall could send you sliding or tumbling into the emptiness between formations.
- **Gaiters**: Sealed over the top of your boots to prevent dust and grit from entering. The fine sediment here will find any opening and fill it, and walking with a cup of abrasive dust in each boot will destroy your feet within a day.

### Water: The Logistics of Survival

There is no reliable water here. The condensation cycle—night-wet, day-dry—cannot be harvested in quantities sufficient to sustain a human. You cannot lick enough moisture from stone to survive. The brief afternoon rains are unpredictable and the runoff is fleeting, lasting minutes before disappearing.

**You must carry all the water you will need.**

Calculate your water needs as follows: at altitude, in dry air, under constant wind, exerting yourself continuously, you will lose water faster than in almost any other environment. The wind will carry moisture from your lungs with every breath. The dry air will pull sweat from your skin even when you do not feel yourself sweating—evaporative cooling is so efficient here that you may not notice dehydration until you are already in trouble. Your minimum water requirement is **6 liters per day**. More if you are exerting heavily. More if the sun is at its worst.

Carry capacity for at least **3 days beyond your planned stay**. If you plan to stay 2 days, carry 5 days of water (30 liters minimum). The reason: if you are delayed—if you are injured, if you lose your way in the emptiness, if the wind makes travel impossible—you will need water to survive the delay. There is no backup source. There is no rescue. There is only what you carry.

30 liters of water weighs 30 kilograms. This is the math of the Heights: survival is heavy.

### Food

Bring calorie-dense, low-preparation food. You will not want to cook. Cooking requires exposing food to the wind, and the wind will carry away heat, flame, and any lightweight food item. You will not want to eat much—the altitude and the Sound will suppress your appetite—but you must eat anyway. Your body is burning calories just to stay warm and upright.

Energy bars. Nut butters. Dried meat. Cheese that keeps. Things you can eat with gloves on, without looking, without preparation, in the few seconds of shelter you can find. Avoid anything that crumbles—the wind will take the crumbs. Avoid anything that requires two hands to prepare. Avoid anything that creates waste you must carry out.

### Navigation

There are no markers here. No cairns, no paths, no signs. The emptiness is total, and the distances between features are enormous. You can walk for a kilometer across bare gravel with nothing breaking the horizontal but the wind-rippled surface of dust. It is very easy to become disoriented. It is very easy to walk in circles when the Sound is disorienting you and the light is bleaching all features into the same white-gray palette.

Bring:
- **A compass**. Not a phone app. A physical magnetic compass. Know how to use it. The compass will work here—the Heights are not described as having magnetic anomalies—but you must be able to take bearings and follow them.
- **A topographic map** if one exists. If mapping the Heights is impossible due to their inaccessibility, create your own as you go. Note the positions of major formations relative to your entry point. The formations are permanent—they do not move on human timescales—and can serve as landmarks.
- **A GPS device** if your multiverse technology includes satellite navigation or equivalent. Test it before you rely on it. Understand that electronic devices may be affected by the conditions—cold, grit, static from the wind.
- **A physical landmark strategy**: Before you venture into the emptiness between formations, identify a prominent feature—a tall pillar, a distinctive ridge—and keep it in sight at all times. When you cannot see your landmark, you are lost. Turn back.

### Shelter: The Problem With No Solution

There is no construction here. The wind takes any structure within a season. Poles bend and break. Fabric tears and carries away. Stone stacked by hand is unstacked by the persistent pressure.

You cannot bring a tent. A tent is a sail, and the wind will fill it and carry it away with you inside or strip it from its stakes and shred it across the gravel. Even mountaineering tents rated for high-altitude expeditions will fail here—they are designed for intermittent wind, not the continuous geological force that the Heights produce.

Your shelter strategy is: **use what the landscape provides**. The deep channels between formations, where the wind's force is partially deflected, are the only viable shelter. The vegetation grows there for the same reason you will shelter there—the wind is broken. Not absent, but reduced to a level where you can rest without being pushed.

Identify these channels early. Move between them. When you must rest, find a channel, find the deepest recess, and press yourself against the stone on the lee side. The stone will be cold. The condensation will wet it. You will be uncomfortable. You will rest anyway, because there is no alternative.

If you must sleep, do so in the deepest shelter you can find, in your full windproof gear, with your water and supplies secured to your body or wedged into rock crevices. The wind will test anything left loose. Do not leave anything loose.

### Additional Critical Equipment

- **Rope**: Light but strong. For securing yourself to stone formations in high wind, for lowering yourself into channels, for hauling gear. Do not use this for climbing unless you are experienced—the stone is polished and may not hold protection.
- **Multi-tool**: With pliers, knife, and scissors. For repairing gear, cutting fabric, removing wind-blown debris.
- **Duct tape**: Wrapped around your water bottles to save space. For emergency gear repair. The wind will damage your equipment. You will need to repair it.
- **Sunscreen**: SPF 50+, applied thickly and frequently to any exposed skin. The UV at this altitude is extreme. Reapply every two hours. Do not forget the underside of your chin and nose—the light reflects off the pale stone and comes from below.
- **Lip balm**: With SPF. Your lips will crack without it, and cracked lips in continuous wind will become bleeding wounds.
- **Headlamp**: For the night. The darkness here is total—no ambient light, no reflected city glow, just the stars in a darkness so complete it feels like falling. You will need your hands free. A headlamp with a red-light mode to preserve night vision.
- **First aid kit**: Focused on trauma (cuts, abrasions, sprains) and environmental injury (hypothermia, heat stroke, altitude sickness, sunburn). Include bandages that will adhere to wind-chapped skin—this is harder than it sounds. Include painkillers. Include anti-inflammatory medication. You will be in pain.
- **Signal mirror**: For emergency signaling. If you are stranded, a signal mirror can catch the sun and flash for kilometers. The emptiness means the flash will be visible at great distance—to anyone who happens to be present, which is unlikely.

## IV. How to Move

### The Crouch

Observe the goats. They move through the wind in a crouch, their bodies low, their weight always on the windward side of whatever they stand on. They have adapted their entire body plan to this environment. You must adapt your movement the same way.

**Stay low.** The wind is stronger the higher you stand. Lean into the wind. Keep your center of gravity forward. Walk with small steps, testing the ground before committing your weight. The sediment shifts. The stone is smooth. A fall is dangerous.

**Keep your weight on the windward side.** If you are standing on a ridge with the wind coming from the left, keep your weight left. If a gust pushes you, it pushes you into the ground, not away from it. The animals have learned this. Learn from them.

**Move in the shelter of formations when possible.** The channels between pillars are routes—use them. The wind is reduced there, the walking is easier, and the vegetation indicates where moisture (and therefore slight cooling) exists. But be aware: the channels are also traps in sudden weather. If rain comes, the water will funnel through those channels in fast, shallow streams that last minutes and disappear. Do not be in a narrow channel when rain arrives.

**Do not run.** Running in wind is inefficient—you lean too far forward, you lose balance, a sudden lull drops you on your face. Walk. Walk steadily. Conserve your energy. You will need it.

### Navigation While Moving

Because you cannot think clearly in the Sound, you must make your navigation decisions *before* you start moving. At the start of each movement segment, while you are in a sheltered channel and your cognition is at its best, determine your next heading. Identify the landmark you are walking toward. Fix it in your mind. Then move.

Do not change your heading while exposed to the wind. Your judgment is impaired. Your spatial reasoning is reduced. If you realize you are off-course, find shelter first, then reassess.

### What to Do If You Lose Your Way

Stop. Find shelter. Do not wander. Wandering in the Heights, disoriented by the Sound and the light and the emptiness, is how people die. They walk in curves. They walk until they are exhausted. They lie down in the sediment and the wind covers them with dust and they become part of the geological record, just another mineral among minerals.

Find shelter. Assess your resources. Make a plan while your mind is working. Then execute the plan.

## V. The Daily Cycle

### Dawn: The Chord

If you have come to the Heights, you may have come for this. At dawn, the wind shifts direction briefly with the thermal inversion. The Sound changes pitch—drops a register, rises again—and the formations *sound*. A chord. Harmonics emerging from the interaction of air and carved stone. A deep hum you feel in your feet and in your sternum. It lasts for perhaps ten minutes as the air mass rotates, and then it is swallowed by the return of the prevailing wind.

The chord is not a message. It is a property—the inevitable sound of air moving through channels shaped by air moving through channels. But it sounds like music. It sounds like the music of the spheres, the order that emerges from physics when the physics is right.

You will want to remove your earplugs for this. You will want to hear it fully. This is a reasonable desire and a dangerous one. The Sound does not stop during the chord—it *adds* the chord. The roaring continues, and the harmonics emerge from within it, and the total volume may actually increase. Removing your earplugs for ten minutes of music in an environment that is actively damaging your hearing is a trade you can make, but make it deliberately, not impulsively.

**Sit down first.** The chord is felt in the feet and the sternum. You will be standing on stone that is vibrating at specific frequencies. The sensation is unlike anything in normal experience—the rock itself is *sounding*, and you are in contact with it. Sit. Press your hands against the stone. Let the vibration enter you through your palms and your sit bones. Close your eyes (inside your goggles). Feel the music.

The goats will not react. The birds will not change their flight patterns. You are the only thing listening. This is part of the experience: the landscape produces order, and you are the only witness, and the landscape does not care whether you witness or not.

### Day: The Assault

After the chord, the prevailing wind returns. The sun rises fully and the light becomes white violence. This is the hardest period. The Sound, the light, the wind, the dryness—all at their peak. Your job is to endure, to move between shelters, to accomplish whatever you came to accomplish, and to not die.

Drink water constantly. Eat despite your lack of appetite. Check your skin for exposed areas. Check your goggles for scratches. Rest in every sheltered channel you pass. Do not push yourself. The Heights will push you; you do not need to help.

### Afternoon: The Rain (If It Comes)

In certain seasons, brief rains come in the afternoon. Sudden storms that dump water onto packed sediment. The water cannot soak in and runs off in thin temporary streams that last minutes and disappear.

If rain comes, **shelter immediately**. The rain is not the danger—the runoff is. Water funneling through the channels between formations will be fast, shallow, and powerful. It will carry loose sediment and small stones. It will knock you off your feet in a channel. Get to high ground—any raised surface, any formation's base—and wait.

The rain will end quickly. The sediment will dry with remarkable speed in the wind and sun. Within an hour, there may be no evidence that rain occurred at all.

### Night: The Condensation

The temperature drops fast in the thin air. The stone that was sun-hot becomes cold, then cold enough to hurt bare skin. Moisture gathers on the windward faces of the formations. The rocks become damp, then wet. In the pre-dawn darkness, you could press your palm against the stone and feel the cold sweat of it.

This moisture is not sufficient to harvest for drinking. Do not plan on it. But it does mean the air is slightly less dry at night, and the condensation on the stone can provide a small amount of relief to cracked lips and dry throat if you press your mouth against the wet rock. The minerals you will taste are not harmful in small quantities. The water is clean, filtered by the stone itself.

The darkness at night is total. The stars appear in a completeness that feels like falling. There is no ambient light. Your headlamp will be your only light source. **Do not move at night without a compelling reason.** The terrain is treacherous. The edges of channels are invisible. A fall in darkness could be fatal.

Sleep in the deepest shelter you can find. Sleep lightly. The Sound continues. The wind continues. You will dream of roaring.

## VI. Psychological Preparation

### The Indifference

This is the hardest thing to prepare for and the thing that will affect you most. The Heights do not perceive you. They do not accommodate you. They do not respond to you. You are a temporary pressure, a passing disturbance, something that moves through and is gone.

In most landscapes, even harsh ones, there is a sense of dialogue. You act on the environment; the environment responds. You build a fire; the fire warms you. You find shelter; the shelter holds. The environment *participates* in your survival, however grudgingly.

At the Heights, the environment does not participate. It does not oppose you—it is not hostile, which would require recognition of your existence. It is *indifferent*. The wind does not blow to hurt you. The light does not shine to blind you. The Sound does not roar to deafen you. These things simply are, and you happen to be in the way, and your presence changes nothing.

This indifference is psychologically corrosive. Humans are pattern-seeking, meaning-making creatures. We look for significance, for response, for acknowledgment. The Heights offer none. You can stand in the Sound, be deafened and blinded and abraded, and the place will simply go on being itself. Your footprints fill with dust. Your warmth dissipates. You are incorporated as the wind is incorporated—a temporary pressure, nothing more.

Some travelers find this liberating. Some find it devastating. Prepare for either reaction.

### The Equilibrium

The Heights are not changing. They are not becoming something else. They are not in process. The formations stand as they have stood. The vegetation is fixed. The animal populations are stable. The Sound is constant.

In most landscapes, you feel time passing. Here, you feel time has stopped. The present is permanent. This can produce a sense of existential vertigo—what is the point of your brief presence in a place that does not notice time? What is the point of your footprints, your warmth, your hearing of the chord, if the place will be identical whether you came or not?

There is no answer to this question. The Heights do not provide meaning. They do not provide narrative. They provide only themselves: wind, stone, light, Sound, and the brief chord at dawn.

### The Weirdness

You will notice it after you have been at the Heights long enough to accommodate the Sound, the light, the emptiness—when your senses have stopped protesting and have begun, reluctantly, to function. The formations are too *organized*. The channels are too consistent. The pillars are too similar. The wind-carved flutes align in ways that suggest pattern.

You cannot be sure. The wind has been working here for so long that any pattern you see could be the product of purely natural processes acting over deep time. But you will feel the intent. You will feel that the landscape has been shaped not only by the wind but *for* the wind, that the forms are correct for the air that moves through them.

This feeling is not dangerous in itself. But it can become a fixation. Travelers who notice the pattern begin to look for it everywhere, begin to see organization in the placement of every stone, begin to believe the landscape is *communicating*. It is not. The landscape is a machine that *is*—that exists in its current state because its state is the only stable configuration for its inputs. There is no message. There is only physics.

But the chord at dawn makes this hard to believe. The chord sounds like music. It sounds like *meaning*. And you will want to find the meaning, and you will not find it, and the search will consume you if you let it.

Do not let it. The Heights are not about you. The chord is not for you. The pattern is not a message to you. You are a temporary pressure, a passing disturbance, and the music of the spheres plays whether you listen or not.

## VII. What to Do When You Leave

When you leave the Heights, you will carry the Sound with you. Not literally—the Sound will fade as you descend, and eventually your ears will clear and your bones will stop vibrating and the world will become quiet in the way you remember quiet. But you will carry the *memory* of the Sound, and the memory will be physical. You will feel it in your chest when you think of the Heights. You will dream of roaring.

Your body will be damaged. Your skin will be windburned and sunburned and cracked. Your lips will be split. Your feet will be blistered. Your ears may ring for days—tinnitus from the continuous acoustic assault. You will be dehydrated despite your best efforts. You will be exhausted in a way that goes beyond physical fatigue, as if the Sound has drained something from you that sleep does not restore.

Rest. Hydrate. Treat your injuries. Allow yourself time to recover. Do not return to the Heights until you have healed, because the Heights will not forgive your weakness.

And know this: you will want to return. The chord at dawn. The clarity of the emptiness. The feeling of standing in a place that does not perceive you, that does not need you, that will continue unchanged whether you live or die. There is a kind of freedom in that indifference, a kind of peace in that equilibrium. You will want to return because the Heights offer something that nowhere else offers: the experience of being truly, completely irrelevant.

Some travelers find this comforting. Some find it unbearable. The Heights do not care which you are.

## VIII. Final Notes

- **Do not go alone if you can avoid it.** A partner can check your judgment, share your load, and carry you to shelter if you are injured. The Heights are survivable alone, but the margin for error is thin.
- **Do not stay longer than necessary.** Every hour in the Sound is an hour of cumulative damage to your body and mind. The Heights are not a place to linger. They are a place to move through, to witness, to survive.
- **Do not take anything.** The obsidian flakes and broken points in the sediment are residues of past passage. They are not yours. They belong to the place. Leave them.
- **Do not build anything.** The wind will take it. The place will not remember it. You will have wasted effort that you could have spent surviving.
- **Do not expect meaning.** The Heights are not a metaphor. They are not a lesson. They are not a test. They are a region of ordered forms that produce an ordered sound, a place of vast emptiness and overwhelming presence, a place that does not need you, that did not expect you, that will continue unchanged whether you are there or not.

Go prepared. Endure. Listen for the chord if you are there at dawn. Then leave, and let the wind erase your footprints, and know that you have been somewhere that did not notice you came.
