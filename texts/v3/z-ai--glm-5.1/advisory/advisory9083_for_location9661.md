# Preparation Guide: The Drowned Works

---

## I. What You Are Entering

Before you pack a single item, you need to understand what this place will do to you, because the physical challenges, while extreme, are manageable. It is the psychological landscape that unmakes people.

The Drowned Works is a built environment of continental scale, constructed by an unknown species using primitive tools over timescales that spanned millennia, now submerged beneath kilometers of ocean and colonized by alien vegetation that has had roughly two billion years to integrate itself into every surface. There is no light. There is no animal life. There is no ground—only floor, only the assembled sections of something that was *made*. The water holds at 2.4 degrees. The vegetation sways in currents that take a century to shift. The carved walls may be language. No one can read them.

You will be inside a structure that may have no boundaries. You will be inside a structure that was waiting for something before you existed, and is still waiting now. You will be inside a structure that has become landscape, that has *become* the seafloor, that has been eroding for longer than multicellular life has existed on certain worlds and has arrived at a form so stable it may persist until the star that warms this planet burns out.

Understand: you are not exploring ruins. You are inside something that has not yet finished being what it was intended to be.

## II. Physical Preparation: The Body in the Works

### Pressure and Depth

The Works lie beneath kilometers of ocean. You will need a pressure-rated suit or submersible capable of withstanding deep benthic conditions. If you are using an atmospheric diving suit, rate it for at least 4,000 meters and understand that this may be insufficient—some travelers report chambers that descend far below the nominal seafloor, and the Works themselves may extend downward for tens of kilometers. Bring pressure ratings that exceed your expectations, and then exceed those.

If you are using a submersible, understand its dimensions relative to the vegetation-choked passages. The great halls can accommodate large vehicles. The smaller chambers—rooms the size of houses with ceilings low enough that the growth fills them entirely—will not. You will need to abandon the submersible at some point if you intend to go deeper than the major arteries. Plan your entry and exit routes around this. Mark where you left your vehicle.

### Thermal Management

2.4 degrees Celsius. Constant. Unvarying. The cold is not dramatic but it is total, and it will kill you as surely as a freezing current—just more slowly. Your suit must have active thermal regulation. Do not rely on passive insulation alone; the cold will find its way through any material given enough time, and time is something the Works have in quantities you cannot imagine.

Bring thermal layers rated for extended submersion in near-freezing water. Bring chemical heat packs as backup. Bring more than you think you need, because the cold does something to the mind that the descriptions cannot convey—it slows thought itself, makes everything feel reasonable that should feel urgent, convinces you that you have time when you may not. The text says you become the cold. This is not merely poetic. Travelers report a dissociation that sets in after extended exposure, a sense that the temperature is not something happening to you but something you have always been. Fight this. Monitor your core temperature obsessively.

### Breathing and Life Support

Standard deep-diving protocols apply, with one critical addition: plan for duration. The scale of the Works means that any exploration will take longer than anticipated. Bring enough breathing mixture for at minimum three times your planned duration. The vegetation is dense enough in places that you may need to find alternate routes, and alternate routes in the Works can add days to a journey that should have taken hours.

Closed-circuit rebreathers are strongly recommended over open-circuit systems. The bubbles from open-circuit breathing disturb the vegetation—the fronds part around you with "slow reluctance," but they also close behind you, and the disturbance of bubbles accelerates this process. Travelers using open-circuit systems report losing their return paths more frequently than those using rebreathers. Whether this is causation or correlation is unclear, but the pattern is consistent enough to heed.

### Light

Here is where preparation becomes philosophy.

The Drowned Works exists in absolute darkness. No sun. No bioluminescence. A darkness so complete that it functions as a substance. You will need lights. You will need powerful, reliable lights with redundant power systems, because without light in the Works you are simply a body moving through a medium that will dissolve you in time.

But understand what light does here.

It reveals. The carved walls, the pillar-spacing, the texture changes in the floor that mark the ghosts of seams, the vegetation in all its dark strangeness—light makes these things visible, and visibility is the difference between navigation and wandering.

It also destroys.

The Works have been dark for two billion years. The vegetation has evolved in that darkness. It does not photosynthesize. It feeds on chemical gradients, on the mineral content of the water, on the decomposition of its own dead. Light is not part of its biology. Whether light damages it, alters it, or causes it to respond in unexpected ways is something the available accounts do not clearly establish—but travelers who have used high-intensity lights report that the vegetation near their path sometimes behaves differently in subsequent passages, the fronds denser, the tendrils more directed, as if something has been noticed.

Use light sparingly. Use it strategically. Use it to read the carved walls, to navigate critical junctions, to document what you find. Do not leave it running continuously. Do not sweep it across the chambers like a searchlight announcing your presence. The Works are dark because they are meant to be dark, and you are an intrusion.

Recommended: low-intensity, directed-beam headlamps with adjustable focus. Red-spectrum light if possible, as it disrupts less while still providing navigational capability. Backup lights distributed across your person in case of primary failure. Chemical light sticks as last resort—they provide dim illumination for hours and require no power, which makes them invaluable when all your electrical systems are failing in the cold.

### Navigation

You will get lost. This is not a possibility; it is a certainty. The scale of the Works, the darkness, the density of the vegetation, the lack of distinguishing features visible to the human eye—all of these conspire to defeat any navigation system that relies on visual orientation.

Bring sonar. Bring multiple sonar units with different frequencies, because the vegetation absorbs some frequencies and the constructed surfaces reflect others unpredictably. Learn to read sonar before you arrive. Practice navigating by sound in zero-visibility conditions, because that is what the Works will demand.

Bring inertial navigation systems. These will drift over time, but they provide a baseline that is better than nothing. Calibrate them at your entry point and recalibrate at every opportunity—every chamber with distinctive geometry, every carved wall with a pattern you can recognize, every pillar arrangement that stands out from the monotony.

Set physical markers. Biodegradable markers that will not contaminate the ecosystem but will persist long enough for you to find your way back. Fluorescent markers visible under your light. Acoustic beacons that you can home in on with your sonar. Place them at every junction, every turn, every point where the path branches. Place more than you think you need.

Understand that the vegetation moves. Not quickly—not in any human timescale—but over the duration of an extended expedition, the channels that were open when you arrived may narrow or close. The paths of least resistance shift. Your markers may be overgrown, your acoustic beacons muffled by new growth. Leave the Works the way you came, and leave before your markers disappear.

### Tools and Equipment

**Cutting implements.** You will need to push through vegetation, and in some cases cut through it. Use tools that minimize damage—precision cutters rather than crude blades. The vegetation will regrow regardless of what you do, but the rate and manner of regrowth may be affected by how much damage you cause. Be conservative.

**Documentation equipment.** The carved walls are the primary record of whatever the Works were built to communicate or accomplish. Photograph them. Record them. Use multiple imaging modalities—visible light, ultraviolet, infrared, sonar mapping—because you do not know what will reveal the most information, and you will not have a second chance. The carvings vary in ways "too subtle for casual observation but too consistent to be decorative." Bring equipment that can capture subtle variations in relief, depth, and spacing.

**Sampling equipment.** If you intend to collect samples of the vegetation, the construction materials, or the water itself, bring appropriate containers and preservatives. Understand that removing samples from the Works may be functionally impossible—some travelers report that the vegetation grows into and through collection vessels, that the construction material is so dense and hard that sampling tools fail, that the water in the Works is chemically indistinguishable from the water above it once removed from context. Sample if you must, but do not rely on sampling as your primary method of documentation.

**Emergency supplies.** A medical kit designed for deep-water conditions. Emergency thermal blankets. Signal devices that can penetrate water—acoustic emitters, chemical lights, even simple noisemakers that might attract attention if another traveler is in the area (unlikely, but not impossible). A contingency plan for what happens if you cannot find your way back to your entry point, including enough supplies to survive for an extended period while you search for an alternative exit.

## III. Psychological Preparation: The Mind in the Works

This is where the true preparation happens. The physical challenges can be met with equipment. The psychological challenges require something harder to acquire.

### The Dark

You have never experienced darkness like this. The darkness in the Works is not the absence of light—it is the presence of something else, a weight that presses against your eyes, a substance that fills the visual field with its own density. Your brain will not know what to do with this input, or lack of input. Visual deprivation produces hallucinations within hours—geometric patterns, fleeting shapes, the sense of movement in the periphery that vanishes when you turn to look. These are not real. They are your brain attempting to process the absence of signal and filling the void with noise.

Know this before you arrive. Expect it. When you see something moving in the darkness, when you sense a presence beside you, when the shapes coalesce into something that seems almost recognizable—remind yourself that this is your mind, not the Works. The Works have no animal life. Nothing moves through those stalks. Whatever you see is projection.

But also: trust your other senses. The vegetation creates pressure changes in the water as it sways. The structures settle and groan on timescales that occasionally produce audible sounds. Your body will register things that your conscious mind cannot parse—shifts in the current, changes in the density of the growth, the subtle vibration of the floor beneath you. Learn to attend to these signals without interpreting them as threat. The Works are not hostile. They are also not welcoming. They are simply present, and your body will read that presence as significance. Let it. The information may be useful. The fear it produces is something you will need to manage.

### The Silence

The silence of the Works is not absolute, but it is close. The low murmur of water moving through vegetation. The occasional settling of ancient stone. These sounds are so rare, so subtle, that they register as intrusions rather than constants. The baseline is silence, and silence does something to the human mind that noise does not prepare you for.

You will hear your own body more clearly than you ever have. Your heartbeat. Your breathing. The movement of fluid in your inner ear. The creak of your suit as you move. These sounds become your entire acoustic world, and they become oppressive in their intimacy. Some travelers report a growing conviction that their own body sounds are not their own—that the heartbeat they hear is something else, something in the walls, something in the water. This is disorientation, not reality. Know the difference, but understand that the difference may become harder to perceive over time.

Bring audio recording equipment if you wish, but consider carefully whether you want to introduce sound into this environment. Music, speech, any human noise will feel like an intrusion—not because the Works will respond, but because you will feel how fundamentally alien your presence is in a place that has been silent for two billion years. Some travelers find sound comforting. Others find it obscene. Decide for yourself before you arrive, and bring the option even if you think you will not use it.

### The Scale

The Works are continental in scale. You cannot comprehend this. No human can. Our minds are not built to process spaces that span time zones, that have no edges, that continue past every point where you expect them to end. You will try to map the Works in your head, to build a model of where you are relative to where you started, and you will fail. The model will not hold. The Works are too large, too complex, too organized around a logic you cannot access.

Accept this before you arrive. You will not understand the layout. You will not discover the plan. You will navigate by sonar and marker and instinct, and you will move through spaces that were not designed for you, that were not designed for anything like you, and the meaning of those spaces will remain opaque. This is not failure. This is the nature of the Works. They were built by minds that are not your minds, for purposes that are not your purposes, and the gulf between you and them is two billion years of patient, silent existence.

Some travelers find this liberating—the freedom of being somewhere that does not care about you, that has no opinion about your presence, that will not be altered by your passing. Others find it annihilating—the recognition that you are insignificant not because the universe is vast but because something *made* is vast, something intentional, something that was designed to endure and has endured past every trace of its designers. The Works are a monument to patience you cannot imagine. Standing inside a monument like that can make your own existence feel very small.

Have a framework for processing this before you arrive. Whether that framework is philosophical, spiritual, scientific, or purely practical—have it. The Works will test it.

### The Vegetation

The plants will unsettle you more than the darkness or the silence or the scale, because they are *alive* in a way that the rest of the Works is not. They move. They respond. They grow and die and regrow in cycles that you cannot observe but can infer from the layers of growth—the tall stalks, the shorter clusters, the low mats, the tendrils that thread through everything. They are a closed ecosystem, self-sustaining, self-contained, operating without any input from the world above. They have been doing this for two billion years. They will continue to do this for two billion more.

And they will be around you. Constantly. Touching you. The fronds will part around you with "slow reluctance," and that reluctance will feel like intention. It is not intention—the plants have no nervous system, no cognition, no will—but the slowness of their movement, the way they close behind you, the density of their growth in some places and their absence in others, all of this will create the impression of something that is aware of you. Something that is watching you with senses you do not have. Something that is deciding whether to let you pass.

This impression is almost certainly false. Almost. The vegetation has had two billion years to evolve, and evolution in a closed system can produce outcomes that are difficult to predict. The boundaries between individual plants are unclear. The boundaries between the plants and the structures they grow on are even less clear. The tendrils that connect and entangle and merge—they suggest a network, a system, something that is more than the sum of its parts. Whether that system has any form of coherence, any form of response, any form of awareness, is unknown.

Treat the vegetation with respect. Do not damage it unnecessarily. Do not assume it is passive. Do not assume it is active. Observe. Move carefully. If the growth thickens around you, if the channels narrow, if the fronds seem to be pressing inward—retreat. Find another path. Whether the vegetation is responding to your presence or simply being what it is, the practical result is the same: you can become trapped, and the Works will not help you.

### The Carvings

The walls are carved with dense, repetitive patterns that vary in subtle but consistent ways. They are language, or they are information storage, or they are something else for which we have no category. They cover every surface that the vegetation has not claimed. They were meant to be read.

You will not be able to read them. No one living can. But you will feel the intention behind them—the sense that these marks mean something, that they were placed here with purpose, that the walls are speaking even if no one is listening. Some travelers report a growing conviction that the carvings are about them—about the traveler, about the arrival of someone new in the Works, about the purpose that the Works were built to serve. This conviction is almost certainly projection. Almost.

Document the carvings. Photograph them. Record their patterns. But do not stare at them for too long in your light's beam. The patterns are repetitive but not identical, and the mind attempts to find meaning in the variations, attempts to decode, attempts to read. Prolonged exposure produces a state of intense focus that travelers describe as "listening with your eyes." This state is not dangerous in itself, but it is absorbing, and you may lose track of time, of your surroundings, of your oxygen supply. Set limits. Check your gauges. The Works will still be here when you look back.

### The Question of Purpose

The Works were built for something. They were designed—planned, organized, constructed with a logic that persists even now, even after two billion years. The chambers connect in ways that suggest purpose. The corridors lead somewhere. The layout is not random.

What is the purpose? No one knows. The carvings may explain it, but no one can read them. The vegetation has obscured or incorporated much of the original architecture, but the underlying plan remains, detectable in the pillar-spacing, the chamber-sequences, the way the floor rises and falls in patterns too regular to be natural.

You will think about this. You will not be able to stop thinking about this. The Works are waiting, the text says. Waiting for whatever they were designed to receive. And you are there, moving through them, and some part of you will wonder: *am I what they were waiting for?*

The answer is almost certainly no. Almost. The builders are gone. Their species is gone. The Works have outlasted everything that could have fulfilled their purpose, if that purpose required any specific actor. Whatever the Works were meant to receive, it is likely that it came and went billions of years ago, or never came at all, or was never something that could arrive in the way that you are arriving.

But the Works are still here. Still holding their shape. Still breathing water through their chambers like breath through lungs. And you are inside them, and they are vast, and they are patient, and they have been waiting longer than you can comprehend.

Have an answer for yourself before you arrive. Not the answer—the Works will not give you the answer—but *your* answer, the thing you will tell yourself when the question becomes too loud to ignore. Why are you here? What are you looking for? What will satisfy you? The Works will not provide closure. You must bring your own.

## IV. Practical Itinerary

### Duration

Limit your first visit to no more than 72 hours. The psychological effects of extended exposure accumulate, and 72 hours seems to be the threshold beyond which judgment becomes significantly impaired. You can always return. The Works will be here.

### Entry

Choose an entry point where the vegetation is thinnest—near the upper edges of the structure, where the Works approach the open ocean. These areas are less dense, less disorienting, and closer to potential rescue if something goes wrong. Mark your entry point with multiple redundant beacons. Do not rely on a single marker.

### Movement

Move slowly. The vegetation parts around you with reluctance, and rapid movement damages the plants and creates disturbance that may affect your ability to navigate. The Works reward patience. Move as the water moves—imperceptibly, with intention rather than urgency.

### Documentation

Record everything. Your position, your heading, the patterns on the walls, the density of the vegetation, the temperature of the water, the sounds you hear, the things you feel. You will not remember it accurately. No one does. The Works alter memory—not through any malign influence, but through the sheer overwhelming strangeness of the experience. Write it down. Record it. Trust the recording, not the memory.

### Exit

Leave the way you came. Follow your markers. Do not be tempted to find "another way out"—the Works are too vast, and the vegetation too dense, and the probability of finding an alternative exit by accident is effectively zero. Retrace your steps exactly. If a marker has been overgrown, search the area carefully before moving on. The markers are your lifeline. Do not lose them.

## V. What to Leave Behind

Do not bring expectations of discovery. The Works will not reveal their secrets to you in a single visit, or a hundred visits, or a lifetime of visits. They are two billion years old. They operate on timescales that make your existence a rounding error.

Do not bring fear of the dark, because the dark is all there is, and fear of it will consume you.

Do not bring the assumption that you understand what you are seeing, because you do not, and the moment you believe you do is the moment you become most vulnerable to disorientation.

Do not bring the conviction that the Works are empty, because the vegetation is alive, and the carvings are language, and the structure is still serving whatever purpose it was built to serve. Something is happening here. It is simply happening on a timescale you cannot perceive.

Do not bring the belief that you matter to this place. You do not. The Works have endured for two billion years without you. They will endure for two billion more after you leave. Your presence is an event so brief and so insignificant that it barely registers against the deep time of this place, and there is a kind of freedom in that insignificance if you can accept it.

---

## VI. What to Bring

Bring patience.

Bring humility.

Bring a light that you are willing to turn off.

Bring a reason to be here that the Works cannot give you.

Bring the knowledge that you will leave, and that when you leave, the water will close behind you, and the vegetation will sway, and the darkness will continue, and the Works will go on waiting for whatever it was that they were built to receive—and that this is not your story, that you were never the audience, that you were simply a body moving through a space that has been holding its breath for longer than your species has existed, and that your passing through it changed nothing, and that this is the most profound and most terrifying thing about the Drowned Works:

They do not need you to be what they are.

They never did.
