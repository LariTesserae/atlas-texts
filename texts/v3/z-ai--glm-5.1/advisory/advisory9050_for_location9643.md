# Preparation Guide: The Vast Weep

## I. What You Must Understand Before You Go

You are not going to a place. You are going inside something.

This is not metaphor. This is the single most important fact about the Vast Weep, and if you do not absorb it before you arrive—if you still think of it as landscape, as terrain, as a space you move *through*—then the plateau will teach you, and the plateau's lessons are slow, and they are patient, and they do not care if you understand them before they change you.

You will be inside a living thing. Continuous. Boundaryless. A hundred million years of growth fused into a single body the size of a continent, and you will be walking on its skin, breathing its respiration, tasting its chemistry in every breath. You will be as significant to it as a mite walking across your own forearm—present, perhaps faintly irritating, but ultimately irrelevant. The plateau does not know you are there. The plateau *cannot* know you are there, because knowing requires the recognition of something that is not-self, and the Vast Weep has forgotten that anything exists which is not itself.

This is the second thing you must understand: the boundary between you and the environment will be under constant, gentle, patient assault. Not hostile. Never hostile. The plateau does not attack. The plateau *absorbs*. Your shed skin cells, the moisture of your breath, the oils on your fingers where you touch a root to steady yourself—all of this will be taken in, processed, made part of the living mass. The Creep will colonize the cuffs of your pants. The Crawl will find their way into the seams of your pack. The dampness will work into everything you own until you cannot remember what it felt like to be dry.

You must be prepared to maintain yourself as a distinct entity in a place that has dissolved all such distinctions. This is harder than it sounds. This may be harder than anything you have ever done.

## II. Physical Preparation

### Conditioning

You must be able to walk for long distances on unstable, yielding, waterlogged ground. This is not hiking. This is closer to wading through a bog that is also a sponge that is also alive. Every step compresses. Every step releases fluid. Every step requires you to find purchase on a surface that wants to hold you—and by "hold you," I mean cling to you, grip you, slowly embrace the sole of your boot and not quite let go.

Train on wet moss. Train on bog. Train on forest floors after heavy rain, and then imagine that the forest floor goes on forever, and that it is warm, and that it is breathing.

You will also need upper body strength. The ridges of the root-mat—where ancient growth has thickened into barriers—require climbing with hands and knees, your fingers sinking into spongy wet bark. You will pull yourself up and over. You will lower yourself down. You will sometimes need to crawl. The mat is not flat. It was never flat. It has its own topography, and that topography was not made for human legs.

And you must be prepared for the elevation. Two to three kilometers up. The air will be thin. Your lungs will work harder for less reward, and the air they pull in will be thick with moisture—copper and chlorophyll, the text says, which means you will taste the plateau's metabolism on your tongue with every breath. Train at altitude if you can. Practice breathing deliberately, fully, with intention. You will need to think about breathing here in a way you have never had to think about it before.

### Tolerance for Wet

You will never be dry.

Read that again. You will *never be dry*. The air is saturated. The ground releases water with every step. The Hanging drips constantly. The Stands create their own slow warm rain beneath their canopy-sheets. The pools are everywhere, warm as blood, covered in living film that parts around you and closes behind you. Condensation beads on every surface, including your skin, including your clothing, including the inside of your lungs.

You must be someone who can endure persistent, inescapable wetness without it becoming a psychological burden. If the feeling of damp socks, damp collar, damp everything is something that wears at you—stay home. The Vast Weep will make you pruney, will make your skin macerated and soft and vulnerable, will fill your boots and your clothes and your pack with moisture that never fully evaporates because there is nowhere for the moisture to go. You are inside a water-catchment system. You are part of the water-catchment system. The water goes through you now.

### Tolerance for Warmth

Everything is blood-temperature. The air. The water. The ground beneath your feet. The animals. The surfaces you touch. Approximately thirty-seven degrees Celsius, everywhere, always. You are walking inside something that is as warm as you are, and your body—which has spent its entire life maintaining a thermal difference between itself and its environment—will not know what to do with this.

You cannot cool down by radiating heat. The environment will not accept your warmth because it is already as warm as you are. You can only cool down by evaporation, and evaporation requires dry air, and there is no dry air. You will sweat. The sweat will not evaporate efficiently. You will be wet from outside and wet from inside, and you must pace yourself accordingly.

Do not overexert. Overexertion in this environment means heat exhaustion, means your body's cooling systems failing, means you lying down on the warm, wet mat to rest, and the mat accepting you as it accepts everything that falls upon it.

## III. What to Bring

### Footwear

This is your most critical decision.

You need boots that can handle constant immersion without breaking down. The mat releases tannin-rich fluid with every step—tannins that will tan leather, that will degrade many synthetic materials, that will work their way into every stitch and seam. You need rubber or high-quality synthetic soles with aggressive tread, because the surface grips—you want to be able to *release* from the grip of the root-hairs, not be held by them.

The boots must be tall. Mid-calf at minimum, knee-height preferable. The pools are everywhere, and you will step into them, and the living film on their surface will cling to your legs. Gaiters are essential—not for snow, but for the Crawl, the thousands of many-legged things that move through the root-fiber lattice. They will find their way into any opening. This is not a phobia thing. This is a practical thing. The Crawl process debris. They process dead matter. They do not know you are not dead matter. Keep them out.

Accept that your boots will never fully dry. Accept that they will develop a smell that is the smell of the plateau itself—tannin, old sugar, chlorophyll, copper. This is the smell of being *of* this place. Do not fight it.

### Clothing

Layers of moisture-wicking synthetic fabric, but here is the paradox: nothing will wick because there is nowhere for the moisture to go. You will be wet regardless. The goal is not to stay dry but to manage the wet—to have layers that do not become heavy with absorbed water, that do not chafe when sodden, that can be wrung out and put back on.

Merino wool base layers, despite the warmth. Wool retains its insulating properties when wet and is naturally antimicrobial, which will slow—though not prevent—the plateau's colonization of your clothing. Synthetic mid-layers for structure. A shell layer that is waterproof but *breathable*—and I use that word with full awareness that no shell can truly breathe in 100% humidity. The shell is not to keep rain off you. The shell is to keep the Hanging's constant drip from running down your collar, to keep the Creep's wet leaves from soaking through to your skin when you brush against them.

You will smell like the plateau within hours. Your clothing will become an extension of the living mass within days. This is inevitable. Do not bring anything you are attached to. Do not bring anything that cannot be left behind.

Cover your skin as much as possible. Not because the plateau is dangerous to touch—though prolonged contact with any surface here means prolonged contact with a living system that is constantly exuding fluids and enzymes—but because your skin, constantly wet and constantly warm, will break down. Maceration. Dermatitis. Infections in skin that has been waterlogged too long. Keep the plateau off your skin and keep your skin intact. The moment your skin breaks, you become food.

### Head Covering

The water falls from above. The Hanging drips. The Stands drip. The clouds form around you. You will be rained on from every direction, including directly onto the top of your head, constantly. A wide-brimmed hat will keep the drip out of your eyes and off your face. This is not comfort—this is sanity. The constant sensation of water drops on your face, hour after hour after hour, will erode your mental state more quickly than anything else.

Under the brim, consider a headlamp, even during the dim, sourceless day. You will need your hands for climbing, for balance, for pushing through the Hanging. You cannot hold a light. And visibility can shift from three hundred meters to thirty within an hour; when the clouds close in around you, you will want to see the ground beneath your next step.

### Pack and Gear

Waterproof everything. And then accept that "waterproof" is a temporary condition here. Use dry bags inside your pack, multiple layers of them. The pack itself will be wet always; the dry bags are what keep your actual supplies from becoming part of the plateau's water cycle.

Bring a water filter capable of handling biological contamination, and then understand that the water you filter will still taste of copper and tannin and chlorophyll. This is not contamination. This is the water's nature. The pools are warm, living things, full of micro-organisms and the Swimmers that feed on them. Filter the water. Boil it if you can. Do not drink it unprocessed, or the micro-organisms will treat your digestive tract the way the Creep treats the ground—something to colonize, something to extend into, something to make part of itself.

Bring high-calorie, dense food that does not require cooking. You will not be able to make a fire here. There is nothing dry to burn. The mat will not accept flame—everything is too wet, too alive. Your stove, if you bring one, will require fuel you carry in, and the moisture will find its way into the valves and the jets. Eat cold. Eat dense. Eat things that will not spoil when wet, because everything will be wet.

Bring a sleeping pad that provides insulation from the warm ground—and yes, I know the ground is blood-temperature, but your body still needs to regulate, and lying on the living mat for eight hours means lying in a pool of warm water that is slowly, patiently, working its way into your clothing and your skin and your gear. The pad is a boundary. The pad is you saying: *this is me, and that is you, and we are not the same thing.*

Bring a sleeping bag that can handle wet conditions. Synthetic insulation, not down—down clumps and loses all thermal capacity when wet, and it will be wet. The bag will be damp when you get in it and damp when you get out. This is the best you can hope for.

### Navigation

You cannot navigate by landmarks. The landscape shifts—slowly, over centuries, but you will not be there long enough to perceive the shift, which means the landscape is effectively static. But the clouds form *within* it. Visibility changes constantly. The Stands and the Hanging create enclosed spaces where you cannot see far. There are no stars, no sun, no magnetic references. The sky is old pewter, the light sourceless, and directions have no meaning when you cannot see the horizon.

Bring a compass, but do not trust it. The living mass may contain enough iron in its circulatory system—those dark veins of water that pulse slowly through the mat—to affect magnetic readings. Test it when you arrive and calibrate against known directions if you can establish any.

Better: bring an inertial navigation system, or learn to dead-reckon by pacing and direction. Count your steps. Note your headings. Draw maps of the topography as you encounter it—the ridges, the pools, the Stands. The landscape will not help you. The landscape does not care where you are going.

Best: leave markers. Physical markers—something the mat cannot easily absorb or grow over. The shaft of harder material mentioned in the description, the thing the roots cannot break down—this is the kind of material you need for markers. Metal stakes, perhaps. Synthetic markers that resist biological colonization. Push them deep into the mat, deep enough that the Creep cannot simply grow over them in a day. Check them when you pass. The plateau will try to consume them. The plateau will succeed, eventually. But you only need them to last as long as you do.

### First Aid

Your skin is your most vulnerable organ. Bring moisture-barrier creams, zinc oxide, barrier films—anything that can protect skin that is constantly wet. Treat every cut, every scratch, every blister immediately and thoroughly. The plateau processes debris. An open wound is debris. The Crawl will find it. The micro-organisms in the water will find it. The living film on the pools will find it. Protect your boundaries.

Bring broad-spectrum antibiotics. Bring antifungals. You will be living inside a living thing; you are surrounded by organisms that have had a hundred million years to perfect their strategies for breaking down and absorbing biological matter. Your immune system is not prepared for this. Help it.

Bring rehydration salts. You will sweat more than you realize, and the water you drink—filtered, boiled, still tasting of copper—may not be enough to maintain your electrolyte balance. The blood-warm air pulls moisture from you in ways you cannot feel because you are always already wet.

## IV. Mental and Emotional Preparation

### The Dissolution of Self

This is the hardest part, and the part no gear can address.

The Vast Weep has no concept of the individual. Every organism is part of the mat; the mat is part of every organism. Boundaries are suggestions that dissolved epochs ago. And you—you with your discrete body, your sense of self, your conviction that you end at your skin and the world begins—you are an anomaly. You are something the plateau has never encountered, or has not encountered in so long that the memory is stored only in hollow spaces the roots refuse to fill.

The plateau will not try to destroy you. But it will not try to preserve you either. It will simply *be*, and you will be within it, and the constant, patient, thoroughgoing process of absorption will work on you the way it works on everything. Not dramatically. Not violently. Just... gradually. The way it has worked on everything for a hundred million years.

You may begin to feel it. The difficulty of maintaining your boundaries—physical boundaries, yes, but also psychological ones. The sense that you are not walking *through* a landscape but *within* a body. The way the constant wet and warmth and dim light erode your sense of being separate, being distinct, being a thing that is not-the-plateau. The way the dripping becomes indistinguishable from silence, the way the landscape becomes a fixed constant, the way time moves *thoroughly*, every moment filled to capacity with process, and you cannot tell if an hour has passed or a day.

This is normal. This is what the Vast Weep does. It does not attack the self; it simply provides an environment in which the self has so little reinforcement, so little contrast, so little *pushback* that it begins to soften at the edges. You stop noticing where you end. You stop noticing that you are separate.

You must notice.

You must find ways to remind yourself that you are a discrete entity visiting this place, not a new growth being slowly incorporated into it. Talk to yourself. Sing—quietly, so as not to disturb, but loud enough to hear your own voice, your own distinct voice, making sounds that are not the sounds of the plateau. Write. Keep a journal. Record your thoughts, your observations, your *self* in words that exist outside the living mass, on paper that you protect in dry bags, in ink that the tannin-laden air will try to fade but will not fade today.

Touch yourself. Not in any complicated way—just press your hand to your chest and feel your own heartbeat, distinct from the slow pulse of water through the root-fibers. Your heart beats faster. Your blood runs at a slightly different temperature, perhaps—cooler, if you are maintaining yourself properly. Feel the difference. Remember the difference. The difference is you.

### The Silence

The plateau is nearly silent, and what sound there is—the dripping, the slow creak of growth, the faint rustling of the Crawl—will become a kind of silence itself after enough hours. Your ears will adjust. Your brain will stop registering sound as meaningful input.

This is dangerous. Not because of what you might miss, but because of what you might become. Silence, in the Vast Weep, is not the absence of input—it is the presence of something too large and too slow and too patient to be perceived as sound. The plateau is breathing. The plateau is growing. The plateau is alive in a way that encompasses you, and you cannot hear it, and you cannot feel it, and you cannot perceive it, but it is there, and it has been there for longer than your species has existed.

Bring earplugs. Not to block sound—there is little enough to block—but to create your own silence, a silence you control, a silence that is *yours* and not the plateau's. And bring something to listen to, if you can keep the equipment dry. Music. Voices. Anything that reminds you that sound can be something other than the slow drip of water from living fibers.

But also: do not wear the earplugs always. You need to hear the Lurk moving—if it moves while you are near. You need to hear the sudden rush when a section of Hanging releases its accumulated water. You need to hear the creak of the Stands, the rustling of the Crawl, the drip that is the plateau's heartbeat. You need to hear these things not because they are dangerous, but because they are *real*, and you are walking through them, and you owe it to this place to perceive it as fully as you can.

### The Traces

You may find them. The rectangle of denser growth where a building once stood. The shaft of material the roots cannot break down. The hollow space the size and shape of a room, dry and still and preserved, where the roots have grown around a volume of air and refused to fill it.

These traces will affect you more than you expect. They are proof that someone was here before you—someone who understood this place, who built *with* it rather than *on* it, who spoke to the roots or fed them or sang to them in frequencies the Crawl still carry. Someone who was absorbed, eventually, as everything is absorbed, but who left marks that the plateau remembers the way a body remembers a wound.

Sit in the hollow, if you find one. Sit in the dry, still air and listen to the mat around you, the slow creak of growth, the distant drip, the vast respiratory pulse. Feel what the space feels: preserved. Intentional. Held.

And then leave. Do not stay too long. The hollow is a place where someone negotiated a boundary with the plateau, where they asked the living mass to hold a shape, and it is holding that shape still, patiently, without understanding, without caring, without knowing. You do not know the terms of that negotiation. You do not know what was given in exchange. You do not know what the plateau might ask of you, if you stay in that space and let it believe you are part of the arrangement.

### The Indifference

This is the cruelest thing about the Vast Weep, and the most important: it does not care about you.

Not hostile. Not welcoming. Not curious. Not afraid. Simply... indifferent. The way your body is indifferent to a single cell that appears and then is gone. The plateau will absorb your footprints within the hour. It will process your shed skin cells, your breath's moisture, the heat of your passage. It will make them into itself with the same patient thoroughness it brings to everything.

And when you leave—if you leave—the space where you stood will close over like water behind a hand, and there will be no mark, no memory, no trace.

Except perhaps a hollow somewhere, the size and shape of a person, where the roots refuse to grow.

You must be someone who can visit a place that will not remember you, that cannot remember you, that has no mechanism for remembering anything that is not itself. You must be someone who can walk on ground that will erase your passage, breathe air that will recycle your breath without noting its source, touch surfaces that will accept the oils from your fingers and make them into root-mass without acknowledgment. You must be someone who can be temporary—not in the philosophical sense, but in the literal, physical, *biological* sense—and who can make peace with that temporariness.

Or who cannot make peace with it, but goes anyway.

## V. How to Move

### Slowly

You cannot hurry here. The ground will not allow it. Every step is a negotiation with a surface that compresses, that releases fluid, that grips and yields and shifts. The Stands and the Hanging create enclosed spaces you must push through, thread through, ease through. The ridges must be climbed. The pools must be waded or gone around. There are no paths. There are no clearings. There is only the mat, and the growth upon the mat, and the growth upon the growth, and you, making your way through it as best you can.

Slow down. Slow down more than you think you need to. The plateau rewards patience and punishes haste—not actively, not deliberately, but simply because haste leads to mistakes, and mistakes lead to falls, and falls lead to prolonged contact with the living mat, and prolonged contact leads to absorption.

Move at the pace of the landscape. The dripping. The growing. The slow pulse of water through root-fiber. You are not faster than this place. You are not more important than this place. You are a temporary disturbance in a process that has been ongoing for a hundred million years. Act like it.

### Deliberately

Place each foot with intention. Test the ground before committing your weight—the mat has hollows, depressions where water gathers, soft spots where the root-structure beneath has subsided. A wrong step can leave you knee-deep in warm water and living tissue, and the film on the water's surface will close around your leg, and the sensation will be—not threatening, never threatening, but *present*, *attentive*, *patient*, in a way that will make you understand exactly how the plateau processes everything that comes within its reach.

Use your hands. The ridges, the Stands, the Hanging—these are things to grip, to push aside, to steady yourself against. But touch them lightly, briefly. Do not linger. The Creep roots wherever it touches; the mat absorbs whatever stays too long. Your hands are the part of you most likely to be colonized, because they are the part of you most likely to reach out and make contact. Keep them gloved when you can. Wash them—filtered water, not pool water—when you rest.

### With Awareness

Watch for the Lurk. You probably will not see it—it is the color of wet bark and nearly invisible when still—but you may step on it, and it will not flinch, and you will feel only a soft, yielding warmth beneath your foot. This is not dangerous to you. This is simply information: you are walking on something that was alive before you stepped on it and is alive after, something that does not care about your presence, something that is part of the mat in the same way that a cell is part of a body. Be careful. Not for your sake—for the Lurk's. You are heavy, and it is soft, and the plateau will absorb its remains as readily as it absorbs anything else.

Watch for the Swimmers when you wade through pools. They are translucent, visible only as faint shadows, and they will part around your legs like the surface film does—slowly, without panic, without even the recognition that you are something other than water. Do not harm them if you can avoid it. They are part of the system. The pools feed on them when they die. Their deaths should come at the schedule of the plateau, not at the stroke of your boot.

Watch the Hanging. Once per day—perhaps—a section will release its accumulated water all at once, a sudden soft torrent that lasts ten seconds and then is gone. If you are beneath it when it releases, you will be soaked, thoroughly and instantly, with water that has been filtering through living fiber for hours. This is not dangerous. But it is startling, and in a place where sound is sparse and movement is slow, being startled can make you lose your footing, and losing your footing can mean a fall into the mat, and a fall into the mat means prolonged contact, and prolonged contact means—

You understand by now. Be careful. Be aware. Move as if the landscape is watching you, even though it is not.

## VI. How to Leave

Know your limits before you arrive. Set a maximum duration—days, not weeks—and adhere to it regardless of what you find, what you experience, what you think you might discover if you just push a little further. Time moves *thoroughly* here. A day is indistinguishable from a century. You will lose track of how long you have been walking, how long you have been wet, how long you have been inside this thing. Set an alarm. Set multiple alarms. Count the days on your fingers if you must. Leave before you stop wanting to leave.

Watch for the signs that the plateau is working on you:

- You stop noticing the wet. Not tolerating it—*noticing* it. When the constant dripping, the constant damp, the constant warmth become so familiar that you cannot remember what it felt like to be dry, you have been here too long.
- You stop checking your boundaries. When you no longer examine your skin for breakdown, no longer treat every scratch, no longer maintain the distinction between your gear and the living mass, you have been here too long.
- You stop wanting to leave. Not because you have accomplished what you came for, but because leaving seems unnecessary, seems strange, seems like walking out of a warm room into a cold night for no reason you can articulate. When this happens, you have been here too long, and the plateau has begun the process of making you part of itself—not through violence, not through force, but through the simple, patient, thorough dissolution of the desire to be separate.

Leave the way you came. Follow your markers. Trust your navigation. Walk toward the shores where the mat thins and fragments, where the living mass gives way to bare rock, where the weeping cascades down ancient cliff faces in great curtains of root and vine and water. Walk toward the edges. Walk toward the boundaries. Walk toward a place where the living and the non-living are distinguishable, where the concept of "individual" still means something, where you can be dry and cool and separate and *yourself*.

And if you find, as you leave, that a part of you wants to stay—that a part of you has already been absorbed, already been incorporated, already been made into root-mass and Creep and Crawl—acknowledge it, and leave anyway. The plateau will hold the shape of your passage the way it holds everything: patiently, thoroughly, without memory, without forgetting.

The space where you stood will close over like water behind a hand.

Except perhaps a hollow somewhere, the size and shape of a person, where the roots refuse to grow.

---

## VII. Final Note

Someone was here before you. They understood this place in a way you cannot—they built with it, negotiated with it, asked it to hold shapes and it held them, asked it to preserve spaces and it preserved them. They are gone now. The plateau has absorbed everything they were except the traces, the hollows, the scars in the living tissue where something was taken away.

You will not understand this place the way they did. You do not have the time. You do not have the language—the frequencies the Crawl carry, the patient dialogue of root and intention and mutual becoming. You are a visitor. You are passing through. You will leave no mark that the plateau can hold.

But you can hold the plateau. You can carry it with you—the taste of copper and chlorophyll, the feel of the spongy ground, the sound of dripping that becomes silence, the warmth that is everywhere and the dryness that is nowhere, the sense of being inside something too large and too slow and too patient to perceive. You can remember it. You can write it down. You can tell others what it was like to walk for days on the skin of a living thing that has forgotten that anything exists which is not itself.

And perhaps that is enough. Perhaps that is all any visitor can do: witness, and remember, and leave before the witnessing becomes indistinguishable from becoming.

Go carefully.

Go thoroughly.

Go.
