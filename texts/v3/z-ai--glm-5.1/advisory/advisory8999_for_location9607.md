# Preparing for Newstrata: A Practical Guide for the Multiverse Traveler

## What You Need to Understand Before You Arrive

You are not going to a place. You are going to a *process*. This is the single most important thing to internalize before you board the lift. Newstrata is not a city that was built on a plateau. It is a city that is being built *by* a plateau, and the distinction will matter more than you can currently imagine.

Most locations you will visit across the multiverse have the courtesy of being finished. They exist. They have properties. You can measure them, categorize them, develop expectations about them. Newstrata is engaged in none of these conveniences. It is a location in the act of becoming, and it will not pause its becoming for your comfort or comprehension.

Prepare yourself not for a destination but for an ongoing event.

---

## The Lift and What It Does to You

You will arrive by cargo lift. There are seven lift-stations along the rim. You will likely use the easternmost one, as it is the one most commonly permitted for visitor use. The ride takes between forty minutes and two hours, depending on the wind and the patience of the lift-operator.

You will pass through the cloud layer. Before you pass through it, the world below you will be visible—whatever city or settlement serves as the base of operations for the lifts, the roads leading away, the ordinary geography of a place that has already decided what it is. Memorize this view. You will want it later, not because you will be unable to return to it but because you will need to remember that places can be *finished*.

When you enter the cloud layer, you will lose all visual reference. The lift-cage will be surrounded by white-grey nothing. The sound will change—the mechanical grinding of the lift-mechanism will become muffled, distant, as though the clouds absorb sound as well as sight. This will last five to fifteen minutes.

*This is the transition.* This is where you move from the reality you understand into the reality of the pre-. Some travelers report a feeling of pressure in their sinuses during this passage, a sensation of their thoughts becoming slightly less distinct, as though the mind itself is being asked to soften its categories. Do not fight this. Fighting it makes it worse. Allow the vagueness. You are being processed by the transition as much as the transition is processing you.

When you emerge above the clouds, Newstrata will be there. The plateau, the city, the dim amber glow of the gas lamps if it is evening, the industrial glare of the foundries if it is not. Your first breath of the upper air will taste of minerals you cannot name. Your second breath will taste the same. You will stop noticing by the third.

## What to Bring

### Clothing

Layers. Always layers. The cool of Newstrata is not dramatic—it will not freeze you, will not send you running for shelter—but it is *constant*. It is a temperature that does not fluctuate with the time of day because the dimness does not fluctuate meaningfully. You will never be truly warm in Newstrata unless you are in a foundry or pressed against a wall of the pre-.

Bring fabrics that retain warmth when damp. The mist reaches up from the cloud layer every morning, and condensation will form on every surface including your clothing. Wool is good. Treated synthetics are good. Cotton is a poor choice—it will hold the moisture against your skin and slowly leach the warmth from you.

A hat. Not for sun—there is no sharp sun here—but for the damp that will settle on your hair and eventually on your scalp. The locals wear close-fitting caps of oiled fabric. Imitate them.

Sturdy boots with grip. The streets of Newstrata are stone, and the stone is the pre-, and the pre- is slightly warm and slightly damp at all times, which means it has a traction quality unlike ordinary stone. It is not slippery, exactly, but it is *uncertain*—your foot will find purchase and then find slightly different purchase a moment later as the stone responds to your weight. Boots that allow you to feel the ground through the sole are preferable to thick-soled boots that isolate you from this feedback. You need to feel what the ground is doing.

Gloves. You will be touching walls. You will want to touch walls. The pre- is warm, and the warmth is not merely thermal—it carries something else, something that residents describe as *attention*. When you press your palm against the pre-, you will feel it pressing back, not with force but with recognition. Gloves will mediate this experience, and you may want that mediation at first. You may later choose to remove them. Bring gloves and then decide.

### Equipment

A sound-recording device, if your multiverse travel kit includes one. Not for documentation—your notes will be sufficient for that—but because the soundscape of Newstrata is like nothing you have encountered, and you will want to listen to it again after you leave. The subsonic vibration of the pre- will not record, but the foundry-rhythms, the lift-grinding, the wind through pillargrass, the odd acoustic releases from the stone walls—these you can capture, and they will help you process the experience later.

Do not bring a compass. It will not function correctly. The pre- is still forming, and its mineral composition has not settled, and the magnetic environment of the plateau is in flux. A compass in Newstrata will spin slowly, or point toward the center of the plateau, or indicate directions that do not correspond to any cardinal orientation you recognize.

Do bring a notebook. Write in it often. The thinness of reality in Newstrata means that memories formed there are subject to the same ambient uncertainty as everything else—you may find, after you leave, that certain experiences have become fuzzy or indistinct in your recollection, as though they were never fully committed to the category of "things that happened." Writing anchors them. The physical act of inscription—ink on paper, a definite mark in a definite location—provides a stability that your mind alone cannot maintain in that environment.

A water container. You will be offered water from the reservoirs. You should drink it. But you should also have water from elsewhere—water from below the cloud layer, water that is fully committed to being water, water that has no ambitions toward dreaming. Drink the local water when you want the experience. Drink your own water when you need to think clearly.

A small object of personal significance. Something from your home reality, something that is *finished*, something that knows exactly what it is. A photograph, a coin, a worn token of some kind. You will need this not for any supernatural purpose but for the psychological grounding it provides. In a place where reality is in formation, holding something that is fully real—something that has no ambiguity, no provisionality, no uncommitted potential—can remind you that such things exist. Hold it when the thin places make you dizzy. Hold it when you turn a corner and find yourself somewhere you did not intend to be. Hold it and remember that you, too, are a finished thing. You have already become yourself. You are not in process. You are not emerging. You are *here*.

### What Not to Bring

Do not bring expectations of efficiency. Do not bring schedules that require precise adherence. Do not bring the assumption that things will happen when you want them to happen. Newstrata operates on geological time, and geological time does not accommodate your itinerary.

Do not bring a weapon. The fauna of Newstrata—the walkers, the burrowers, the fish-things in the reservoirs—have no fear of you, and you will have no reason to fear them. A weapon would be an assertion of a boundary that does not exist here, and the pre- does not respond well to boundaries. Leave it below the clouds.

Do not bring anything you cannot bear to lose. Not because theft is common—the opposite; the concept of permanent ownership is somewhat relaxed in a place where the ground itself is still deciding what to be—but because reality thinness has unpredictable effects on objects. Items left on surfaces may migrate. Items placed in rooms may be found in different rooms. Items set down in the newest districts may simply become part of the pre- given sufficient time, their forms still recognizable but their substance slowly participating in the ongoing mineralogical conversation.

## What Your Body Will Do

Your body will adjust. It always does. But the adjustment to Newstrata is specific and worth anticipating.

The first three days, you will feel the vibration. The subsonic pulse of the pre- becoming itself will register as a pressure behind your eyes, a tightness in your temples, a sense that something is pressing against the inside of your skull. This is normal. Your body is interpreting a geological process as a somatic sensation. The locals no longer feel it. You will stop feeling it by the fourth or fifth day, or you will stop noticing that you feel it, which is the same thing for practical purposes.

Your sleep will be different. The constant dimness means your circadian rhythms will have no external reference to anchor them. You will sleep when you are tired, which may be at odd intervals, and you will wake without the cue of light changing. The locals have adapted to this—they sleep in shifts, they work in shifts, the city operates in a continuous present that does not divide itself into day and night in the way you are accustomed to. Allow your sleep to become irregular. Resist the urge to impose a schedule. The plateau does not observe your schedule.

If you drink the reservoir water—and you should, at least once—your dreams will change. They will become vivid, immersive, architectural. You will dream of landscapes forming and reforming, of structures emerging from formless material, of boundaries being drawn and then erased and then drawn again in different locations. You will wake from these dreams with a sense of having witnessed something important that you cannot quite articulate. Write the dreams down immediately. They fade faster than ordinary dreams, as though they, too, are subject to the provisionality of the pre-.

Your appetite may fluctuate. The food brought up on the lifts is hearty and simple—preserved meats, dried grains, root vegetables that grow in the strange soil at the plateau's edge. The local cuisine incorporates the pillargrass, which is edible when young and which has a flavor that defies comparison—green and mineral and slightly warm, as though you are eating something that is still deciding whether it wants to be a vegetable or a stone. Eat when you are hungry. The pre- seems to interact with metabolism in ways that are not fully documented; some visitors report needing less food than usual, others report needing more. Your body will tell you what it requires.

## How to Move Through the City

Stay near the rim for your first two days. The oldest parts of the city—where the buildings tilt slightly outward, where the stone has had three centuries to settle into something like stability—are the closest to the reality you understand. The pre- there is older, more committed, more certain of what it is. The walls still listen, but they listen the way an elder listens—with the patience of having heard it all before, with no urgency to respond.

When you move toward the center, move slowly. The center is where the pre- is newest, where the ground is still arriving, where the stone is warm and uncertain and the rules are thinnest. You will feel this as a lightness, a slight disconnect between your intention and your movement, as though the air has become marginally less cooperative about the business of being displaced by your body.

If you find yourself somewhere unexpected—a different district, a tunnel that should not exist, a view of the plateau as it was centuries ago—do not panic. This is not a malfunction of reality; it is reality functioning differently. Walk back the way you came. If the way you came is no longer there, sit down and wait. The thin places are not permanent. The plateau is still forming, and its formations shift and settle. A passage that does not exist now may exist in ten minutes. A corner that led somewhere unexpected may lead somewhere expected if you approach it again.

Do not run in the thin places. Running implies urgency, and urgency implies a fixed destination, and fixed destinations are not how the thin places operate. Walk. Pause. Observe. Let the city reveal its current configuration to you at its own pace.

Avoid the very center—the point where the rock is freshest, where you can see it *arriving*—until you have been in Newstrata for at least a week. This is not because it is dangerous but because you need time to calibrate. Your mind needs to adjust to a reality that is still in formation before it can witness formation itself without becoming disoriented. The locals go to the center to think, to create, to begin projects that may not have definite outcomes. You will go there eventually. But earn it first.

## How to Interact with the Fauna

The walkers will not harm you. Understand this immediately and completely. They are tall and thin and their legs do not organize themselves in ways that make sense to you, and their faces are all mouth, and they move through the streets at dawn and dusk with a browsing patience that seems almost ceremonial. They will not harm you. They have never harmed anyone. They eat pillargrass. They are interested in pillargrass. You are not pillargrass.

Do not stare at the walkers. Not because staring is rude—they do not have a concept of rudeness, or if they do, it does not include being observed—but because staring implies an attempt to categorize, and the walkers resist categorization in a way that can become uncomfortable for the person doing the staring. You will try to figure out what they are. You will fail. The failure will feel like a small mental injury, a strain in the part of your mind that organizes experience into types. Glance at them. Acknowledge them. Move on.

The burrowers you will rarely see. You will see their tunnels—low, broad, smoothly excavated passages that the city engineers later widen for human use. If you do see a burrower, it will be a brief glimpse of a wide, flat body moving with surprising speed into a hole it has just made. Do not follow it into the hole. The burrowers go where the pre- is newest, and the pre- is newest at depths that are not yet stable for human navigation. The engineers will make those depths accessible in time. Wait for the engineers.

The fish-things in the reservoirs are beautiful. Watch them at night, when their bioluminescence pulses through the lower streets like a slow heartbeat. Do not attempt to catch them. Do not enter the reservoirs. Not because the fish-things are dangerous but because the reservoir water is deeper than it should be, and the rules of depth are somewhat provisional, and you may find that descending into the reservoir involves descending further than the physical dimensions of the basin should allow.

The sounds in the highest, newest construction—those movements that resolve only in peripheral vision—leave them be. They are the plateau's newest expressions, forms that have not yet committed to being fauna or flora or mineral or something else entirely. They are shy. They are uncertain. They are experiencing the same process of becoming that the stone is experiencing, and they deserve the same patience.

## How to Interact with the Stone

Touch it. I cannot emphasize this enough. Touch the pre- whenever you have the opportunity. Press your palms against it. Lean your back against it. Rest your forehead against it when you are tired.

The pre- is warm. It holds sound. It seems to listen. These are not metaphors. The stone absorbs acoustic energy and releases it slowly—hours later, days later—and what it releases is not always what was absorbed. You may hear your own voice coming back to you changed, made more melodic, or more mournful, or spoken in a language you do not recognize. You may hear conversations that occurred in front of that wall decades ago. You may hear nothing but a low hum that seems to be the stone's own voice, its contribution to the soundscape.

The warmth of the pre- is not merely thermal. It is a warmth that feels like attention. When you touch it, you will have the distinct sensation of being noticed—not judged, not evaluated, simply *registered*. The stone knows you are there. It does not mind. It does not welcome. It notices, and it continues its process, and you are briefly part of that process, a small thermal event in its long mineralogical becoming.

In the oldest parts of the city, the pre- has become more like ordinary stone. It is still slightly warm. It still holds sound. But it has settled. It knows what it is. Touching it is like touching an elder—something that has completed the journey you are witnessing in its younger layers.

In the newest parts, the pre- is almost too present. Touching it can be overwhelming. It is warm enough to be uncomfortable. The attention it pays you is more intense, more curious, as though it has not yet learned the older stone's patient disinterest. You may feel your thoughts become slightly disordered in contact with the fresh pre-, as though the stone is trying to incorporate you into its process, trying to figure out what kind of mineral you might become. You will not become a mineral. But the sensation of being considered for that role is unsettling.

## How to Speak to the People

The residents of Newstrata do not think of their city as strange. They have lived with the pre- for generations. They have adapted to its rhythms, its provisionality, its slow becoming. When you ask them about the reality breakdown—and you will ask, because it will fascinate and disturb you—they will look at you with a mild confusion that is not performative. To them, the thin places are simply places. The walkers are simply animals. The stone that listens is simply stone.

Do not treat their home as a curiosity. Do not treat their adaptation as something that requires explanation or correction. They are not primitive or deluded or exceptionally tolerant. They are residents of a place that operates on different principles than the places you are accustomed to, and they have learned those principles the way anyone learns the principles of their home—by living in them until they become invisible.

Ask them about the pre-. They will tell you what they know, which is considerable and largely practical. They know which walls listen most attentively and should not be spoken in front of if you value your privacy. They know which tunnels the burrowers have recently excavated and which are still settling. They know the schedule of the plateau's growth—not the precise timing, because the pre- does not operate on a schedule, but the rhythm, the way you know the rhythm of a breathing thing.

Ask them about the lifts. The lift-operators are the city's connection to the world below, and they have a perspective that most residents lack. They see the contrast every day—up through the clouds into strangeness, down through the clouds into familiarity. They will tell you about visitors who could not adapt, who spent three days feeling the vibration and fled back down the lifts. They will tell you about visitors who stayed. They may tell you about themselves, if you have earned their trust, and how they tried to leave once and found that they could not, or could not want to, which amounts to the same thing.

Do not ask them why they stay. They have been asked this by every visitor who has ever come up through the clouds. The question implies that leaving would be the default, the rational choice, and that staying requires justification. This is not how they experience it. Staying is the default. Leaving would require justification, and they cannot provide one, because the pre- has become part of their reasoning, and the pre- does not justify itself. It simply becomes.

## The Dreams

You will dream in Newstrata. Everyone does. The reservoir water intensifies the dreams, but even without it, the proximity to the pre- affects the dreaming mind. Your dreams will be of formation. You will watch landscapes build themselves from nothing. You will see structures emerge from formlessness, achieve a temporary stability, and then revise themselves. You will witness the process of becoming from a perspective that is not available to the waking mind—the inside of the process, the view from the thing that is being formed.

These dreams are not prophetic. They are not symbolic. They are participatory. You are not dreaming *about* the pre-; you are dreaming *with* it, your sleeping mind briefly joining the process of formation, contributing whatever raw material minds contribute to a geology that is still deciding what it wants to be.

If the dreams become too intense—and they can, especially in the first week—drink water from below the clouds. Sleep in the oldest part of the city you can find, where the stone has settled. Avoid the reservoir-creature bioluminescence before bed; its slow pulse can entrain your dreaming rhythms in ways that amplify the pre-'s influence.

But do not avoid the dreams entirely. They are part of the experience. They are what the plateau sounds like when it speaks a language you can almost understand.

## What to Do When the Thin Places Find You

Not *if*. *When*.

You will be walking through a street that was not there yesterday. You will open a door and find a room that cannot exist in the building you entered. You will look out a window and see the plateau as it was before the city, bare rock and pillargrass and nothing else, and then you will blink and the city will be there again, and you will not be sure which view was the real one.

The answer is: both. Neither. The question of which is real assumes that reality is a binary state—that a thing either is or is not—and in Newstrata, reality is a spectrum. The pre- is in the process of becoming real, and its process affects everything around it, including the spaces between things, including the relationships between locations, including the simple question of where you are in relation to where you were.

When the thin places find you, do not attempt to force your way back to where you intended to be. Do not panic. Do not run. Sit down if you can. Look around. Notice where you are, not where you expected to be. The thin places are not malevolent; they are *indifferent* to your intentions, which can feel the same but is not.

If you are lost—truly lost, not temporarily misplaced—find a wall of the pre- and press your hand against it. The stone listens. It may not respond in a way you can understand, but the act of contact seems to stabilize your position in relation to the plateau. The locals do this instinctively. Visitors must remember to do it deliberately.

If you find yourself in a version of Newstrata that seems wrong—older, newer, differently configured—know that you have not traveled in time or space in any conventional sense. You have encountered a version of the plateau that the pre- is considering, a possibility that exists in the reservoir of potential from which the plateau draws its becoming. It is temporary. It will pass. You will return to the version of Newstrata that is currently committed to existing.

Usually.

## What to Do Before You Leave

Spend an evening at the rim. Watch the cloud layer below you, lit by whatever light is available—the amber glow of the gas lamps, the industrial glare of the foundries, the faint phosphorescence of the pre- itself. Listen to the city behind you—the foundry-rhythms, the lift-grinding, the wind, the subsonic pulse of stone becoming stone. Let the soundscape settle into you. Let the vibration in your sternum become familiar, become part of your body's understanding of where you are.

Touch the pre- one last time. Press both palms against it and feel the warmth and the attention and the slow upward pressure of a plateau that has been rising for centuries and will continue to rise for centuries after you are gone. Listen for whatever the stone has to say to you. It may be nothing. It may be something you cannot articulate. It may be the sound of your own voice, returned to you changed, made strange by its long residence in the mineral memory of a place that is still learning what it is.

Drink the reservoir water. Have the dreams. Let the plateau show you what it looks like from the inside of a process that has no endpoint, that is not working toward a conclusion but is simply working, endlessly, patiently, centimeter by centimeter, toward the continuation of its own becoming.

And then go down through the clouds on the lift, and re-enter the world of finished things, and carry with you the knowledge that somewhere above the clouds, a city is being built by the ground itself, and the ground is still arriving, and the stone is still listening, and the people who have chosen to live there are witnesses to the slowest and most patient act of creation you will ever have the privilege of visiting.

## A Final Note

You may not want to leave.

This is not a supernatural effect. The pre- is not a trap. The city does not hold you against your will. But something happens to visitors who stay long enough—they begin to appreciate the logic of geology, the pace of becoming, the strange comfort of living in a place that is not finished and will never be finished. They begin to see the finished world below the clouds as somehow *incomplete*, because it has stopped becoming and simply *is*, and the act of being seems to them a lesser thing than the act of becoming.

This is not true. The finished world is not lesser. It is different. It has the stability that Newstrata lacks, and stability has its own value, its own beauty, its own way of being in process that is no less meaningful for being less visible.

But you may not feel this way when the lift begins its descent. You may feel that you are leaving something important unfinished. You are not. You are leaving something that was never yours to finish. The plateau's becoming does not require your participation. It does not require anyone's participation. It simply continues, as it has continued, as it will continue, rising centimeter by centimeter into the cool dim air, arriving at itself slowly, patiently, without urgency or endpoint or conclusion.

You are a visitor to a process. The process will continue without you. And that, in the end, is what makes Newstrata what it is—not the strangeness, not the thinness, not the fauna that should not exist, but the profound and unsettling and beautiful knowledge that you are witnessing something that does not need you, that was happening before you arrived and will happen after you leave, and that your brief presence in its midst has been noted by the stone and held in its mineral memory and will be released someday as a faint sound in an empty street—a footstep, a voice, a breath—emerging from a wall that listens, a contribution to the long slow conversation between a city and the ground that is building it.

Go to Newstrata. Witness what is being made. And when you leave, remember that witnessing is enough.
