# Preparing for The Thresh

---

## Before You Even Think About Going

Ask yourself why.

I don't say this to be poetic. I say it because The Thresh is one of those locations that selects for a certain kind of traveler — the kind who confuses emptiness with clarity, who thinks that because a place demands nothing of them, it is therefore safe. It is not safe. It is demandless in the way a vacuum is demandless. It does not want to hurt you. It does not want anything. And that is the entire problem.

You need a reason to go that you can hold onto when the sound becomes you and the cold stops being information and the blue-gray light stops meaning "day" and starts meaning simply "visibility." You need a reason that is not curiosity, because curiosity dissolves around hour six. You need a reason that is not research, because research requires novelty and The Thresh does not do novelty. You need a reason that is not escape, because The Thresh will let you escape into it forever.

Write your reason down. Physical medium. Waterproof it. You will need it later.

## The Cold

Let me be precise about the cold, because "cold" is insufficient in the way "wet" is insufficient for describing the ocean.

The Thresh's cold is not the cold of deep water. Deep water cold is a simple thermodynamic fact — you lose heat, you insulate, you manage. The Thresh's cold is *displaced altitude cold*, and it behaves differently. It does not leach. It *asserts*. It is a condition, as the description says, and conditions do not negotiate.

You will need a dry suit, not a wetsuit. This is non-negotiable. The water temperature will be in the range of 1-4°C, and you will be in it for extended durations. A wetsuit works by trapping a thin layer of water against your skin and allowing your body to heat it. In The Thresh, your body will not heat anything. The cold will take the heat from that layer faster than you can generate it, and then it will take the heat from you.

Your dry suit must be:
- **Commercial diving grade or better.** Recreational dry suits are designed for water where the surface exists as a concept you can return to. The surface of The Thresh's ocean is a theoretical construct that may as well be in another dimension.
- **Fully sealed at every entry point.** Neck seal, wrist seals, boots integrated or sealed. A single failure point means the cold finds its way in, and once it's in, it does not leave.
- **Layered with active heating.** You will need heated undergarments — electrical, battery-powered, with redundant systems. Carry at least twice the battery capacity you think you need. Test the system at full depth in similar temperatures before you go. The cold of The Thresh will find the weak spot in your heating grid. It always does.
- **Fitted with a helmet, not a hood.** Your head is where you lose the most heat, and a hood can shift, flood, or compress at depth. A rigid helmet with a thermal liner gives you a sealed thermal envelope. It also gives you a place to mount lights and communication equipment, which you will need.

Your hands are a problem. You need manual dexterity to operate equipment, but gloves are the weakest thermal link in any diving system. Consider heated gloves with separate battery systems. Test them until you trust them. Then bring two backup pairs.

Your breathing gas will be cold. Every inhalation will pull frigid air across your lung tissue. This is a real hypothermia vector that divers often overlook. A full-face mask helps — the dead space in the mask retains some warmth from exhalation. Some commercial systems include gas pre-heaters. If you can get one, get one.

## The Sound

This is where most travelers fail.

The sound of The Thresh is not a problem for your ears. Let me say that again because it's counterintuitive: **the sound is not primarily an auditory problem.** Earplugs will not save you. Sound-rated helmets will not save you. The sound transmits through the rock, through the water, through your bones. Your chest cavity will resonate. Your teeth will vibrate. You will feel the thrum in your long bones like a second pulse.

What the sound actually is, is a **neurological problem.**

Prolonged exposure to low-frequency vibration does things to human neurology that we are only beginning to understand. Submariners report it. Commercial divers report it. The vibrations can induce:
- Spatial disorientation
- Time distortion (separate from The Thresh's own temporal effects, and additive with them)
- Nausea and vertigo
- Difficulty concentrating
- Altered states of consciousness — not mystical, just dissociative
- Sleep disruption (when you try to sleep, which you will need to do)
- Emotional blunting

After roughly an hour, the description says, the sound becomes indistinguishable from silence. This is accurate, and it is the most dangerous moment. When the sound becomes silence, you have lost a primary orientation cue. Your brain no longer registers the thrumming as external. It has been incorporated. You are now part of the machine's operating frequency.

This is not metaphorical. Your body is literally vibrating at The Thresh's frequency. Your sense of self — the background hum of your own existence that you don't notice until it's gone — has been overwritten.

**Countermeasures:**

1. **Active noise cancellation will not work.** The frequency is too low, the amplitude too high, and the transmission path is bone conduction, not air conduction. Do not waste weight on ANC systems.

2. **Vibration isolation in your suit.** This is the best you can do, and it's not great. Some commercial diving suits incorporate vibration-dampening layers — viscoelastic polymers, gel inserts, anything that disrupts the direct transmission of mechanical vibration from the water to your skeleton. It will reduce the amplitude. It will not eliminate it.

3. **Structured timing.** You must limit your exposure to the sound in discrete blocks. I recommend no more than four hours at a stretch before returning to a shelter or vehicle where the vibration is attenuated. Set a timer — a physical timer, not your sense of time, which the sound will corrupt — and obey it even if you feel fine. Especially if you feel fine.

4. **An anchor sound.** Bring something you can listen to that is *yours.* A piece of music, a recording of a voice, anything with a different frequency profile that you can play through bone conduction or a helmet speaker during your rest periods. The sound of The Thresh has no variation, no rhythm that wasn't imposed by a machine. Your anchor sound should be the opposite: irregular, human, warm. You need to remind your nervous system that it is not, in fact, part of the plateau's infrastructure.

5. **Know the signs of incorporation.** If you catch yourself feeling *comfortable* in the sound, you have been in it too long. If the sound stopping seems like it would be wrong or disruptive, you have been in it too long. If you can no longer imagine silence, you have been in it too long.

## The Breathing Problem

You are underwater. You need to breathe. This is obvious, but the specifics matter.

The depth of The Thresh is ambiguous — the plateau is 4,000 meters above the seafloor, but the surface is unreachable. You will be at some depth where diffuse light still penetrates, which suggests somewhere in the mesopelagic to bathypelagic transition — perhaps 200 to 1,000 meters. This is technical diving territory. Recreational scuba is not an option.

You will need:
- **Closed-circuit rebreathers,** not open-circuit scuba. Open-circuit exhales bubbles, which create noise (adding to the acoustic environment), disturb the fauna, and waste gas. Rebreathers recycle your exhalations, scrub the CO₂, and add oxygen as needed. They are quieter, more gas-efficient, and warmer to breathe. They are also more complex and have their own failure modes. You must be trained and current on rebreather operation before you go.
- **Redundant gas supplies.** At minimum, two independent rebreather systems or one rebreather plus bailout cylinders. Know your bailout limits — how far can you swim, at depth, on bailout gas, to reach your shelter? The distances on The Thresh are vast. A bailout cylinder that gives you fifteen minutes of emergency gas is useless if the nearest shelter is two hours away.
- **CO₂ scrubber material in excess.** The cold and the depth will affect your scrubber efficiency. Carry more than the calculated requirement. Scrubber failure means CO₂ poisoning, which begins with headache and confusion — symptoms that The Thresh's sound will mask until you are too impaired to respond.

## Navigation

You will get lost. Accept this and prepare for it.

The Thresh defeats navigation through several mechanisms:
- **Visual monotony.** Basalt ridges repeat. Obsidian flows repeat. The fauna clusters and falls away in patterns that look deliberate but aren't. There are no landmarks, only instances of the same landmark.
- **Temporal distortion.** When you cannot feel time passing, you cannot estimate distance traveled. A swim that took two hours feels like twenty minutes. A swim that took twenty minutes feels like two hours.
- **Acoustic saturation.** Sonar — your primary underwater navigation tool — will struggle. The Thresh's own sound will clutter your returns. The basalt ridges will give you hard reflections but no differentiation. The installations will stand out, but which installation? They repeat too.
- **Scale.** The plateau is continental. You are not. Even with perfect navigation, you are a mote on a surface that extends beyond your operational range in every direction.

**What to do:**

- **Trail line.** If you are exploring from a fixed shelter or vehicle, deploy a guideline behind you. Physical, tangible, impossible to misinterpret. Follow it back. Always. The Thresh will try to make you forget the line exists. It will make the water feel so uniform that movement seems pointless — you were always here, you will always be here, the line is just another unchanging thing. Do not listen to this feeling. The line is your way out.
- **Inertial navigation unit.** Military-grade if you can get it. These track your movement by accelerometer and gyroscope, requiring no external signals. They drift over time, but for the duration of a single dive, they'll give you a position fix accurate enough to find your shelter.
- **Beacons on your shelter.** Acoustic beacons, set to a frequency that cuts through the thrum. Test the frequency before deployment — you need something The Thresh's sound doesn't swamp. When you navigate back, you navigate toward the beacon.
- **Mapping discipline.** Every feature you encounter, log it. Bearing, distance from last feature, description. Even if the descriptions feel identical. Even if you're certain you're logging the same ridge for the third time. The act of logging forces your brain to treat the environment as information rather than ambience.

## The Fauna

The fauna of The Thresh will not attack you. Let me be clear about this. The tube worms do not care about you. The bottom-feeders do not care about you. The large, slow grazers in the gray distance do not care about you. You are not food. You are not competition. You are not a threat. You are furniture.

This does not mean the fauna are safe.

**The tube worm groves** grow thick around the installations — thick enough to obscure structural details, thick enough to block your path. Their chitin shells clatter in the current, which adds to the acoustic environment. Do not push through them. The shells are sharp, and your suit — your thermal envelope, your life support — is not armored. A torn dry suit in 2°C water at depth is an emergency that becomes a fatality fast. Go around. It will take longer. Take the longer way.

**The bottom-feeders** scuttle between the structures. They are dog-sized and armored and they do not see you because they have no eyes. They detect you through vibration — the vibration of your movement, your breathing apparatus, your presence in the current. You are, to them, just another mechanical output. They will not flee you. They will not approach you. They will simply incorporate you into their spatial map and move around you, which is unsettling in a way I cannot fully convey until you've stood on a basalt shelf and watched a pale, eyeless creature the size of a Labrador treat you as an obstacle no more significant than a pipe. You will feel what the description means by *irrelevant.*

**The large grazers** are the only real risk, and the risk is not predation. It is simply mass. Something large moves in the gray distance. If it moves through you, your suit will not protect you. The risk is minimal — the open water is mostly empty, and the grazers seem to stay at distance — but it is not zero. If you see something large, give it space. It will not adjust for you.

**The real fauna danger is indirect.** The fauna cluster around the installations because the installations leak heat and chemicals. The water near the structures is warmer — slightly, noticeably — and chemically distinct. This is attractive to the organisms and may seem attractive to you, a warm spot in a cold place. But the chemical compounds in the runoff have not been studied, and you have no way to know what they will do to your suit materials, your rebreather scrubber, or your body. Do not use the structures as warming stations. Do not linger in the fauna-dense zones any longer than your exploration requires.

## The Structures

You will want to explore the installations. This is why you came, probably. The half-built rigs, the orphaned pipelines, the dormitory module that connects to nothing — these are the closest thing The Thresh has to narrative, and humans are narrative animals. We want to know who built them and why and what happened.

You will not find out. I tell you this not to discourage you but to set expectations. The Thresh does not explain itself. The structures are partial and running and empty and they will remain so. But you will explore them anyway, because that is what travelers do, and you should do it with the following in mind:

**Structural integrity is unknown.** These installations were built for a world that may have different physics than yours. They have been running unattended for an indeterminate period. Metal fatigues. Seams corrode. What looks solid may be one current-stress away from collapse. Do not enter enclosed spaces without a tether and a buddy. If you have no buddy, do not enter enclosed spaces.

**The dormitory module** will tempt you. A dry space — flooded, yes, but a space designed for human occupancy, with walls and a floor and the ghost of purpose. You will think about shelter. You will think about what it would be like to drain it, to seal it, to have a place where the water isn't. Resist this. The module connects to nothing. Its seals are compromised. And even if you could make it habitable, you would be living inside the machine's architecture, and the sound — the sound would be worse inside the structures, not better. The basalt conducts it. The steel amplifies it.

**The active machinery is dangerous.** The extraction engines run. They have moving parts, intake grates, exhaust flows. The fauna can survive being pulled into an intake. You cannot. Stay clear of operating machinery. Do not touch it. Do not investigate it closely. You are not a maintenance technician and this is not your machine.

**The heat.** Some parts of the installations will be hot — engine housings, exhaust stacks, smelter vents. The water near them will be visibly distorted by thermal gradient. The fauna cluster as close as they can stand. You should not match their tolerance. A suit breach near a hot surface means the cold and the heat meet at your skin, and your body is not built for that negotiation.

## Time and Mind

This is the hardest section to write, because it is the hardest part to prepare for.

The Thresh does things to time. Not through any supernatural mechanism — just through monotony, through uniformity, through the removal of every cue your brain uses to mark duration. The light does not change. The cold does not change. The sound does not change. The fauna cycle through their generations but their behavior does not vary. You cannot feel hours passing.

Your internal clock, which is already unreliable under the best conditions, will simply stop working. This is not a metaphor. Circadian rhythms require light cues. Temporal awareness requires event boundaries — transitions that signal "something different is happening now." The Thresh has no transitions. It has no events. It is one long continuous event that does not end until you leave.

**What this does to you:**

- You will lose track of how long you've been submerged. Gas management requires accurate time tracking. If you don't know how long you've been down, you don't know how much gas you have left. This can kill you.
- You will lose track of how long you've been *present.* Days? A week? The Thresh does not care, and after a while, neither will you. This is the "permission to stop changing" the description mentions. It is not malevolent. It is just what happens when the environment offers no resistance to your presence and no impetus for your movement.
- You will begin to dissociate. The sound becomes your body. The cold becomes your state. The blue-gray light becomes your vision. You are no longer a person in a place. You are an aspect of the place. The plateau thrums, and you are part of the thrum, and the thrum has always been, and the thrum will always be, and you cannot remember a time before the thrum and you cannot imagine a time after it.

**Countermeasures:**

- **External timekeeping.** Multiple redundant timers. Dive computer, waterproof watch, helmet display. Set them to count *up* — elapsed time, not time remaining. Time remaining requires you to know when you started, and you will forget. Elapsed time is just a number that gets bigger.
- **Scheduled activities.** Every X minutes, you do something. Check your gas. Log your position. Play your anchor sound. The schedule gives you a rhythm that is not the machine's rhythm. It is artificial and it is fragile and it is the only thing standing between you and incorporation into the system.
- **A exit time, decided before you enter.** Not a feeling — a number. "I leave after 72 hours." Write it down. Set alarms. When the alarm goes, you go, regardless of what you feel. The Thresh will make 72 hours feel like 12. It will make leaving feel premature, unnecessary, absurd. This is the Thresh talking. Leave anyway.
- **A companion, if at all possible.** Another person is the best anchor you can have — someone who shares your timeline, who can check your gas when you forget to check it, who can say "we've been down too long" when you have lost the ability to judge. The Thresh works on individuals. It is less effective on pairs. Not immune — less.
- **Your written reason.** The one I told you to waterproof at the beginning. Read it when the thrum becomes silence. Read it when the cold becomes condition. Read it when you start to feel like the plateau is where you've always been. It will not snap you out of it — nothing will snap you out of it — but it will give you something to hold while you orient yourself.

## Practical Checklist

**Thermal Protection:**
- Commercial-grade dry suit, fully sealed
- Active heating system with 2× battery capacity
- Heated undergarments, gloves (2 backup pairs), and helmet with thermal liner
- Full-face diving mask or helmet
- Gas pre-heater if obtainable

**Breathing:**
- Closed-circuit rebreather (trained and current)
- Redundant bailout system
- Excess CO₂ scrubber material
- Spare oxygen and diluent cylinders

**Acoustic/Sensory:**
- Vibration-dampening suit layers
- Personal anchor sound device with bone conduction or helmet speakers
- Structured exposure schedule (4 hours max per block)
- Warning card (written, waterproof) listing incorporation symptoms

**Navigation:**
- Guideline reel with at least 500m of line
- Inertial navigation unit
- Acoustic beacons for your shelter/vehicle
- Mapping log (waterproof slate or housing-protected device)

**Time Management:**
- Multiple redundant timers (dive computer, watch, helmet display)
- Pre-decided exit time, written and waterproofed
- Scheduled check-in intervals (gas, position, time, mental state)
- Alarms set to cannot-ignore volumes

**General:**
- Powerful dive lights (the light is diffuse but dim; you will want to see details, and detail matters for navigation and safety)
- Cutting tool (entanglement in tube worm groves or damaged netting)
- Surface marker buoy (if you can reach the surface, which you probably cannot, but carry one anyway)
- First aid kit focused on hypothermia, CO₂ exposure, and barotrauma
- Written reason for visiting, waterproofed, on your person

**Psychological:**
- A companion, if at all possible
- Pre-departure counseling with someone who understands dissociative environments
- An exit plan that does not rely on your judgment at the time of exit
- Something to return to — a next destination, a commitment, an obligation. The Thresh offers a kind of peace that is hard to refuse. You need a reason to refuse it that exists outside the water.

## A Final Note

The Thresh is not evil. I want to be clear about this. It is not malicious, it is not predatory, it is not even hostile in the way that some locations are hostile — actively, personally, as if the environment resents your presence. The Thresh does not resent you. It does not register you. You are below its threshold of notice.

This is worse.

Hostility is a relationship. It means you matter enough to oppose. The Thresh offers no relationship at all. It offers equilibrium — a steady state in which nothing changes, nothing needs to change, and your presence is so inconsequential that the system does not even bother to reject you. It simply continues, and you continue within it, and the boundary between you and it dissolves not through violence but through sheer, grinding, unrelenting sameness.

People have not come back from The Thresh. Not because they died — though some have — but because they stopped coming back. They found a rhythm in the thrum. They found a comfort in the cold. They settled into the blue-gray light and let their timers run out and did not leave because leaving seemed like an interruption of something that was, finally, complete.

This is what you are preparing for. Not danger. Not strangeness. *Surrender.*

The cold will not kill you if you prepare. The sound will not kill you if you manage your exposure. The depth will not kill you if you respect your gas limits. The fauna will not kill you if you keep your distance.

But The Thresh will offer you a peace that is indistinguishable from cessation, and you will be tempted to take it, and the only thing that will stop you is the part of you that remembers what it means to *change.*

Feed that part. Keep it warm. Bring it home.

Then leave.
