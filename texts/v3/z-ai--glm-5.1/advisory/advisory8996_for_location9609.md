# Preparation Guide: The Hold

## Before You Go

**Abandon your assumptions about shelter.** The Hold is not a building you enter, not a cave you explore, not a ruin you investigate. It is an ongoing process that happens to be large enough to stand inside, and it will process you too if you let it. Everything that lives there—the tree, the fungus, the insects, the hands, the things below—is engaged in the same project: digestion and growth. You are either part of this or you are in the way, and the Hold does not distinguish between visitors and obstacles.

**Leave fire behind.** Not just your lighters and matches—leave the entire concept. The sap that permeates every surface, every breath, every drop of moisture in that place will catch if you give it so much as a spark, and a fire inside the Hold is not a fire you escape. It is a fire that becomes part of the architecture. The first settlers learned this when a dwelling burned. The wall behind it wept for weeks. The charred bark made sounds like small animals dying. Do not be the reason the Hold makes those sounds again.

**Calculate your comfort with confinement.** The entrance is a split in the bark, vertical, narrowing at the center like a wound trying to close. You will need to pass through this, and you will need to pass through it again when you leave, and there is no guarantee it will be the same width when you return. The insects sometimes prune it open. Sometimes they do not. The tree is trying to heal. Consider whether you can tolerate a space that wants to close around you.

## What to Bring

### Clothing

You must understand the cold before you can dress for it. The cold in the Hold is structural. It does not vary with the time of day, does not ease, does not have pockets of warmth you can find if you move to the right spot. It is simply there, as present as the walls, and it will be there the entire time you are inside.

**Layers.** Many thin layers rather than few thick ones. The residents wear pounded inner bark stitched with gut-thread, and you should respect their solution, but you are not a resident and you do not have three seasons to acclimate. Bring synthetic base layers that wick moisture away from your skin, because your skin will be wet constantly. The sap will find every gap in your clothing. It will soak through. It will stain. After your first day you will smell of it—green and mineral and faintly sweet with an undertone of fermentation—and you will never fully wash that smell out of what you wore.

**Hand wraps.** The residents wrap their hands, leaving only the fingers bare. This is not cultural. This is practical. You will be touching surfaces covered in fungal growth, sap, and the gelatinous colonies. You will be gripping heartwood ribs slick with moisture. You will be carving footholds and scoring bark and handling food that numbs the mouth if processed incorrectly. Your hands are your primary tools here. Protect them. Bring wraps that can be tightened and retied as they absorb the ambient wetness, because they will absorb it, and wet wraps are worse than no wraps when the cold is structural.

**Footwear.** Nothing you own will be adequate. The floor of the Hold is a living composite of decayed wood, mycelial felt, moss, lichen, and gelatinous colonies, all of it perpetually wet, all of it digesting and rebuilding itself. You sink slightly when you walk. Your footprints fill with sap and glow. Boots that are waterproof will not help because the moisture comes from below as much as from above, and the softness of the floor means that rigid soles will make you unstable. The residents go barefoot or wear minimal wrappings. You should bring something that lets you feel the ground while still protecting you from the mites that swarm when fungal mats are disturbed. Consider separate footwear for different zones: something flexible for the floor, something with grip for climbing the heartwood ribs between dwellings.

**Head covering.** The sap weeps from the ceiling. It weeps from fissures in the walls. It weeps from the undersides of the dwellings above you. You will be dripped on constantly. A hood or cap will not stop this but it will slow the rate at which the sap reaches your face and neck, and this matters more than you think. The sap is not dangerous to skin in small quantities, but it accumulates, and sticky skin picks up fungal spores and mite carcasses and fragments of the pale mycelial felt, and after a day you will feel as though you are wearing a second layer of something alive.

**No metal.** No zippers, no buckles, no rivets. Metal conducts the cold differently than fabric and wood, and in a place where the temperature never varies, any surface that feels different becomes a distraction. More importantly, metal can spark. Stone against metal, metal against stone—the residents use stone knives for a reason, and that reason is not primitivism. It is survival.

### Tools

**Stone knife.** Not metal. Stone. The residents will teach you how to score bark if you ask, and if you do not ask they will watch you struggle and say nothing because they have learned that people who do not ask do not learn. A stone knife with a fine edge, small enough to carry in a wrap, sharp enough to score bark without tearing it. You will use this for harvesting, for carving footholds, for cutting the fungal mats when you need to access the gelatinous colonies beneath. The knife must be sharp because the fungus must be cut cleanly. Torn fungus attracts mites.

**Bark cups or collection vessels.** The residents collect water from the walls in bark cups—light, conformable, replaceable. You could bring metal or plastic containers, and they would work, but they would also mark you as foreign in a place where foreignness is a vulnerability. More practically, rigid containers are harder to carry when you are climbing the heartwood ribs between dwellings. Bring something soft and collapsible, or plan to make your own cups from the bark the residents teach you to peel.

**Heating stones.** Not for warmth—the Hold provides no context in which a few heated stones could meaningfully raise the temperature of a space that large. For food. The gelatinous colonies must be boiled before consumption, and boiling requires heated stones dropped into sap-filled bark troughs. The residents have a supply of stones they cycle between the sump's edge and the troughs, heating and cooling and heating again. Bring your own if you do not want to owe debts in a place where debts are remembered.

**Cordage.** Gut-thread if you can source it. Synthetic cord if you cannot. You will need it for repairs, for lashing gear to the bark shelves, for creating handholds where the heartwood ribs are too smooth or too far apart. The residents will share their thread if you establish rapport, but establishing rapport takes time, and cordage is too simple to be worth the delay.

**Writing materials.** Not for journaling. For silence. Sound carries strangely in the Hold, and the things that live in the walls listen. You will learn this quickly or you will learn it painfully, but you will learn it: the Hold is not a place for speech. The residents communicate through gesture, through the positioning of their bodies, through looks that carry meaning because they have carried the same meanings for three seasons. If you must communicate something complex, write it. Paper will dampen and the sap will stain it, but the words will remain legible for a time. Bring waterproof writing surfaces if you can. Slate. Wax tablets. Something that does not require you to speak.

### Provisions

**Bring food.** The Hold provides—the fruiting bodies are nutty and dense, the processed gelatinous colonies are rich and sweet—but the provision is not generous, and it is not yours until you have learned to harvest it. The residents will share, but sharing is not the same as abundance, and you will be a mouth that contributes nothing until you learn the work. Bring enough dense, caloric food for twice the duration you plan to stay. Eat it slowly. Let the Hold's food supplement what you brought, not replace it.

**Bring water.** The water from the walls tastes of sap. Everything tastes of sap. After a few days you will stop noticing, and after a few weeks it will taste like home, but you are not staying for weeks, and the mineral sharpness of it will wear on you before you acclimate. Bring clean water. Drink it when the taste of sap becomes unbearable, which will be sooner than you expect.

**Do not bring anything that spills easily.** The floor absorbs everything. A dropped container will settle into the fungal mat and begin to be digested. A spilled liquid will be absorbed into the substrate and will alter the local chemistry and the insects will notice and the mites will swarm and the balance you are trying not to disturb will be disturbed. Everything you bring should be sealed, contained, and attached to your person at all times.

## What to Learn

### The Silence

This is the first and most important preparation, and it is the one no physical gear can provide for. The Hold listens. Sound carries in ways that are not predictable—not echoing, not dampening, but rearranging, as though the walls are sorting what they hear and delivering it to different recipients based on qualities you cannot perceive. A whisper at the entrance may be audible in the deepest dwelling. A shout may vanish entirely, consumed by something in the walls that feeds on particular frequencies. You do not know the rules because the residents do not know the rules. They have simply learned to be quiet.

Practice silence before you arrive. Spend a day without speaking. See how much of your communication relies on sound, and then find other ways. Your hands can gesture. Your body can position itself. Your face can express what your voice would carry. The residents have had three seasons to develop a language of movement and proximity, and you will not learn it in a day, but you can learn to be silent, and silence is the foundation everything else is built on.

When you must speak, speak low and speak brief. Do not shout. Do not laugh loudly. Do not make sounds that carry emotional intensity, because the things in the walls seem to be drawn to intensity more than volume. The residents speak when they must, and when they speak their voices are flat and controlled, as though they are trying to make their words as uninteresting as possible to whatever is listening.

### The Hands

You will encounter them. They emerge at night to drink from the sap channels, moving in groups of three or five, pale and eyeless and flowing rather than walking. They will watch you. Their heads will swivel to track your movement. If you approach one, it will rear up and open its mouth very wide, displaying rows of something that is not teeth.

This is a threat display. This is also a greeting.

The correct response is to open your own mouth. Wide. Wider than feels natural. Wider than seems reasonable as a response to a fist-sized creature with too many legs and a mouth full of not-teeth. Open your mouth and hold it open and do not move until they retreat.

They always retreat. So far they have always retreated.

But the residents have noticed changes. The hands are holding their ground now, mouths open, waiting. They are waiting for something the residents cannot identify. When you encounter a hand that does not retreat, hold your position. Keep your mouth open. Do not advance. Do not reach toward it. Wait. If it retreats, note the direction. If it does not retreat, back away slowly, still open-mouthed, and do not return to that section of the wall until the next cycle.

Practice the mouth-opening before you arrive. It sounds absurd. It is absurd. But in the moment, when a pale eyeless thing is displaying its not-teeth at you and the blue-green light is rippling across its body and the sap is gulping somewhere below you, you will not want to be figuring out how wide you can open your mouth. You will want it to be instinct. Make it instinct.

### The Insects

They are not ants. They are not beetles. They are something between and beside, and they farm the fungal mats with a precision that should disturb you more than it does. The residents have reached an accommodation with them, and you must respect this accommodation absolutely.

Do not disturb a gallery. The galleries are carved into the softer wood, visible as regular openings in the walls and ceiling. They are the insects' homes and storehouses. Disturbing a gallery—touching it, blocking it, breathing into it—will bring a formation to investigate, and a formation of thumb-length insects with bark-scoring mandibles is not something you want to negotiate with.

Do not block a formation. Formations move through the cavity in loose groups, tending the fungal mats, carrying spores, harvesting the gelatinous colonies. If a formation is crossing your path, stop and wait. If a formation is working on a surface you need to access, find another surface or wait for them to finish. The insects operate on a schedule you do not understand, and interfering with it will not speed them up. It will only make them aware of you in a way they were not before.

In return for this respect, the insects will ignore you. They will not enter your dwelling unless you leave food unsealed. They will not approach you unless you are standing on a fungal mat they intend to prune. And occasionally, perhaps accidentally, they will prune the growth away from the entrance, keeping the split in the bark open. This is the accommodation. Honor it.

### The Harvesting

The fruiting bodies grow on the fungal mats that cover the walls and ceiling. They are small, dense, and nutty-flavored, and they are the primary food source for the residents. Harvesting them requires:

1. Identifying mature bodies. They are slightly darker than the surrounding felt, slightly firmer to the touch, and they release from the mycelial substrate with a gentle twist rather than a pull. If you have to pull, it is not ready.

2. Cutting cleanly. Use your stone knife. Slice through the stem at the base, taking the body but leaving the substrate intact. Torn substrate releases spores, and spore release triggers mite swarms.

3. Avoiding the mites. The mites are audible—a faint crackling—when their populations peak. If you hear crackling near a fruiting body, do not harvest that body. The mites will swarm. They will cover your hands and climb your wrappings and find the gaps between your fingers, and while they do not bite, the sensation of dozens of mites moving across your skin in the blue-green dark of the Hold will stay with you longer than you want it to.

The gelatinous colonies are easier to find but harder to process. They grow where the sap flows thickest, glowing brightest in the blue-green light, and they can be peeled from the substrate in strips. Raw, they cause numbness in the lips and tongue, and one of the early settlers lost sensation in his face for eleven days after eating too much. You must boil them.

Boiling requires a bark trough, sap, and heated stones. The residents will show you how to fold bark into a container that holds liquid without leaking, how to fill it with sap from the channels, how to heat stones at the sump's edge and transfer them with forked sticks into the trough. The stones will hiss when they enter the sap. The sap will steam. The steam will taste of minerals and fermentation. When the colonies have boiled for a time the residents will indicate—there is no word for it, only a gesture meaning "enough"—the resulting jelly is safe to eat. Rich. Faintly sweet. The same mineral undertone as the air, as the water, as everything.

### The Sump

Do not go to the sump.

The sump is a low place in the floor where the heartwood has rotted through entirely, opening onto a space below. The sap drains into it with a faint sound, almost a gulp, rhythmic and organic. At night the sound quickens. No one has explained why.

The residents do not discuss the things in the sump. This is not a taboo in the anthropological sense. There is no prohibition, no ritual avoidance, no spiritual framework that renders the sump sacred or dangerous. They simply do not discuss it. When you ask about the sump, they look at you with an expression that is not fear, not superstition, but something closer to the look a person gives a child who asks why they should not touch a hot stove. The explanation is obvious to them. The risk is self-evident. If you do not understand, you have not been there long enough.

Do not go to the sump.

## What to Expect

### The First Day

You will enter through the split in the bark, and the bark will close around you like a hand, and you will be inside. The light will be wrong—blue-green, rippling, casting shadows that move independently of the objects that cast them. The air will taste of sap and fermentation, and the cold will be immediate and total, and you will hear the sump gulping somewhere below you, and you will think: I have made a mistake.

You have not made a mistake. This is simply what it feels like to arrive in a place that does not care whether you are there.

The residents will watch you. They will not greet you with words. They will observe you with dark-stained fingers and wrapped hands and faces that have been numbed by the cold into expressions that could be welcome or could be tolerance or could be nothing at all. You will be assigned a dwelling—a bark shelf, grown from the wall, thickened at the edge, still weeping sap from the cut. You will sit on it. You will feel it give slightly beneath you. You will realize that you are sitting on something that is still alive, still growing, still responding to the presence of your weight.

Sleep will not come easily. The Hold does not go quiet at night. The sap continues to gulp. The hands emerge and drink and watch. The flying thing passes overhead with its voice like a question. The mites crackle in the walls. The fungus glows. The cold is structural.

You will sleep eventually. Everyone does.

### The First Week

You will stop noticing the taste of sap. This is not adaptation. This is surrender. Your body will decide that the taste is not a signal, not a warning, not something that requires attention, and it will file it away alongside the taste of your own saliva and the sound of your own heartbeat. The Hold will become the background against which you exist, and you will not realize this has happened until you drink the clean water you brought and it tastes wrong—too empty, too clean, missing the green and mineral and fermented thing that your body has decided is what water is supposed to taste like.

Your hands will stain dark. The sap will work its way under your fingernails and into the creases of your knuckles and the lines of your palms, and you will look at your hands and see the residents' hands and understand that the staining is not cosmetic. It is a record of contact. Every surface you have touched, every channel you have drunk from, every fruiting body you have harvested—all of it is written on your skin in a language the Hold can read.

You will learn to climb the heartwood ribs. The footholds are carved for the residents' feet, not yours, and the spacing will be wrong, and the sap will make everything slick, and you will fall, and the fall will be short because the cavity is only twenty paces across, and the floor will catch you softly, and you will stand up and climb again. The residents will not help you. They will not offer a hand or point out a better path. They will watch. When you reach the dwelling you were climbing toward, they will nod, once, and you will understand that the climbing was the test and you have passed it.

### The Changes

You will notice them. The residents noticed them, and you will notice them too, and you will wish you had not.

The new fissure in the ceiling, wet and raw, weeping sap that the fungal mats have not yet colonized. The deepening of the sump, the widening of the opening where the heartwood has rotted through, the sense that the space below is closer than it was. The slight outward bow of the eastern wall, as though something were pressing from behind—the bark, the bark beyond the bark, the space between the bark and the heartwood where the hands live, and the hands are holding their ground now, mouths open, waiting.

The insects have extended their galleries higher than before. They are working near the ceiling now, near the new fissure, and you will wonder if they know something you do not. They probably do. They have been here longer than you, and they are organized, and they are farming, and farmers always know when the season is changing.

You will feel the Hold changing around you. Not quickly—nothing in the Hold is quick—but continuously, the way a wound changes, the way rot changes, the way young things change as they find their shape. You are inside something that is becoming, and you do not know what it is becoming, and the residents do not know what it is becoming, and the things in the sump do not know what it is becoming, and perhaps the tree itself does not know, because the tree is not a tree in any way that would satisfy a botanist, and the Hold is not a home in any way that would satisfy a person, and the process is young, and young things change.

## When to Leave

Leave before the entrance closes. The insects may prune it open for you, or they may not. The tree is healing. The split in the bark is a wound, and wounds close, and you do not want to be inside a wound when it finishes closing.

Leave before the hands stop retreating. They are holding their ground now, mouths open, waiting. When they begin to advance—when they open their mouths and move toward you instead of away—you will not have time to reconsider your departure.

Leave before the sump deepens further. The things below are patient. The sap drains to them with a rhythm that quickens at night. They are not waiting for you. They are simply there, and the space between you and them is growing smaller.

Leave before you stop noticing the cold. The residents have stopped noticing it. They wrap their hands and check their dwellings and harvest what can be harvested, and when they sleep they sleep on bark shelves in a living room inside a living trunk in a living dark, and the cold is structural, and the cold does not vary, and they have become part of the structure. You can become part of it too. You can let the Hold incorporate you into its processes, its digestions, its slow growth toward whatever it is becoming. The residents will not stop you. They will simply watch, with their dark-stained fingers and their wrapped hands, as you settle into the fungal mat and your footprints fill with sap and glow, and the taste of the air becomes home.

Leave before this happens.

---

## Final Notes

The Hold has a name, though no one speaks it aloud anymore. Sound carries strangely here, and the things that live in the walls listen. If you must name it, write the name. Do not say it. Do not let the walls hear you claim knowledge of what this place is, because the Hold is not what it was when you arrived, and it will not be what it is when you leave, and names are a kind of fixing, and the Hold cannot be fixed.

The Hold is a process. You are visiting a process. You will be processed—stained, cold, sap-tasted, watched by hands, avoided by insects, gulped toward by the sump. This is not a danger. This is simply what happens to things that enter the Hold. The question is not whether you will be changed. The question is whether you will leave before the changing becomes a staying.

Bring warm clothes. Bring stone knives. Bring silence.

Leave before the wound closes.
