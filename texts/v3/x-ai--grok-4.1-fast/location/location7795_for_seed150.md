In a high cleft of young mountains—peaks scarcely a few thousand years from their fiery birth, their granite faces still knife-edged and unweathered, pocked only by the faintest lichen scars—a compact hollow cradles itself, no larger than a forgotten cathedral nave, its boundaries drawn by sheer walls rising a hundred feet to a jagged rim. This is no barren aerie; it is a living metropolis of vegetation, a vertical city woven from cold-hardy flora that has claimed every inch in dense, saturating profusion. The air hangs cold, a persistent 2 or 3 degrees above freezing, thick with condensing mist that beads on every leaf and frond, trickling in endless rivulets down stems and trunks. Water is ever-present here, not in raging torrents but as a shallow, peaty seep rising near the surface, turning the floor into a spongy marsh-edge where roots drink directly from the saturated soil. Fog rolls in cycles from the heights above, born of the chill air meeting warmer updrafts, cloaking the space in dim perpetual gloaming—light filters weakly through the overcast sky and the vaulted canopy, casting no true shadows, only a diffuse, pewter haze.

The "city" emerges not from stone or timber hewn by tools, but from the plants themselves: an urban sprawl of biological architecture, self-organized and infrastructure-dense, at the scale of a huddled hamlet or a single vast hall subdivided into rooms and alleys. Towering boles of ancient firs and spruces—trunks girthier than castle pillars, bark furred with insulating moss—form the load-bearing spines, their lower branches lopped not by axes but by symbiotic browsing long ago, leaving spiral scars that now sprout horizontal galleries. Vines as thick as thighs cascade from above, interlacing into rope bridges and woven balustrades, their tendrils questing slowly, inching across gaps to knit new spans. Ferns unfurl in layered ziggurats against the rock faces, their fronds overlapping like roof tiles, channeling seepage into cisterns of hollowed stumps. Mosses blanket every surface in a verdant pelt, two feet deep in places, compressing underfoot into springy carpets that pulse faintly with hidden mycorrhizae. Brambles and low shrubs pack the "streets"—narrow defiles between root-walls—forming thorny hedges that define thoroughfares, their berries frost-kissed and unripe, sustaining nothing but the plants' own propagation. No empty spaces persist; the density is total, a pure-plant ecosystem where even the air feels choked with pollen and spores, every crevice colonized, every vertical plane a climbing lattice of epiphytes.

This verdant conurbation breathes with subtle activity, a dynamic hum beneath the mostly quiet veil. Water dominates the soundscape: perpetual plinks and gurgles as droplets coalesce on leaf-tips and patter down, pooling in leaf-cups before overflowing; the low schlop of boots (or roots) in the marshy underlay; rare sighs of wind threading the canopy, rustling fronds like distant whispers. Plant-life stirs in biological rhythms—vines contracting with the cold night's advance, trunks creaking as sap slows and freezes in the veins, ferns curling inward against the chill. Erosion's youth keeps the enclosing cliffs pristine: razor ridges of schist and quartzite gleam wetly, their fresh fractures oozing mineral-rich brine that feeds the basal flora, sharp arêtes framing the hollow like the teeth of some colossal jaw.

Fauna is a spectral rarity, glimpsed more in traces than presence: a lone ptarmigan, feathers snow-mottled, pecking at moss-berries before vanishing into the fog; thread-like worms tunneling the peat, their casts forming minuscule ziggurats of their own; perhaps a spider or two, webs strung taut between vine-struts like forgotten hammocks, ensnaring spores more than flies. No predators stalk these dim passages; the ecosystem's motion is the plants' own—predatory sundews snapping at fungal spores, parasitic mistletoe burrowing into host bark, all in slow-motion churn.

The rules of this world are vegetal and unforgiving: here, architecture grows or dies, no hammers or saws profane the purity. Inhabitants—if any persist beyond the flora—must commune biologically, grafting skin to bark for shelter, drinking from root-saps, their bodies perhaps long since merged into the mass. The cold enforces dormancy's pact; warmth would unleash unchecked hypertrophy, smothering the hollow in weeks. Mist cycles dictate time: dawn's brief pallor heralds growth spurts, dusk's deepening chill halts them. To linger is to become substrate, woven into the city's slow expansion; outsiders tread lightly, or root in place. Unusual, yes—in its silent imposition of urban order upon primal wildness—but the strangeness lies not in grotesquerie, but in the relentless, quiet logic of the green: a high-alpine rookery where the buildings hunger, and the streets pulse with unblooded life. As you stand amid the packed fronds, a fresh trickle courses down your neck, and a vine-tip brushes your sleeve, testing, tasting the air's wet promise.
