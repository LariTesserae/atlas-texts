The air bites with a crystalline chill, the kind that seeps into bones without wind to carry it, hovering around freezing but never quite committing to ice. You stand—or rather, teeter—on the fractured lip of a vast highland plateau, elevation clawing toward five miles above the unseen sea, where the world below fades into a hazy rumor. This is raw birthrock country, the earth's crust still flexing and spitting itself into form: knife-edge ridges thrusting up like they were hammered yesterday, uneroded basalt columns sprouting embryonic and unweathered, their hexagonal faces polished only by frost and the first tentative licks of weather. No ancient scars here—no glacial polish, no riverine fluting—just proto-landscape, geologically newborn, a continental knot tightening under orogenic fury. Peaks and spires cluster in loose regional sprawl, a town-sized arena of jagged theater opening onto further ranges that dissolve into dimness, multi-featured but unpeopled, the scale pulling your gaze across miles of near-void expanse where one rock outcrop might lord over emptiness for half an hour's hike.

Density is the true sovereign: hollow immensity. Presences are ghosts in the volume—solitary boulders the size of houses marooned in pebbled plains, vents or spires separated by football-field voids of unbroken slab. Nothing crowds; the land breathes sparse, skeletal. Vegetation clings in miserly patches: wiry tussocks of sedge and cushion moss, pale green and desiccated, huddled in micro-pockets where snowmelt pools briefly before freezing overnight. Lichens crust the rocks in faint yellows and grays, embryonic smears on the raw basalt, but no forests, no meadows—just this thrift of life, 1 percent coverage at best, the rest naked stone under a condensing sky.

Water haunts without dominating: not crashing surf or swampy mire, but a perpetual damp intimacy. Fog wreathes the heights, born from upslope currents hauling moisture from distant lowlands, condensing on every surface in pearled rime. Rocks weep—black basalt slick with meltwater films, trickles from snow-choked crevices threading downslope in silver filaments. Hot springs bubble in scattered basins, geothermal outliers steaming defiantly against the cold, their mineral-blue pools fringed with sinter terraces, edges crusted in travertine like frozen lace. The atmosphere itself is saturate, a misty veil dimming the weak sun to a diffused glow behind overcast slabs, light levels stuck in perpetual gloaming, shadows soft and treacherous on the unweathered terrain.

Yet amid this emptiness, life thrums abundant and feral, an ecosystem in ceaseless churn. Fauna dominates the pulse: squadrons of alpine choughs wheel in loose flocks, their krark-krark calls echoing faintly across the voids, scavenging insects from thermal updraft plumes. Hardy ibex navigate the embryonic cliffs, hooves clicking like Morse on stone, males clashing horns in muffled contests that resonate for valleys. Pikas dart between talus scatters, stuffing cheeks with sparse moss; marmots whistle alarms from boulder thrones, their burrows honeycombing the raw substrate. Around the vents, thermophiles cluster—midges and flies in buzzing clouds, drawn to the warmth, prey for pipits and ravens. Arctic foxes prowl the fringes, silver coats blending with frost, shadowing the herds. Predation cycles visibly: a golden eagle stoops on a ptarmigan flushing white against the rock, the chase a white streak vanishing over a ridge. No superabundance in any spot, but regionally teeming, animals stitching motion into the static stone, their tracks the only developed paths in this pre-eroded youth.

The industrial thrum emerges not from artifice—zero trace of human hand, no cables, no shacks, no quarries scarring the purity—but from the land's own mechanized vitals, raw geothermal industry writ large across the plateau. Fumaroles pock the expanse like exhaust stacks, spaced kilometers apart in the hollows: steel-gray basalt fissures hissing superheated steam in rhythmic pistons, plumes roiling 200 feet high, condensing into acid rains that etch the first faint erosion channels. Geysers erupt in factory cadence— one distant plume, the size of a grain silo, surges every quarter-hour with a subterranean groan, blasting scalding jets that rainbow briefly in the dim. Mud pots churn like vats: thick, gray slurry burping methane bubbles, boiling in cauldrons rimmed by sulfur blooms yellow as brass. These are the engines of the underworld, scaled production of heat and gas from mantle depths, electrifying the air with ozone tang, mineral vapors scaling the rocks in terraced factories of nature. No builders, untouched by tool—just the planet's internal forge, active and relentless, cycling heat against cold, steam against frost, birthing vapors that feed the fog and wet the stones.

Soundscape holds mostly quiet reverence: the plateau's vastness swallows noise, leaving a hush broken by subtleties—the low whoosh of condensing mist, the distant pop-hiss of a fumarole priming, ibex hooves stippling stone, a chough's call fading into echo trails. Wind murmurs intermittent, riffling fog but rarely howling; geyser surges boom distantly, then subside. Dynamic weaves it all: weather fronts march across the open scale, birthing snow squalls that dust the ridges; ibex migrate seasonally along fault-line corridors; geothermal pulses quicken or slow with seismic whispers felt in the boot soles. This is a world in motion without frenzy—predator shadows on prey trails, steam sculpting sinter lips, frost wedging basalt cracks wider, life carving footholds in the emerging stone. Untouched, industrial at core, coldly wet and sparsely alive, the highland endures as its own unfinished forge.
