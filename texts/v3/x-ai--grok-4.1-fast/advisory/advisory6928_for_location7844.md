### Health and Acclimatization Preparations
As an ordinary human multiverse traveler stepping into a world suspended at 10,000 feet, your biggest immediate threat is **altitude sickness (acute mountain sickness, AMS)**. The thin air—roughly 30% less oxygen than sea level—will hit fast, causing headaches, dizziness, nausea, fatigue, and shortness of breath within hours. In severe cases, it escalates to high-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE) or cerebral edema (HACE), which can kill without descent.

- **Pre-trip acclimatization**: Spend 1-2 weeks at progressively higher altitudes (e.g., start at 5,000 ft, move to 8,000 ft). If that's impossible, use a hypoxia tent or mask at home to simulate low oxygen. Take acetazolamide (Diamox) starting 24 hours before arrival: 125mg twice daily to speed acclimatization by acidifying blood and stimulating breathing.
- **Medications and kit**: Pack ibuprofen or paracetamol for headaches, dexamethasone for severe symptoms, and nifedipine for HAPE prevention. A portable pulse oximeter is essential—aim for SpO2 above 85%; below 80% means retreat. Carry supplemental oxygen (small canister or mask) for the first 48 hours. Hydrate aggressively: 4-5 liters/day minimum, as dehydration worsens AMS.
- **Fitness baseline**: Train with cardio (hiking stairs, running at altitude if possible) and strength for legs/core to handle uneven stone paths. Avoid alcohol, tobacco, and heavy meals pre-travel—your body needs every edge.

The perpetual chill (likely 40-50°F/4-10°C day, dipping to freezing at "night") combined with dampness amplifies hypothermia risk. Skin will prickle constantly; breath fogs. No sun for warmth—diffused light means no solar heating.

- **Cold/damp prep**: Get a full medical checkup for circulation issues (e.g., Raynaud's). Pack a hypothermia thermometer and emergency mylar blankets.

### Clothing and Personal Gear
Layering is survival here—the mist beads on everything, soaking non-breathable fabrics, while windless chill conducts through stone. Dress to wick moisture while trapping heat; the stasis means no drying fires beyond faint embers.

- **Base layer**: Merino wool or synthetic long underwear (top/bottom)—wicks sweat, insulates when wet. Avoid cotton; it clings and chills.
- **Mid layer**: Fleece or wool sweater for insulation. A lightweight down puffy (packable, hydrophobic treated) for core warmth.
- **Outer layer**: Gore-Tex or eVent hard-shell jacket/pants—fully waterproof/breathable with pit zips for venting. Gaiters to seal boots from gravel/mist ingress.
- **Head/hands/feet**: Balaclava or buff (merino), wide-brim hat for diffused UV (altitude burns despite haze). Insulated gloves (liner + waterproof shell). Double boots: vapor-barrier liners inside high-traction mountaineering boots (Vibram soles for slick granite/gravel). Extra wool socks; foot powder for dampness.
- **Extras**: Goggles for mist-driven irritation, UV sunglasses (category 4 for glare off wet stone), neck gaiter for fogged breath.

Pack light (20-30 lbs total) in a 40L alpine pack with hip belt—narrow paths demand agility. Include a sit pad (closed-cell foam) for resting on cold stone.

### Shelter and Tools
The hamlet's stone hovels offer shelter: dry-stacked walls block wind, eternal embers provide faint radiant heat (place hands near but don't disturb stasis). No beds—sleep on your gear.

- **Shelter**: Bivy sack or ultralight tarp-tent for overflow (e.g., if embers feel off-limits). Inflatable sleeping pad (R-value 5+) with down quilt (20°F rating, 900+ fill).
- **Tools mimicking locals**: Flint striker and firesteel (ironic, since no fuel burns normally—but for signals?). Multi-tool with stone-age vibe: knife, awl. Rope (dynamic 30m) and harness for cliff edges/drop-off navigation. Headlamp (red mode to preserve night vision; batteries last weeks in chill).
- **Hygiene**: Biodegradable soap, trowel for cat holes (sparse veg means few spots). Wet wipes/toilet paper—dampness aids but risks infection.

### Sustenance and Water
Ecology is barren: nibble alpine grass/sedge (tough, low-cal), but no foraging jackpot. Seeps and condensation provide water—pure but mineral-heavy, possibly stagnant in stasis.

- **Water**: LifeStraw or Sawyer filter (removes bacteria/protozoa). Iodine tabs backup. Collect from moss fissures/pools; boil if embers allow (pot from hamlet?). 3L bladder + purifier yields daily needs.
- **Food**: High-calorie, no-cook: nuts, jerky, energy gels, dehydrated meals (add seep water). 3,500-5,000 kcal/day for altitude burn. Electrolytes (salt tabs) combat "mountain sickness" diuresis. Vitamin C for immune boost in isolation.
- **Duration**: Plan 3-7 days max—stasis might trap you temporally; ration accordingly.

### Psychological and Cultural Prep
This isn't adventure—it's **timeless equilibrium**, unnerving for humans wired for change. No voices, motion frozen (tools mid-use, water unboiled). Loneliness amplifies: ptarmigan calls mock isolation; ravens judge from afar. Expect derealization—days blur sans sun arc.

- **Mindset**: Meditate on impermanence; journal to mark personal time (stasis ignores clocks). Noise-cancelling earplugs ironic—embrace hush for introspection.
- **Etiquette**: Observe, don't alter. Touch embers/tools sparingly—stasis might bind you. Huddle by hearths for warmth/invitation without intrusion. If "residents" implied but inert, treat as sacred monument.
- **Risks unique**: Temporal stasis—your watch stops? Biological freeze? Pack morale boosters: book, music player (offline), photography gear to capture layers (mid-carve tools, dew patterns).
