Beneath an endless gleam of white-gold light, somewhere far from anything entirely natural yet undeniably alive, stretches a monumental structure—a city of infinite terraces carved into a vertiginous mountainscape. This place wears the skin of ancient construction, as though carved into existence at the dawn of purposeful making and then shaped further by countless generations of ideas, labor, and weathering. It is both deeply artificial and inexpressibly old, like a machine that forgot it was ever a machine.

The air is dense—not suffocating, but full of moisture that beads on every surface and nurtures a riot of green. Vines snake over stone and steel, plump mosses carpet even the tiniest cracks, and vast hanging gardens—woven into the terraces like a tapestry—spill their colors outward in bursts of yellow, scarlet, and chlorophyll-deep emerald. Water is present, but only in fragments: thin cataracts cascading down the steep walls from reservoirs above; shallow channels cut into walkways, crisscrossing the city like veins; glittering droplets that condense out of the air, sliding lazily off railings, beams, and leaves. Everything here has settled into a humid equilibrium, as though it has always been this way.

The terraced city clings precariously to spires and sheer cliffs that rise impossibly high, carved by geological forces that few alive have studied or understand. The elevation dwarfs the imagination—peaks and plateaued ridges reach beyond view into a sky of unbroken brightness. The "ground" is a concept lost to memory; the city's inhabitants seem to live wholly in this lofty domain, transitioning between layers and gradients of height as a matter of routine. The terraces spiral upward, connected by lifts, sprawling stairways, and cable trams that hum faintly as they glide through the air.

But this is no meadow of serenity. The air roars—not with the bombast of chaotic turmoil, but with a steady, industrial rhythm that feels as ancient as the architecture. Chugging pistons, groaning gears, and the constant low-spun hum of hidden turbines resound through the place, an undercurrent to the occasional hiss of steam or distant clattering of moving metal. Everything in this city moves slowly, with the patience of a system so old it has forgotten how to hurry.

Here, life thrives in unexpected nooks and spaces, a mingling of the organic and the built. Flocks of brilliantly plumed birds circle the upper terraces; they nest in cavities between gigantic stone blocks, their calls both sharp and musical, punctuating the industrial soundscape. Mammals of lithe build—something between wildcats and lemurs—leap deftly between the vines and steel beams, occasionally pausing to gaze out over the jagged horizon. In the lowest, mist-draped reaches, colonies of bioluminescent insects drift lazily, their glow reflected in the pools seeping between polished flagstones.

The human presence is omnipresent but simultaneously subdued. Industry thrums here—a slow, meticulous pulse—but little suggests frantic toil or rush. This world feels as if it fell into a rhythm long ago, and its people have settled into it, becoming stewards rather than masterminds. Their machinery is industrial, their engineering precise, but their city’s architecture reveals a respect for balance—they have sown life among their works and allowed nature to reclaim what it can. Windows and doors are overhung with greenery; houses disappear behind trellises of flowering plants; stairwells wind through high canopies. Everything is built, but nothing feels separate from the growth around it.

Light plays constantly among these terraces. It is bright, an almost alien brightness that seems more diffuse and pervasive than direct sunlight. Shadows are sparse here, muted by the ever-present radiance that reflects from pale stones, metallic surfaces, and the glinting leaves of plants that stretch to lap up more of it. Yet the brightness is not uncomfortable—it emboldens the greens and yellows of the flora, catches the iridescent sheen of insect wings, and lends a faint glimmer to every water droplet clinging to railings or tendrils of moss.

Weirdness is subtle here, hidden beneath layers of familiarity. The fauna, though abundant, seem finely adapted to an ecosystem that should not exist—trees who stretch horizontally out from terraces, rather than upward toward the improbable sky; amphibians who move with peculiar grace between water trickles and polished mechanical surfaces; the way the birds fly far higher than air should comfortably support them. Even the air itself seems peculiar—not thick, but weighted with unseen moisture that tastes of distant seas and ancient earth.

It is a world of patience, balance, and slow change. Time erodes the edges of stone blocks and fades the sheen of metal, but here the weathering only adds to the sense of permanence. Vines creep, moss spreads, water trickles, and humanity tends its labyrinthine garden-city. Machines hum endlessly, never losing step, appearing as much a part of the landscape as the cliffs themselves. In this place, everything is built, and yet nothing seems out of place. It is a world of many hands—human, animal, plant, and machine—all weaving something ancient and, in its way, harmonious.
