The world sprawls endlessly, a place both immense and suffocatingly dense, tangled in contradictions yet absolutely unyielding in its peculiar stability. The air here is heavy with the damp weight of vapor—hot and crackling, dense enough to almost taste. Heat radiates outward like a solid thing, pressing on the skin and seeping into the bones. Everything is wet, gleaming as though the land itself is constantly sweating. This place feels ripe, overripe even, always on the verge of dissolving into liquid yet mysteriously held together.

The terrain rises in sharp, impossible thrusts—craggy cliffs that pierce through massive canopies of vegetation, sheer walls streaked with veins of water that tumble lazily like molten silver. High peaks loom, jagged and black, thrusting toward a sky barely visible, hazed over with steamy mist and strange rippling distortions. These mountains defy comprehension in their geometry. They appear weathered, like ancient relics, yet their edges are jagged and new, carved sharper as though the world is perpetually on pause between erosion and formation. The ground below them alternates unpredictably between sunken marshes teeming with roots and stagnant pools, and brittle plateaus barely large enough to stand on, edged by precipitous drops. 

A suffocating lushness dominates the ecology. Plant life blooms here as if it consumes the very essence of the world. Towering columns of moss-covered stone are tightly encased in twisting vines with translucent, bioluminescent fruit that drip viscous beads into the pools below. Fleshy ferns with fronds like parasols rise from shallow water, exuding a slick, oil-like sheen. Lichens congeal in mosaics of jewel tones, bright enough to rival gemstones. This greenery sprawls in layers: tiny luminescent fungi crest the mossy bases of ancient, smooth-skinned trees, whose canopies stretch far above and blot out much of the sky. It is lush and labyrinthine, a sensory overload of competing greens, yellows, and subtly shimmering blues.

And yet, for all this life, nothing scuttles or crawls, flies or wriggles here. If the plants rustle, it is not from any living creature. Instead, sound rains like hammers from the sky to the skin of this world, filling it to the brim, drowning it entirely. The air is deafening, an immovable cacophony of noise that cannot be localized—carrying tones that seem mechanical without machinery. It is impossible to determine its source. Waves of inscrutable tones rise and pulse: resonant howls that fluctuate between deep guttural bellows and high-pitched keens, echoes that span the extremes of the audible spectrum.
