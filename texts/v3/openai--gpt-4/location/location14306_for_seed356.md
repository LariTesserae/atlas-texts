A planet-wide darkness cradles a labyrinthine world of endless plants. They sprawl in every direction — towering columns twisting skyward, sprawling canopies weaving into one another, root networks braided so tightly they form impenetrable floors. This is an ecosystem that has achieved totality. Every surface alive. Every form a variation of green, of bioluminescent glow, of creeping vegetative invention. The air is humid and heavy, saturated with the metallic tang of dissolved minerals. Somewhere unseen, water seeps and murmurs.

The ground is spongy underfoot, though there is no foot to step upon it. Layers of moss, pressed leaves, and roots intermingle to form a surface as soft and yielding as skin. Occasionally, small puddles of water pool in hollows, trembling faintly with the tremorless weight of a thousand stems holding the world upright. The light is almost a memory. What little illumination exists is born from the vegetation itself — a pale blue-green radiance leaking softly from the undersides of fern-like fronds, the vein-webs of massive jungle leaves, the bulbous sacs of translucent fruits filled with mist. It is not enough to see far; everything dissolves into shadow just a dozen meters away.

The sound here is not silence but a spectral stillness. As the water slides between unseen crevices and drips patiently from tendrils above, its echoes play a slow, rhythmic music — as though the planet hums softly to itself. Now and then, a vine shifts, or an enormous mushroom cap tilts, releasing a gentle spray of spores into the thick air. These disturbances are rare. Mostly, the world feels as if it has already found its center, its equilibrium. It has stabilized.

You might call the temperature warm, though here, without animal breath to give meaning to the concept of warmth, it is simply the thermal weave of things alive. The heat, like everything else, rises gently from the plants — perhaps from a slow chemical digestion happening deep within their roots, or from the slight windless friction of leaves pressing on other leaves.

The sky, if it can still be called such, does not exist as open space. Instead, canopies interlock and braid above, forming an uninterrupted ceiling of foliage so dense that no outside starlight can breach it. What lingers is a diffuse glow, like the memory of a sun long-set. Perhaps there is no outside. Perhaps the ecosystem is so vast and so ancient now that it has enveloped whatever once marked the boundaries between land and air, inside and out. No weather moves here. No change seeps through the layers. It is a world at rest, folded into itself.

The water is palpable in the air; you might imagine the atmosphere itself sweating from the closeness of a million intertwined photosynthesizers. Condensation gathers in slow, weeping rivulets along the jagged rock faces that jut from the tangling roots at the planet’s lower elevations. These rocks are newborn — sharp-edged, raw, as though forced upwards through layers of life by some ancient geological whim. They lack the roundness of time-worn stone, standing out brutally against the softened mass of the plant world. Water pools at their bases as if drawn there irresistibly, creating an intricate system of shallow streams and rivulets that feed the roots from below.

Though devoid of animals, the world is not alone in its strangeness. Evidence of some immense and unknowable technological process resides deep within the ecosystem, its presence hinted at not by noise and motion but by perfect, seamless regularity. Huge cylindrical plants pulse softly with the rhythm of an invisible engine, transporting fluids in ways that defy biology. Some protrude immense, flower-like structures that appear to radiate energy, though their light is swallowed almost instantly by the immersive gloom. At certain intervals, you can detect rows of spiral growths or flattened leaves emitting mechanical buzzing tones, seemingly just above the range of human hearing. Whether these forms arose organically, or if they were placed here by hands long gone, is impossible to know.

This is not a place for animals, nor for life as you might recognize it. The ecosystem has consumed the role of architect, engineer, and steward of this planet. Any previous living or mechanical entities seem to have been absorbed, digested, and perhaps reorganized as part of the ecosystem’s internal logic. Whatever rules govern this sprawling, photosynthetic world operate on a timescale so alien that human perception can only grasp its surface.

Beneath the hush of dripping water and faint electrical thrumming lies deep silence. It is the silence of eternity, of something that has grown to its fullest extent and settled into a strange permanence. The lightless world lacks hunger, lacks competition. It is a place where growth is no longer an act of becoming, but of sustaining. Every fiber of plant matter – from the smallest root filaments to the gargantuan leaf systems that span unbroken for kilometers – seems content with its purpose. 

It might even seem harmonious, in its dark and crowded way. But there is a feeling, a deep one, that this harmony came at some unknowable cost. A silence this complete feels haunted by absence – of light, of sound, of creatures that breathe. Here, every possible space has been consumed, every niche filled.

The deeper truth might be that you do not find this place. It finds you, or it finds a way to loosen your being so it can enfold you into its vast web. Should this happen, there would be no more movement. No noise. Only stillness, and the lightless warmth of life persisting forever.
