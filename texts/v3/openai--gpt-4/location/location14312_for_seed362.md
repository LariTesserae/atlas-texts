The landscape stretches out like a dream of excess, a place so alive it seems paradoxically lifeless—an infinite expanse where all things are plant, and yet there is no water. The terrain is low and settled, an ancient plane of existence that has grown into a logic of its own. The air has the crisp, dry chill of a neglected storage room that has been untouched for centuries, though the environment itself is far from stagnant. 

The substrate underfoot is plant-made but unrecognizable as soil. What serves as ground is a densely woven mass of fibrous roots and trunks, a jigsaw of interlocking plates and spiraled growths. Some sections feel springy underfoot, others like petrified wood, and others still are gelatinous and strangely warm, as though the architecture of life has stumbled into mimicry of stone, rubber, or living tissue. It is all plant, but nothing is green. Instead, there are shades of muted bone, coppery reds, and soft golden hues, as if the photosynthetic spectrum here has rewritten its own rules.  

The space is vast, horizonless, bordered only by an unimaginable scale of brightness that floods the landscape. The light seems inherent, emanating from everywhere rather than a single source. Sunlight? No. It's a glow that rises in thin beams and then scatters, refracted by crystalline plant structures that rise in dizzying fractals like coral reefs built on glyphs from a forgotten mathematics. Some of these towering forms feel like chimneys, hollow and alive with a pulse that carries through the air like some long, low hum just out of hearing.

Everywhere, plant-life twists and sprawls with aggressive purpose, forming walls, platforms, and structures that climb to improbable heights. Immense columns of braided vines loop and arc, their spongy surfaces scrawled with fractal-like growth patterns. Some areas feel almost like a city of impossible proportions—vaulting arches formed of knotted branches lead to amphitheaters of ferns large as arena domes, their leaves sheltering other strange flora that emit pale lights like streetlamps. Yet, while the place feels made, even designed, there is no clear sapient hand in its construction. It *is* its own creator.

Despite the density, it burns with absence. There are nearly no signs of fauna save for rare, flickering presences—small, insect-like creatures formed of translucent fibers, or drifting bursts of some pollen-based intelligence. These creatures appear only briefly, then vanish, leaving behind the echo of motion. The silence is broken only by faint creaks and groans, the sound of organic scaffolding expanding, or a distant, rhythmic thrum: not quite mechanical, but too precise to be purely natural. Perhaps it's the rhythm of the ecosystem itself resetting, cycling energy through its interconnected life-signs.

Signs of erosion are subtle but discernible in the slight curvature or sagging of the enormous plant structures, as if the place has lived through countless cycles without collapse. And though it feels tranquil, resting-state movement betrays enormous unseen processes. Shifts in temperature ripple softly through strata of the towering plant mass, and structures expand or contract with their own internal timing, a world quietly metabolizing itself. 

Gravity feels tenuous here and indifferent, coupled with a strange reality breakdown: the rules of motion distort at odd intervals. In one direction, you might feel light and buoyant; in another, your steps become slow and deliberate, as though pushing against some immense weight. And the laws of time itself feel warped, stretching and contracting like breathing, deeply folded into the active mechanics of the living world-entity that surrounds you.

It is a place of overwhelming brightness, yet one populated by quiet, constrained soundscapes. You might glimpse something shifting in your peripheral vision—a pillar moving slightly as though it acknowledged your presence—before resolving itself as still. The emptiness of fauna amplifies the feeling that something *more* lives here, unseen and indifferent, larger than any notion of "living thing" could encompass.

And strangest of all, in some areas, faint, almost imperceptible echoes of machines hum on the fringes of sound. Vast swathes of the ecosystem have clearly industrialized themselves—but not in human terms. Engines are alive here, crafted entirely of organic material, their movements powered by plant chemistry or photosynthetic fuel. Pipes of flexible stalks pump fluids to other parts of the ecosystem, and intricate fractal plant-factories exhale plumes of shimmering spores instead of smoke. It is an industrial system incorporated into the rules of biology itself.

Here, existence seems less a battle than a self-sustaining loop: a vast, sprawling entity that devours its own light and breath to grow and evolve. It is as if the planet is one single, continuous organism shaped by blind will, and any trace of individuality has long since been erased. To step here is to enter an ecosystem so total it cannot distinguish predator from producer, plant from place, life from framework. It simply exists, consuming and creating in endless, symbiotic repetition.
