The place begins in shadow, pinpricks of light glinting off slick stone and drooping fronds. It is cool here, an eternal twilight where the air is dense, cool, a fraction too heavy, like it’s about to condense into droplets if you breathe too deeply. The ground underfoot is irregular, raw in a geological sense, a place that feels like it is only just deciding what it will become. Cracked and fractured cliffs rise steeply, folded into sharp ridges and jagged terraces as if heaved upward by some restless force not too long ago. The smell of wet minerals, metallic and sharp, mingles with the damp, earthy scent of vegetation.

The vegetation seems subtle at first glance, almost sparse—but look closer, look longer, and slender vines creep hesitantly down stone faces, each leaf speckled with dew. Fungal glows pulse dimly in the hollows, their rhythms staggered and mismatched, like breathing organisms. Mosses wobble softly in the cool drafts, shards of vivid green clinging to raw rock edges. Here and there, pockets of denser growth erupt in surprising bursts: lush, untamed tangles of moss-draped trees and thick underbrush collected in sheltered crags. This isn't a lush rainforest—this place clings to precarious perches, growing where it can, where conditions permit.

Above all, the sensation of height dominates. The landscape sprawls like an open wound—vast, irregular terraces that seem carved more by tension and upheaval than by erosion. The scale is daunting; elevations stretch so high that cloudframes cling midway to mountainsides, mist spilling into river-cut ravines below. Every ledge and plateau is like a world unto itself, an isolated ecosystem waiting to thrive or diminish. Glimpses of distant peaks reveal improbably steep spindles of rock like frozen waves lurching upward. Their surfaces shimmer oddly even in the dim light, as though they are not yet solidified, still resolving, waiting to settle into their final form.

But the industrial presence looms in counterpoint. Though the cliffs and slopes seem ancient and embryonic in their grandeur, human activity has sliced into the rawness with sharp intent. Great machines squat precariously on outcroppings, mechanical leviathans spitting intermittent roars and clouds of steam at irregular intervals. Conveyor belts snake through impossible slopes, moving unseen products down into what might be factories embedded into the stone. Dim lights buzz from flickering posts and sconces clinging to cliff faces, casting too-little light and creating long shadows on the uneven stone. Metal walkways span impossible drops. Hissing valves and screeching wheels echo across the heights, a cacophony punctuated by the groaning shriek of growing infrastructures forced to conform to the landscape's savage architecture.

The air is heavy with life, though much of that life is difficult to see. Birds wheel low along aching updrafts, their cries drowned by the grinding of gears. Something buzzes—thick, mechanical? Insectile? Strange biologies dart from ledge to ledge; winged forms that can't be fully glimpsed, bodies sinuous yet spiny. They congregate where the fumes are thickest, seemingly unbothered by the noxious tendrils of vapor spewing from the manufactories. In the craggy rock faces, movement catches the corner of the eye—a brief suggestion of slithering limbs or a ripple as if the stone itself stretched under an unseen will.

Perhaps the strangest sensation is the pervasive wrongness of it all. The boundaries between life and machine seem thin, as though the two are constantly exchanging elements of one another. In some overgrown stretches, gleaming metallic veins and gears grow fused into the bark of trees, as if organically integrated—and conversely, certain factories emit clouds of spores instead of smoke, their chimneys glowing faintly green. Some pieces of machinery seem to “bleed” black oil, viscous and pooling, and the air sings with electrical hums that morph inexplicably into organic chirps on the wind. 

The dimness doesn't help your sense of orientation. There is light, but it isn't conventional: a mixture of bioluminescence from the fungal lifeforms, occasional bursts of industrial sparks, and a faint reddish-orange haze that seems to come from nowhere and everywhere all at once. And the shadows—the shadows are wrong. Or maybe the problem is the way that, sometimes, your own shadow fails to fall where you expect it to. Or the way the horizons blur with vertiginous length, making it unclear where this place might end.

Sounds come and go in waves—quiet static here, the metallic groan of something shifting there. The industrial elements dominate at times: factories churn, machines screech, rivets are driven deep into rock harder than steel. But the natural sounds rival them. Water drips endlessly from somewhere just out of sight. Wind wails as though caught in enormous, invisible hands. There are animal calls, too—sometimes familiar, sometimes unplaceable. And far away—too far to be real—there is an unearthly hum, low and persistent, threading through the other noise like some buried heartbeat.

Everything here seems poised between formations, settling into grooves that haven’t yet been carved or rolling downhill in a slow, crumbling acceleration. Whatever is happening here is patient yet relentless, the way mountains are born. But the weirdness—or more precisely, the way reality itself wavers at the edges—suggests that this world might not even solidify before it shatters into something entirely different. There is no promise that gravity will remain consistent, that time will flow in a straight line, that the lights will stay dim. Everything, even the foundations, feels negotiable.

Is this a city being born or dying? Does it evolve, struggle, or simply persist? Hard to say. But one thing is certain: it is not like anything you've known before.
