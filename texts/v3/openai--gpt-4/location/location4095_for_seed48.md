The world opens up in a haze of wet heat, where the air hangs thick, forming beads of moisture that collect on every surface. The ground underfoot is soft, not from fertile soil or loam, but from layers of fine sediment that have patiently settled over millennia, worn down by the relentless caress of water and time. The land undulates slightly, resembling a shallow, endless basin that seems like it is waiting for a flood. The horizon is dim, smudged by a perpetual twilight sky, the light filtered as if through heavy gauze, giving everything a muted, amber glow.

There is little life here as you would know it, and yet the world is alive with other presences. Vegetation is absent, but the landscape brims with motion — a teeming cacophony of creatures scuttling, wriggling, and spiraling through the air. The eye struggles to make sense of them. Most are soft-bodied things, translucent and iridescent, varying in size from the barely discernible to head-height monstrosities. Arms and appendages twitch and reach into the moisture-rich air, seeming to pluck sustenance from it. At times, they float in intricate, choreographed patterns; at others, they pulse and dive low into the water-laden ground. Despite their overwhelming presence, their movements are hushed, diminished in the thick atmosphere; the occasional plop of water and faint slithering sounds are all that remain of their activity.

Everywhere, the air carries the scent of minerals — metallic, tangy, with fleeting notes of salt and sulfur. The underfoot layers occasionally reveal unexpected hardness: veins of blackened rock or slick tongues of volcanic glass breaching the soft terrain. On closer inspection, these are not wholly natural. Thin channels and grooves carved into them suggest the touch of design, long abandoned but weathered elegantly into the ever-damp land. These ancient architectures, if they are that, have melted into functionless beauty — spires reduced to smooth pillars, hexagonal grids barely distinguishable from natural basalt, shells of broken structures turned into strange, hollow monuments.

Yet this is not a ghostly or empty place; something has taken up residence here. The constructed forms of an industrial past rise defiantly out of the haze. Low, square silhouettes of factories and workshops form a scattered mosaic, their surfaces crawling with condensation. Pipes extend out from the structures like the limbs of some enormous metallic spider, running along shallow ravines and threading into hills that are almost entirely artificial. Glistening rivulets of warm water seep endlessly from the corroded conduits, feeding the wet ground where fungal-like crusts cling. The faint hum of active machinery breaks the quiet, and a rhythmic hiss suggests ongoing work deep in these weathered factories or beneath the land. Towering chimneys spew faint, near-transparent vapors that join the humid air, rising into the thick, dim skies.

The fauna that dominates this space has synchronized itself with these human-built remnants. Nests made of long-forgotten industrial metals cling to the undersides of structures, and luminous sluglike creatures follow the warmth of steam vents. The largest organisms — resembling a hybrid of insect and jellyfish — float ominously close to the ground and seem unfazed by the occasional discharged spark or puff of gas vented from the crumbling machinery. They maneuver on unpredictable currents in the heavy air, their jelly-like masses pulsating in rhythm with the faint vibrations of the earth below.

As for rules, gravity holds sway here, but lightly, like a suggestion. The wet air noticeably weighs things down as much as the soft, pliant ground does, imparting a dreamlike slowness to larger movements. Energy lingers unnaturally; the drip of water from a pipe hangs in the air just slightly longer than it should, and a tossed stone seems to curve on descent. Temperature rises abruptly near certain areas of activity, as if heat clings to some places and forgets others. Subtle variations in sound give away unseen shifts — the faintly metallic swish of unseen machinery within the earth, the hiss of wind that carries heat without force, and the rare crackle of static discharges as the humidity gathers into sudden energy.

In this place, nature, technology, and time seem to have formed an uneasy, perpetual truce. The landscape is active but not imposing, strange but not surreal. It thrums gently with a rhythm that is neither life as we know it nor death — just motion, process, and a balance too ancient to notice human concepts like peace or purpose. This world is old and strange, but not broken. It continues, as it always has, as it must, shaped by things that were never meant to be.
