The world unfurls itself as an aquatic realm, a sunken landscape veiled in washed-out darkness, haunted by cataracts of muted sound. The air and water have become one indistinguishable medium, a fluidic atmosphere that presses down yet also lifts its contents—immersing everything within a ceaseless, shifting suspension. It is not the crushing abyss of the deep ocean. It is not wholly an ocean at all. Here, a cool, mist-laden fluid pervades the air, thick and brine-sweet, forming a planetwide gelid flood. This is a wound of water imposed on what seems to have once been dry terrain, yet now there is no horizon, only the dissolution of forms into shadows and blurred luminance.

The landscape beneath the rippling world feels ancient and deeply changed by time. There are no sharp cliffs or jagged peaks here; the land wears the heavy touch of bygone violence. Folds of stone sag like old leather beneath drifts of verdant, flowing vegetation. The terrain is elevated—submarine ridges or mountain tops that were once wind-scoured but are now submerged. Islands of smooth hillocks and half-formed plateaus rise from the depths, disconnected yet somehow balanced, connecting this drowned sprawl in concentric rings of broken terrain.

The vegetation cascades like aquatic forests, hanging loosely in threads and tendrils, or swaying in great, fluid forms that evoke trees and vines. But it isn’t quite what you’d know as forest. This growth belongs nowhere you can easily categorize. Violet fronds lace themselves against a background of translucent green membranes, while pale, wiry arms unfurl mechanically against unseen currents. Everything pulses faintly with its own soft bioluminescence, but these aren’t creatures—just alien plants, alive in ways that defy gravity. Patches of bubble-like seedpods cling to rocks, dangling at uneasy angles, shivering at some primordial beat you can't hear but feel. Rust-colored kelp forests form distant silhouettes, the linear curves manic yet hypnotically natural. It is a world still lush, though what you see is a warped lushness, its rules nuanced in some bizarre architectural dialect formed in years of profound change.

There are no schools of fish darting through these underwaters, no sleek eerie predators breaking the stillness. No buzz of subtle insect or chatter of lurking animal. Fauna, wherever it existed, seems to have long fled—or perhaps was never here at all. Their absence disturbs you more than their possible presence might have.
