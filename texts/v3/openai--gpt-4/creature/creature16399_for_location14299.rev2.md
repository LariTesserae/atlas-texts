The inhabitants of this place are as elusive and contradictory as the land itself, shaped by the uneasy truce between man and marsh, industry and wilderness. They are not easy to see at first glance. The sunlight flattens the world and blinds the curious, casting such stark shadows that the figures moving within them seem to phase in and out of reality, like mirages on the horizon.

Among the knee-high grasses by the pools, stooped shapes can sometimes be spotted, bent like reeds in the wind. These are the gleaners: thin, wiry figures whose faces disappear beneath wide-brimmed hats frayed like they’ve been chewed at the edges, or beneath layers of draped fabric to guard against the biting heat and biting insects. They squat low to the ground with slow, deliberate movements, scooping up small crustaceans or plucking clusters of herbs or roots that thrive in the brackish puddles. Their hands are quick, but their gazes are languid, patiently scanning the ground as if listening for the land to reveal its secrets.

Somewhere amidst the rusting sprawl of the old factory ruins live the keepers of machinery. These residents are never entirely seen, glimpsed instead as shadows among the rusted stairs and walkways, oil-stained sleeves darting into the gap of a broken window to retrieve a tool or to pull levers in the belly of a forgotten turbine. They are practical folk who still believe in the bones of what came before, the relics of a world turning fuel into power, grit into purpose. The light sometimes catches their grease-slicked faces, and their expressions hold an intensity fueled not by hope or pure determination, but by simple necessity. With mechanics' hands, they coax the corroding heart of the factory to beat just loudly enough, its outdated rhythm suffusing their lives with both purpose and noise.

Near the rust-streaked water tower are the drifters—people who pass through, leaving only temporary traces behind. Their tarps, stretched across the remnants of pipes or rusted vehicles, form shelters precariously balanced between necessity and impermanence. They move through the world like smoke, their voices too soft to rise above the machinery's drone or the occasional sighing wind. What little they carry with them always seems damp, as though the marsh has seeped into their very possessions—canvas bags warped with moisture, shoes whose soles squelch with every step. In the sultry heat of the day, they vanish entirely, reappearing only in the cool liminality of dawn and dusk. They are not rooted but are patient, knowing the land's secrets better than those who linger too long.

And then there are the dwellers of the deeper marsh, the ones who trade in secrets rather than goods. Few see them. You might notice the ripple of water where a boat ought not to be, or the faintest wisp of smoke curling from behind one of the low, fern-choked islets that dot the farthest reaches of the wetlands. Their homes—if they can be called that—are concealed in the marsh’s labyrinthine heart, constructed atop platforms made of scavenged wood and metal, constantly under threat from the restless water. There is something feral in them, something of the land itself. They speak in voices that carry the same lilt as the marsh grass stirred by wind, fluid and coarse all at once, their language laced with words no one else remembers the meaning of.

The heat-mirage atmosphere cloaks them all, yet there is an almost palpable sense that the life that resides here is not easily confined to the forms of men or women. Among the trees that teeter on the land’s edges, strange, birdlike figures perch at sunset, their silhouettes as sharp and ancient as those of cranes—but they do not fly. They brood on the world with impossible stillness and vanish by morning, their clawed footprints in the mud seeming too large for anything that ought to live here.

Sometimes at night, when the sound of the machinery subsides or grows distant, the marsh itself seems alive in a way that humankind can't quite replicate. The pools occasionally emit faint bioluminescent glows, and a low, ceaseless hum seems to rise from their surfaces—a vibration more felt than heard. Locals speak of something beneath the land, older than the factory and its ambition, older than the marshgrass’s quiet dominion. They do not give it a name, but their eyes flicker toward the pools and the ruins when asked, wary of betraying too much.

Whoever it is that lives here, whether human, animal, or something older and stranger, they are all bound by the marsh. They navigate this damp, unrelenting terrain with an understanding that is never spoken aloud, perhaps because they know the land itself is listening. The sense of truce between mankind and this waterlogged earth is precarious, an agreement whispered in sleep and forgotten upon waking. But here they endure, each a part of the landscape, caught between erosion and persistence, soaked through with the damp and timeless pulse of the marsh.
