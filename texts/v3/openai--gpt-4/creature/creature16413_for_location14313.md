Who lives here? They are not as you are, though they might have been, long ago. They are the inheritors of a world that has learned the art of persistence, a world that no longer tolerates the wastefulness of fragility or haste. They live in skin pressed thin against an ancient storm, in bones that have been carved and recarved by generations. Whatever they were has been simplified, reshaped, pared down to an essence as efficient and enduring as the plants that cradle their homes. If their ancestors were human, it is now hard to tell; they have long since ceased their reckless rebellion against their environment and allowed it to imprint itself upon them.

Their forms are lean and angular, their limbs long and their fingers tapering. Their skin carries a texture: porous, perhaps, or with faint ridges, as if something crystalline were embedded deep within. It gleams faintly, as though it shares in the phosphorescence of the plants, catching the dim, throbbing light and reflecting it gently. Their eyes are larger than a true human’s would be, not for seeing great distances—no horizon here would demand it—but for parsing dense thickets and navigating in perpetual twilight. Pupil-less, their irises are wide disks of reflective pigment, shades of molten amber, obsidian black, or gem-like indigo, feeding on what little light exists and shimmering faintly in turn.

Voices seldom rise above the music of the place, but their speech, when shared, is less a sound than a vibration felt in the chest. A soft hum, articulated in rhythms that ripple outward to touch the environment—sometimes barely audible, other times reverberating like honey poured into the air. To hear them speak to the plants is perhaps impossible for an outsider to parse; yet it is clear that they do communicate, not just with each other but with the world they move through. They are stewards, yes, but also parts of the whole, symphonies themselves in the constant thrum of life. Their gestures are slow, deliberate, touching here, brushing there, and the environment responds. Fronds fan outward as one inhales deeply. Sacs expand and contract rhythmically to feed nourishment to the roots. Even the vibrant insectoid hordes seem to move toward or away from them with quiet precision, as if drawn by an unseen tide.

Their tools are not weapons; no conflict exists here save the subtle and perpetual friction of survival. Instead, their lives revolve around balance: nets to gently pluck ripe spores from bloated pods, shears that trim just enough from a plant to keep it healthy without threatening it, thin ceramic vessels for catching condensation or drawing energy from the volatile warmth that radiates throughout the air. Every action is deliberate, every artifact a symbiotic expression of life thriving at the edge of what existence allows. 

The most startling aspect of their existence is their communal selfhood. They are individuals in form and identity, yet also something shared—each one a single cord in a ridged symphony of being. The pulse of this place moves through them, not as metaphor but as biological fact. Their hearts beat in time with the wider rhythms of the ecosystem. The vibrational language carved into stone and air is as much heartbeat as thought, a resonant system in which they exist as interconnected parts of an unknowably complex whole. To meet one of them is to meet all of them in some ineffable way; connection flows through the air like an extra ingredient they breathe, a current you cannot access but can almost feel moving around you.

Though they can make sounds, they seldom speak aloud unless among themselves, their contact with one another more often tactile. The brushing of fingers across patterned skin, a forearm pressed to another’s sternum to share a wordless cadence of experience. Outsiders would struggle to communicate here, for your words would feel hollow in a place where every notion born of sound is accompanied by resonance, by rhythm, by tangible energy. Should you speak, they would tilt their heads and listen, not unkindly, but distantly—like hearing radio static barely tuned to an understandable frequency.

They do not worship in the way of primitive religions, but there is something sacred in all their movement. They regard the plants much as one might a pantheon of ancient deities—not in submission, but with respect, with reverence for a compact their people must honor to continue breathing. They understand the cost of their existence in a way so thorough it is etched into their biology, a debt that never lessens and must be paid daily, rhythmically, endlessly. 

Young? Perhaps they have children, or their equivalent, but juvenility here is difficult to imagine. Just as the place itself seems carved to its final state by eons of pressure and heat, so too do the young—if they exist—emerge already half-formed, more like buds unfolding along the plant-life or spindly foals standing moments after birth than the fragile, dependent young of other creatures. If they learn, they do so quickly, converging on comprehension as swiftly as a seed springing open in response to the rare passage of wind.

And as for what might pass for art here, you need only look to their tools or their shelter. Their art is the world around them, and they treat everything they touch as an extension of their own forms. When one of them carves patterns into stone or branches to create tools, it is an act of devotion, not to an intangible god, but to the continuous fabric of life that swaddles them. They mold the world without dominating it, letting the environment guide their creation. Some structures appear almost grown rather than built, as if these people know the language of the plants so profoundly that they can persuade the fronds and stalks to curve and harden as needed.

And yet, for all this perfection, the shadows of their world are deep. Literally, yes—the dim, pulsing half-light threads darkness through every tangled branch. But also metaphorically. Something about this place feels *too far gone*, as if its equilibrium has been achieved at great cost. Here, the people live not for themselves, not for their desires or dreams, but for the place itself, for the perpetuation of a balance that demands and absorbs their every moment. Perhaps, long ago, they sought freedom from such compacts, but what use is freedom in a world that burns or crumbles without cooperation? What would they trade for that freedom now? Would they, too, warn you not to linger, lest the place claim you? Or would they watch, detached, as you succumb to it?

Much is left unanswered about the lives of these beings and their fathomless ecosystem—what they were, what they are, what they might become in another eon of erosion’s artistry. But one thing is clear: this is their final dance, a beautiful, vibrant, endless choreography in an opera where the curtain may never fall. Or, if it does, it will do so all at once, and they will meet it not with screams, but with a rising hum that sharpens against the dust-laden air until the echoes fade back into silence.
