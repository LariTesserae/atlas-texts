Here, in this place that feels like a forgotten dream soaked through with water and time, lives something equally enigmatic. It doesn’t belong to any strict taxonomy you might recognize. What resides here is neither wholly animal nor spirit, but something in between—an amalgam, or perhaps an evolution spurred by the very nature of this land’s unique, patient turbulence.

They are called, by those who have glimpsed them and returned with sense enough to speak of it, the Vélaud. It is unclear whether the word was invented to name them or gifted from their own inscrutable language—if they even speak, which is debatable. The Vélaud are not numerous but seem bound to this place as water binds to its low points, as moss finds stone. They are elongated, their forms indistinct and wavering as though they move through a veil of their own making. From a distance, they might appear humanoid—limbs that suggest arms or legs, a silhouette that hints at a torso—but as you draw closer (if you dare), all sharpness fades. Their outlines ripple, profiles splitting and merging like silt dissolving into murky water streams. You wouldn’t call them transparent, but they seem saturated with their environment, as if their substance is drawn from the weave of the terrain. Their skin—or what passes for it—is slate gray with an oily sheen, broken by bioluminescent streaks: pale blues, greens, and violets that slowly pulse, the patterns labyrinthine, like phosphorescent veins traced in stone.

The eyes, if they have them, are what stay with you. They are absurdly large and faintly glowing—soft embers of light hovering in their fluid-like faces. The longer you look, the more you feel they've tilted the balance of scrutiny; you are being *examined.* Something about those lights in the dark—or through the diffuse gray mist—suggests calculation, or maybe contemplation. And when they blink, the action is slow and deliberate, much like this place itself.

The Vélaud do not leave footprints, though they move easily across the yielding, sponge-like soil. They seem disinclined to disturb—each motion purposeful, quiet, as though they are careful stewards of their environment. When they wade through the shallow pools or streams, they barely displace the water, leaving only rings that close in their wake. It’s as if they exist slightly out of sync with the physical laws around them, straddling the boundary between corporeal and incorporeal. But you sense that their care is not necessarily benevolence—more a grudging equilibrium, as if they are tethered here not by affection, but by necessity.

Their existence feels old, older than the rusting industrial traces bleeding copper into shallow pools. The Vélaud seem out of place in time but utterly integral to this location. You suspect they were here long before the first rivet was driven into metal by human hands, and will remain long after the last remnant of machinery collapses into rust and silt. Some say they remember the land before its scars, that they are keepers of something deeper—layers of the earth that predate everything but water itself. Their shapes shift so gradually that you might stand watching for hours without ever truly discerning change. And yet, when you blink, they’re suddenly—impossibly—elsewhere, often accompanied by an improbable echo of sound, like a sigh drawn from the marrow of stone.

What do they want? What do they do? The answers to these questions fluctuate as much as the water-laden light filtering through this basin. If they have a purpose, it’s as obscured as their features. Some claim the Vélaud are observers, cataloging the events that fail to leave marks upon the stones. Others think they are architects—not of buildings or devices, but of subtle shifts in this ecosystem, moving water here, loosening a ridge of soil to redirect a stream there. Some even whisper that their work is not benign, but driven by indifference to anything beyond their own cycles of transformation, their remorseless need to sustain the balance that binds them here. A balance that doesn’t bend for the transient presence of animals, humans, or anything else.

In rare moments when thick mist suppresses the distances and edges of the place, the Vélaud will sometimes congregate in silence. They drift together at the edges of shallow pools or around rotting machinery half-consumed by mud. Here, their vague outlines overlap, exchanging pulses of faint light that flow between them like currents connecting their forms. No sound accompanies this, except the ever-present dripping and trickling of water—as though their meeting is not to speak, but to resonate, to hum in slow harmony with one another.

And what of sound? Surely you’d expect something alien to wail or warble; to echo with otherness. But the Vélaud make no cry, no roar. Instead, their presence manifests as the low, timeless hum that suffuses the landscape. When they move, especially nearby, the frequency changes—thickens somehow, draping your chest in a resonant heaviness you feel less with your ears than deep behind your ribs. It happens so subtly you only later realize you’ve been vibrating along with it, adjusted to a tune you can’t quite parse.

Occasionally, the creatures seem to direct their glow toward fragments of the rusting machinery embedded in the ground. It is unclear if they are mourning, dissecting, or rousing these relics from some dreamless slumber. The faint tremor you sometimes feel underfoot during these interactions makes you wonder this: are the Vélaud crippled caretakers of the earth, resentful of what humanity has done, trying feebly to patch the world back together? Or do they require these remains for themselves, feeding on the slow decay of man-made material to sustain their quiet, endless lives? It’s hard to say if they are healing or harvesting—or whether they even distinguish between the two.

At twilight, amid the shadowing moss and bending reeds, you might catch a glimpse of one hovering near, close but not too close, their dim bioluminescent glow mirrored in the dark pool beneath them. You freeze, unsure if it sees you. But of course it does. You are part of its domain now, just another disturbance in the soil-soft history it nurtures. It watches you not with malice, not even with curiosity, but with the steady, patient attention of one who has seen time pass so thoroughly that even your odd existence is a rhythm it instinctively understands. 

In this desolate, sodden basin where nothing is still but everything is searching for equilibrium, the Vélaud are its breath. And while they are no more inclined to harm you than they are to welcome you, the longer you linger, the more you come to feel that being here at all is dissonance. You are not part of the song they hum gently to the soil. You are only a passing refrain.
