The inhabitants of this paradoxical highland realm must be as strange and singular as the place itself. They are creatures of adaptation, living symbiotically with the land and its contrasts, shaped by the contradictory forces of vitality and desolation.

Here, in the cracks of frosted stone and the tangled depths of frost-bitten greenery, small, quick beings thrive. Many are multi-limbed and fuzzy, covered in fur so thick and iridescent that it feels like moss to the touch. They are the color of lichen and stone, making them nearly invisible as they scuttle over the rocks or dive into clusters of icy ferns. These creatures feed on the mosses, lichens, and fungi of the highlands, their transparent teeth evolved to scrape away vegetation without damaging its substrate. At night, their eyes glow softly in the dim light, tiny lanterns of biological phosphorescence that trace curious pathways through the wilderness. They chirp and trill to one another in hypnotic, rhythm-like sequences, communicating over vast distances through the sound and vibration carried by the cold air.

Overhead, strange birds with shaggy, angular wings cut black silhouettes against the sky. Their feathers absorb light rather than reflect it, helping them stay unseen in the diffuse and eerie glow of the place. They are neither swift nor particularly graceful; their wings creak like the bending boughs of a tree, an odd mimicry of the environment around them. They are scavengers and opportunists, picking apart the detritus left behind by more industrious creatures. Their calls are infrequent but haunting, melodic arpeggios that seem part bird and part something older, elemental.

On the ground, larger forms move with deliberate purpose. These are lumbering, quadrupedal beasts, their forms richly furred or cloaked in moss-like growths that serve to camouflage them as massive, moving boulders. Stoic and patient, they graze on the greenery that struggles to grow in this cold desert, their broad mouths adapted to grind tough, fibrous growths into pulp. Their humps store not fat, as with desert camels, but thick fatty tissue that protects against the intense cold. When they breathe, plumes of steam rise from nostrils hidden beneath broad, flat snouts. But these beasts are more than survivors—they are the keepers of memories in this harsh place. Their eyes are deep and pool-like, and it’s said that looking into them will show you slivers of an ancient world, when the highlands were once novel and warm.

Beneath the stones live burrowing creatures, elongated forms with sheens of frost clinging to their hairless, tunneling bodies. These animals churn the grit and soil ceaselessly, creating an underground world as vast and complex as the snowy surface. Their lives below sustain the ground above; they break apart clumps of soil, nourish the green organisms clinging desperately to the surface, and forge channels for the icy waters to flow into the veins of the landscape.

But the true masters of this place are neither beast nor bird nor even insect. In the half-light of the permanent twilight that cloaks the land, tall beings emerge, walking slowly but gracefully amid the impenetrable waves of snow and ice. They are humanoid in the vaguest of terms, walking upright and bearing features we recognize, but they are of the land—sinewy and pale under their enshrouding white cloaks, with long limbs that appear capable of mimicking the knotty curves of trees. Their faces are smooth and featureless except for their eyes, which glint like pools of frozen water, reflecting the glacial light. They seem more plant than flesh, their skin textured like frost-touched bark, and their slow, measured movements suggest lives unmarked by haste. Their language is low and rumbling, carried by the wind and felt in the bones. It is a language not easily heard with human ears, as it rides on vibrations and the subtle shifting of air. 

They are the shepherds of this place, tending to its organisms as one might tend a garden. When the snow grows too thick on certain trees, they brush it away lovingly with hands like gnarled branches. When a mound of vegetation thins, they kneel to nurture it, displacing snow, rearranging stones, or exposing just enough sunlight where necessary. They are guardians and cultivators, perhaps the remnants of the humans who once inhabited this land, or perhaps something much older—a blending of man and highland, frozen together in the slow violence of millennia.

It is to these beings, perhaps, that the stone structures owe their existence. Though the forms of crude tools and hearths suggest human hands, the structures themselves bear signs of renovation and care over centuries, as if someone—or something—didn't want the memory of those early settlers to be eroded entirely. Flat against the driving wind, these shelters still stand scattered across the land, their interiors lined not just with ancient carvings but living mosses and luminous fungi encouraged to grow into intricate, decorative patterns.

These guardians, beings of frost and time, make no effort to disrupt their neighbors—the scuttling, glowing creatures, the slow-grazing mammals, or the birds with analogue wings. Instead, they exist alongside all other life here, as keepers or mediators. They seem not to eat or sleep but to function in harmony with the rhythms of this cold, verdant desert. Perhaps they are the spirits of the highland itself, risen from its stony depths to oversee the unsteady, ever-shifting balance.

The highland, then, is a world unto itself—a place stark and severe, yet filled with life so strange and so persistent that it humbles all who step within its borders. It is a cradle of contrasts, where creatures scuttle underfoot and shadows soar above, where vibrant greenery takes root in a bed of frost, and where ageless beings keep silent watch over a land they have pledged to preserve, long before memory began.
