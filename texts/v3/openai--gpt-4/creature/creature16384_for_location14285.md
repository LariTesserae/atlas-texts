Those who live here are as enduring as the land, shaped by its unyielding demands and steeped in the slow rhythm of its damp, cloaked hours. They are a small, close-knit collective, not bound by blood but by trust, forged over long years of shared labor and the ceaseless negotiation with this land that neither welcomes nor rejects them, but simply exists in its brooding permanence.

Their faces are worn, etched deeply like the weathered stones they build with, each furrow a record of the town’s constant push and pull with the world around it. Their skin carries the pallor of muted light — not pale from illness or fear, but softened by the constant embrace of mist and fog. Hair, when seen, tends to be thick and earth-toned, often hidden beneath practical caps woven of grasses or rudimentary wool, each stitch meant for survival more than ornament. They move with a purposefulness that seems almost tectonic, each step deliberate, every gesture deliberate. There is no fidget or waste, not even in idle moments.

They are people who wake early, rising with the dim greys of morning, the air still heavy with the echoes of the night’s last murmurs. Tasks begin immediately: bread to knead, water to draw from wells that seem to refill from the very bones of the Earth, tools to mend or create. Their hands carry the scars of their labors — calluses like stone ridges, fingers blistered by the ropes they pull and the rock they stack, nails forever fringed with the grit of the place no amount of scrubbing can entirely remove.

Their voices too feel as though carved from this wet and rocky cradle. Conversations are low and pragmatic, their words weighed carefully like the stones in their buildings, each having its place. When laughter comes — and it does, now and then, surprising in its warmth among so much greyness — it is rough-edged, caught somewhere between irony and affection. They laugh with each other, never at, for they know too well the cruelty of isolation. The mist can steal away sound, but it cannot steal away the bond they have made in sharing this life.

Though few in number, these people have roles around which their lives revolve. There is the Smith, whose hands are never still; their forge bright and guttering amid stone walls, birthing tools as simple as spikes to drive into the ground or hinges for doors to hold fast against the creeping cold. There is the Forager, who moves through the mist like a slip of shadow, knowing the map of green shoots and bitter roots that curl through the land, pulling sustenance from the stubborn earth with a reverent sort of efficiency. There is the Herbalist, bound to an understanding of mosses, lichens, and the shy needles of trees that cling to the higher reaches of this low land, their collected wisdom a cool salve for the ailments that damp and chill inevitably deliver. And there is always the Elder, not often one person but a revolving understanding, knowledge passing hands, changing shape, surviving in stories murmured over low hearthfires, tales stitched together with myth and memory.

Children exist here, though they seem fleeting, spectral, and as much a part of the mist as anything else. They work alongside their elders, weaving what play they can into daily tasks: a game of balance on a moss-slick log, a contest to leap quarry puddles without being swallowed by the forgiving mud. Their laughter lingers fleetingly, brightening the undercurrent of endurance that hangs over the town like a damp shawl. Yet their resilience mirrors that of their parents — scraped knees become badges, splinters removed with stoic resolve.

They have animals, though only as companions in necessity, not leisure. A small clutch of goats grazes on whatever sour greenery can be coaxed from the outlying slopes, their shaggy forms as rugged and indifferent as the people they live alongside. A few hardy birds, akin to those famed perhaps for their pigeons, are trusted to deliver messages to settlements only whispered of, far beyond the constant veil of grey here. These birds carry the people’s only faint connective tissue to a world that may be brighter and louder, but which cannot possibly hold the same quiet reassurance this place offers.

And still, there is something *other* here. Beyond the townsfolk and their flocks, past even the stoats and sparrows that flutter or flicker, there is a sense that unseen presences roam at the edges of the mist, moving softly as though the stones and lichens know them. Whether spirits roused from the land itself or some fault memory carried from troubled dreams, they are not feared but respected, treated with neither curiosity nor aggression. Occasionally, when a heavy silence descends and the mist folds too thickly, a child might whisper tales of shapes seen just outside the lamplight—a hunched figure, a glow, voices that sound like water dripping into deeper water. The adults stoically acknowledge it but move on, muttering simple prayers or lighting an extra lantern as a matter of precaution rather than dread.

There are rituals here, but not in the overly ceremonial way of older traditions. They are subtle and woven into daily life: a crust of bread left on a windowsill for the unseen, a nail pricked into each doorframe to keep the heavy mist from seeping in too boldly, small polished stones placed at the base of the few trees still standing. Once every year — though the exact timing remains a closely guarded knowledge passed down — the entire town gathers at dusk for a simple offering. At the edge of the rocky lowland, in the shadow of its gnarled trees and fog-draped silence, a modest bonfire is lit, made damp and smoky as wet logs crackle and moan. Into the flames they cast the carved remnants of the year's burdens — a broken tool, a shattered pot, the wooden effigy of an illness overcome or a hunger endured.

The essence of these people is intertwined with the land’s own contradictions: steadfast in the face of an ever-shifting, mist-bound reality but alive with traces of quiet mysticism, of unwritten stories that hum low and deep within the vitality of the gray. Here, existence is cemented in hard work and enduring bonds, yet softened by the ways each moment is imbued with the significance of simply persisting. Like the mist over their homes or the water seeping from endless rock, their life flows gently but unmistakably forward.
