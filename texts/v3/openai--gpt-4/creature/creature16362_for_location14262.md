The inhabitants of this layered citadel are, like the place itself, an intricate blend of nature and artifice. They are human—or near enough to it—but their existence has been shaped by the heights they’ve made their home and the slow, timeless rhythm that pervades their world. They move with the unhurried purpose of those who know no urgency, their lives threaded into the hum and pulse of the city around them. There is no need for haste here; everything follows patterns that will endure long after any single inhabitant has gone. 

For all their patience, they are makers—craftspeople, engineers, artists, gardeners—working in harmony with their environment rather than against it. Their hands bear the calluses of labor, their tools are precise, but their creations lean towards subtlety: intricate railings that might double as seedbeds for creeping vines, machinery that operates in seamless tandem with the flow of cascading water, floating sculptures of light that shift and shimmer like reflections on a sunlit pond. Their clothing mirrors this union of utility and grace, woven from plant fibers and dyed in hues that reflect their surroundings—deep greens, earthy browns, soft ochres, and the occasional vivid streak of azure or red. These people belong to the terraces, and the terraces have, in turn, shaped them.

Their faces are weathered, not by hardship but by years spent beneath the relentless golden radiance of the sky, their skin kissed to warm, earthy tones. They bear a quiet sturdiness, their bodies strong from the constant scaling of stairways and the labor of tending their city’s gardens and machinery. Yet there is also a fluidity to their movements, a practiced ease born from knowing every pathway, every incline, every quirk of the seemingly endless terraces. Their features are varied, a testament to innumerable generations merging and diverging atop this vertical expanse, but their expressions are almost universally placid. This is not a world that cultivates extremes—it is deeply balanced, and its people reflect that balance.

Among them are the builders, working to maintain the city’s delicate equilibrium. They patch the weathered stone, mend the endless systems of pipes and channels that carry water throughout the city, and tend the lifts and trams that hover between the terraces. Their tools are strange to an outsider’s eye—less like wrenches and gears, more like extensions of their hands that flex and adapt to the task at hand. Rust and decay are considered natural here, not enemies to combat but inevitabilities to guide; what is worn down is carefully replaced or repurposed, and so the terraces evolve over time.

Then there are the growers, the stewards of the city's abundant flora. They are as much gardeners as they are ecologists, coaxing life from the terraces in luxuriant abundance. They weave the hanging gardens, train the vines, and shape the mosses, but their role is less about imposing order and more about nudging growth in beneficial directions. Some seem almost plantlike themselves—frequently stained with chlorophyll and dirt, hair peppered with seeds and spores, hands slightly green-tinted from long hours tending shoots and roots. These are the individuals who, when walking past a tree growing from a precipice or a strange bloom unfurling in a nook of stone, pause instinctively to touch and observe, ensuring the harmony of their living city remains intact.

Others tend to the many animals that have made the terraces their home. These are the watchers and whisperers, those who know the quirks and calls of the vibrant birds that flit overhead and who sometimes raise their hands skyward, coaxing the creatures to perch with a soft whistle. They care for the kin of wildcats and lemurs who prowl the beams and ledges, leaving offerings of food to keep the animals from wandering into trouble—or trouble from wandering into the animals. It is whispered that they know the nocturnal lives of the bioluminescent insects well, and that they have even guided the pulsing colonies into cooperative clusters, shaping their drifting, glowing forms into mesmerizing patterns to illuminate certain parts of the fog-sunk lower depths.

Perhaps the most curious of all are the custodians—those who tend not to animals or plants, but to the silent, ancient machines humming beneath the surface of this city. They know every piston’s rhythm, every turbine’s song, every shift in the low groan of the city’s deep gears. No one can say with certainty whether the machines were made by the ancestors of these people, or whether they inherited them unknowingly, but the custodians do not question. They preserve, maintain, and—even when their tools seem almost archaic beside the seamless vastness of the city’s engines—somehow keep things running. Their tools glint in the endless golden light, and their gestures to the machinery are often reverent, like silent prayers or conversations with the unseen minds of the city’s past.

Children run freely across the terraces, their nimbleness born not of long practice but of instinct—for none raised here could ignore the yawning heights below them. They play cooperative games that seem to mirror the delicate interdependencies of their world, racing along vine-thick walkways, venturing through open archways to watch the insect colonies pulse in rhythm, or trying to imitate the cries of birds that spiral overhead. They, too, seem unhurried; abundant time stretches before them, years in which to come into alignment with the place’s pace.

But these people are not entirely isolated. Somewhere in the deepest reaches of memory, perhaps in the time when this city was first born of stone and steel, they learned—or invented—crafts that could make their home self-sufficient. Trade and contact are infrequent, yet treasures from beyond the terraces occasionally find their way here: strange seeds that sprout unnatural flowers, tools wrought from unfamiliar metals, news of distant places where the sky might not shine quite so brightly. The people of the terraces rarely seem surprised by these external gifts; their patience has bred acceptance of even the strangest arrivals. They are not a people of dreamers, but neither are they closed off from mystery.

The city demands no rulers, no hierarchical governance. There are guides, advisors, and elders, but leadership here is collective and intuitive, arising naturally from shared purpose. The real authority is the city itself—its rhythms, its needs, the slow march of moss over stone and the endless murmur of water coursing through channels. People do not command the terraces; they harmonize with them.

These inhabitants, in sum, are not quite like you or me, but they are not wholly alien either. They are the inheritors of patient industry, of stone and vine in embrace, of machinery that forgot it was machinery. They are, in a way, caretakers of time itself—living in a city forever on the edge of the natural and the built, a place impossible in its union yet profoundly alive. Here, where the endless gleam of white-gold light stretches far beyond the reach of memory, these people and their city thrive as one.
