Within this secluded, ancient hollow, there are beings both seen and unseen, each woven into the fabric of this timeless place like threads in an ever-growing tapestry of moss and mist. They are as old as memory and as blurred as a half-forgotten dream, their existence neither entirely of the physical nor fully of the ethereal. These inhabitants are not intruders here; they are the valley's living memory, its breath and pulse.

One of the more tangible creatures here is a race of shadowed quadrupeds, low to the ground and pitched in hues of wet bark and curling smoke. Their fur glints slickly when the rare beam of refracted light threads its way through the veiled canopy, revealing patterns like maps—contours of rivers, lines of vanished trails—that shift fleetingly before vanishing back into their obscurity. They move silently over the mossy carpet, their claws curved not for predation but for stability, navigating the dripping, uneven terrain. Their eyes are pallid orbs, cloudy as weathered quartz, and yet within them swirls a depth of ancient knowing. No sound comes from their movements—no snap of twig, no rustle of lichen—but on occasion, a low and resonant hum emanates from deep within their forms, a drone so intertwined with the white noise of the cascades that it feels like an extension of the valley itself. 

Above and between the dripping trees, there flits another presence, ghostly and unnervingly silent. Winged creatures as thin and brittle as dead leaves or fractured bark, their flight doesn’t disturb a single current of air. Their limbs hang jagged and angular, extending into membranous wings shot through with veiny traces that glow faintly when the air grows suddenly colder. They do not eat as other creatures eat, nor seem to move with intent; their purpose feels almost ceremonial, as if their mere existence in this place is some tribute to time's stagnation. They drift on unseen thermals, their eyes (if they indeed have eyes) always turned skyward, though no one has ever found where they roost.

Deeper within the cracks and fissures of this place, there are whispers of something more enigmatic—a being or collection of beings that dwell not in sight or sound but in the dense, dark quiet between the acres of noise. It is said that, on the stillest evenings, just as the noises of the cascades subside in the rare glimmer of calm, the valley itself exhales in groaning, growling tones, like the contortion of a vast, ancient spine beneath the earth. Whether this sound belongs to the shifting bedrock, the wind funneled through hidden gorges, or some conscious entity coiled within the land—no one knows. But its presence can be felt in the tremor beneath your feet and the reverberation in your chest. Perhaps it is the valley itself, alive in some immeasurable, elemental way.

And then there are the relics. The stonework remnants and iron shards themselves seem almost alive here, imbued with an uncanny stillness that feels like waiting. Moss creeps along their edges as though trying to lull them into invisibility, but something resists, as if the artifacts refuse to abandon their identity. Woven through the artifacts is the faintest sign of another presence—a spark of something once human and yet broken apart by time, like a lantern shattered and scattered across centuries. It lingers in the guttural etchings on stone slabs, which spiral in no language understood but seem almost to breathe when the wind shrieks by. It moves in the faintest flickers of warmth emanating from the heart of corroded metals, as though hands had only recently let go.

And what of the foliage itself? Even the lichen, the mosses, the strangling vines here carry an air of sentience—not in an overt way, but as if their relentless growth, their hunger for damp and decay, has developed an awareness of their dominion. Clumps of moss fall in familiar, eerie shapes—vague simulacra of faces, gestures. Lichen stretches like ghostly arms over stonework, embracing it or consuming it; it is difficult to tell.

Those who have happened upon this valley and lived to leave often speak of dreams that trail them in waking life—dreams of luminous rivers flowing backward, of shadow-creatures staring wordlessly through clouds of dew, their eyes heavy with something unreadable. They speak of feeling watched, but not threatened—observed deeply, as if appraised and catalogued by innumerable eyes both living and remembered. They even speak, sometimes, of whispers—a language-less crooning, as though the valley were trying to tell them something but could never quite form the words.

What lives here, then, is not a single race or species. It is something plural, interwoven, and inseparably tied to this cold and boundless realm. It is a world alive with its own rhythm, breathing a breath that cycles slower than years, exhaling centuries and inhaling millennia. It is the joined survival of moss, stone, shadow, water, and memory—a congregation of the forgotten, preserved like an amber-glossed fly in time’s hollow heart.
