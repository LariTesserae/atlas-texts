Those who call this place home are as much artifacts of harmony as the city itself, shaped and reshaped by the collision of nature's will and human intent, molded like clay in the hands of two competing gods. They are delicate hybrids—neither solely of the soil nor wholly of the machine, something in between, something emergent. Warm-blooded and metallic, with veins that pulse both with restless, red vitality and the faint shimmer of liquid circuitry. Their flesh, mottled with creeping mosses or dusted with spores, is supple and strong enough to soften life's blows, while their skeletal frames hum faintly with the vibrations of gears running in tandem with bone and sinew.

Their eyes were once human, but now glint faintly in the dim, primordial daylight like polished gemstones, silver and green, reflecting some untold history engraved in the city around them. They seem older than their years, their gaze weathered like the stones of this ancient-industrial ecosystem, harboring a quiet patience born of generations adapting to a place that leaches into you and grows back out. Every step they take is deliberate, as though being careful not to disturb the ancient symphony unfolding beneath their feet.

Their voices, when heard, sound like low, resonant chords, carrying faint echoes of dripping water, grinding metal, and the hushed rustle of vegetation. They rarely raise their tones or speak in haste, for in this world of perpetual noise, shouting feels jarring, almost sacrilegious. Their language is an odd mixture of fluid and metallic: modulated clicks, hums, deep reverberations, and soft vowels, the dampness of the air lending their words a liquid quality that erases all sharpness.

Communal and cooperative out of necessity, they are not cloistered by walls and roofs like the humans long gone from this place; they are instead woven into the natural-industrial continuum of the city itself. Their homes defy the dichotomy of inside and outside—there are no impermeable barriers, no clean separations. One might find a crèche nested in the roots of a hundred-years-old iron tree, its branches threaded through with crystalline pipes that bleed steam as they curl skyward. Or a shared worktable surrounded by fireflies of bioluminescent moths, where glowing tools of bronze-flecked wood click and spin in careful hands as fresh leaves spiral lazily from above.

These people, if they themselves think of themselves entirely as "people," do not dream in the same vivid fantasies as distant humans might. Their sleep is lighter, more ephemeral—a settling of their being into the rhythms of the city, their tendrils of thought seeping into the roots of the great trees or the pulsating hum of faraway turbines. When they awaken, they're not quite sure where their consciousness ends and where the world around them begins.

Children here grow not in houses but in groves—small clusters of ironroot saplings where the air tingles electrically with new life. They climb trees and scaffolding alike, using both to build their games and early understanding of the world. By adolescence they've already taken on the city's rhythm, joining the symphony, not as followers but as contributors. Every hand has its role, every mind its particular attunement, and no one is left adrift in isolation.

They do not hunt animals—they have no animals left to hunt. The only fauna here are those locked in amber recesses of memory or replicated in bioluminescent machinery, graceful statues that shift as if half-dreaming. Instead, they cultivate and recycle: foodstuffs drawn from algae pools and vine crops that flourish in the tangled streets, harvested with tools that seem grown rather than forged. Water, ever-present and sacred, is captured, redirected, purified, and shared in ways invisible to the casual observer. Waste is not cast away but absorbed, dissolved back into the cyclical alchemy of this world.

To see them move in unison, you come to wonder if their unity is telepathic, or if this landscape, with all its infinite connections between root and steel, acts as a shared brain—a neural web that holds them all in delicate synchronicity. One may kneel by a broken aqueduct and rise, hours later, having fixed it with a seamless patch of living resin; another may sculpt a device of breathtaking intricacy whose purpose is opaque but whose beauty whispers that it belongs.

But they are not unblemished utopians. Survival here demands constant work, constant balancing acts—defying torrents of rain that threaten to swallow their dwellings, mending vine-strangled machinery, deciding whether to allow one creeping fern to take over the gears of another fading mechanism. Disputes arise, hushed but intense, over how much to let the city evolve on its own without losing their precarious foothold. Sometimes they build, sometimes they dismantle, across strands of thought divided yet bound by mutual need. If they hold grudges, you’d never hear them spoken aloud, but the vines might tangle a little closer between one house and another, the moss grow a little thicker on a neighbor’s tools.

They are caretakers but also something more profound: inhabitants who have erased the notion of dominion. They do not control this place or serve it in submission; they have become it, and it them. The city lives because they live; they breathe because it breathes. If they ever sang outright, setting their strange voices to melody, the sound would be an ode of fern and steel, steam and dew—a hymn that has no word for division.
