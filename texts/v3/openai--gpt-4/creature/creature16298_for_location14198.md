The creatures, people, and spirits of the High Steppes are as much a part of its strangeness as the wind-sculpted mesas and dew-teased cacti. They belong not merely to its ecosystem, but to its soul, the vital forces knitting this improbable world together. One cannot tell the story of the Steppes without telling the story of those who live within its crags and crevices, who dream and eat and thrive among its jagged teeth and its mossy warmth.

At first glance, you might think the High Steppes owned only by tiny, tenacious creatures: clawed rodents the size of a shared fist, bounding shoulder over shoulder through scrubby drifts of hairy wild grass, or clusters of jewel-eyed beetles weaving shining geometric patterns in the sand as if to map what little water there is. But wait—and listen. At certain hours, when the sun slants low and the gaps between crags turn gold as a deity's hidden vault, the very rocks seem to pulse with sound. That cacophony of song comes from the "steppe goblets," rounded, gray-blue birds as large as a child’s two hands pressed together. They crowd the hollows, their feathers like pebbled glass, their chorus a melodic chaos. They are not entirely birds, or not any bird you’d recognize—each one carries a small, soft crest of coral-colored flesh atop its head, which it drums gently against the stones to shape their resting hollows or guide its flock. If you pay attention, you might notice how they tap the rhythm of rain—long, practiced beats, as if invoking it.

Among them, sharing their spaces and shadows, spiral-tailed lizards dart along the textured crags. These creatures balance on hind legs as often as all fours, their posture oddly upright. They look like tiny, twisting dragons rather than lizards, their tails an elegant swish of curls. The lizards' agility is almost spiteful in its ease; they never slip, even on the most crumbling limestone. Long ago, it is said—but said by whom, no one knows—they whispered to the lost builders of the High Steppes, guiding their hands in shaping rocks, marking cracks where a well might be carved, nudging stones into place during frigid, star-bitten nights.

Then there are the "hollow drifters," not animals exactly, but gossamer-thin beings of perpetual twilight that flutter in and out of view as if they exist on the periphery of eyesight. They are incorporeal things: gauzy, nearly translucent, stitching patterns between the steppes’ mist-veiled spires like lazy seamstresses rather than ceaseless laborers. They are so light that they seem to follow not the air's flow, but its memory; currents from yesterday still trace their paths. They make their homes in shadowed overhangs or in pockets of windless air beneath the ledges, their forms folding and unfolding like slow amber flames.

But there is more. Move higher—crawl up further along the tight, winding goat paths that overlook the endless plane of clouds below, and you will sometimes spot the glimmer of eyes. They belong to no beast, but to figures shaped like men, the Remnants of the Stones. Long ago, so long ago that even the most ancient spirits of the Steppes speak of it only in fragments, a short-lived people conquered this harsh geography for their homes. They hewed dwellings directly from the cliffs and built bridges sturdy enough that echoes of their structure still linger today. Their physical forms have long returned to dust, but what remains of them drifts across the Steppes—sentience transfigured into a kind of waking dream, bound to the high rocks they once called their own. The Remnants are pale, composed of greys and crumbly whites and faint shades of violet, with fingers that curl like weathered roots. They are not so much a single people as the memory of a people. They are guardians and witnesses now, muttering ancient customs and calling out to one another across the void-like gorges, voices carrying on the wind like the cries of raptors.

Among them, though scarce, there are rumors of another kind of inhabitant entirely. The rumor is wordless, alive only in the gestures of trembling birds and skittering lizards when the twilight deepens into the black of night and cold enough to seal dew into frost. Far away, in the unscalable spires at the Steppes' unreachable zenith, there are flickering lights bright as stars that occasionally drift close to the edge of visibility, illuminating sharp, angular figures on the ridges. The few surviving tales from the elder days of the Remnants speak of sky-borne beings, entities that came from the goldening edge of the world and offered knowledge, fire, and the secrets of the stone to the long-vanished architects of the Steppes. Were they gods? Demons? Or something in between? No one knows for sure. The shapes are seen so briefly that they might be illusions. But the feeling of eyes—silent, weighty, watchful—never quite leaves.

Even for those who spend long lives peering into every cavern and gouge, the High Steppes keep their best secrets. Curious, though, aren’t they? The tightly woven puzzle pieces of this elevation—this blend of hard lines and tender moss, the practical small creatures and ancient spirits interwoven, breathing together like veins in a single body. This place endures not by defiance but by a soft, patient unraveling. It grows. It waits. It remembers, inch by inch, drop by drop.

Who lives in the High Steppes? The High Steppes themselves live. They breathe, albeit slowly. They live through the persistent roar of insect wings, the curve of moss against water-stripped stone, and the creatures and remnants who cling to its fabric not as trespassers but as parts of the form itself.
