In this place of rugged harmony — where the dense breath of nature entwines with the ghosts of human ambition — dwell the Keepers. They are not wholly human yet, perhaps, not entirely something else. They live at the confluence of stone and spirit, a people or a presence born of the mountain’s marrow and the idle dreaming of derelict machines. Their very existence seems to stitch the natural and the industrial together, as though the landscape itself birthed them to maintain its precarious balance.

Invisibly present yet always felt, they blend seamlessly into the world around them. Their bodies appear wrought of moss and metal alike, a patchwork of soft green texture and gleaming, rust-slicked surfaces. Their limbs flex with the pliancy of growing vines but hold the strength of steel girders. Light slides off them differently, catching in gradients that seem alien and familiar all at once, like the damp sheen of a leaf slick with dew or the burnished glow of copper in sunlight. They are both hulking and delicate—a contradiction as natural as the land they inhabit.  

Their eyes are luminous points of muted radiance, glowing faintly like isolated lanterns in the fog. To look into their gaze is to glimpse something ancient and ongoing, something immeasurable. They carry no clear expression—neither malevolent nor benign, neither curious nor indifferent. And yet, the air bends differently around them, as though it knows every breath shared with these Keepers is borrowed oxygen from a cycle neither man nor earth fully understands.

The Keepers move with eerie silence, a quiet counterpoint to the constant music of their world. They are the overseers of the crumbling factory, the ancient crux where humanity’s progress once pit itself against nature’s patience. The gears and cogs, long abandoned and slowly consumed by ferns, vines, and rain, occasionally rumble back to life under their touch. Their work is deliberate but enigmatic, as though the how and why of what they labor on is important only to forces older than even the mountain itself.

Sometimes they kneel beside the trickling silver streams, their fingertips brushing against the water as though sifting for whispers. At others, they are seen toiling at the regenerating earth, their moss-meet-metal hands tending roots or setting rusty struts upright amid the foliage. Even more bizarre, in the hushed twilight, their forms can be glimpsed deep within the factory, turning the levers of forgotten machines, breathing life into mechanisms destined to corrode. The factory responds in kind: grinding out vibrations that stir the roots above and twist the webs of lichen clinging to the walls. But its outputs, whatever they may be, remain hidden deep within the mountain's veins, beyond human comprehension.

The fauna of the region have a symbiotic closeness with these spirits of metal-and-moss. Birds seem to trail the Keepers as though mapping their paths, voices catching on their mechanical reverberations like harmonies to an old chant. This is not a place where beasts fear humans or other unwelcome presences. Instead, they coexist as if recognizing the Keepers as an extension not only of the landscape but of the legacies embedded in its soil — human, nonhuman, and otherwise.

Time itself behaves strangely around them. The moisture that clings thick to the stones holds their movements like faint echoes, visible only through a trick of light hours after they've passed. They might appear to you as one solitary figure on a ridge, watching the mist unravel at dusk. But look away and back again, and they could be gone, replaced by nothingness or a faint stirring in the trees.

For all their alien wonder, the Keepers do not seem to seek or acknowledge companionship from those who might stumble into their domain. They are silent witnesses to intrusions, impassive sentinels among the rusted and verdant remnants of civilization. Yet it is said that if you attempt to damage the trembling balance of this place — uproot the moss, strip the relics from the factory, or divert the streams — the Keepers move with sudden, terrifying clarity. They might block your path with a sweeping arm of vine and iron, or their whispered presence might press against the back of your neck, pushing the air heavier onto your skin. They have no need for violence; the living, breathing heart of this land has a way of defending itself through them, and its will is undeniable.

It’s whispered that the Keepers share some underlying origin with the workers who once toiled in that factory — perhaps descendants of their restless dreams, or guardians born when the breath of humanity intermingled too deeply with the breath of stone and water. Others speculate that they are neither born of nature nor humanity but are instead manifestations of time itself—walking reminders that all things, no matter how seemingly different, will one day bind to each other again.

Whatever their purpose, the Keepers embody what holds this paradoxical place together. Their existence is less about balance and more about synthesis—a living fusion, an evocation of opposing but deeply interconnected forces. And so they walk between roots and rivets, between rain-laden ferns and oxidized beams, between all the stories this mountain could ever tell, as custodians of a fragile peace.
