In this hanging hollow at the edge of the world—this suspended basin of moisture and moss, half-real in the haze—dwells something that is not easy to see, to name, or to fully comprehend. The inhabitants of this uncanny enclave are not as straightforward as animals or spirits. They are a confluence of the place itself, living embodiments of its moisture-laden breath, its creeping light, and its muttered rhythms. To speak of them as singular creatures would be a disservice; they are the hollow made animate, the perpetual exhale of its ancient existence.

Among them are the **Driftlings**, ephemeral entities that seem more liquid than solid. When they move, they shimmer with faint iridescence, like oily water splitting sunlight on the surface of a puddle. They do not walk, and "swimming" might also be a stretch of a word for their movement—they glide, seeming to evaporate into mist one moment and reform the next, wafting between the hanging veils of vines and over the brimming pools. Their forms are ever-changing, never quite committing to a shape. Limbs stretch and vanish. What sometimes looks like a tail dissipates into a plume of vapor. Eyes—whether singular or in clusters—appear in brief flashes, scanning, seeing, but not in the way creatures bound by flesh and bone might. The Driftlings are less inhabitants of the hollow and more its articulate gestures, consciousness made visible and mobile.

Then, tucked within the lush sprawl of moss and fern, there are **Lumineers**, creatures of bioluminescent filigree that seem almost plantlike in their delicate construction. They are part of the fungal network that peppers the moss, yet they are also something more. When the mist grows thicker and the light dims, they unfold themselves, revealing unfathomably fine membranes adorned with glowing veins of golden-green and cerulean blue, like stained glass come to life. They sway slightly in the nonexistent breeze, shifting their orientation with a grace just beyond what wind would dictate, as if they can taste the air and lean toward some subtle flavor of the world. The fungi from which they appear are immobile, rooted in the moss, but the Lumineers themselves float like embers in place, pulsing faintly to their own inaudible rhythm. They gather where the broader ferns cluster, basking in some spectral heat invisible to eyes not their own.

In the pools—those shallow, shivering mirrors of rock and water—live the most tangible of the hollow’s denizens. Known simply as the **Echofish**, they are rare to see and more often felt: a subtle displacement of the water, a ripple too smooth to ascribe to falling drops. When seen, which is easiest when the mist is for a fleeting moment less dense, they are glass-bodied with only the faintest tracery of their anatomy visible through their translucent skins. Long, ribbonlike tails trail behind them like comet paths. Their movement is hypnotic, measured by the slowness of the place itself, making their appearance like spotting something from a dream: present for an instant, then lost. Some whisper that these creatures have no mass at all, that they are reflections rather than beings, deformities in the water’s surface drawn into life by the hollow’s peculiar forces.

And then there are visitors—the rarest of them all. They might once have been human. You would hardly know it now. The **Lichen-Kin**—entities shrouded in moss, trailing long pennants of vine, their backs bristling with tiny bursts of bioluminescent mycelium—move so excruciatingly slowly that you might first confuse them for nothing more than parts of the terrain. There are so few of them you might only encounter one in a lifetime, and even then, the meeting may pass unacknowledged: a motion in the corner of your eye where moments ago you thought there was only a particularly swollen cluster of fungus-covered stone. It is whispered that they are those who have lost themselves in the hollow, never to leave again
