They live here.

Not *someone*—not exactly—but no one thing lives here in isolation. The marshlands are home to overlapping threads, tangled lives enmeshed so deeply you can't distinguish one from the other. The obvious answer might be to say it’s the factory workers who dwell here, or the creatures crouching in the wetland hollows, or even the ambitious plants rising like thin banners of defiance between pools of oily water. Yet, none of these live *on their own*. This isn't a place for clear lines. Life here emerges not as individual entities but as entanglements, deeply enmeshed with iron foundations, smog-filled winds, and the persistent insistence of moist earth.

Still, an attempt must be made to describe them—or it.

The factory workers—perhaps they arrive first to the mind, as visible as they are. Calloused hands and quick eyes, their spines bent just slightly, their footfalls wet with condensation or marsh-mud depending on the day. They wear boots crusted with thin layers of both factory ash and the sticky residue of marsh clay. The air clings to their skin, humid and faintly metallic, their very lungs breathing in the pulse of their world: the hiss, the grind, the occasional unruly explosion of steam escaping iron lungs far larger than their own. Many have a quiet resilience about them, a rhythm learned from years of machinery’s demands, their movements deliberate, efficient—not unlike the gears they tend to.

Each has their own routine, well-defined if watched closely. Mornings begin humid and slow, with workers shuffling alive to rust-stained facades to gather beneath factory gates. Evenings, by contrast, hold an electric crackle in the air. Small fires in terra-cotta chimneys blaze across what scraps of sky can be glimpsed through the smoke. They ripple out voices in clumps—children’s laughter mixing with old arguments, the occasional song humming like smoke itself. But even in their leisure is an inoffensiveness, a soft presence that fits itself uneasily into this hard-edged land.

Scattered throughout the marshes themselves: things that slither, crawl, and leap out unexpectedly. Croaking calls resonate off empty tanks and along shallow ditches, traveling much farther than they should. The frogs here do not quite match what one would expect of frogs elsewhere; where nature kneels lower to the industry, these creatures wear faint crystalline structures growing within their slick green bodies. Salamanders: black with streaks of disturbingly bright white, their shapes rippling when underwater as if reality itself can’t hold onto their forms.

The water’s depths protect stranger beasts still, silhouettes olive-dim and massive; some say part organic, some say part machine. The denizens of the factories tell whispered stories about them in their off hours: gigantic things that drift just outside the edges of canals, their tails drumming out faint rhythms with the factory pumps. No one knows what they eat—or how they’ve lingered for years without surfacing—but their wake sometimes sends ripples large enough to pass as tidal waves in an otherwise flat-lit, stagnant world.

Within this strange world of scattered life, certain plants take on roles far greater than their simple, rooted existence would imply. Narrow-leafed reeds form tight kindred clusters, bending to the industrial breeze like worshippers in a congregation. Their shallow green hues appear almost pale amidst the frequent washing of factory ash. Their lives are steeped in sacrifice, rooted in poisoned soil yet thriving with a kind of defiant purity. Every so often, taller stalks burst into improbable blooms—halophilic flowers the color of rust mixed with lichen, petals so thin the light glows through them like stained glass. Rumor has it those wandering the marsh during storms sometimes find them folded shut and pulsating faintly, serving as a lighthouse for weary creatures—human or not.

And then there are those other inhabitants — beings neither fully of flesh nor fully of stone. They navigate both factory-tangled alleys and marshland pools with equal ease, moving like ideas given form. No one has seen them clearly, though not for lack of trying. There are only glimpses: long limbs stepping soundlessly across steel beams or a reflection too vivid moving slyly across oil-glimmered water before disappearing altogether. No one knows what they are—shadows of human regret? Projections of the machines’ memories? They come and go without notice, leaving faint patterns etched in the mud near quieter canals, their shapes reminiscent of twisted gears and fern leaves fused together.

And yet, for all the strangeness visible or unseen, the marshland itself feels like the true heart of this place. It seems vast and alive, less a location than an organism, breathing steadily beneath the weight and heat of the factories. Listen carefully, and you’ll hear it—a sluggish, ancient rhythm to the squelch of footsteps, the whisper of reeds, the grind of gears. The marshland’s heart pulses with almost deliberate slowness, allowing these innumerable overlapping lives to step in time, their dissonant chorus combining into a peculiar, haunting beauty.

No one truly owns the marshland, not even those who work between its sinews of steel and earth. They all — humans, creatures, plants, and even the machines spewing iron breath — are merely tendrils of one intertwined organism, each life braided into the damp soil, clinging tightly to what is and perhaps dreaming softly of what was.
