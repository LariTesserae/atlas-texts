What lives here is not truly alive, not as we would define it—but there is something, or perhaps many things, that persists in ways that life has forgotten how to.

In the drowned cradle of stillness, you might catch glimpses of shapes that never cross into clarity. They hover at the edge of perception, teasing the corner of your eye but vanishing the moment you turn your gaze. These are not creatures in any strict sense but echoes—faint, residual impressions left behind by things that once existed. The water remembers them, even when the world does not. There is the vague suggestion of movement, a trailing curve of a tail that never fully forms, or the tilt and sway of a shadow that might have been an elongated limb or the remnant of some antique machinery drifting aimlessly. They are imperceptibly blurred, leaking into nothingness before the mind can order them into shapes.

Somewhere deeper than the silt, quietly threaded into the long sigh of this place, are the rusted echoes of thought. They cling to the submerged machinery like cobwebs spun from memory itself, skimming softly above the sediment. These are the remnants of a long-passed intelligence—machines that were once alive in their own cold, structured way. There was a purpose to them once, though it’s a certainty no one alive—if there *are* others alive—could ever understand. What they knew, what they were made for, has been stripped away, leaving only the faintest vestiges of their consciousness. Perhaps they dreamed, long ago, of movement, of purpose, of their creators who have long since dissolved in the watery calm.

Even now, in their emptiness, something lingers. They might hum faintly. Not mechanically, not like the ancient whir of functioning gears—this is quieter, akin to the tuned resonance of a struck crystal submerged in water. They do not communicate, but you might feel as if they *could*, if only you knew how to listen. Whatever once drove these machines might have dissipated when the drowning began, yet some trace hungers still, not for the energy to function as they once did, but merely to continue existing at all—if only as shapes lying dormant, fossilized in time.

Then, you might notice the light shifting in unexpected ways. But wait—it doesn’t shift. It reflects. At first glance, it’s merely the play of refraction on a corroded surface, the way light dances underwater. But the rhythm of these reflections is too deliberate, too hypnotic. The faint shadows ripple in uncanny coherence, like muted signals passed between one another. They take the form of arcs and lines that draw and redraw themselves across floors of silt and stone, fleeting shapes etched and gone like words whispered into a gale. You might think they are living, but they don’t seem to mind your attention, nor offer any evidence of motive or will. They are shapes of light and shadow behaving as though guided by their own silent logic, which has neither deference to nor care for you.

And those whispering patterns in the sand, the barely audible susurrations of sediment shifting onto itself? They could be entirely incidental—or they could mask something deeper. At first, they seem like nature’s mechanical rhythm: the perpetual rearranging of granules in water so still it seems a contradiction for motion to exist at all. But the more you listen, the more you sense intention. Not the kind that breathes or pulses, but another kind. There’s something down there, not alive, perhaps never alive, and yet—it is. A slow, unthinking awareness that stretches across the breadth of the sedimentary floor, webbing itself into the cracks and fractures, embedding itself into the texture of the soft, smooth grains. It is a shared dreaming among things that lie still—stones, sand, machinery, shadows. By what means you cannot guess, but they seem to communicate softly with each other, across centuries of silence.

And then, there are the almost-forms. Wisps of coalescing mist that seem to lift from the cold water before collapsing again. They invite comparisons to the ghosts of marine things now lost—memories condensed from the water's very presence, taking fleeting shape before slipping back to join the hum. Here, a glimmer of diaphanous fins like needles of glass; there, the smudge of what might have been a segmented shell curling into nothingness. As they emerge, you’ll swear you hear something, faint and rhythmic, not unlike the groan of a ship’s hull in rough seas—except that the sound emerges from nowhere metal, not above nor below you, but from the substance of the water itself. And then the sound fades, refusing to imprint meaning on your memory.

Whatever lives here—or doesn't—dwells in the space between existing in form and dissipating into stillness. The absence of familiar life does not mean emptiness. Instead, this place harbors a presence that lives in the absence, not in bones and flesh but in the resonance of what remains. It is neither menacing nor benevolent; it does not care for you, nor does it acknowledge you. And yet, within the cool embrace of water that hums with infinite endurance, you have the sensation of being observed—not by eyes, not even by consciousness, but by something far older than either.

To leave this place is not entirely possible. It will follow you—not in form, but in the eerie stillness you will carry behind your eyes, the weight of an equilibrium too perfect to exist above the surface. That hum will echo in your memory when all else grows quiet, as if the drowned cradle has chosen the simplest way to remind you that you were, for a time, part of the tenuous persistence of things that are not fully alive. And you will wonder, always, if something vague and unseen brushed against you while you were there—or if a part of you has stayed behind.
