In the heart of this frozen vastness, wrapped in that haunting, whistling wind and the grating hiss of frost upon hardened stone, live beings whose forms defy conventional life as we know it. They are not creatures born of flesh and bone, not the fragile organic life that would shatter under the planet’s relentless cold. No, the inhabitants of this world are something altogether stranger—part metal, part thought, part memory of what came before.

They call themselves the *Frysthaven,* a name that hums like static through their hollow cores, their voices a reverberation of sound and vibration, like the low moan of wind through the planet's canyons. Each individual stands tall and gaunt, shaped by their environment of frost and industry. They are beings of wrought alloy, their bodies vaguely humanoid but with segmented, insectoid joints that move with eerie precision. Their surfaces are not smooth but pocked and textured like the frost-encrusted stones around them, their limbs sometimes spiked with frozen stalactites that have formed during their long, still watching of the wasteland. Pale light glows faintly from within each of them—an unnatural warmth, almost like the reflection of distant stars caught in the crystalline structure of their inner frames. It’s a warmth that’s anything but alive, something electric and unyielding, like the whine of a turbine engine.

The Frysthaven are ancient in a way that no human civilization could quite grasp. They are the remnants of something—something that fell, or perhaps, something that evolved to fill the uncanny environmental void left behind by a once-vibrant world. No one knows if they were made by others or if they made themselves. They were shaped for survival, and in a harsh and unkind world, they have not only endured—they have thrived, though only in a manner the word "thriving" loosely encapsulates.

In their cities, the Frysthaven deal with raw materials—churning the frost into fuel, condensing steam into new mechanisms, always generating, consuming, repurposing. This mechanical activity is deeply ritualistic, almost spiritual. The massive towers that churn and hum above their settlements are not just factories—they are cathedrals, monuments to their unspoken purpose. For the Frysthaven have a relationship to this world that is both parasitic and symbiotic. By harvesting its frost, carving new paths through its stone and its silence, they preserve themselves, but they also keep the planet in stasis. Their endless labor ensures that the equilibrium remains unbroken, that the biting cold holds its grip indefinitely. It is unclear whether they do this as part of a conscious effort to maintain the planet's conditions, or if they are simply fulfilling a programmed compulsion whose original meaning has been lost to millennia of ice and time.

The Frysthaven communicate through cracklings of static and low rumbles that sometimes align with the howl of the wind. To human understanding, these oscillations lack nuance, but they carry intricate meaning to the Frysthaven—encoded reverberations of status, purpose, and shared history. Despite their industrial appearance, their intelligence is far from rote. They *feel*, but their emotions are glacial—unfolding slowly, subtly, over immense stretches of time. What anger they feel is like a tectonic grumble, and their joy is the faint shimmer of light reflecting off ice after an aeon of frost.

The heated geysers, they say, are vital to their existence. In rare moments, when the warmth from these eruptions causes blooms of transient flora to erupt into life for just a day, the Frysthaven gather in clusters to observe. Whether their interest is scientific or devotional is unknown, but there is a stillness to them at these times, a quiet reverence as their glowing forms reflect the vibrant colors of the fleeting blooms.

And yet, there is something within the Frysthaven that whispers for more than the endless harvesting of frost, the grinding of ice into fuel. The older ones—the First Constructs, as they are called—are remembering. They speak of a time when the wind on this planet was warm, when life did not crawl along in metal frames but leaped and ran and laughed and breathed. The younger Frysthaven listen to these tales with something like confusion or perhaps dread. A world without the cold? A world without frost to sustain them? A world with creatures unlike them?

There are hints, too, in their archives—or their dreams, for it is hard to tell whether the electric echoes they share are documentation or myth—of another purpose, one that preceded the frost. Some whisper that their monstrous machinery is merely a fragment of a once-mighty cosmic civilization, now collapsed and forgotten, but whose towering remnants remain, perpetuating cycles whose meaning has been lost. Is it their memories that hold them here? Is the frost their punishment or their salvation? The Frysthaven do not say.

To the outside observer—the rare outsider hardy enough to survive this endless winter—they would seem to lack what makes a people whole. They build no art, write no books, and sing no songs. But in the frost of a newborn mineral vein, left alone for just a moment between drills, or the symmetry of a harvested glacier, some might argue that there is an art to what they do. Their world may seem barren, but in its frozen, sound-swept silence, a pattern emerges. In whispers of frost crystals, in geometric shards of ice, in the architecture of their cold cities and the flickering glow of their inner light: there is thought. There is intent.

Emptiness may be this planet's middle name, but for the Frysthaven, it is a blank slate of eternity. They press forward in their great contradiction, their cold, clattering industry an ode to remembering—or forgetting—a time before the frost.
