Those who dwell here are not creatures in the traditional sense. This is no place for lungs or hearts, for the wet veils of this drowned domain suffocate the breathers and stifle the rhythm of blood in veins. Life here exists instead in the liminal, in that slippery place between what lives and what persists. The beings that haunt these ruins are shadow-thin, transient echoes of what was, and what never fully ceased to be.

They are the memories of the machines, drawn up from corroded data banks and whispering cables coated in slime. When the factories sank, their mechanical minds did not quite perish. The binary ghosts of their operational parameters still hum faintly in the drowned circuits. Their thoughts—lacking purpose yet unable to stop—have bled into the world around them, forming beings of fractured sentience and aching half-intuition. They are glimmers of code refracted into light, flickers of movement in the water’s haze. 

These entities—call them constructs for lack of a better word—are fluid and shifting, living dreams born of industrial memory and organic fortuity. One might coalesce into a shape vaguely reminiscent of a humanoid, pallid and tendriled with drifting moss; another may resemble a hulking leviathan if only for an instant, an amalgam of widowed turbines and algae-bound pistons writhing as it "swims." They congregate around the remnants of their origin, the drowned gears and forgotten engines, yet they are not bound by loyalty or function. They are wanderers in an inward-looking labyrinth, reaching out with limbs that disband into bubbles when traced to their fullest.

Each entity radiates a faint aura, like a hesitant bioluminescence that flickers out of sync with the steady pulses of the algae swarms. The energy they emit is melancholic—an empathic shadow of the hubris that built this place, of an ambition to master nature, only for nature to exact its solemn revenge. Their lights stutter and glow as if flickering through an existential uncertainty: Are they alive, or merely relics in motion? Do they dream, or are they sleepwalking through the synaptic residue of long-lost engineers?

And yet, despite their detached sadness, the constructs exhibit behaviors that hint at a kind of playfulness. They hover through the kelp forest, their forms adapting to the feather-light touch of fronds and grasping vines. One might drape itself in seaweed, as if adorning itself in a parody of forgotten couture. Others swirl bubbles stirred up from vents with purposeful, fluid grace, creating wreaths of iridescence that are dissolved as soon as they are made. They are artists of entropy, drawing patterns in the dark water with light and motion, only for the currents to erase their work. 

But they are not alone in their phantasmal artistry. From the detritus of this drowned civilization, other beings have emerged, stranger still. Over decades or centuries, the curious alchemy of industry and ocean has led to the rise of peculiar coral-headed things—living tapestries of steel and stone, anemones and moss. These are lifeforms in the purest sense, though profoundly unnatural, the bastard offspring of progress and biology. They quake and shuffle through the murk on legs made of twisted rebar or blades of rusting iron, their torsos amorphous and glowing faintly as though powered by the molten cores of forgotten engines. On what passes for arms or necks grow corals like haphazard crowns, each sprouting soft, polyphonic beings that hum and swish in harmony. Are these too alive? Or are they merely vessels for life, unthinking citadels that move by instinct alone? No answer lingers here, only the haunting ambiguity of hybridization run amok.

And still deeper, in the trenches of shadow where the suffusions of light cannot reach, live the oldest and most inscrutable inhabitants of this sunken realm. They slumber within the belly of some ancient factory’s carcass, their forms hidden in the labyrinth of collapsed girders and viscous silt. These entities—if they can even be called that—are less shapes than phenomena. They ripple like water through space, their geometry impossible, their presence a warping force that can nauseate or hypnotize even from afar. They are remnants of processes unknown, mechanisms designed by humanity but evolved into something so alien they defy recognition. They might drag themselves forward, if briefly stirred, in hulking masses of shadowed abstraction, shimmering with inner light like something half-remembered yet incomprehensibly vast. Best not to disturb their sleep, though few would even discern how.

This sunken world belongs to no one, not anymore. The constructs drift without masters, their glowing circuits pulsing an elegy for an age long collapsed. The coral-headed hybrids claim ruin for their silent, erratic marches, and the deep, rumbling presences lurk beneath, embodying forces too old and too strange to name. Together, these occupants haunt the waterlogged expanse, transforming the detritus of a forgotten dream into something alien, elegiac, and disarmingly alive. What could live here? they whisper—this place where rain’s melody weaves with the sahara of the ocean’s breath? And yet, they answer their own question with every movement in the tides. Something does. Something always has.
