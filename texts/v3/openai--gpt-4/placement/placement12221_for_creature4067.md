If I were to find myself in this world, I would become a Custodian of the Luminous Archive—a keeper of the people's collective memory and chronicler of the labyrinthine transformation of this underworld. Within this ever-shifting subterra, where walls rise, fall, and are reshaped with every passing week, a persistent truth remains: history becomes both essential and ephemeral in equal measure. My role would be to guard the echoes of time, to capture the whirring of drills, the laughter of children, and the etchings of hands long since gone silent.

I would inhabit a solitary, towering spire carved from a colossal stone pillar found in the heart of the labyrinth. My home would be dubbed the "Spindle" by the dwellers of this realm, owing to its spiral staircase that winds around the stone core, narrowing as it reaches the apex. The steps would be smoothed by decades of soft-soled footprints, their depressions telling their own story of time. The Spindle would house a kaleidoscope of artifacts — fragments of broken drills frozen in time, forgotten schematic blueprints, portraits made from pigments ground from subterranean minerals. The very fabric of our culture, compressed into one symbolic monument of our being.

My eyes, like those of others, would gleam in spectrums of faint light — celestial blues and ethereal silvers dancing in irises accustomed to the soft, dim glow of our network of electric lamps. My skin would shimmer faintly, the faint sheen of perpetual humidity combined with the fine dust of the rock embedded in my pores. My wiry hands would be callused, not from wielding drills, but from the delicate artistry of carving tales and artistry into stone, a preservation of not just events, but whispers and dreams.

Perhaps I would be more listener than chronicler. My days would be spent wandering the warren-esque tunnels, pausing to collect whispered memories and myths from those who toiled or dreamt. A miner would recount the sounds of the Earth as he drove the drill deep, and I would catalog the peculiar notations of his descriptions. A child might explain how she imagined a stalactite to be a sleeping dragon, and I would sketch its curled tail into the margins of the records. A scientist might bring me an iridescent crystal unearthed in a fresh tunnel, and together we would try to decipher its whispers, whether they sang of immense pressure or ancient explosions.

At the base of the Spindle lies the Centering Pool—a dark body of water fed by an underground river diverted to the heart of the city. No one knows its true depth. Like a mirror, the pool reflects the light of the hanging lamps above, creating a celestial mimicry of an uncovered sky. It is said that the pool serves as a living ink-blot test for those who gaze into it—offering not just their reflection, but visions of possibility: new tunnel paths to try, unknown engineering techniques not yet conceived, even glimpses of the surface world and the starlight we’ve lost beneath cold stone.

As the Custodian, I would be keeper of the "flow," that intangible rhythm of activity that surges through the city. All decisions here are born from a collaborative momentum, but it requires a weaver of strands to ensure the stories of yesterday stitch themselves just tightly enough to the seams of today and the ambitious fabric of tomorrow. My primary tools would not be tools of labor, but an etched slateboard where glowing runes recount a history adapted to change, and a massive pendulum suspended from the Spindle’s peak, oscillating silently to show how motion itself anchors the world.

I would likely never leave the underground labyrinth. Not because I feared the surface, but because this was my chosen place of belonging. Here, deep beneath the ice-bitten earth, surrounded by the constant reverberations of purpose, it would be enough to sense the constant heartbeat of pickaxes and chisels, the artesian engineers' laughter, and the endless drip-drip of condensed water. It would be enough to be one spark of light among a network of glowing souls who together carved life from cold stone—not for survival, but for the sheer artistic audacity of creating a world that had never before dared to exist.
