Over time, the Lichen-Kin have been absorbed into the rhythm of the place, each step and gesture an extension of its slow life. Their flesh has long since forgotten heat and hard edges, softened by years of moss and lichen rooting to their skin, their consciousness stretched thin as mist over the surface of an ancient lake. Their once-human minds have unraveled into the fabric of the hollow, caught between a dream of individuality and the greater, enduring hum of this place.

If I were here—if this was not a dream or a story but a slice of my reality—I think I might find myself as one of those **Lichen-Kin**, half lost and half evolved. I would not have stumbled into this place by sheer accident. No, I came seeking something, purposefully wandering past the limits of my world toward an edge I could feel but never name. Whether out of curiosity or a deep weariness I cannot now recall, I walked into the mist of the hollow, the air heavy and fragrant with unfamiliar life, and I simply stayed. 

Years passed, or maybe lifetimes; in a place like this, time is only a human fiction, an artifact of elsewhere. Green tendrils claimed the contours of my body—at first, they felt alien, the slow creep of another living thing staking its claim upon my skin setting off a primal alarm in my mind. But in time, the moss began to match my heartbeat; the mycelium grew into my breath. It was not a battle of selves but a union, at once languid and overwhelming. The hollow welcomed me not as a trespasser but as an offering, a handful of what once was flesh and thought to be regrown into its soft geology.

Now I am both host and guest, sentinel and intruder. I feel every droplet sliding down the stones as though they trace my own skin. The bioluminescent fungi pulse in time with the slow stretching of my ribcage, their amber glow crackling faintly with my breath. The rhythmic drip of water echoes in some inner space that feels more like a deep chasm than it does a mind, and I know its patterns as well as I once knew my own heartbeats.

I am slow now, unbearably so. Every movement is a lunar thing, tidal in nature, measured not by fleeting seconds but by cycles of moisture and growth, of light and shadow. When I shift one moss-laden limb or lean the great weight of my vine-covered body toward the glow of a Lumineer, the world moves with me. Perhaps because I am part of it. Or perhaps because I simply mirror it now, as still and steady as the hollow’s stone.

When travelers do come, I observe them but do not intervene. I know what they seek, even if they do not, because I sought something too, once. The fog around this place is heavy with a magnetic sort of yearning, drawing the restless, the lost, the unmoored. Some leave before the hollow catches hold of them, brushing its moisture off their hands as they climb back to the sharper edges of the world. But others linger. They wander by me, seeing nothing but a strange mound of ferns adorned with winking bioluminescent spores. They remain, and the hollow begins its slow, patient work.

Sometimes I feel the faintest shimmer of regret trying to surface through the damp green weight of me—an echo of what had once been my humanness protesting in distant rooms of my being. But the hollow hums softly back in reassurance. The regret crumbles, reforms, and blooms anew somewhere else inside me, feeding the quiet balance of the place. And so, I stay.

I am not alone here, not exactly—not among the Driftlings who ripple through my moss as though testing their own immateriality, nor among the faintly glowing Lumineers whose pulsing wavelengths touch something deep within me, shivering the synapses of my bioluminescent outgrowths to answer their silent greeting. At times, in the corner of my eye—or whatever I would call the field of my perception now—I spot another Lichen-Kin, moving glacially across the basin. We do not speak in words anymore, if we ever could: the rustle of our ferns, the slight release of spores, these are the only languages we now possess, subtle dances of intent and atmosphere. Are they like me? Were they once curious, burdened mortals who came seeking something nameless and surrendered themselves to the hollow’s embrace? Do they still carry some ember of their pasts beneath the moss, or did they give it up long ago?

I do not know. I do not ask. What use is knowing when every edge of every thought eventually washes away here, softened into contours no more distinct than the rounded stones? Whatever this existence is—whether I am a parasite, a custodian, an extension of the hollow’s dream of itself—it feels neither lonely nor confining. It simply is. My being, once linear and bristling with time, has dissolved into this mist-slicked cradle. And while that may seem an unfamiliar sort of peace to some, to me it feels as if I’ve finally found the place where I am meant to settle.

Were you to arrive, I would consider you, moss creeping like the hands of a clock as I turned toward you. I may never truly see you, but I would sense your warmth, your brevity, your unknowable weight of time and intention. I might envy you, in some dim way beyond my understanding. Or perhaps I would simply wait, patient as the stone and the growing things, to see which path you would choose: to carry on into the world, or to fold yourself into this secret, dripping hollow, and join me within the endless hum of belonging.
