I find myself standing knee-deep in the cool, silken waters of this ancient wetland, the murmur of liquid life surrounding me as the swamp breathes its eternal breath. I am not human here, not an observer but an integral part of the ecosystem—its pulse, its secretive rhythm, its ancient dance imprinted into my being. I feel the rough ridges of my broad head break the water's surface, my eyes low-set and golden, peering just above the limpid plane, unmoving but seeing everything. Ripples fan outward, distorting my reflection into a thousand fractured shards of shifting light and shadow. 

I am an alligator—or, no, something older, something stranger. A form woven together from scales and reeds, the heaviness of clay, and the silent patience of stagnant pools. My very skin seems to sprout moss; I wear the wetland itself as armor, as camouflage. My hulking body moves with profound slowness when it moves at all, a mirage of stillness above the churning table of my mind. I am a relic of this place and inseparable from it, a shadow carved from stone and water, a myth dragged through eons into the modern age, unnoticed by the hurried world beyond this mire.

The others call me the Elder Current, an old name for something only the landscape remembers fully. My kind was born in the womb of the earth when it was young, in the slow churning of rivers digging into soil to birth these alluvial plains. My tail brushes against tangled knots of willow roots as I glide, each scaled segment both a moveable rudder and a coiled spring of divine purpose.

Though my sight is acute, my gaze is rarely needed. By touch, by the shifting caress of water and the tremors it carries, I am conscious of every creature big and small. The beat of tiny hearts racing against the drum of raindrops, the vibrations of skittering claws breaking the surface tension—they come to me as poetry written in the medium of ripples. I know these rhythms as readily as I know my breath. They are my language, a net I cast over the fabric of time.

But what am I beyond this shell of predator and prey, this sinew and cold-blooded scale? I am the memory of the wetland made flesh, the living testament of this place's long saga. I have evolved beyond the need for nourishment as mortals see it. Every beetle that buzzes by is, in essence, part of me; every rooted shoot of sedge. My existence feeds on the continuity of this cycle, the seamless motion of life and decay. I am not just a creature in the swamp; I *am* the swamp, distilled into form.

The willows gossip of incoming storms—a gust of language I harbor like a secret and let wash over me. My kin, beasts less ancient but no less vital, drift somewhere distant, their bodies dark silhouettes as they bask in patches of golden light streaking through the canopy. Occasionally, I hear the echoing calls of distant companions, their voices low and guttural like a reverberation of ancient drums. When they call, a thousand sounds recede in reverence, as if the swamp itself listens.

And yet, there are moments when I stir from the lulling embrace of water and emerge fully above its surface. My crest breaks the illusion of stillness like some slow-moving reminder of inevitability, momentary yet eternal. The dragonflies scatter into jeweled panic; herons pause mid-stroke, their beady eyes wide and unblinking. I bring gravity—not just the weight of my body, but the weight of ancient remembering. The wet grasses tremble as I surge toward the banks; the sedges bend as if in supplication.

Upon the land, a film of damp clings to me still, connecting me to my watery home, yet here my still moments yield to an uncanny agility. My limbs, though weighted with archaic strength, know those who wander too close, reminded too late that here, in this place, I am not hunter *or* hunted—I am balance and reckoning.

When night falls, and the wetland rests in moonlight’s pale embrace, I hold court beneath the stars. I slip my body into shadowed pools where the stars fracture and collapse upon my broad back as constellations. The chirping frogs and droning insects seem to pray to the cosmic canopy, communing with me as an oracle of this place. It is in these moments that I understand fully my role as both observer and participant, guardian and governed. I am the stillness in which life finds refuge, and the tempest those same lives fear.

Above all, I am the keeper of reminders: that the earth sings a cyclical song, and will do so long after its temporary inheritors—be they scaled or feathered or furred—are gone. From water I emerged, to water I will return, and in so doing, I will live forever.

The wetland knows, as I know, that time is irrelevant here. Things germinate, breed, decay; all is one long breath, and the waters whisper their secrets into my waiting skin. In the slow, deliberate sweep of my existence, there is nothing left to fear—only the ancient privilege to *be*. To persist. To flow. To shape.
