If I were to find myself in this world, I would not be one of the surface folk who stumbled upon it by accident, nor a displaced intruder picking my way through the damp and dimly lit mazes. I would belong here, as one of the beings, a thread within this intricate weave of life, stone, and purpose.

My form would reflect my connection to this place—slim and compact, no taller than a human child. My limbs would possess a jointed, elegant strength, fingers tipped with claws capable of both carving stone and stitching fine fibers into nets. My skin would be a dark umber laced with gleaming streaks of greenish bioluminescence, its patterns thickest on my forearms and legs, betraying both individuality and a certain fluid harmony with the organic chaos around me. The feathery filaments fanning from my temples would constantly ripple in search of vibrations and subtle movements, a part of my awareness as instinctive as breathing.

I would be a gatherer. My long, tapering limbs and sharp reflexes make me suited to this role, as does my incessant curiosity. When the light filters weakly down from the surface, it would be my time to venture out through tunnels that spool upward, where the rock begins to give way to soil and scrappy roots. I’d slip from crag to crag with precise, deliberate movements, a bundle of tools and empty waxy sacks strapped across my back, leaving the deeper warmth behind me as I ventured into the twilight threshold.

At the surface openings, just below the fractured world above, I would lean close to cracks in the ceiling through which shreds of sky peer down like shy eyes. My filaments would twitch as they attune themselves to the rhythms of this other world—the faint tremor of birds in flight, the almost-imperceptible shifting of vines swaying in the wind. My clawed fingers would gently ease seeds from the roots that snake down into our caverns, fingers sifting through compacted soil for minerals nestled in pocketed crevices. Every scrap matters, and I would hoard my finds in the folds of my bag, careful not to hiss too loudly in triumph, lest I attract something larger.

But my work would not always keep me in these perilous upper reaches. Often, I’d turn back into the labyrinth, retreating downward to rejoin my kin. My slender legs would spring with practiced momentum across bonewood beams suspended over narrow crevices, my free hand brushing against the carved tunnel walls for balance. In my returning steps, my body would follow the hum of their activity—the hiss and tap of tools striking stone, the chitter of younglings quarreling over a game, the steady drip of irrigation water as it fed hungrily cultivated moss pits. It would be a soundscape as familiar and indispensably nourishing to me as breathing.

At the communal hub, I would become part of the swarm’s movements. Around the central steaming vents, I’d pick my way carefully toward light-catchers, where my finds could be exchanged for scraps of soft fiber or bottles of iridescent liquid—a fermented extract from the fungi my kin still prize. I’d marvel again at the arrangements of prisms and bone-glass circles crafted by others, how the refracted glows crisscrossed to tint the dark walls with shifting ripples of soft gold, green, and blue. It could almost look like the surface sky, if you squinted. But I wouldn’t dare squint too long. Even beautiful things could stir ache.

My role in the deeper spaces would be more than practical. I’d feel deeply rooted to the rituals of the place. I might be among those trusted to ascend to the highest niches to gather light-slivers, the shattered remnants of the sun’s rays that trickle past the cracks of the world above. Placing them with careful reverence into the network of refractors that amplify and redirect the precious illumination, I would feel a glimmer of connection to something vast beyond our tangled existence.

But more often than not, my real joy would emerge in the hidden nooks and forgotten corners of these tunnels. I’d feel the lure of vacant spaces, the hollowed beds of muck and stone long abandoned by my ancestors, and the thriving, tenacious life they would now host. I’d sit among the soft swirls of moss and watch the segmented crustaceans scatter against fungal blooms, my clawed finger tracing idle loops in the dirt. There, perhaps, I would scratch an abstract, curling pattern on the nearest wall—a thread of thought externalized, a little mark of myself left behind to join the tapestry of centuries.

At times, I would congregate with my kin in moments of collective play or ceremony. I imagine myself darting through suspended nets, twirling in weightless freedom as the gently swaying web creaked softly beneath me. At night, when silence falls and even the scurrying slows, there’d be room in the darkness for music—the long, low notes pressed out of hollowed trunks over hot vents, while a multi-tonal murmur rose from my kin, vibrating as much in the stone as in our sensory filaments.

I wouldn’t fear the above world, not really. But I would recognize its strangeness and alien vastness, just as I would know that I did not belong there for reasons I might never understand. The light is not where we dwell. Here, in this snug and endlessly refracting web of tunnels and steam, I belong to something older. Something shared. It is this closeness that defines me—the feeling of the stone snug around me, of my warmth mingling with theirs, of vibrations carried in delicate chains between us. If we are a secret, let us stay whispered. If we are a bending fold in the world, let us remain held inside its curve. After all, the surface has its brilliance.

But this is our glow.
