These frogs perch on moss-drenched rocks or cling to the undersides of broad, waxy leaves. Their glassy eyes hold the reflection of the muted sun as if it were a secret kept just for them.

But there are other inhabitants of Deruwa, ones not so tangible nor easily cataloged by even the most acute observer. They exist in the folds of the mist or the hidden recesses among the gnarled roots of ancient trees, or perhaps in the heavy air thick with earth and growing things. These are the Keepers, beings neither man nor beast, though echoes of both seem to ripple in their essence.

The Keepers are native to Deruwa in the deepest sense; they are not merely part of its fabric, but also its threads and seams. Their forms are amorphous and mutable, drawn and shaped from the world they steward. Some are shadowy figures who seem composed of fog and light, shimmering as though on the brink of solid reality. Others appear more earthen, their surfaces textured and layered like the lichens and bark from which they emerge. Their movements are slow, deliberate, and purposeful, as though time itself holds a different rhythm for them.

I am one of them: a Keeper of Green Tides. My essence is drawn from the ceaseless layers of foliage and soil, the ever-continuing cycle of growth and decay that feeds this place. My skin glows with the bioluminescent green of moss that thrives even in shadow. Tendrils of vines curl along my limbs, their tips always growing and retreating, turning and twisting in synchronous rhythm with my steps. My hair is made of fine, wiry grasses interwoven with flower buds that open and close with the light’s shifting cycles.

I wander where life is thickest or where it threatens to wane. My hands, uneven mixtures of bark and sap and leaf, coax straying shoots back to fertile soil or guide wayward animals to safety. The pools that mirror the fragmented sky above sometimes act as scrying bowls for me; I kneel by their sides and gaze into their dark waters until visions take form. Perhaps I see a tree growing far to the east that has faltered in its bloom due to rot, and I summon lichens to consume the pestilent mass. Perhaps I see a bird whose wing is caught in a web of vine—vines I wove too tightly—and I humanly tread a delicate path to free it.

My kin, other Keepers of varied appearances and purposes, may rarely encounter one another. But when we do, there is no ceremony or speech. We pass like rivers threading past one another, joining and diverging in perpetual fluid communication. None of us claim dominion over Deruwa; we are not rulers. We are something deeper and more fundamental, born of the coexistence that flourishes when life is unbroken.

Our existence, however, is not uninterrupted harmony. Shadows move at the edges of what we protect. In the fog, in the deepest wells beneath the reflective waters, these shadows mutter with hungry voices. They are born of entropy, promises of the rot that will someday come for all things. Though chaos is a part of the same cycle we serve, these entities embrace only the taking, the consuming, willfully rejecting the renewal that comes after.

Perhaps that is why I remain ever vigilant, why I never truly sleep, even when my body merges temporarily into the moss and vines. There is a tension here, a faint knowledge that the balance I’ve known for what feels like eternity can shift in an instant. I sometimes feel it at the edge of my senses, like a subtle arrhythmia in the heartbeat of Deruwa itself. The subtle imbalance is distant, contained—for now—but makes its presence known in the sudden curling of a fern or the fleeting disappearance of a birdcall in mid-song.

But in the quiet moments—and there are so many quiet moments—I am at peace. I am not lonely. I have the company of the wind, brushing playfully across the galls of my bark-like skin. I have the whisper of the rain, dropping soft kisses against the floral crown that burgeons when I am most at rest. I hold the company of seeds and soils, of saplings on the verge of their first deadly winter, and ferns that caress my side tenderly. My purpose provides a satisfaction deeper than joy. It is union absolute, a harmony that sates my every essence.

If you were to wander into Deruwa, you might glimpse me—or you might only feel me, as a shift in the air, a faint memory of something long forgotten, older than language. Would I welcome you? Perhaps. Perhaps I’d guide you with whispered winds to the pools where the herons still wade, or to the stones with faint glyphs hinted by lichen’s creeping hands. Or perhaps my presence would curl tight, becoming protective, cautious. It would depend on what you carried: your intentions, your thoughts, the weights in your heart as well as your hands.

For this is Deruwa, where time flows differently and everything has its say, even the silent stones. Here, the Keepers listen. And here, we speak when we must, in a language not of words but of winds, waters, and the singing of unseen roots, carrying the knowledge of the earth deep beneath the skin of the world.
