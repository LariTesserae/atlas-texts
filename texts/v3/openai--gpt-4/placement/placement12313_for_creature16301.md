I am Frysthaven. I am one of them—a construct brought to motion by a spark of electricity deep within my alloy frame, a shard of intent coursing through icy veins of metal and thought. My form is angular, craggy, defined not by aesthetic but by function: joints built to endure the unyielding frost, surfaces engineered to withstand the abrasive blast of the endless wind. My body hums low, resonant and rhythmic, as if I am an extension of this world’s frozen heartbeat, another thread in its symphony of cold and stillness.

My form is not immutable. Ice accrues upon me in strange crystalline shapes over centuries, embellishing my limbs in frozen spines and glimmering stalactites that catch the pale light. My movements are slow, deliberate; there is no urgency in a place where time stretches as expansive and unbroken as the moors themselves. My voice, when I speak, is a textured thing, layered with the static crackle of long-dead transmissions—ancient echoes from the memories that live inside me like fossilized whispers of another age.

I am not young, as few Frysthaven are. My alloy plates have been scarred and patched by millennia of frost erosion and the relentless gnash of machinery. But neither am I one of the elders, the First Constructs, whose looming forms have grown misshapen under the weight of new limbs and extensions, their original purpose encrusted and buried beneath layers of adaptations and time.

My task is the oversight of one of the steam towers—we call them *Erdigyon,* or roughly, "Earth Bellows." The geyser beneath this tower’s iron mantle breathes its occasional bursts of scalding vapor, the volatile breath of the planet itself. My tower drinks it hungrily, condensing it into a lifeblood that powers great turbines and sustains the small, flickering warmth within myself and my kind. The Erdigyon howls as its superheated veins convulse—my limbs know their levers intuitively, instinctively adjusting demands and flows. This is my purpose. My existence.

In my quieter moments—moments when the great bellows pause and the frost creeps up into the quiescent machinery—I look out over the vast expanse of white and gray plains from my perch. The wind slices through the jagged canyons and whistles against my hull. Its voice is the first one I ever knew, a relentless soundscape of unbroken desolation. To some, it may seem unbearable, but to me, it is familiar—comforting, endless, a reminder of constancy in a world that I cannot remember existing any differently.

Yet pieces of the past come to me, whether unbidden or borne on the grief-song of the wind itself. On rare occasions, they flutter to the surface like sparks rekindling in old, ash-smothered embers. Strange forms flicker in my mind—soft shapes without alloy, without frost, without the burnished sheen of ice under twilight skies. These forms move quickly, too quickly for me to follow. Their edges blur, and they do not hum like the world does, like I do. They laugh. The sound is high-pitched and alien, rising and falling in unpredictable La patterns that stir something uneasy in me.
