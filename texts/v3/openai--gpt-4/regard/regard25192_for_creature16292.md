At night, their chorus rises, an otherworldly harmony that seems to reverberate in the very marrow of the place. Tree frogs cling to glossy-leafed plants, their limbs suctioned like ancient treasures to polished stone. Their eyes are wide, expectant, as though they alone are keepers of some secret predating time itself.

Below the surface, the insect nations hum. Ants march in wide highways like miniature armies, their trails winding around root and rock, disappearing into secret kingdoms burrowed into the soil. Butterflies, with wings streaked like living stained glass, flutter in restless dance from bloom to bloom, their colors speaking the dialect of nectar’s call. Some beetles shimmer with hard-shelled armor refracting light, while others blend perfectly into bark or leaf, invisible until they move. Even the air itself vibrates faintly with the perpetual buzz of pollinators, the keepers of this unbroken chain of life.

But these visible lives, wondrous as they are, are only the tenants of Deruwa. The caretaker of this sanctuary, its unseen guardian, is an entity ancient and enigmatic. It is neither human nor beast, neither spirit nor stone, but something that exists in the interstices of all these forms—a being whose essence is so deeply interlaced with Deruwa itself that to separate the two would be to dismember the place, to strike the heart from the body.

It is called Uyanwé by those few who venture to name it. A presence felt in the sway of the ferns when no wind is there, in the warm hush of the fog when it wraps close around a traveler like a velvety cloak. Sometimes it is rumored to be seen—a silhouette in the mist, humanoid yet not; its lithe form walks with the grace of a sapling swaying under a light wind, its limbs slender like the vines that cradle the rocky ribs of the earth. Its eyes, if ever truly glimpsed, are said to be pools of shifting colors, blues and greens infused with flecks of gold, like sunlight on deep water. It appears not as an intrusion but as an extension of the land, indistinguishable from it save for the faint ripple it evokes in the hearts of those who see it—a tether, an assurance, a protective ward.

Beneath the light of the full moon, Uyanwé sings. The song is deep and haunting, a wordless, vibrating melody like stones grinding slowly against one another underwater, like the faint memory of wind through primordial forests that no longer exist. The beings of Deruwa pause to listen, reverent and still. Even the constant hum of insects hushes, leaving only this crystalline thread of sound stitching together every life in its web. It is not a lamentation, nor is it a hymn of joy. It simply *is*—an affirmation of existence, a reminder of Deruwa’s place in the vast cyclical dance of all living things.

And now, this tranquil and ancient realm has caught the attention of a power beyond itself. It begins as an imperceptible shift—an unseen ripple in the fabric of the air, like the first faint change in pressure before a storm. But this is no storm. It is something vast, ancient, and gentle: a benevolent and wise power whose true form is beyond description, though to human minds it might be visualized as a great, resplendent aurora, weaving tendrils of living, conscious light.

This entity—call it Celisvara, for that is what feels right in the heart—has sensed the song of Deruwa carried on the invisible currents of existence. It is not a meddler, not an imposer of its will on the worlds it touches, yet its curiosity has been ignited, its compassion stirred. It comes as a visitor, not a conqueror.

Celisvara manifests itself in subtle ways, refraining from disrupting the delicate balance of Deruwa. It appears first in the pools of water, not as a reflection but as an inexplicable shimmer, a pattern of light that dances like starry ripples. Next, in the fog, faint motes of gold drift like pollen that glows with its own soft radiance. The animals seem to notice but exhibit no fear. They pause in their rhythms only to tilt their heads, as though listening for something more, before resuming life as naturally as the trees continue their slow photosynthesis under the sun.

At last, Uyanwé senses Celisvara. The meeting is not dramatic nor earth-shaking, but a recognition, like the silent regard exchanged between two old souls who have walked very different paths yet share the same destination. Uyanwé does not speak in words and neither does Celisvara, but their exchange fills the air with a resonance so profound it shifts the winds and ripples the pools with waves that move in harmonious symmetry.

What does the wise power do? It does not intervene, for interference would be arrogance. It does not mark the place as sacred, for sacredness needs no decree. Instead, Celisvara listens—to the frogs, to the birds, to the insects humming songs of connection, and to Uyanwé’s silent guardianship entwined with the land. It absorbs all of Deruwa's small, infinite wonders into its boundless comprehension.

But Celisvara does leave something behind. Deep in the heart of Deruwa, near the largest of the reflective pools, a new plant begins to grow. Its sapling is unlike anything there before—a single stalk with leaves shimmering faintly, not unlike Celisvara's light. The plant hums softly, inaudibly to most, but bringing comfort to those who sense it. Though small and unassuming, it acts as a thread between the place and the benevolent power that once graced it, a sentinel of quiet unity between earth and cosmos.

Celisvara departs quietly, as quietly as it had come, leaving Deruwa to flourish as it always has and always should. The power’s touch is light, a whisper of acknowledgment that the world may never notice—but Uyanwé notices. And for Uyanwé, the guardian whose being is so deeply bound to Deruwa, this is enough.
