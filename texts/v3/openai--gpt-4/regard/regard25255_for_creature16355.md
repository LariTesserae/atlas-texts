Far away, yet impossibly near, the Grey Flats brush against the periphery of other realms—realms where time thrums louder, color riots untamed, and life spreads itself into every nook and shadow. It is from one of these higher or outer planes that the attention of a wise and benevolent power turns toward the Flats. Not summoned, for no Stonezar would summon; their needs are silent as the rain-carved grooves upon their forms. Rather, this power arrives unbidden and watchful, drawn by the singular, resonant hum of the Flats themselves—a song of quiet endurance, of beauty forged by erosion, of an ancient and sacred patience.

Of all the forms it could assume, the power chooses one familiar, yet unfamiliar—a great clockwork bird. Its feathers, glossy interlocking plates of brass and silver, glint soft rose-gold in the dim and changeable light. Around its body flows a thin, steady pulse of blue energy, not unlike veins of water streaking through stone. Its many eyes—a mosaic of crystalline lenses—sparkle with omniscience, scanning this hushed world not with judgment, but with the ineffable love of one who sees what others overlook. Within its chest, nestled beneath spun copper ribs, hums a heart-like engine, the rhythm audible only to the most attuned: tick-tock-tick-hum, tick-tock-tick-hum, a sound both impossibly ancient and entirely newborn. 

The bird alights without ceremony upon the plateau of a smooth, flat rock. Here, it waits, patient as the Flats themselves, for the Stonezars to emerge—not as an invader, nor as a conqueror, but as a guest, ready to honor unspoken rites of belonging. Its presence is not a disruption; it knows that such places hold their own sacred ecologies of slowness, where even a ripple in a pool may be an event marking the passage of decades.

The Stonezars approach, not furtively, but deliberately, their movements as slow and mindful as the rains that have chiseled their bodies. They are hesitant—not with fear, for they know no fear—but with curiosity born of eons of introspection. The eldest Stonezar among them, one whose craggy arms house entire networks of moss and etched histories, tilts their head in acknowledgment.

The bird bends its gleaming brass beak toward the ancient one, and an unspoken current of understanding bridged the space between them, carried without word or sound, flowing in streams of pure essence. The question in the bird's many-hued eyes: "What do you seek? What do you need? What can I give?"

The Stonezar stares back, and for a moment, there is silence. Then, slowly, their posture shifts, a slight opening of arms, a gesture both ancient and vulnerable, a response only the Flats could teach—"We are as we are. We ask only to be seen."

In this, the benevolent power finds not destitution but a kind of wholeness. For the Stonezars, even in their immense solitude, feel no lack, no despair. Their lives are woven with cycles of rain and calm, of persistence and stillness. They suffer no hunger their mushrooms cannot sate, no loneliness their symbiosis with the clockwork critters cannot soften. And yet, the power perceives something intangible that even the Stonezars themselves cannot articulate: a gossamer-thin desire to share their beauty—not out of vanity, but for its own sake, as rain shares itself with the earth.

The bird clicks softly, the sound of a cogwheel finding its groove, and lifts its great gleaming wings. Then, it begins its work—not to change or fix, but to amplify the Flats’ voice, so that it may carry further into the vastness. Ripples of light unfurl from the bird’s outstretched feathers, dancing over rock and water alike, painting the Flats in a luminous twilight glow that does not impose but enhances, like drawing out the soul of the land itself. The soft fungal blooms are coaxed into subtle bioluminescent patterns, constellations of life scattered across the barren plane.

To the distant, alien eyes who might gaze upon such a sight, this would appear to be a miracle wrought by the power itself. But the Stonezars, and the bird, know otherwise. Nothing has been added that was not already there, hidden in the quiet heart of the Flats. The Flats needed no improvement, no correction. All that the benevolent power has done is act as mirror and lens, showing the Flats to the universe, and indeed to themselves, as they already are: unyielding, fluid, alive, and enduring.

The bird does not linger after the display fades to whispers of light. It leaves behind no monument, no great machine, no relic of itself. Its presence, like the gentle rains here, is absorbed into the land without ripple or residue. Yet the Stonezars know they have been seen—not in passing, not through the mistaken lens so many insist upon turning toward stoic places and things. They have felt the encompassing dignity of being recognized as radiant in their own quiet truth.

Perhaps nothing will change in the Grey Flats except this: a sense of being held, now, within the memory of the wider cosmos. Yet that, the power understands as it soars away, is more than enough.
