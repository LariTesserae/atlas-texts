They do not let you see them at first.

That is how they live here. From standing height the pan is stone, only stone — dun pavement, dust in the seams, a scatter of pebbles. Then one pebble lifts a leg a centimeter to resettle, and in a wave outward a hundred others shiver. And then you understand the ground is not ground.

They are crabs. If you need a first word.

Pale amber, dust-colored, matte as sun-baked pottery. The smallest the size of your thumbnail, the largest the size of a helmet, and every size between, so many that counting stops meaning anything. Millions. Shoulder to shoulder from rim to rim, in the open and piled two and three deep in the thin shade of the hollow boulders, tucked shoulder-in around the lip of every tinaja, wedged into burrows under slabs where the entrances have been polished by a thousand passages to a soft shine.

Their carapaces are pitted by sun and age and many of them wear a living coat — dust, lime, and map-lichen in black and orange spreading its millimeter-a-decade script right across a back. You mistook them for pebbles because they wanted you to. Stay low and pale or dry out. That is the first law, written on their bodies.

Crouch. Wait. Be quieter than your boots.

In the cool undercut of a pit rim you will find them stacked like dishes. It is not random. The big old ones on the outside, forming a scaly windbreak, and in the middle the soft ones, the just-molted, pale as damp paper, breathing slowly. You can see the breath if you are close enough: the flank plates fluttering, the gill chambers working the damp air. Every so often a dry tick of chitin on chitin as someone shifts weight, a soft scrape of claw on lime. In a pit that still holds water, an almost inaudible *plip* — a crab lowering itself down the lime slope to sit for hours, belly in the shallows, simply wetting its gills.

They move slow because slowness is water. A crab here might travel ten meters in a day, mostly at night and in the two fog hours at dawn when the cloud ocean spills over the rim and everything drips. Tick, tick in the hollows. Condensation beads on every pebble and runs down. In that hour the juveniles gather in the damp centers and drink through their legs, through tufts of setae that wick like lamp wicks. By late morning the stone is warm again and they have gone still, sealed in. By late afternoon the pits glint and the plain holds its breath until morning. You can hear your own heart. Then, if you are very still, you hear the plain answer: a thousand tiny closings.

Follow their memory and you will see it drawn on the dust. Faint polished highways, no wider than a crab, converging from all over the basin toward the deepest pits — the house-sized bathtubs that hold longest after the sudden hard rains. Each crab walks a great, decades-long circuit around the pan, following where moisture paused last year and the year before. Water here does not travel. It waits. Find where it pauses and wait with it.

They build for that waiting.

Scattered across the pavement are low rings — a foot high, three to ten feet across — circles of gravel, broken shell, cemented lime. From above they look like ruined foundations. They are dew-traps. Grain by grain, claw by claw, a crab piles rubble until the rough surface condenses fog just a little faster than flat stone, and the trickle runs inward to a damp center. That is the nursery. At dawn the thumbnail young, too small to walk to the pits, huddle there shoulder to shoulder and drink off each other’s legs.

Some rings are fresh, sharp-edged, the cement still pale. Others are ancient, collapsed, fused into bedrock, lichen-grown, with generations of molted carapaces crushed into their walls until crab and geology are the same thing. Beside them the white middens: heaps of picked claws and molts, broken down to lime to build the next ring. Nothing is wasted. Eat everything twice. Calcium is the currency here, more precious than water, because to live two thousand meters above any tide, this far from the sea that made this table when it was seafloor, you must make your own sea inside your shell and never spill it. A crab will eat its own molt within hours of shedding it, and its neighbor’s if the neighbor is slow.

Stay a day and you learn to tell them apart by age, which is to say by weight and garden.

The middle-aged are the walkers, clean-backed, amber, wary, endless trundlers of the highways.

The old ones do not quite move like crabs anymore. They have grown heavy, their joints stiff with lime, their steps a deliberate heave. You could mistake them for boulders, and lichen does. On their backs the map-lichens have thickened into a true turf, a little mobile garden. In that garden live tiny mites and springtails, grazing algae and fungus. The small mottled lizards that flick between shells know this. They do not eat the crab — what would be the point of cracking a helmet for a sip of salt water? They eat the mites, picking delicately around the eyestalks while the crab sits patient as a stone. A cleaning station that walks.

Even death is slow incorporation. An old crab that finally stops in the shade of a tafoni dome does not disappear. Wind sands it, lime films it, lichen overgrows it, juveniles pick the last calcium from its joints and carry it away in their own bodies to build the next ring. The ammonites weathered in relief on the slabs below are its distant cousins. The ripple marks frozen mid-ripple on the hillside are the memory of the water it still carries inside.

They are not alone, but everything else lives in the spaces they allow.

Grey-green cushion plants wedged so tight into cracks they look like moss-covered fists. A succulent with a swollen bottle trunk no taller than your knee, waxy to hold the morning drip — the crabs do not eat it, except fallen leaves after rain. The thorn scrub, leafless most of the year, that puts out tiny red flowers for three days after a pit fills, and for those three days the edge of the pit hums with small crabs snipping fallen petals. And above, a few high hawks, circling, infinitely patient. They are the only thing that hunts the crabs, waiting for a straggler caught between shades at noon, flipped and soft-bellied.

But mostly, quiet. Make no noise you cannot afford. No chorus. Only wind smoothing over worn stone with nothing to catch it, and underneath, the living gravel breathing.

By evening you stop seeing individuals and start seeing what they really are: not an infestation on the pavement, but the pavement itself, alive. Animal weight instead of forest. A reef lifted into the sky and taught to drink fog, to stack like dishes, to build circles to catch cloud, to carry the sea inland in ten million small, amber, fiercely guarded cups — and to hold it, breath by breath, until morning.
