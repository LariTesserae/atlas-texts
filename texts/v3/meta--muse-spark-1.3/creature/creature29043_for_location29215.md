What lives here lives at a burn.

Not spread thin and not settled in. It clots. In a valley eight kilometers long and almost all raw stone and standing water, life pulls itself into the few places where summer has softened the till enough to hold a root — the peat skins, the sedge tussocks between the kettle ponds, the drier moraine spines — and there it crowds shoulder to shoulder and burns hot to stay alive in air that has almost nothing in it.

Start with the bulk, because you hear them and smell them before you see them clearly in the flat light.

Wild yaks. Three hundred head, dark as the wet rock when they are wet, which is always. Bulls almost black, cows a little smaller, calves the color of scorched tea. The pale saddle — that dusty grey-white mantle over shoulders and back — is winter hair already coming in long. Their barrels are huge because they have to be: great fermenting vats slung under a ribcage that houses lungs twice what a lowland cow would need, hearts like furnace pumps. Their horns are long, upswept, polished yellow at the tips from hooking frozen sedge. Their muzzles are broad, fringed with ice from their own breath.

They do not graze so much as demolish. Head down, they rip. You hear it even at distance: a hundred mouths tearing sedge and cotton-grass at once, a wet shredding, and under that the steady piston of hooves in peat — thud-thud, thud-thud — as the whole mass shifts forward a meter, stops, tears again. The cows keep late calves tight against their flanks. The calves are leggier than they should be for September, still gangly, still bawling in high, thin wails that cut through the thin air and are answered instantly by a low grunt. That grief-panic is constant work. A calf that drifts three meters too far toward the marsh edge is already being looked at.

In and around their legs, as if they were moving boulders that had learned to walk, flick the gazelles. Tibetan gazelle, pale fawn above and white below, rump patch flashing when they prick and run a half-circle and stop. They are a tenth the weight of a yak and live on nervousness. They pick what the yaks leave — the short forbs between tussocks, the tips of dwarf willow — with quick, dainty bites, ears swiveling for the whistle that means wolf. They do not belong to the herd, they orbit it, using its mass and its stink and its noise as cover.

On the water, more weight than water seems able to hold.

Bar-headed geese by the thousands. Slate grey bodies, white heads barred twice in black, pink legs tucked under them on the ponds, or paddling in loose rafts that break the new window-ice with their breasts. When they go up they go up together, and the valley, for a minute, has an engine. The roar of three thousand wings beating thin air to find lift — labored, loud, desperate — as they climb for the rim and then drop back, circling, calling in high, barking honks that do not carry. They are staging. They have flown over Everest to get here and they will fly over it to leave. Right now their blood is the most efficient blood in the valley, hemoglobin that grabs oxygen where there is almost none, and you can see the cost in how hard they work for every meter.

Apart from them, always apart, the black-necked cranes. Not flocks. Pairs. Tall, slate-bodied, black neck and head with a bare red crown, standing knee-deep on the peat islands. They probe the quaking mat with long bills for roots and snails and frogs that should not be able to live this high and do. Even now, cold-stiff, a pair will suddenly remember spring and dance — two or three bows, wings half-open, a hop, a tossed sedge head — then stop as if embarrassed by the cold and go back to feeding. Their calls are brassy, ancient.

Below and between them: ruddy shelduck the color of rust, upright and quarrelsome, Eurasian wigeon whistling from the pond edges, and coots — black, white-billed — that pack the open water in black sheets until something, a wolf shadow, an eagle shadow, spooks them and they run across the surface clattering, feet slapping, wings hammering, and settle again.

On the dry ground, the small engineers.

Himalayan marmots live on the moraine ridges where the permafrost dips just enough to dig. Fat now, rolling fat, the size of badgers, tawny-grey, they sit up on boulders like foremen and whistle — one sharp, falling note that stops the whole ridge. Then dive, then reappear. Their burrows are deep enough to escape the freeze, lined with the same bleached sedge the yaks eat. Inside, a huddle of bodies is already slowing down, heart rates dropping toward winter torpor. Outside, for a few more weeks, they still gorge, waddling between tussocks, clipping mouthfuls and stuffing them in with both paws.

Under the boulders, where the marmots cannot go, the plateau pikas. Mouse-sized, round-eared, dun-colored, absurdly busy. Each one owns a crack between stones and in front of that crack a haypile — tiny, precise stacks of sedge and willow twigs laid to cure in the cold sun that never quite arrives. You hear them before you see them: *eep*, dry as two stones clicked, from everywhere. They dart, freeze, dart, a mouthful of green in their teeth three times their head size, vanish. They will not hibernate. They will live all winter under snow on these piles, in tunnels steaming with their own breath, fermenting hay in their guts like the yaks do, only smaller and faster.

Holding the edge of all this, two grey wolves. Tibetan wolves, paler and woollier than forest wolves, yellow-eyed. They do not run. They loiter at the marsh edge at what the yaks have decided is a respectful killing distance — two hundred meters — and lie down with heads on paws and watch. Shift workers. They are testing calves with their eyes: which one lags, which cow is thin, which breath rattles. They will not charge a tight island of three hundred horns. They will wait for dark that is only dimmer, for a calf to bed down outside the circle, for the cold to do half their work. When one lifts its head and scents, you see the whole herd ripple without moving.

Above them all, waiting for what the wolves leave and what the cold takes, the lammergeier. Bearded vulture, three meters wingtip to wingtip, stiff-winged, it circles the rim without a single beat for an hour, black against steel-blue. It does not want meat. It wants afterbirth, dropped calves that did not make it, bones. It will take a yak femur, carry it high, drop it on the black rock to crack it, and eat the marrow. Its stomach acid is strong enough to dissolve bone. It is the cleanup crew for a factory that has no floor.

And under everything, holding it up, the ones you step on without seeing:

Dwarf willow, no higher than your ankle, but lift a stem and count the rings: twenty, thirty years to grow a fist. Moss in olive cushions that come apart like fresh bread when you touch them, already steaming faintly as they photosynthesize in cold. Lichens — grey-green, perfect coin circles on the north sides of boulders, first generation, no overlap yet, just starting to etch rock into something that could one day be soil. Hard sedge with last summer's dead blades still standing like wire. Glasswort reddening in the hollows. Cotton-grass nodding white in the mist.

None of it is old. No old bones whitened here. Bones are still bloody or still inside animals. No old peat, no deep soil. Just the first thin, tough carpet laid over geology that melted out twenty years ago, and on that carpet, bodies — furnace-hearted, big-lunged, shaggy, feathered, furred — tearing, chewing, whistling, honking, dancing, watching, circling, turning over and over, until the whole empty bowl hums with work.
