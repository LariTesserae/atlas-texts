They don't look like animals at first.

Put your face to the crack and all you see is sweat. Black glass beaded with water, yellow slime at the bottom, white steam that bends the light. Then the sweat moves sideways. Then a bead of water grows legs.

That is how they come to you — not as forms, but as a density. As texture that rearranges itself.

The base of them isn't animal at all.

It coats everything below the splash line: a skin, milky-yellow and rust-red, iridescent where it's thick, translucent where it's stretched thin over a needle of basalt. It smells faintly of boiled eggs and old pennies. It is not moss. It never learned green. It drinks heat and sulfur and iron and exhales food. If you watch long enough you can see it pulse when a fresh seep surges up from below — a slow tightening, like a sleeper breathing.

Everything else grazes on that.

**The snow.**

The air is full, though you would swear at first it is only steam. Thousands of them, so pale they disappear if you look straight at them. Midges, but wrong. Most have no wings at all, or only feathery stubs too small to fly with, and yet they do not fall. They ride. They let the heat column take them up, legs spread, bodies shivering, then they tip and drift down again to drink from the wall. Snow that never lands.

They make no hum. You keep waiting for it. A whine should be there and isn't. When two drift into each other — and in that press they constantly do — they sometimes pass through each other slightly, a shoulder overlapping a shoulder like smoke, and both go on. You tell yourself it is steam in your eyes.

They land to drink, and that is when they are eaten.

**The tide.**

The walls are not white with condensation. They are white with crickets.

Blind, milk-white, eyeless lumps with long antennae always working, always tasting ahead. The smallest are rice-grains, just hatched and almost clear enough to see the slime glowing greenish in their guts. The largest is your thumbnail, opaque, scarred, missing legs and still climbing. They pack shoulder to shoulder, heads pointed down toward the pan, mouths to the weeping rock.

They do not hop. There is nowhere to hop to. They flow. A slow, continuous jostle uphill and down, climbing over each other's backs, pausing to lap bacterial slick and mineral water off the glass. When one finds a drowned fly — one of the wind-thrown strays from miles below, a housefly huge as a god here, sodden on the wall — it stops and the tide eddies around it.

Because the larger ones are armed.

At first you think it is a glint. A speck of light in its jaws that isn't water. Then you see it move with intention.

A flake of obsidian, no longer than an eyelash, knapped to a curve. You can see the pale conchoidal pits on the wall behind them where they have bitten the flakes free with their own mandibles. They hold them in forelimbs or gripped sideways in the mouth, and they work.

Sawing at a fly's wing joint until the wing comes free. Scraping slime off a ridge too sharp to put your tongue to. Prising a midge out of a pit of silica foam. It is clumsy and precise at once; the whole body leans into the cut. They carry their knife for life. There are no nests, no caches, no piles. The rock is too hot and wet and sharp to let anything sit. So they carry. And when one kills another over meat — which happens constantly, silently, a grapple that lasts three seconds — the winner walks off awkwardly holding two flakes, one in each side of its mouth, meat dragging behind.

Drop it, die, and your knife becomes floor. The bottom is paved with lost knives.

Try to count them and you fail. Twelve on that patch, thirteen — blink the steam out — nine. One is chewing with a flake you are certain, a second ago, was in another's mouth. You follow one with a chipped left antenna and lose it in a knot of identical backs and find it, or another identical, three inches higher, facing the wrong way.

**The pan.**

At the pinch at the bottom, palm-deep, milky and almost too hot to keep a finger in, lives the red.

Copper-red amphipods, soft as boiled shrimp, hundreds layered belly-to-back so thick the water looks solid. All eyes. If you look down, hundreds of black pinpricks look up. They graze, they mate, they cannibalize without pausing between actions — a flick of the tail, a coil, a bite. They surge when a midge falls in or a cricket slips off the wall above. Half a dozen will lunge half out of water at once to drag the cricket down, and the cricket's neighbors will immediately lean down to drink from the ripple, antennae flicking, unafraid.

**The rim-walker.**

She — it — owns the edge between wall and water.

Long as your finger, blind and white and almost completely translucent, a centipede made of steam and gristle. You can see the dark line of its gut, usually full, usually red at the front and yellow at the back, telling you what it ate last and what it is turning it into. It never stops. It works the sulfur rim endlessly in both directions, dozens of legs moving without sound on wet glass, head sweeping. It takes crickets. It takes amphipods that venture too high. It takes midges by rearing up into the snow-fall and letting them stick to its wet sides.

It is the only thing here that looks like it knows it is hunting. Everything else looks like it is only eating until it is eaten, which is also true for the centipede, only slower. When it dies — wedged hot and twitching in a crack too narrow for its length — the tide covers it in minutes and scrapes it clean, flakes flashing: *click, click, click*. Glass on glass. The faintest tool-sound under the tick of water.

That is their sound. They have no song. The crickets, so like field crickets below, have left chirp behind entirely. The midges beat their stubs and make no buzz. For all that press and slaughter the pocket holds its breath. Only water detaching, steam sighing out through the hand-wide crack into cold wind, and underneath, intermittently, that tiny knap and scrape of their house being broken into knives.

Together they are more than the sum. The snow feeds the tide. The tide knocks food into the pan. The pan throws food back up to the tide. The rim-walker prunes all of them. The bacterial skin eats their droppings, their shed skins, their dead, and the mountain's heat, and makes more skin. And every day the updrafts tithe more: moths, gnats, stray flies blundered in through the crack to drown and be sawn apart. Windfall from a green world they will never see.

Packed shoulder to shoulder in a bowl you could step over, near the top of the sky where nothing should live, with no green, no soil, no building, only carried edges of their own sharp house — sweating, scraping, dripping, endlessly replacing each other so fast you cannot count them before they are someone else.
