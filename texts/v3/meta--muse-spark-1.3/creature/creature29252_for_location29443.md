At first you think no one could live here on purpose.

That is your lowland mistake. You come up expecting the kind of people an extraction town makes — hard, loud, temporary. Men with burnt forearms and a elsewhere stare, counting shifts until they can ride the rail-crawl back down. You look for that and you don't find it.

What lives here has stayed.

You notice it first in the way they move. No one hurries, and it isn't laziness. In air this thin and hot, hurry is a tax you can't pay. They walk slow on the stair-streets, one hand trailing — not touching — the warm handrail. Shoulder turned sideways when the tram creeps past on its trestle. They breathe audibly, a soft in-through-the-nose sip, out through pursed lips, like tasting soup that is too hot. Old chests are deep as barrels. Young ones learn early to stop and stand still when they get winded, without shame, palm on a cool pipe-belly, waiting for the dizziness to pass.

They speak low. You thought at first it was reverence. It's physics. Shouting in this air just shreds and goes nowhere, and leaves you gasping. So they've grown a town of low voices and hands. On the factory terraces, where rows of ingots go from cherry to black over days, the crews talk in nods and two-word calls and open-palm gestures. A flick up means *lift*. A flat hand held low means *wait, it's breathing*. When they do speak to you, they lean in a little closer than lowland manners allow, eyes narrowed to slits against the white noon, and you smell on them what the wind smells of: hot iron, machine oil, ash, faint sulfur, and underneath it the clean tin smell of harvested dew.

They are sun-marked in a way you learn to read. Neck burned bronze-black behind, face pale where the wide, low-brimmed hats shadow it. Feet wide and callused even through thick wooden soles, because the ground itself is warm and you feel it. Hands — everyone's hands — are roped and nicked from basalt, fingertips permanently grey with fines. Women tie thin undyed canvas over nose and mouth when the ash lifts, men pull the same cloth up. Children go bare to the waist until the white hours, then vanish.

Because the children are the first true natives. You see them at dawn.

The town wakes an hour before the sun burns through, when the peak snags the cloud and everything sweats. That's their hour. Barefoot gangs with squeegees of split rubber, tin funnels, jars slung on cord, herding beads of water down the sun-sails and into gutters that chatter like rain. They know every drip-line on their street the way lowland children know shortcuts. Whose roof feeds whose jar. Where the canvas sags best. They hang the overnight drip-bags under the eaves and unplug them with a practiced pinch, without spilling a drop — because to spill is the worst rudeness here, worse than shouting. You learn to stand back and wait to be offered from a household jar, with both hands.

Later, when the stone has steamed dry by nine, those same children press handprints into the warm tar-plastic curb where the street crew is laying, laughing when it gives. And they will bring you back a month later, proudly, to show you how their print has lifted an inch higher. The hill grows under them. They grow with it. They don't find it strange that surveyors re-chalk marks every spring and find them wrong. To them the ground moving is normal. A floor that stays level would be suspicious.

The adults have made a whole trade out of that motion. There are jack-men, not quite masons, not quite engineers, who go house to house with basalt shims and great threaded jackscrews, re-leveling when doors jam. There are pipe-fitters who will not build straight — everything gets a loop, a belly, a bellows to take up the swell. There are quarrymen who work the still-warm fissures, prying up slabs where the heat shimmers, in short shifts with long rests, faces wrapped wet. There are kiln crews, electrolysis tenders behind their spark screens, who watch dials that barely move. And there are the engine-tenders, the slowest of all, who sit in the shade of machine halls where flywheels the size of houses turn so slowly you have to watch the painted spoke for a full minute to be certain. They oil, they listen. They put an ear to a standpipe in the evening to hear tomorrow's water condensing, drop by patient drop, inside the cooling coil. That is skilled work here: listening.

They don't farm — there is nothing to farm in. No soil, no green, only sulfur bloom and copper salts that look like moss until you touch them and find them dry and crystalline. Everything edible rides up the rail-crawl: dried beans, salt fish, lowland meal, oil in drums. Everything metal rides down: those dark ingots cooling in rows. In between, the town lives off gradient — the difference between upwelling rock below and thin sky above. The pistons sigh once an hour. Chains inch ore buckets upward. Braided copper lines hum after a surge and the air smells of ozone. The machines are not pets, not gods, but they are cohabitants. People pat a warm housing as they pass the way you'd pat a cow's flank. They know which bearing groans in your molars, which expansion joint clicks.

The old ones are the record. They sit out the white hours in the deep shade under sails, when handrails will brand you if you grab them, and they do nothing — which is the rule: *Rest in the white hours. Work early, work late.* They watch the noon mirage when the heat lenses the air over the caldera and a kiln row half a mile off detaches and floats, rippling a hand's breadth above its foundations. You think it's a trick. They say the mountain is dreaming its future shape and showing it. They navigate by real shadows, not doubles. On the way to the kilns they touch the leaning doorway in the Upper Tier plaza — set plumb twenty years ago, now six degrees north, kept as a clock — for luck. Not prayer, exactly. Acknowledgment. *The mountain is working slower than you, but it is stronger.*

And alongside them, thinly, the other residents.

The pale, almost translucent lizard under the cooling sheds, belly cool on ceramic, tongue flicking condensate off a pipe. No one bothers it. It's considered good for the coils, bad to name. The swifts very high, circling the thermal for hours without a flap, crying once, thinly, before they drop away into the haze. And every few months, the dog — or the story of a dog — gone feral in the ash fields, living on scraps and drip. No one has caught it. Children leave jar lids of water out past the last terrace just in case. Whether it exists hardly matters. The gesture tells you what kind of people leave water for something that may not exist.

By evening, when the light finally slants and ambers the black glass to bronze, they come back out. Shutters lifted. Low calls across courtyards: *drip-line clear?* Because your neighbor's jar depends on your roof. Women uncap cistern cellars cut into older, cooler flow. Men bleed steam traps. The factories exhale their stored heat in long shimmers. You sit with a household that has decided you can be trusted with water, and they hand you the jar — heavy, cool, white-painted to hold the morning — and you drink carefully, both hands, not spilling, while the basalt around you pings as it contracts, and dripwater begins to patter into tin, and the whole mineral town ticks itself toward dawn.
