Nothing lives here, at first.

That is what the place tells you, loudly, with all its dripping. Green and wet and empty, air between things by agreement. A brown bird going over without stopping. A throat pulsing in a pool if you are still enough to earn it. You think: plants, and a few frogs that got lost on the way up and stayed.

Stay a little longer. Get wetter. The wet finds you again every time you think you have shaken it.

Then you start to see that the emptiness has a shape.

Each of the great rosettes sits in the middle of its own nothing. Not the nothing of chance. A swept circle, a hand-high island of peat heaped over rock, and nothing rooting in it except itself. No seedlings crowding, no grass stealing. In a place this soaked, where a fallen leaf stays legible for months before it becomes mat, where moss will beard anything that does not move — there should be a scrabble. There isn't. Twelve, maybe fifteen great crowns, each with its cleared court around it, spaced the way animals space themselves when they can hear each other breathing.

Crouch by one. Its leaves are as long as your arm for a reason. They are thick as wrist, waxy on top so water sheets off, felted underneath so water gathers. Veined like the back of a hand, and like a hand they have work in them. The green is too saturated to be only leaf-green; in the folds where the light doesn't quite reach it goes blue-black, bruise-colored. In the axils ferns have made their small green fires. Underneath, orchids hang upside down, white roots in air. Moss beards the outer rims. Only the inner cup is clean.

Because the cup is mouth.

Every rosette cups upward and every cup is full to trembling, tea and sky. You thought rain. But it hasn't rained. The rock is sweating without rain. The underside of every leaf is beaded without rain. The air itself is on the edge of condensing. The cup is full because it keeps itself full. It drinks the sweat. Put the back of your hand near it — not in, near — and you feel the cold coming off it in a slow sheet, in this warm close air. That is the first wrongness you can put a hand to: the whole saddle is warm as breath, your shirt stuck to your back, and the water is cold as a high spring.

They are cold inside. Big-bodied and cold, though the sun falls straight down on them all day. They pump up the deep water, the mountain's slow breathing that lifts and lowers the middle pool week by week and leaves those pale silica rings like bathtub marks. Cold water up through a peat-heaped caudex thick as a thigh sunk into old rock, up through the swollen bases you mistook for leaf-stems, and out to the skin. The leaf-flesh sweats it. On warm skin each drop lands with that disproportionate shock — as if it had fallen much farther, from much higher. It has. It has come up from a long way down.

So the thousand drips are not all accident. Plink rock to pool, thock leaf-tip to leaf below, glug under the mat where water moves where you can't see it, the sudden slap when one, overfull, tips — slowly, so slowly you don't see the movement until it has happened — and empties a cupful all at once with a shudder that runs out to the leaf tips. They are sweating and tipping and sipping all the time, maintaining. The water leaving by seep equals the water arriving by sweat. They do not spread and do not retreat. They hold.

And what holds light? Watch a bead on the underside at noon when the high veil thins. White-gold straight down. The bead catches it and keeps it. The sun goes behind and for a full second the bead is still lit, a tiny lingering lamp under the leaf. Every leaf has a hundred of them. At night — you won't stay the night, but you can guess — the saddle must glimmer faintly with all that kept sun, enough to turn a moth, enough to turn the one brown bird that crosses hourly in a straight unwilling line, as if it knows not to land. The swifts stay high, screaming thin screams that do not echo. They know too.

Nothing here has been alive long enough to be stupid. Leave a stick. There are straight sticks everywhere, shed petioles light and strong, loseable stones that fit the hand too well — gastroliths they work around in their cups to grind drowned leaf and the rare drowned fly, then eject onto the peat. A whole tool kit lying about with no user. Someone once sat by the middle pool and struck a pale chert flake twice to make an edge and cut something. You can find it half-sunk, edge still sharp under green film. What did they cut? Probably a cup-rim to drink. The wound is long sealed with silica, a pale ring. The moss is closing over the flake. The place has no use for tools.

What uses the frogs?

One frog per pool. Widespread, patient, the color of peat. If you crouch very still you see only the faint pulse of the throat, and then the eye, which is also the color of peat until it catches you. They do not hop. They do not call. They sit in the tea-water with their chins out and pulse.

They belong to the rosettes. Each crown keeps one, the way you keep a tongue. The frog eats what would foul the cup — the wrigglers, the film — and the cup feeds the frog with what it cannot digest, and at night the frog carries the kept light in its skin from one pool to another, a slow ferry. Put your ear to the peat near a pool and under all the wet percussion there is a low tone, more in the sternum than in the ear. It stops when you hold your breath. You think it is your own blood until another person with you nods — they feel it too, and it stopped for them when you stopped. They are listening. The mat hears your weight sigh water up around your boot. The water under the mat carries your step to every island. When you hold your breath they hold theirs, so as not to miss you.

That is why you make noise no matter how careful you are, and why the place makes more noise than you. The drips fill the little room and bounce off the ribs until a drip behind you sounds like it is in front of you, and you turn for animals that aren't there. You are the animal that is there, briefly, turning. They cover you. It is kind, or it is hunting manners so old they look like kindness.

The crooked trees near the ribs are the same thing, older. No taller than two men, trunks swollen with stored water, bark peeling in wet paper strips, crowns flattened by wind into umbrellas. A rosette that lived a century and lifted itself on wood to get its cup out of the splash zone. Same cold sweat, same cleared court, same frog somewhere below in its shade. Everything that lives here looks well-watered to the point of bursting, and alone. They are alone the way deep-sea things are alone — complete.

You will not get dry. The rock, the leaf, the air will find a way to wet you again, dab you with cold to taste your salt.

You will not get lost. Black rib, blue drop, green crown — a room you can cross in forty paces, and every crown watching the crossing in its own slow way.

You will not see change, only maintenance. A bead you swear has not grown for a full minute, then blink and it is gone, fallen. A leaf that does not rot but bleaches and flattens into paper and becomes part of the island. A level that breathes over weeks.

When you climb down out of the warm wet breath back into ordinary thin mountain air, your boots will be heavy for a hundred steps with cup-water and peat-water, and then light again, as if something let go. Behind you the dripping continues, loud, patient, unheard by anyone, keeping the little saturated room exactly itself.

And in the cups, cold and full and lit from within for a second after the sun, the sitters sit on, sweating winter in summer, holding their circles empty around them so nothing has to fight, pulsing their frog-throats out in the pools, humming low in your chest as long as you breathe with them — vegetable, mineral, aqueous, and attentively, undeniably animal.
