At first you think nothing can live here.

Not really. Not year-round. Something visits, something passes through, but nothing *stays* — that is the first lie the bowl tells you. The light is too hard, the air too thin and too loud, the ground too miserly. You kneel by a cushion and see nothing move for a full minute and you think: sterile.

Then a pebble walks.

That is how they come to you — not all at once, but as motion resolving out of stillness. The longer you look, the more the rock is alive, until you realize you are sitting in the middle of a city that only pretends to be stone.

Start low, because everything here starts low. Below the birds.

In every wind-hole, in every fist-deep pit pocked into the tors, in the lee of every frost-shattered slab, there are mouths waiting for the sky to feed them.

The wolf spiders are first. Big as your thumbnail, long-legged and soot-black to drink the sun, their eyes like four wet pinheads catching white light. They do not make webs to catch. They make trip-lines — single silks strung low across the corridors between cushion domes, anchored to shale. When the wind drops its stunned fallout, the midges and aphids and ballooning spiderlings from forests three thousand feet below stumble and hop, and touch a line, and the black shape is already there. If you put your ear down close, under the roar, you can hear them: a dry tick-tick-tick of tarsi on shale, too fast to follow. The females carry their young on their backs through autumn, a seething gray mantle of spiderlings clinging to the mother while she hunts. In winter she will go torpid under a slab and they will eat the unviable eggs off her own abdomen and then each other until spring thins them to the strongest three.

Beside them, slower and armored: the black alpine beetles. Flightless. Their wing cases have fused into a single polished shield — they gave up the sky millions of years ago because the wind here punishes anything that flies without knowing how. They walk the polygons at midday when the sun-warmed stone gives them an inch of heat, bulldozing pollen and dead flies with their heads, storing fat that will last them nine months under ice. Crack one open — you won't — and it smells of resin and iron. They live seven, eight years. Some of the big males under the nesting wall are older than any bird in the flock.

And the midges that belong here, not the ones that fall: tiny, wingless, slate-gray hoppers that live their whole lives inside a single cushion dome, leaping flea-like from flower to flower, drinking dew off the hairs of leaves. That dome is their continent.

None of them eat what grows here. There isn’t enough green to eat. They eat the weather. They eat what the western notch vomits up every day — that endless glittering snow of cold-stunned insects — and in turn they are eaten.

Because above them, the air belongs to the screamers.

They are the ones you hear before you understand them. Thousands. In this emptiness the number feels obscene.

Mostly choughs. Red-billed and yellow-billed, black from tip to tail except for those beaks like cuts of color — coral-red, butter-yellow — and legs to match. They are not large birds, but here they seem large because there is nothing else at scale. They live shoulder to shoulder on the dark teeth, on cracks no wider than your hand, thousands of bodies packed in guano-white ledges, muttering and preening at dusk in a low chatter that somehow survives the wind.

At dawn they leave. You have to see it once to stop thinking of them as individuals. The whole mass pours off the east wall and funnels down toward the notch and then, caught by the morning updraft, wheels and returns — the same funnel, the same numbers, every day. In the air they almost never flap. They *ride*. Wings half-folded, tails fanned and twisting, they surf the hammering river of air that funnels through the notch, tumbling, rolling on their backs, falling a hundred feet to snatch a moth in midair and letting the wind throw them back up. Young birds play in pairs, locking feet and cartwheeling down until the ground rushes up and they let go at the last second. Their cry — *chee-ow, chee-ow* — is harsh enough to cut straight through white noise, and the amphitheater throws it back until it comes from everywhere.

They are not just eating machines. Watch long enough, kneeling by a tarn with the sun hammering your neck, and you will see the learning.

A young red-bill lands by a wind-hole, cocks its head. It picks up a stiff dry grass stem in that curved red beak, probes it into the crevice, rattles it. Nothing. It drops the stem. Picks up another, shorter. Probes again, deeper, twists. A spider bolts and the bird is on it. The older birds do it faster, neater, with favorite tools they carry for an hour before discarding. Stripped willow twigs sharpened by nibbling at one end. A shard of black obsidian — chipped, not broken, an edge that does not occur naturally among all those identical natural shards — used to lever a beetle from under shale. You find these things at the tarn edges and along the sedge strip and you cannot say if a hand left them last season or a beak left them this morning. That is the point. In the bowl, tool-use is not human. It is just what the clever hungry do when they have no hands to spare and fingers would freeze anyway.

With them, smaller and more nervous: the snowfinches and alpine accentors. Clouds of brown-streaked, white-winged bodies that settle onto the cushion domes in a single breath — fifty birds on fifty domes — stabbing at trapped fallout, at seeds, at the wingless hoppers, then lifting all at once when a shadow passes, wings making that dry rustling like surf. Ten thousand wings at midday make a pressure you feel in your sternum. They drink from the pans without disturbing them, a quick dip that takes no measurable water. They nest lower, in holes in the tors, lining them with beard lichen and chough feathers.

And above all of them, so high it only becomes visible when you stop looking for it: the turners.

One pair. Maybe the same pair that has always been here. Lammergeiers, bearded vultures, ten feet across, rust-stained chests from bathing in iron seeps below. They do not flap. Ever, it seems. They turn slow circles inside the cirque for hours, riding the lenticular's rotor without effort, heads swiveling down. They are the undertakers and the memory of the place. When a chough dies — and with thousands, one dies most days — they take it. When a sheep or a goat dies on the slopes below and its bones are picked clean, they carry the long bones and tortoise-shells of turtles up here, high, and drop them again and again onto the bone-anvils, the frost-shattered slabs, until they crack for marrow. You can find the anvils by the splinters, white on white quartz. The birds are twenty, thirty years old. They know every gust in the notch by name.

That is who lives here. Not visitors. Residents.

They do not build. There is nothing to build with that the wind would not take. They do not store. There is no need when the sky snows food every day and the pans neither fill nor empty. They huddle, they ride, they probe, they wait.

At evening, when the light thins blue but does not leave and the insect snow slackens, they settle back onto the teeth in that great chattering mass, spiders reeling in their lines, beetles wedging under stone, finches tucked into lichen-lined holes, choughs shoulder to shoulder with beaks tucked into back feathers, the old lammergeiers folding down onto a favorite tooth like closing a book.

Under the wind-roar, if you have stayed still long enough for your ears to learn how to hear inside deafness, you can hear the bowl breathing with them: tick of feet, plink of condensation into black mirror water, thin whine, blood in your own ears — all stitched together, all holding.

Nothing accumulates. Nothing leaves. They found the point long ago and have no intention of moving off it.
