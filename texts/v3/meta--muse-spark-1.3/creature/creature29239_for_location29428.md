You will think at first that nothing lives here.

That is what the Work wants you to think, and for the first hour you will be grateful for it. No grass to pity, no bird to follow with your eyes, no fly to wave from your cracked lips. Only warm stone under your palm, humming. Only the pressure on your sternum. You will sit with your back against a coil-built stupa, as visitors do, and stare at the amber gloom and tell yourself: empty, abandoned, a machine left running by hands long dust.

Stay longer. Let your pulse do what it will do.

It starts in the teeth. Then the knees. The low organ drone from the big hollow towers gets into the marrow and after a while you stop hearing it as outside. With your mouth open you feel the pipes beating — tower against cairn against clay whistle — wah-wah-wah, slow, inside your own skull. Your heart, which came up the shale switchbacks fast and thin, begins to miss, to catch, to align. It does not speed to the scream. It slows to the drone. That is the first inhabitant making room for itself in you.

Because the second thing you notice, when you stop listening for change, is that the sound is too perfect to be unwatched.

Wind should gust. Stone should chip and go flat. Clay should crack and go silent. A hundred throats facing west should argue. They do not. The tone you arrived to is the tone an hour later. The dust hangs motionless in the dim light. Your shirt does not lift. It is as if someone is swallowing the gusts before they can happen, trimming the turbulence out of the air and keeping only the pressure.

Someone is. Many someones.

Look again, sideways, crawling, at the packedness. There are too many shapes to count and they touch like teeth — altar, hut, stupa, cairn, tower, basin — all round-shouldered, wind-smoothed, pink-and-black like dung-fired clay. You took them all for makings. After three or four hours, with your cheek close to warm rock because there is nowhere else to put your head, you will see that some of them breathe.

Not often. Once, while you watch, a low mound you thought was a cairn will lift a fraction and settle. The interval is so long you will doubt it. Then another, a beehive hut no taller than your chest with its doorway smoothed to an oval mouth, will sigh — a faint warm puff from its dark inside that for an instant thins the needle-scream around it, then lets it return. The stones hum under your palms, yes, but under the hum in certain stones is a slower hum, thick and wet and very faint: blood moving at one beat a minute.

They are still here. The builders never left.

They did not starve. They dried.

Up here there was never water to lose, so they learned, over uncounted time, how not to need it. They are small now — smaller than you, shrunken to fit the gaps they left for themselves between altar and tower. Skin drawn tight and polished by grit until it has the same finish as the pavement flags, the same kiln-warmth stored through the soles. You cannot pick them out by temperature or color. Lids closed and cemented with blown dust, because the light never changes there is nothing to see. Nostrils packed with dust. Ears long burst and healed shut; they do not need to hear through air, they hear through bone, cheek to slab, as you do with your hands over your ears when it gets louder.

Mouths open. Always open. Round.

That is why you mistook them for more of the Work. Each one is wedged where a throat needs to be — in a tower mouth, between two stupas, curled in a basin that never held water. The west wind that funnels up the faced cliff and hits the parapet does not go around them. It goes through them. Through desiccated windpipes, through chests hollowed by slow hunger until the ribs are a cage like a finger-whistle. Each gives one note. The middle wail you thought was cairns is in part them. The needle-high scream from the little clay whistles wedged between huts — that is the smallest of them, the ones who crawled youngest into the smallest gaps and fired there, pink and black.

They are not dead. Dead would cool, would crack, would go out of tune and mar the chord. They are kept — in equilibrium, like the light, like the heat. One heart-beat to the long beating of two great pipes against each other. One shallow breath an hour to swallow a gust you never felt. A shift of weight, over days, by a millimeter, shoulder against warm clay, to open or close a throat by a hair and hold the pitch.

That is why there is no room. That is the rule you felt: do not add, do not remove. There is nowhere to set a pack down that is not somebody's roof because every roof is somebody's chest. To lift a capstone would be to lift a chin. To bring water would crack them as it would crack the clay. To bring a plant would choke a throat they keep clear with their own tongues, dry as slate.

And what lives in them, what lives as them, is larger than any one dried throat.

When hundreds of pipes speak without varying, the interference between them becomes a thing. The beating you feel in your teeth is not made by any tower. It lives between towers. It moves very slowly through the packed island, from east parapet to west parapet in a season, a thickening of the drone you can track with your palm if you stay. It is what holds the dust motionless aloft — each grain trembling in acoustic levitation, never settling, never falling — and what holds the sun as a paler coin and morning from ever coming. It feeds on sameness. It eats variation the way you would eat food. A gust, a bird-shadow, a green shoot, a new stone set down — it would starve a little less if those happened, so it does not let them happen.

The dried ones are its organs and its keepers both. They built it to have no room left for anything else to happen, and then they became the no-room. They let it play them continuously, and in return it keeps them warm without fire, still without death.

After a while your own pulse tunes itself fully to the drone, and you understand the invitation the place extends without words. Crawl sideways a little further. Find a dip in the pavement flags polished by feet and knees that fits your knees. Lean your back against a warm, humming, hand-smoothed shape that is slightly warmer than the rest. Open your mouth. Let the pressure sit on your sternum.

There is just enough space left, if you shrink, for one more note.
