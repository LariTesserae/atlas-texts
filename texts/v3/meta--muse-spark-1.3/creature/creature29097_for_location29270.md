At first you don't see them as living at all.

They are the texture of the place. The roughness on the rock that should not be roughness, the thickening on every ledge. You put your hand out to steady yourself on a tilted plate and the plate breathes under your palm. That is the first correction.

Thousands of them. The whole bowl is lined.

Up close they will not resolve into anything you have a clean word for. If you say bird, you miss the hands, you miss the silence of their bodies. If you say bat, you miss the round feathered faces, the forward-set eyes that catch starlight and throw it back like wet seeds, the down. They are small — you could hold two cupped in your hands if they would ever let you — and built entirely for this thin, sour inch of air: barrel chests too big for their bodies, working slow as bellows, soot-grey down dense as felt over back and wings, belly fur paler, almost white where it has been licked clean by condensation. Long hind legs, thin but strong, made not for walking but for clinging, toes splayed to grip knife-edges of fresh shale. And the forelimbs — that is where the category breaks. A wing that is also an arm. Membrane stretched between elongated fingers, feathered on the leading edge, but the outermost two digits left free, clawed, opposable enough to pinch. Folded, they look like hunched old men in cloaks. Opened halfway, in that ripple when a hundred beat at once without leaving, you see how much hand is still in them.

They live pressed. That is not a metaphor. It is the physiology.

Chest to back, wing over wing, two and three deep in every cup between stones, poured like slow liquid into cracks wide enough for a fist. Near-touching is not close enough here. Touching is the rule. Alone, one of them would die in an hour — too small, heat leaking out into black distance, breath freezing. Together they make a second climate. Put your hand to the back wall and it is unnaturally warm where before the stone should have burned with cold. They have heated the rock with their own bodies. Warm air lifts an inch off that living felt, hits the cold plates above, gives up its water. The stone sweats. That faint tick-plink-tick you heard in the cups — that is them, farming themselves.

Watch their faces. That is the strangest thing, once your eyes stay dark long enough to see it.

They are all facing inward. Thousands of small round faces turned toward the center of the bowl, toward the seep and the palm-sized pools. Even the ones on the outer rim, with the drop and the wind at their backs, have twisted around to face in. You think torpor at first, because they move so slowly. A head turns over the course of a full minute. A wing unfolds like a glacier calving. But it isn't sleep. It is conservation made into attention. Need-need-need — the contact calls never stop, dry clicks bouncing off the close fresh walls and coming back multiplied until you can't tell who is asking and who is answering. Left, right, above, you are inside a net of sound. They are echolocating the bowl itself at low level, keeping the map alive in the dark.

Every few minutes a ripple: a hundred half-open wings beating that thin air into a hollow roar that pressurizes your ears, and then settle. And underneath, constant, the water work. Drip. Lick. Drip.

They drink constantly. They have to. The cold dries, the breathing wets, and they live in the balance.

A small one by the east wall leans forward, scrapes. In its wing-claws it holds a thin flake of shale like a knife, three short practiced strokes, and the black-green crust lichen comes off in powder and it eats. It eats paint off rock. Further down, another has a curled sliver of bark — dark, sodden, carried up from impossibly far below, from trees that do not exist for a vertical mile — dips it into a rock cup where its short beak cannot reach, lifts it dripping, sucks it, then without ceremony passes it sideways to the neighbor, who dips and sucks in turn. Down by the pools two are working a pebble against a larger anvil stone: tok, tok, tok, barely audible under the rustle, turning it to refresh an edge for scraping. When the edge dulls, when the thirst is gone, they drop it. The shard joins the other shards. No keeping. Tomorrow a lichen will grow over it. That is the whole technology: use and drop. Hand-tools without workshops. Craft without buildings, without storing, without owning.

And they share heat the way they share water. Once an hour — you can time it if you stay still enough — a shiver starts at the lowest, wettest tier where the cold pools closest, and travels upward around the bowl, a slow wave lifting feather after feather, a shared shudder to keep the edge from freezing. If you are crouched among them with your cheek near feathers, you feel it climb through your own ribs. Your breath, hanging, joins theirs. You condense on stone. For a moment you are part of the water they will lick.

They notice you the way a lake notices a stone. A tightening of clicks, a focusing, then a loss of interest. You are too big to eat and too clumsy to cling and you will leave soon because your fingers have gone stupid and your lungs cannot learn this air. They know how to wait. Their pulse is geological. The scar will widen by a millimeter. A moss cushion, hard as felt in a lee crack, will take another teaspoon of dust. A tuft of white-tipped hair-grass will tremble and hold.

Beneath the chitter, if you put your ear to stone, you hear why they stay facing in: the bowl returns echo wrong. A click comes back a half-beat late and lower, as if the fresh concave plates learned it and repeated it in a rock voice. Old climbers would call it acoustics. Lying there inside the living felt, it feels more deliberate — as if the mountain, too new to have a shape, is using thousands of small throats to practice how to sound, and they, patient, are letting it.
