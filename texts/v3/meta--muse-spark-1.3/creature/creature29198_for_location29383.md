At first you think they are workers.

Standing shoulder to shoulder in the trams coming down the grades, in oilskin that has gone soft and black with wet, in wool gone grey at the cuffs, saying nothing. Men and women both, breath smoking faintly blue in the cold and then caught at once by the sagging steam. You think: mill-hands. A cold-climate shift.

Then you are down among them for an hour and the category will not hold.

They are not loud enough to be workers. They are not hard enough at the edges. They move like people on a boat.

Watch their knees. Everyone walks with knees a little bent, weight low, felt boots finding duckboard and gravel bar and sudden six inches of braid-water without looking down. The ground heaves under them overnight — frost lenses jacking a lane a foot sideways, a privy tilted, a whole terrace suddenly out of level — and they have learned not to trust level. In the morning the crews go out with screw-jacks and long levers to lift the houses back, and the people lift themselves back the same way. No stumbling. No cursing. A small give at the hips. A hand out to a doorframe that is also moving. Children pole punts between warehouses standing up, bare-handed in aching water, and they do not fall in. Old women cross moss bridges between roofs with baskets of cotton tufts, stepping from pelt to pelt fifty feet above an alley, without a rail, chatting in eyebrows.

Eyebrows, elbows, hands. That is the second thing.

They speak low because the town asks them to. Sharp noise, they will tell you if you ask low enough, cracks the new clay. Startles the ground-mud. Brings down rime from the wires. So after a day the loudest things are drips and long-tailed tits and the soft *whump* of a press under its quilt. The people have gone under that.

Their ordinary talk is not talk. In the market braid where it is shoulder to shoulder — fur traders with frost in their beards, eel smokers smelling of sweet oil, moss-cutters shouldering long green bales that drip, ironmongers, wick-sellers, boys trotting with live coal in insulated boxes strapped to their chests — no one shouts prices. A lift of two fingers. A brush of an elbow: *move with me*. A raised eyebrow over a bale: *is this good?* A blink: *yes.* Their voices, when they do use them, are hoarse and underpowered from disuse, more breath than tone, meant to travel three feet and be eaten by moss. Even the dogs have learned it. Big yellow-white yard dogs that could bark will open their mouths and produce a soft chuffed *wuf* under their breath, and look embarrassed.

Physically they are damp people. You become one within an hour. Your coat gains weight. Your eyelashes bead. Theirs never dry. Hair is always a little plastered, skin always a little pruned and pale from condensation, cheeks reddened not by health but by cold steam. Women feeding white-hot rivets to a press — lift the quilt-corner once and see them, in felt boots and leather aprons over wool, faces lit orange from below — sweat and instantly have it bead and chill on their upper lips. Moss seeds lodge in their seams. Most of them have a green tinge at the cuffs, at the shoulders where they have brushed a wall, in the cracks of their boots. They do not pick it off. Keep the quilt on, is the rule for houses and also for bodies. Bare iron screams in the cold, they say, and bare skin does too. So they stay quilted: layered, patched, tarred, mossy at the edges.

Their hands are the most alive part of them. Short, broad, red-knuckled, constantly moving. Because everything here is built light and loose — on skids, on rollers, on stacked peat blocks, nothing with foundations — everything needs tending. A man does not fix his door, he jacks it. He carries wedges in his pocket the way other towns carry money. A woman does not sweep water out of her shop when a braid comes through overnight with eels in it; she cuts a hole in the floor, lays a plank, charges punts a halfpenny toll, and guts the eels for breakfast. Children learn early to mend twine-stitching on a wall-quilt, to comb moss with wooden combs so it lies thirty centimeters thick and drinks sound, to seed a fresh hummock the morning after the warm grey milk wells up at the edge of town and skins into new pitted stone. The survey boys mark it with flags. The moss crews quilt it green before it can erode. By spring a family has a lean-to on it. That opportunism is not greed. It is midwifery. The valley floor was ice not long ago. The till is still blue-grey and unsorted. Erosion hasn't started. The town is acting as its skin, and the people know themselves as skin-cells: to cover, to muffle, to keep warm what is still being born.

So they are not separate from the other labor.

Because the town works with more than people. Above you on the roof-pelts flows the second traffic, as dense as the market, and no one finds it strange. Runners with message tubes, light-footed teenagers who can walk half the town without touching ground. Harbor foxes — small, dark, wet-nosed — trotting purposeful lines with scraps, with bolts, with twine ends in their jaws. Iron-grey rats, sleek and almost blue in winter coat, moving in disciplined columns along the steam pipes, hauling twine, finding lost washers and bringing them back to the shop door for a dab of eel fat. No one owns them exactly. They have joined the industry. Foxes keep the rats honest. Rats keep the shops from losing time. Long-tailed tits erupt in white flurries from a gutter-birch and resettle, and everyone glances up because the tits find the drafts first, the thin spots in the quilt. Hobbled flocks of grey geese weed the roof gardens — crowberry vine, dwarf fern, cotton tuft — honking softly through closed bills, and as they waddle they turn the little paddle-wheels in the culverts. Eels keep the culverts clean, sliding black through braid-water under houses on stilts. Geese, eels, foxes, rats, tits, dogs, children with punts: a single crowded metabolism.

Eat with them once and you taste the place. Smoked eel, oily and sweet. Crowberries black and sharp from a wall-vine outside the window. Goose egg, fish-box herring, peat-tea that tastes of iron. Everything a little damp, even indoors, where condensation beads on every rivet and sweats from the underside of every walkway and drips from eaves onto your neck as you eat. No one wipes it away. Damp is not weather here. There is no big water, only pockets and braids and breath. Damp is the medium, like air.

And they are, for all the silence and wet and constant jacking-level, not gloomy. That is the last thing to resolve, after you have watched long enough for the quiet to stop feeling empty.

In a doorway, a father levels his toddler by the shoulders the way he leveled his house that morning, with a grin and an exaggerated tilt, and the child laughs without sound, mouth wide open. On a wall, among basalt block and tar paper and moss-quilt and lichen and frost and a steam pipe sweating and a fresh crack welling raw mud from last night's heave, there is a child's chalk mark: a boat, a goose, a sun smeared like the real one. Two old moss-cutters, passing on a trestle, touch foreheads briefly instead of speaking. A woman on a tram, standing pressed wall-to-wall, reaches without looking and reties another woman's oilskin hood against the drizzle that is really everyone's breath come back down.

They are frantic and they are gentle. That is what lives here in the pre-erosion green, in the sodden engine-room that will not raise its voice: a people who have agreed to be soft on top and hot underneath, like their factories. Lift a corner, and inside is black heat. Drop the quilt, and the sound is gone.
