At first you think they are workers. That's what the aprons say, the baskets, the bobbins, the short practiced calls across a gap.

Then you watch longer and you revise it: they are gardeners who happen to work machines. Then you watch longer still and you revise it again.

They are the valley's way of keeping the water moving.

They are pale. That is the first thing close up. Not sickly, exactly — but un-sunned, the way something kept under cloud for three generations gets. Faces the color of skim milk, reddened at the nose and cheeks from damp cold. Children pinker, old men greyer, women with hair pulled up hard under oilskin hats to keep it out of the drip. Their eyes are good in gloom. They squint less than you do. They find the edge of a step in flat grey light without looking down.

They dress against wet that never ends. Nobody owns a dry coat. Sweater over sweater, wool gone baggy and smelling always faintly of lanolin and smoke even when clean. Oilskin aprons, cracked at the fold lines and patched with bicycle-tube rubber, tied high. Trousers tucked into boots. Boots laced tight — everyone laces tight, you see them stop to re-tie without thinking, because a loose boot on a dished, moss-slick stair is how you fall three terraces onto somebody's kale boxes. Hats that shed: sou'westers, cut-down hard hats with a brim riveted on, knitted caps under plastic hoods. Gloves with the fingers cut out, because you need fingers even if they go numb and white on wet metal. And their hands — their hands give them away. Cuticles stained permanent green-black that no scrubbing lifts. Nails short. Palms calloused from handrails and hemp rope and basket handles. Even the loom girls, even the clerks in the tally offices behind milky glass, have that stain.

They move like water themselves. Nobody strides. It is too steep, too slick, too narrow for striding. They trot, short-stepped, sideways a little on the steepest stairs, one hand always trailing the rail or the wall — not for balance so much as for reading. The rail tells you who passed, how cold it is, whether the flume above has risen. Porters come through in files, bent under baskets that steam faintly: wet chard, bobbins of grey yarn, coal in sacks, a slopping bucket of eels from the lower sump traps, silver-black and knotting. They don't shout "mind." They click tongue, or tap the basket rim twice with a knuckle, and the person ahead tucks to the weep-wall side without breaking pace. Children learn it before speech: keep to the dry side of the stair, which is never dry, leave the center channel for the water skin.

Because they hear the valley the way you don't yet. You hear muffle. They hear information. A pump kicking on two levels down means the inspection sump on Nine is filling again. A change in the shush of a half-pipe means leaves blinding a grille. A loom thudding off-beat means a shuttle about to jam and someone's brother sleeping behind that wall. So they work quiet — not from politeness at first, but from necessity. Sound carries oddly in the cloud bowl, bounces off wet block and hangs. A shout wakes three shifts. So their language has gone short and low. Single syllables. Names cut to one beat. A hand up means hold the sluice. Two fingers flicked means pull the tarp, rain coming through again. A whistle through teeth, barely louder than a drip, calls a cable bucket to stop. Even arguments are muttered, shoulder to shoulder under an eave while water drums on tarp overhead. Laughter, when it comes, is sudden and brief and covered with a hand.

You think there must be bosses and owned plots, and there are — tally tallies, foremen with oil pencils behind their ears — but the real law is older than any firm and everyone obeys it without being told, because the penalty is immediate and wet.

Keep it moving. Every household, every shed-crew, every loom bay owns a length of gutter, a stretch of drain, a grille. Dawn and dusk you see them out with hooked poles, with hand scoops, with bare hands in cold rush: clearing lady-fern fronds, potato tops, a drowned pigeon, silt black as ink. The boy you saw with the pole at the side channel — there are a hundred of him. Girls too. At 3 p.m. the market alley packs up in seven minutes, stalls on wheels rolling back, trestles folded, because the drain beneath must be rodded and everybody knows the rod-man's trot. If you block a drain for more than minutes your neighbor's roof — which is your floor, which is someone else's ceiling — floods, and they will be up through your hatch with a bucket and a look before you have finished apologizing. No one steals water and no one wastes it. You lift seep to the header tanks, you bleed it to the boxes, you let it fall to the turbines. A child can trace for you where your sink water goes for the next eleven levels down.

Down is someone else's roof. That is the second law, learned in the knees. There is no ground to appeal to. A toddler's first geography is vertical: up-terrace, down-terrace, across-flume. Old women sit on upturned drums with their feet tucked under them and shell beans exactly where the roof is strongest, near the parapet where the joists bear. Men mend a cableway sitting astride it, boots dangling over a sixty-foot drop into mist that is really only the next street. Nobody leans on a parapet without first glancing at what is growing on it — brambles trained along wires, runner beans up strings from oil drums, potatoes in sacking — and nobody steps off a path without checking for moss depth. Moss is both carpet and warning. An inch thick and springy means that roof is tended and walked. Thick enough to swallow your boot to the ankle means no one has been there for a season and the membrane beneath may be soft. They scrape it with flat spades, harvest it in sheets for packing and insulation and mushroom bedding, and leave exactly enough to keep the quiet. A doorway mossed over is a shame. It means someone stopped tending, died, moved down-mist.

And grow, they all grow. There is no farmer caste because there is no farm. There is only wherever you can put a container. The south-facing mill walls are owned in ten-foot vertical allotments, fought over, inherited, marked with paint: tiers of boxes and pallets, straw bales tucked under stairs fruiting oyster mushrooms in pale shelves, kale gone leggy in dim light but sweet from cold, potatoes earthed up in ash and leaf mold, geraniums in every window box not for food but for something to be red. Old men who spent forty years listening to turbines spend their off-shifts pricking out seedlings in eggshells on a windowsill. Loom women keep herbs in tin cans on the milky windows where condensation waters them. The mushroom caverns on the third under-terrace are run by a family that barely sees daylight, white-eyed, magenta-lit, their skin almost translucent, their coats smelling of earth and bleach. The eel trappers are boys and grandfathers, waders patched, hands quick in black water. Up on the inspection bridge you meet the header-tank keeper, a woman in her sixties with a wrench on a lanyard and a bunch of grapes in her pocket for her grandchildren, who can tell by vibration through her boots whether No. 2 turbine is cavitating.

They eat what the vertical allows, and it has made them lean and broad-shouldered. Potatoes, kale, beans, mushrooms, watercress cut from the clean flumes, eels smoked over coal dust, bread brought up by funicular from below-mist where you cannot see, tea drunk scalding from enamel mugs held in both hands to warm them. At noon the stalls ladle soup under tarps — barley, nettle, eel — and the queue is quiet, breath showing, spoons clinking on tin. No one eats walking on stairs. You stand aside, under cover, out of the water lane.

They sleep in shifts, thin-walled. Behind every loom wall is a bunk, behind every pump house a cot with a curtain. The night shift sleeps while the day shift thuds, so day voices stay low. You see a man coming off nights pad past a market in socks to spare his mates' sleep, boots strung round his neck. Curtains are heavy, blankets are army surplus, earplugs are moss rolled in wax. Children are taught to play without screaming — a game of chasing along a terrace becomes a silent, breathless, splashing race, ending in stifled giggles under a conveyor housing with the pigeons.

They are not healthy in the lowland sense. There is rheum in the mornings, coughs that hang on, chilblains, knees that go early from stairs. But they are strong in the calves and forearms, and their lungs have learned thin air. Women carry pregnancies up and down six hundred steps a day until the week they deliver. Old people keep working until they can't climb, and then they get a ground-floor — which means a level with no more stairs below it for a while — and a south box to tend, and children bring them roddings news.

If you ask where they are from, they look puzzled. From here, two levels up. From the dye-works terrace where my mother rodded. Some came up the funicular as apprentices and never left because the mist closed over and the stairs got into their legs. No one talks about leaving. Why would you? The valley provides — not richly, but completely: work, water, greens, heat from the mills, light amber at noon, company so close you can hear your neighbor turn in bed. The outside is a rumor of dryness, of sun that hurts, of flat ground where water just sits and does nothing, of silence without machines. It sounds lonely.

By the third day you stop seeing them as individuals hauling panels and rodding drains. You see the pattern they make together — the sudden scaffold crewed, the tarp pulled just before rain, the sluice opened and the grille cleared in one motion by hands that never needed to be asked. Not an army. Not a family. More like a tissue. Like moss itself, inch-thick, velvet, holding the concrete together, drinking the drip and softening the sound, green forcing up through grey.
